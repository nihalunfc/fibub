**Chemistry · Class 11 · Chapter 9 · about 120 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name and draw alkanes, alkenes, alkynes and arenes, explain conformations and geometrical isomerism, write their preparation and reactions, apply Markovnikov’s rule and the peroxide effect, decide whether a ring is aromatic and predict where a second group enters a benzene ring.

<div class="bigidea">Hydrocarbons are made only of carbon and hydrogen, but they are the raw material for fuels, plastics, medicines and dyes. They fall into three behaviours. <b>Alkanes</b> (only single bonds) are unreactive and mostly burn or are substituted. <b>Alkenes and alkynes</b> have π bonds whose loosely held electrons are attacked by electrophiles, so they <i>add</i> things. <b>Benzene</b> has a special, extra-stable ring of electrons, so it keeps the ring and <i>substitutes</i>.</div>

## 1. Classification and the four families

| Family | Bonds | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkanes (paraffins) | all single | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | CH₃CH₃ ethane |
| Alkenes (olefins) | one C=C | CₙH₂ₙ | CH₂=CH₂ ethene |
| Alkynes | one C≡C | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | HC≡CH ethyne |
| Arenes (aromatic) | benzene ring | e.g. C₆H₆ | benzene, toluene |

Alkanes are **saturated**; alkenes, alkynes and arenes are **unsaturated**. The number of H atoms missing from the saturated formula tells you the **degree of unsaturation**: `$\dfrac{2C + 2 - H}{2}$`. Each ring or double bond counts 1 and each triple bond counts 2. Benzene, C₆H₆, has 4.

## 2. Alkanes

### 2.1 Structure, naming and isomerism

Each carbon in an alkane is sp³ with bond angles of about 109.5°. Naming follows the IUPAC rules of the previous chapter: the longest chain gives the parent (methane, ethane, propane, butane, pentane...), branches are named and numbered from the end that gives the lowest numbers: 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane. From C₄ onward, alkanes show **chain isomerism**: C₄H₁₀ has 2 isomers, C₅H₁₂ has 3, C₆H₁₄ has 5, C₇H₁₆ has 9.

### 2.2 Physical properties

Alkanes are non-polar and held together only by weak **London (dispersion) forces**. These get stronger with more surface contact, so:

- C₁ to C₄ are gases, C₅ to C₁₇ are liquids, higher ones are waxy solids (candle wax, paraffin).
- Boiling point rises steadily with chain length (about 20–30 K for each extra CH₂).
- **Branching lowers the boiling point**: a round molecule touches its neighbours at fewer points. Compare pentane (309 K), isopentane (301 K) and neopentane (283 K).
- They float on water, because they are less dense and not soluble in it, but dissolve in non-polar solvents. Petrol and water do not mix.

### 2.3 Conformations

Rotation about a C–C single bond is almost free, so a molecule such as ethane can take countless shapes called **conformations** (or rotamers). Two extremes of ethane are **staggered** (hydrogens as far apart as possible, lowest energy) and **eclipsed** (hydrogens directly in front of each other, highest energy). The eclipsed form is about **12 kJ/mol** higher, because the electron clouds of the hydrogens repel each other (torsional strain). The molecule passes through the barrier millions of times a second at room temperature, so the conformers cannot be separated. A **Newman projection** looks straight down the C–C bond; the front carbon is a dot with three bonds, the back carbon is a circle.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@chem-newman-ethane"><figcaption>Ethane turning about its C–C bond, with the energy curve: three staggered minima and three eclipsed maxima.</figcaption></figure>

Butane has more interesting conformers because the central carbons carry CH₃ groups. When those two CH₃ groups are 180° apart (**anti**) the energy is lowest. At 60° (**gauche**) it is about 3.8 kJ/mol higher, and when the CH₃ groups eclipse each other the energy is highest (about 19 kJ/mol).

### 2.4 Preparation (laboratory methods)

- **Hydrogenation of alkenes and alkynes** (Sabatier–Senderens): CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃ with Ni, Pt or Pd.
- **Reduction of alkyl halides:** R–X + H₂ (Zn, dilute HCl) → R–H + HX.
- **Wurtz reaction:** 2R–X + 2Na (dry ether) → R–R + 2NaX. It makes alkanes with twice the carbons: 2CH₃Br → CH₃CH₃. It works best for symmetric alkanes.
- **Decarboxylation:** sodium salt of a carboxylic acid heated with soda lime: CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃ (one carbon fewer).
- **Kolbe electrolysis:** electrolysis of aqueous sodium or potassium carboxylate: 2CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃CH₃ + 2CO₂ + 2e⁻ at the anode.

### 2.5 Chemical properties

Alkanes are unreactive (strong C–C and C–H bonds, non-polar). The main reactions:

- **Substitution (halogenation):** CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl in light or at 520–670 K. It is a **free-radical chain reaction**. Fluorine is too violent and iodine too slow and reversible.
- **Combustion:** `$\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y + \left(x+\tfrac y4\right)\mathrm{O}_2 \to x\,\mathrm{CO}_2 + \tfrac y2\,\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$`, strongly exothermic: the reason for LPG, petrol and CNG. Incomplete burning gives CO and soot.
- **Controlled oxidation:** methane with Mo₂O₃ gives methanal; with Cu at 523 K and 100 atm, alkanes give alcohols; with KMnO₄, tertiary alkanes give tertiary alcohols.
- **Isomerisation:** n-butane → isobutane with AlCl₃/HCl at about 570 K.
- **Aromatisation:** n-hexane at 773 K, 10–20 atm over Cr₂O₃/V₂O₅ gives benzene.
- **Pyrolysis (cracking):** heating large alkanes without air breaks them into smaller alkanes and alkenes (the way petrol is made from kerosene).

<div class="step">Initiation: UV light splits the Cl–Cl bond homolytically into two chlorine radicals.</div>
<div class="step">Propagation 1: a Cl· atom removes H from methane, giving HCl and the methyl radical ·CH₃.</div>
<div class="step">Propagation 2: ·CH₃ takes a Cl from Cl₂, giving CH₃Cl and a new Cl·.</div>
<div class="step">The new Cl· starts the cycle again: one photon can cause thousands of cycles. That is why it is a chain reaction.</div>
<div class="step">Termination: two radicals meet and combine. The chain stops, but this happens rarely.</div>
<div class="step">With excess Cl₂ the substitution goes on: CH₃Cl, CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄.</div>

## 3. Alkenes

### 3.1 Structure and nomenclature

The C=C carbons are sp² and the groups around each lie in a plane at about 120°. The double bond is one σ bond and one π bond. The π electrons stick out above and below the plane and are easy for electrophiles to attack. Naming: choose the longest chain that **contains the double bond**, number it so the double bond gets the lowest number, and end the name in **-ene**: but-1-ene, 2-methylpropene, pent-2-ene.

### 3.2 Isomerism

- **Structural isomers:** chain (but-1-ene and 2-methylpropene), position (but-1-ene and but-2-ene), and ring-chain (but-1-ene and cyclobutane).
- **Geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism.** The π bond stops rotation, so groups fixed on each side of the double bond are either on the **same side (cis)** or on **opposite sides (trans)**. It needs each carbon of the double bond to carry two *different* groups. Trans-but-2-ene has a higher melting point (it packs better), cis has a slightly higher boiling point (it is slightly polar).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11c-hc-geom"><figcaption>Twist the back carbon. For C–C only 12 kJ/mol is needed at the top of the barrier, so rotation is free. For C=C you must break the π bond: about 260 kJ/mol at 90°, which never happens at room temperature, so cis and trans are separate compounds.</figcaption></figure>

### 3.3 Preparation

- **Partial hydrogenation of alkynes:** Lindlar’s catalyst (Pd/BaSO₄ with quinoline) gives cis-alkenes; Na in liquid NH₃ gives trans-alkenes.
- **Dehydrohalogenation** (elimination) of haloalkanes: CH₃CH₂Br + alcoholic KOH → CH₂=CH₂. When more than one alkene can form, the more substituted one is the major product (Saytzeff’s rule).
- **Dehalogenation of vicinal dihalides** with Zn: BrCH₂CH₂Br + Zn → CH₂=CH₂ + ZnBr₂.
- **Dehydration of alcohols** with conc. H₂SO₄ at 443 K: CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O.

### 3.4 Properties

**Physical:** like alkanes, but slightly more polar, and the first three (C₂ to C₄) are gases.

**Chemical:** addition across the double bond. A weak π bond is exchanged for two stronger σ bonds, so addition is exothermic.

- **Hydrogen** (Ni, Pt or Pd): alkene → alkane.
- **Halogens:** CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → BrCH₂CH₂Br. The orange bromine water is **decolourised**: a test for unsaturation.
- **Hydrogen halides:** CH₂=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CH₂Br. For an unsymmetrical alkene, **Markovnikov’s rule** applies: *the negative part of the reagent adds to the carbon with fewer hydrogens.* In modern terms: the electrophile H⁺ adds to give the more stable carbocation.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11c-hc-markov">
<div class="step">Propene meets HBr. The H of HBr is δ+ (an electrophile) and the C=C π bond is electron-rich.</div>
<div class="step">The π electrons attack the H; the H–Br bond breaks, with the pair going to Br.</div>
<div class="step">Two carbocations are possible: secondary CH₃–C⁺H–CH₃ (6 α C–H) or primary CH₃CH₂C⁺H₂ (3 α C–H).</div>
<div class="step">The secondary carbocation is more stable and forms much faster, so nearly all the reaction goes by this path.</div>
<div class="step">Bromide attacks the carbocation, giving 2-bromopropane: the Markovnikov product.</div>
<div class="step">With a peroxide, HBr adds through free radicals, and the order is reversed: 1-bromopropane (anti-Markovnikov, the Kharasch effect, only for HBr).</div>
</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@chem-markovnikov"><figcaption>An energy race of the two HBr addition paths for four alkenes. The polar path favours the more stable carbocation; with peroxide the radical path reverses the result.</figcaption></figure>

- **Sulphuric acid:** CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂SO₄ → CH₃CH(OSO₃H)CH₃; with water, an alcohol is formed (propan-2-ol), following Markovnikov.
- **Oxidation.** Cold, dilute, alkaline KMnO₄ (Baeyer’s reagent) gives a **diol** and its purple colour fades; hot, acidified KMnO₄ cuts the double bond and gives acids or ketones. **Ozonolysis** (O₃, then Zn/H₂O) cuts the bond and gives aldehydes or ketones; it is used to find the position of a double bond.
- **Polymerisation:** many alkene molecules join: n CH₂=CH₂ → (–CH₂–CH₂–)ₙ polythene; propene gives polypropene.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11c-hc-ozone"><figcaption>Choose an alkene and a reagent. Ozonolysis cuts at C=C and caps each end with C=O; hot KMnO₄ also turns aldehydes into acids; cold dilute KMnO₄ only adds two OH groups.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“Anti-Markovnikov addition means Markovnikov’s rule is broken.”** Not at all. The rule is a consequence of carbocation stability: in the polar mechanism the more stable (secondary) cation forms faster. With a peroxide and HBr a different intermediate, a carbon radical, forms, and the more stable radical is again the more substituted one, but now Br· is the first to add to the less-substituted carbon. A different mechanism gives a different product; the logic of “most stable intermediate” still holds.

</div>

## 4. Alkynes

### 4.1 Structure, naming, isomerism

The C≡C carbons are sp, bond angle 180° (linear). The triple bond is one σ and two π bonds, and is shorter (120 pm) and stronger than a double bond. Names end in **-yne**: ethyne (acetylene), propyne, but-1-yne, but-2-yne. Isomerism: chain and position (but-1-yne and but-2-yne), also ring-chain with alkadienes (buta-1,3-diene has the same formula, C₄H₆).

### 4.2 Preparation

- **From calcium carbide:** CaC₂ + 2H₂O → HC≡CH + Ca(OH)₂ (the lab and industrial method; welders’ “carbide lamp”).
- **From vicinal dihalides:** BrCH₂CH₂Br + 2KOH(alc) → HC≡CH + 2KBr + 2H₂O.

### 4.3 Acidic character and reactions

An sp carbon holds its bonding electrons close to the nucleus (50% s-character), so the **terminal C–H is weakly acidic** (pKa about 25, much more acidic than alkenes and alkanes). It reacts with sodium (H₂ is released) and with ammoniacal silver nitrate (white precipitate) or ammoniacal cuprous chloride (red precipitate): a test for a terminal alkyne.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11c-hc-alkyne"><figcaption>Ethane, ethene and ethyne side by side. As the s-character rises from 25% to 50%, the C–C bond shortens, strengthens and the C–H becomes more acidic.</figcaption></figure>

Addition reactions follow those of alkenes, in two stages:

- **H₂** (Ni): alkyne → alkene → alkane. Lindlar’s catalyst stops at cis-alkene.
- **Halogens:** HC≡CH + Br₂ → CHBr=CHBr, then → CHBr₂CHBr₂; decolourises bromine water.
- **HX:** follows Markovnikov: propyne + HBr → 2-bromopropene, then 2,2-dibromopropane.
- **Water** (HgSO₄, dilute H₂SO₄, Kucherov reaction): an unstable enol forms and rearranges to a carbonyl: HC≡CH → CH₃CHO (ethanal); other alkynes give ketones.
- **Polymerisation:** ethyne passed through a red-hot iron tube at 873 K gives benzene (cyclic polymerisation, 3 C₂H₂ → C₆H₆).

<div class="myth">

**“An alkyne has two double bonds.”** It has one σ bond and two π bonds, a different thing. Allenes (C=C=C) and conjugated dienes have two double bonds. The cylinder of π electrons around a triple bond gives it a different reactivity and the acidity of its terminal hydrogen.

</div>

## 5. Aromatic hydrocarbons

### 5.1 Nomenclature and isomerism

Benzene, C₆H₆, is the parent. Monosubstituted: toluene (methylbenzene), chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene, phenol (hydroxy), aniline. For two groups on the ring use **ortho (1,2), meta (1,3), para (1,4)**: o-xylene is 1,2-dimethylbenzene. The aromatic ring name is used with a number if there are three or more groups.

### 5.2 Structure of benzene and aromaticity

Benzene is a planar hexagon of sp² carbons, with C–C–C angles of 120°. Each carbon contributes one p orbital, and the six p orbitals overlap sideways to form a **continuous cloud of 6 π electrons** above and below the ring. All six C–C bonds are 139 pm, between a single (154 pm) and a double bond (134 pm). Kekulé drew two structures with alternating double bonds, and the real benzene is their **resonance hybrid**: this is why benzene does not decolourise bromine water or KMnO₄, and why it substitutes rather than adds. It is extra stable by about **150 kJ/mol** (the resonance energy: the hydrogenation heat is 150 kJ/mol less than three times that of cyclohexene).

**Aromaticity (Hückel rule):** a species is aromatic if it is **cyclic, planar, fully conjugated** and has **(4n + 2) π electrons** (n = 0, 1, 2…: 2, 6, 10, 14…).

### 5.3 Preparation of benzene

- **Cyclic polymerisation of ethyne** through a red-hot iron tube (873 K).
- **Decarboxylation:** sodium benzoate heated with soda lime gives benzene.
- **Reduction of phenol:** phenol passed over heated zinc dust gives benzene.

### 5.4 Properties

Benzene is a colourless, burning liquid, insoluble in water, with a characteristic smell. It is **carcinogenic** (it is known to cause leukaemia) and toxic. Larger polynuclear hydrocarbons (benzopyrene, from burnt coal, tobacco smoke, charred food) are also carcinogenic: they slip into DNA and distort it.

**Electrophilic substitution** is the main reaction. The attacking electrophile E⁺ is made by a reagent, attacks the π cloud, forms an unstable **arenium ion**, and then loses H⁺ so that the aromatic ring forms again.

- **Nitration:** conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ at about 330 K: C₆H₆ → C₆H₅NO₂ (electrophile NO₂⁺).
- **Halogenation:** Cl₂ or Br₂ with FeCl₃ or FeBr₃ (Lewis acid): C₆H₆ → C₆H₅Cl.
- **Sulphonation:** fuming H₂SO₄ (oleum): C₆H₆ → C₆H₅SO₃H (electrophile SO₃).
- **Friedel–Crafts alkylation:** R–Cl + anhydrous AlCl₃ → C₆H₅R. **Acylation:** RCOCl + AlCl₃ → C₆H₅COR.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11c-hc-fc">
<div class="step">A Lewis acid (AlCl₃) pulls a chlorine from CH₃Cl and produces the electrophile CH₃⁺.</div>
<div class="step">The ring’s π electrons attack the electrophile.</div>
<div class="step">An arenium ion forms: the ring loses its aromaticity for a moment and the positive charge is spread over three carbons.</div>
<div class="step">The H on the attacked carbon leaves as H⁺ and the ring becomes aromatic again.</div>
<div class="step">The result is methylbenzene: one hydrogen of the ring has been replaced.</div>
</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@chem-eas-nitration"><figcaption>Nitration of benzene step by step: NO₂⁺ forms, attacks the ring and H⁺ is lost. The figure also compares how OH, CH₃, Cl and NO₂ direct a second group.</figcaption></figure>

**Addition reactions** are possible only under forcing conditions: hydrogenation (Ni, 475 K, high pressure) gives cyclohexane, and chlorine in sunlight gives hexachlorocyclohexane (BHC, an insecticide). Benzene ozonolyses as well, giving glyoxal.

### 5.5 Directive influence of a functional group

A group already on the ring decides *where* the next one goes, and whether the ring reacts faster or slower than benzene.

- **Ortho–para directing and activating:** –OH, –NH₂, –OR, –CH₃ and other alkyl groups. They give electrons to the ring (resonance or +I), making ortho and para carbons richer.
- **Meta directing and deactivating:** –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COOH, –SO₃H. They pull electrons out of the ring, so the ortho and para positions are the poorest and meta is the least poor.
- **Halogens (–Cl, –Br)** are the exception: deactivating (−I effect) but ortho–para directing (resonance by their lone pairs).

<div class="keypoints">

- Alkanes: sp³, saturated, unreactive; main reactions are free-radical substitution (chain: initiation, propagation, termination), combustion and cracking. Boiling point rises with chain length and falls with branching.
- Conformations of ethane: staggered (lowest) and eclipsed (about 12 kJ/mol higher). Butane: anti < gauche < eclipsed < CH₃/CH₃ eclipsed.
- Alkenes: sp², π bond, cis–trans isomers. Add H₂, X₂, HX (Markovnikov); peroxide with HBr reverses it. KMnO₄ cold gives diol, hot or ozonolysis cuts the bond.
- Alkynes: sp, linear, terminal C–H is acidic (pKa about 25); give Ag/Cu acetylides; hydration gives ethanal (from ethyne) or ketones.
- Benzene: planar, 6 π electrons, all C–C 139 pm, resonance energy about 150 kJ/mol; substitutes, does not add easily.
- Aromatic: cyclic + planar + conjugated + (4n + 2) π electrons.
- Electrophilic substitution: nitration, halogenation, sulphonation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation, via the arenium ion.
- o/p directors: –OH, –NH₂, –CH₃, –Cl (deactivating); meta directors: –NO₂, –CN, –COOH, –SO₃H.
- Combustion: CₓHᵧ + (x + y/4) O₂ → x CO₂ + (y/2) H₂O.

</div>
