**Chemistry · Class 11 · Chapter 3 · about 85 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe how the periodic table grew, read the period, group and block of any element from its configuration, name elements with Z above 100, and explain and predict the trends in atomic radius, ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, electronegativity and valence.

<div class="bigidea">If you line up the elements by atomic number, their properties do not just change: they <b>repeat</b>. Every so often there is another soft, reactive metal, then a group of metals, then non-metals, and then an unreactive gas. The periodic table is a map made from that repetition. And the repetition has one cause: after each noble gas, a new electron shell begins, so the same outer-shell patterns come round again.</div>

## 1. How the periodic table was built

By 1860 about 60 elements were known, with no order. Chemists looked for patterns.

- **Döbereiner’s triads (1829).** Groups of three elements with similar properties, in which the middle atomic mass is roughly the average of the other two: lithium, sodium, potassium (7, 23, 39: (7 + 39)/2 = 23); chlorine, bromine, iodine. Only a few triads exist.
- **Newlands’ law of octaves (1865).** Arranged by atomic mass, every eighth element resembled the first, like notes in music. It failed after calcium, and the name was laughed at.
- **Mendeleev’s periodic law (1869).** The properties of elements are a periodic function of their **atomic masses**. He arranged elements by increasing mass but put similar ones in the same column, **even if that broke the mass order** (tellurium before iodine). Most importantly he **left gaps** for elements not yet found and predicted their properties.

## 2. The modern periodic law

Moseley found that the square root of the frequency of X-rays from an element rises in a straight line with its atomic number Z. Mass order fails for some pairs (Ar 39.9 and K 39.1, Te 127.6 and I 126.9), but Z order never does. So the **modern periodic law** is:

<div class="meaning">

**The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.**

<dl>
<dt>Why periodic?</dt><dd>Chemical properties depend on the outer electrons. Every time a new shell starts (Li, Na, K, Rb …) a similar outer configuration (ns¹) is repeated, so similar properties recur.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

## 3. The long form of the periodic table

The modern table has **18 vertical columns (groups)** and **7 horizontal rows (periods)**. A **period** is a row: elements in it have the same highest shell. The length of each period is set by how many electrons fill that shell’s subshells:

| Period | Fills | Elements |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1s | 2 |
| 2 | 2s 2p | 8 |
| 3 | 3s 3p | 8 |
| 4 | 4s 3d 4p | 18 |
| 5 | 5s 4d 5p | 18 |
| 6 | 6s 4f 5d 6p | 32 |
| 7 | 7s 5f 6d 7p | 32 (118 so far) |

A **group** is a column: elements in a group have the same outer configuration, so similar properties. The 14 lanthanoids (Ce–Lu, after La) and 14 actinoids (Th–Lr, after Ac) are drawn below the main table to keep it narrow.

### Electronic configuration and the four blocks

The block of an element is the type of subshell that receives its last electron.

- **s-block:** groups 1 and 2 (ns¹ and ns²): the alkali metals and the alkaline earth metals. Soft, reactive, very electropositive. (Hydrogen also sits at the top of group 1; helium (1s²) is a p-block noble gas placed with group 18.)
- **p-block:** groups 13 to 18 (ns² np¹ to ns² np⁶): includes metals, metalloids and non-metals. Group 17 are the **halogens**; group 18 the **noble gases** (full outer shell, very unreactive).
- **d-block:** groups 3 to 12, the **transition elements** ((n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻²): hard metals with variable oxidation states, coloured compounds and catalytic activity.
- **f-block:** the inner transition elements, lanthanoids and actinoids ((n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴ (n−1)d⁰⁻¹ ns²).

Rules for reading the position: the **period** is the highest value of n; the **block** is the type of the last subshell filled; the **group** is the number of outer s electrons for the s-block, 10 + the number of outer (s + p) electrons for the p-block, and the number of (n−1)d + ns electrons for the d-block.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11c-period-find">
<div class="step">Write the configuration of bromine (Z = 35): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵.</div>
<div class="step">Period = the highest n, which is 4. Bromine is in the fourth row.</div>
<div class="step">The last electron enters a 4p orbital, so bromine is a p-block element.</div>
<div class="step">The outer shell 4s² 4p⁵ has 2 + 5 = 7 electrons, so the group is 10 + 7 = 17.</div>
<div class="step">Answer: period 4, group 17, p-block, a halogen (non-metal).</div>
</div>

**Metals, non-metals and metalloids.** About 80 % of elements are metals (shiny, malleable, conducting, forming positive ions, found to the left and centre). Non-metals (top right) are poor conductors and form negative ions or covalent bonds. A zig-zag line separates them; the elements next to it, **metalloids** such as B, Si, Ge, As, Sb and Te, have in-between properties and are used as semiconductors.

### Names of elements with Z above 100

New elements are made in laboratories in tiny amounts, lasting seconds. Until the discovery is confirmed and a name is approved, IUPAC gives a **temporary systematic name** built from the digits of the atomic number: nil (0), un (1), bi (2), tri (3), quad (4), pent (5), hex (6), sept (7), oct (8), enn (9), with “ium” at the end. The symbol uses the first letters. Element 114 was *ununquadium*, symbol Uuq; element 118 was *ununoctium* (Uuo), and is now **oganesson (Og)**.

## 4. Periodic trends

Trends across a period (left to right) are driven by a rising nuclear charge pulling electrons into the **same** shell; trends down a group (top to bottom) are driven by each new element adding a **new inner shell**. Two ideas explain nearly all of them:

- **Effective nuclear charge** `$Z_{\text{eff}}$`: the pull the outer electron really feels. Inner electrons **shield** it from some of the nucleus, so `$Z_{\text{eff}} < Z$`. Across a period Z rises by 1 each step while shielding by same-shell electrons is poor, so Z_eff rises. Down a group, new shells add shielding, so Z_eff changes little but the outer electron is much farther away.
- **Distance:** a bigger shell means the outer electron is farther away and less tightly held.

### Atomic radius

The **atomic radius** is half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms (covalent radius for non-metals, metallic radius for metals, van der Waals radius for noble gases, which is larger). It **decreases across a period** (greater Z_eff, same shell) and **increases down a group** (a new shell each time). Cation radius is smaller than the parent atom (fewer electrons, and often a whole shell lost); anion radius is bigger (more electrons repel each other while the nuclear charge is the same).

**Isoelectronic species** have the same number of electrons. For them the radius falls as the nuclear charge rises: `$\text{N}^{3-} > \text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^{-} > \text{Na}^{+} > \text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Al}^{3+}$`.

### Ionisation enthalpy

The **first ionisation enthalpy** (ΔᵢH₁) is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from one mole of gaseous atoms: `$\text{X(g)} \to \text{X}^{+}\text{(g)} + e^{-}$`. It is always positive. It **increases across a period** and **decreases down a group**, with two familiar dips in period 2 and 3: Be → B (the 2p electron is easier to remove than a paired 2s) and N → O (O’s paired 2p electron is repelled by its partner). Successive enthalpies always rise (ΔᵢH₂ > ΔᵢH₁) because it is harder to remove an electron from a positive ion; there is a huge jump when the next electron has to come from a stable inner shell.

### Electron gain enthalpy

The **electron gain enthalpy** (Δ_egH) is the enthalpy change when a gaseous atom takes up an electron: `$\text{X(g)} + e^{-} \to \text{X}^{-}\text{(g)}$`. For most elements it is negative (energy is released). The more negative, the more easily the atom gains an electron. It becomes more negative across a period (halogens have the largest values) and less negative down a group. The most negative are not fluorine but **chlorine** (−349 kJ/mol) because fluorine’s small 2p shell is crowded and its extra electron is repelled. Noble gases and Be, Mg have positive values (they would need a new subshell). The second electron gain is always positive (O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻ needs energy) because a negative ion repels the electron.

### Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** is the ability of an *atom in a molecule* to attract the shared electrons. Unlike ionisation enthalpy, it is not a measured energy and has no unit; the **Pauling scale** gives F = 3.98 (highest), O = 3.44, Cl = 3.16, N = 3.04, C = 2.55, H = 2.20 and Cs about 0.7. It follows the same trend as the other attractions: it increases across a period and decreases down a group. A difference of 1.7 or more usually gives an ionic bond; smaller differences give polar or non-polar covalent bonds.

**“Atomic size increases from left to right across a period because the atomic number increases.”** The opposite is true. More protons pull the electrons of the same shell in more tightly, so the radius falls from lithium (128 pm) to fluorine (57 pm). Radius grows down a group only because a whole new shell is added.

## 5. Periodic trends and chemical reactivity

**Valence.** The valence is the number of electrons an atom uses to bond. Across period 3 the valence in oxides rises 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 (Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₃, Cl₂O₇) and in hydrides falls 4 to 1 (SiH₄, PH₃, H₂S, HCl). Elements of a group have the same valence.

**Metallic and non-metallic character.** Low ionisation enthalpy means an element loses electrons easily: metallic character. It **decreases across a period** and **increases down a group**. So the most metallic element (apart from the very rare ones) is at the lower left (caesium, francium); the most non-metallic is at the top right (fluorine).

**Nature of oxides.** The oxides of metals are basic (ionic); those of non-metals are acidic (covalent); and some, like Al₂O₃ and ZnO, are **amphoteric** (react with both acids and bases). So along period 3, the oxide changes from strongly basic (Na₂O) to strongly acidic (Cl₂O₇).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11c-period-oxides"><figcaption>Slide along period 3. The oxide changes from basic and ionic (Na₂O) through amphoteric (Al₂O₃) to acidic and covalent (Cl₂O₇), with the reaction with water for each.</figcaption></figure>

**Reactivity.** In the s-block metals, reactivity grows down the group (Li reacts gently with water, Cs explosively). In the halogens, reactivity falls down the group (F₂ is the most reactive). Across a period, reactivity first falls and then rises: the most reactive ones are at the two ends, while the noble gases, with their full shells, are not reactive at all. Cricket analogy: the openers and the last-wicket batters are the most “reactive” in an innings (they want to give or take runs), while the set batter in the middle, with a perfectly balanced shell, just stays.

<div class="history">

**Beyond the syllabus (trimmed from the rationalised Class 11 book; check your board’s list).** Anomalous properties of the second-period elements (Li, Be, B) and the diagonal relationship (Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si) are no longer asked in many boards. In short: the first element of a group is unusually small and has no d orbitals, so it differs from the rest of its group and resembles the element diagonally below and to the right. Likewise, Slater’s rules for calculating Z_eff are beyond the syllabus.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Döbereiner (triads), Newlands (octaves), Mendeleev (atomic mass, gaps and predictions), Moseley (atomic number).
- Modern law: properties are periodic functions of the atomic number, because the outer configurations repeat.
- 18 groups and 7 periods; period = highest n; block = type of the last subshell; p-block group = 10 + outer electrons.
- IUPAC names for Z above 100: nil, un, bi, tri, quad, pent, hex, sept, oct, enn + ium.
- Across a period: radius falls, ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy (more negative) and electronegativity rise. Down a group: radius rises, the others fall.
- Isoelectronic ions shrink as Z rises. A cation is smaller and an anion bigger than its atom.
- A big jump in successive ionisation enthalpies shows the number of valence electrons.
- Oxides go from basic (Na₂O) through amphoteric (Al₂O₃) to acidic (Cl₂O₇) across period 3.

</div>
