Try each question on paper before opening the answer. Marks go for a clear structure, the correct rule, correct numbering, working shown and units where needed.

<div class="bigidea">Most questions in this chapter use one of five skills: name or draw a structure, list isomers, rank stability by electronic effects, pick a purification method, and calculate a percentage from analysis data.</div>

## How to set out an answer

1. For a name: draw the longest chain, number it, then write prefixes, parent and suffix.
2. For isomers: change one thing at a time: the chain, then the position, then the functional group.
3. For stability: count the alkyl groups or the resonance structures and say which effect operates.
4. For analysis: write the formula for the percentage, substitute with units, and give the answer with a unit.

## 1-mark questions

1. State the hybridisation of carbon in CH₃–C≡N (at the nitrile carbon).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>sp (a triple bond and one single bond: two σ regions, linear).</details>

2. Write the IUPAC name of CH₃CH₂CHO.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Propanal.</details>

3. Which effect operates in the pull of electrons by chlorine in CH₃CH₂Cl through sigma bonds?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The −I (inductive) effect.</details>

4. Give one use of Lassaigne’s sodium extract.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It converts N, S and halogens in an organic compound into ionic salts (NaCN, Na₂S, NaX) that can be detected by simple tests.</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

5. (2) Write the IUPAC names of (a) CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ and (b) (CH₃)₃C–CH=CH₂.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) Propan-2-ol. (b) 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene (the double bond gets the lowest number, 1, and two methyl groups sit on C3).</details>

6. (2) What is the difference between a nucleophile and an electrophile? Give one example of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A nucleophile donates an electron pair (electron-rich): OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃. An electrophile accepts an electron pair (electron-poor): NO₂⁺, H⁺, a carbocation.</details>

7. (2) Why is ClCH₂COOH a stronger acid than CH₃COOH?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Chlorine has a −I effect that pulls electrons away from the carboxylate group and stabilises the anion ClCH₂COO⁻, so the acid ionises more easily.</details>

8. (3) Draw the chain, position and functional isomers of C₃H₈O and C₄H₁₀O that you can find, naming each type.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

C₃H₈O: propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol (position isomers); methoxyethane (functional isomer).

C₄H₁₀O: butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol (position); 2-methylpropan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol (chain); diethyl ether and methyl propyl ether (functional; the two ethers are metamers of each other).

</details>

9. (3) Explain why the (CH₃)₃C⁺ ion is more stable than the CH₃CH₂⁺ ion.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The tert-butyl cation has three methyl groups, each pushing electrons (+I) and each offering three α C–H bonds for hyperconjugation: 9 in total. The ethyl cation has one methyl group and 3 α C–H bonds. More delocalisation of the positive charge means more stability.

</details>

10. (3) Which separation method would you use for (a) a mixture of two liquids with boiling points 329 K and 338 K, (b) aniline in water? Give a reason each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) Fractional distillation: the difference is only 9 K, so one distillation is not enough and a fractionating column repeats the process. (b) Steam distillation: aniline is immiscible with water and volatile in steam.</details>

## 5-mark questions (model answers)

11. (5) Explain the Lassaigne test for nitrogen, sulphur and halogens with the colour of each positive result.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The compound is fused with sodium: N, S and X become NaCN, Na₂S and NaX; the melt is boiled with water to give the sodium extract.

Nitrogen: boil with FeSO₄ and NaOH, acidify with H₂SO₄ → Prussian blue.

Sulphur: sodium nitroprusside → violet; or lead acetate in acetic acid → black PbS.

N and S together: acidified FeCl₃ → blood red (SCN⁻).

Halogens: boil with dilute HNO₃ to remove CN⁻ and S²⁻, add AgNO₃: white AgCl (soluble in NH₃), pale yellow AgBr (partly soluble), yellow AgI (insoluble).

</details>

12. (5) Describe the Kjeldahl method for the estimation of nitrogen. In an experiment 0.50 g of a compound gave ammonia that neutralised 25.0 mL of 0.20 M H₂SO₄. Calculate % N.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The compound is digested with hot concentrated H₂SO₄ to give (NH₄)₂SO₄; NaOH is added and the NH₃ is distilled into a known volume of standard H₂SO₄; the unused acid is titrated.

Moles of H₂SO₄ = 0.20 × 25.0/1000 = 5.0 × 10⁻³ mol; moles of NH₃ = 2 × 5.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.0 × 10⁻² mol.

Mass of N = 1.0 × 10⁻² × 14 = 0.14 g. `$\%\,\mathrm{N} = \dfrac{0.14}{0.50}\times100 = 28\%$`.

</details>

13. (5) Define and give one example each of inductive effect, resonance and hyperconjugation. State how each changes the stability of a species.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Inductive effect: permanent shift of σ electrons toward a more electronegative atom or group, which fades along the chain. Example: ClCH₂COOH is stronger than CH₃COOH.

Resonance: delocalisation of π electrons or lone pairs, so the real molecule is a hybrid of two or more structures. Example: all C–O bonds in RCOO⁻ are equal. The extra stability is the resonance energy.

Hyperconjugation: overlap of C–H σ bonds with an empty p orbital or π bond. Example: the 9 α C–H bonds of (CH₃)₃C⁺.

In each case charge is spread over more of the molecule, so it is lower in energy and more stable.

</details>

## Case-based question

14. **The chromatography of leaf pigments.** A student crushes spinach leaves in a little propanone, puts a spot on a silica plate and develops it with a petroleum ether and propanone mixture. After the solvent front has moved 10.0 cm, four coloured spots remain: an orange one at 9.5 cm, a yellow one at 7.0 cm, and two green ones at 5.5 cm and 4.0 cm.

(a) (1) Name the technique.

(b) (1) Find Rf of the yellow spot.

(c) (1) Which pigment is held most strongly by the plate?

(d) (1) What will happen to all the Rf values if a more polar solvent is used?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Thin-layer chromatography (adsorption chromatography).

(b) Rf = 7.0 ÷ 10.0 = 0.70.

(c) The green spot at 4.0 cm (Rf = 0.40) moved least, so it sticks most strongly to the silica.

(d) Polar pigments will move farther with a stronger (more polar) solvent, so their Rf values rise.

</details>

## Assertion-reason questions

Choose: (A) both true and R explains A; (B) both true but R does not explain A; (C) A true, R false; (D) A false, R true.

15. **A:** Benzene has two kinds of C–C bond lengths. **R:** Benzene is a resonance hybrid of two Kekulé structures.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(D) A is false: all six C–C bonds in benzene are equal (139 pm). R is true: that is the reason they are equal.</details>

16. **A:** Carbocations are stabilised by electron-donating alkyl groups. **R:** Alkyl groups have a +I effect and hyperconjugation.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) Both true and R explains A.</details>

17. **A:** Steam distillation is used to purify aniline. **R:** Aniline is immiscible with water and volatile in steam.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) Both true and R explains A: aniline boils with the water below 373 K and the two layers separate after condensing.</details>
