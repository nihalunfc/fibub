<div class="bigidea">Four small formulas carry this chapter: <b>E = hc/λ = 1240/λ(nm) eV</b>, <b>E<sub>n</sub> = −13.6 Z²/n² eV</b>, <b>λ = h/mv</b> and <b>2n² electrons per shell</b>. Write the formula, put in the numbers with units and check the order of magnitude: visible photons are about 2 to 3 eV.</div>

## How to set out a numerical

1. Write the formula in symbols and name each quantity.
2. Change units first: nm to m, eV to J only if the formula needs joules (1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J).
3. Substitute with units and keep three significant figures.
4. Say what the answer means (colour of light, region of the spectrum, allowed or not allowed).

## 1-mark questions

1. What is the atomic number of an element?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The number of protons in the nucleus of one of its atoms.</details>

2. Name the scientist who discovered the neutron.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>James Chadwick (1932).</details>

3. State the Pauli exclusion principle.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers; so an orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins.</details>

4. Which quantum number decides the shape of an orbital?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The azimuthal (angular momentum) quantum number l.</details>

5. Give the electronic configuration of Mg²⁺ (Z = 12).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (the same as neon).</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

6. Give two conclusions of Rutherford’s alpha-scattering experiment.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(i) Most of the atom is empty space, because most alpha particles passed straight through. (ii) The positive charge and nearly all the mass are in a very small, dense nucleus, because a few particles were deflected by large angles or bounced back.</details>

7. Why can a stronger beam of red light not eject electrons from a metal whose threshold is in the green?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Each photon gives all its energy to a single electron. A red photon has less energy than the work function, so it cannot free an electron; a stronger beam only gives more photons, each still too weak.</details>

8. Calculate the wavelength of a photon emitted when a hydrogen electron falls from n = 5 to n = 2.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ΔE = 13.6 (1/2² − 1/5²) = 13.6 × 0.21 = 2.86 eV. λ = 1240/2.86 = 434 nm (violet, a Balmer line).

</details>

9. Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a 0.20 kg ball moving at 30 m/s. Why is the wave nature not seen?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

λ = h/mv = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ / (0.20 × 30) = 1.1 × 10⁻³⁴ m. This is vastly smaller than anything we could detect (an atomic nucleus is 10⁻¹⁵ m), so the ball shows only particle behaviour.

</details>

10. Write the four quantum numbers of the last electron added in boron (Z = 5).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Boron is 1s² 2s² 2p¹. The last electron is in 2p: n = 2, l = 1, mₗ = −1 (or 0 or +1), mₛ = +½ (or −½).

</details>

11. Why is the configuration of chromium [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s²?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A half-filled d subshell (five parallel spins) has extra stability from symmetry and exchange energy, and 3d and 4s lie very close in energy, so one 4s electron shifts to 3d.</details>

12. Which of these sets of quantum numbers is not allowed, and why? (a) n = 3, l = 3, mₗ = 0 (b) n = 2, l = 1, mₗ = −1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) is not allowed: l must be less than n (0 to n − 1), so l = 3 cannot go with n = 3. (b) is allowed.</details>

## 5-mark questions

13. State Bohr’s postulates for the hydrogen atom, and use them to say why hydrogen gives a line spectrum.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Postulates: (1) electrons move in circular orbits of fixed energy (stationary states) without radiating; (2) the angular momentum is an integral multiple of h/2π; (3) energy is emitted or absorbed only in jumps between orbits, ΔE = hν. Because only certain energies E_n = −13.6/n² eV are allowed, only certain differences ΔE are possible, so the light has only certain frequencies: sharp lines, not a continuous band.</details>

14. Light of wavelength 200 nm falls on a metal of work function 3.8 eV. Find (a) the energy of a photon, (b) the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons, (c) the threshold wavelength, (d) what happens at 400 nm.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) E = 1240/200 = 6.2 eV.

(b) K = 6.2 − 3.8 = 2.4 eV.

(c) λ₀ = 1240/3.8 = 326 nm.

(d) 400 nm gives E = 3.1 eV, which is less than 3.8 eV, so no electrons are emitted whatever the brightness.

</details>

15. Write the configurations of Fe (Z = 26), Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ and find the unpaired electrons in each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Fe: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², 4 unpaired. Fe²⁺: remove the two 4s electrons: [Ar] 3d⁶, 4 unpaired. Fe³⁺: remove one 3d electron too: [Ar] 3d⁵, 5 unpaired (half-filled, so Fe³⁺ is especially stable).

</details>

16. State Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle and explain why it is important for an electron but not for a cricket ball.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π: position and momentum cannot both be known exactly. For an electron (m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg) the uncertainty in speed is huge compared with its speed, so a fixed orbit makes no sense. For a ball the product h/4π (about 5 × 10⁻³⁵) is negligible compared with m·v, so its path can be treated as exact.</details>

## Case-based question

Meera’s school science club looks at sodium street lamps and neon signs through a spectroscope. The sodium lamp shows two close yellow lines near 589 nm. She learns that a photon of 589 nm is given out when a 3p electron of sodium drops to 3s. (h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.)

17. (a) Find the energy of the 589 nm photon in joules and eV. (b) Why does the lamp show lines, not a continuous rainbow? (c) Write the configuration of sodium. (d) Which orbital does the 3s electron occupy: give n, l and mₗ.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) E = hc/λ = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.0 × 10⁸ / 589 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.37 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.10 eV.

(b) Electrons exist only at certain energy levels, so only certain energy jumps and certain colours are possible.

(c) 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.

(d) n = 3, l = 0, mₗ = 0.

</details>

## Assertion and reason

18. **A:** The 2s orbital is bigger than the 1s orbital. **R:** The size of an orbital increases with the principal quantum number n.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) Both true and R explains A: larger n means a higher energy and, on average, a greater distance from the nucleus.</details>

19. **A:** All the electrons in the 2p orbitals of nitrogen have parallel spin. **R:** Pauli’s exclusion principle requires electrons in different orbitals to have the same spin.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(C) A is true; R is false. Hund’s rule (not Pauli’s) says electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins. Pauli’s principle says that two electrons in the <i>same</i> orbital must have opposite spins.</details>

20. **A:** Increasing the brightness of light above the threshold increases the kinetic energy of the photoelectrons. **R:** Brighter light has more photons.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(D) A is false; R is true. More photons release more electrons (greater current) but each photon’s energy, hence each electron’s maximum kinetic energy, is the same.</details>
