**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 8 · about 75 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name the microbes behind curd, bread, wine, antibiotics, enzymes and drugs, explain sewage treatment and biogas production step by step, and describe how microbes help as biocontrol agents and biofertilisers.

<div class="bigidea">Most microbes are harmless, and many are very useful. A single bacterium can become a billion in ten hours, and we have learned to put that speed to work: turning milk into curd, juice into wine, waste water into clean water and cattle dung into fuel. We also use microbes as medicines, as natural pest-killers and as living fertiliser.</div>

## 1. Microbes: small and very fast

Microbes (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, some algae, viruses) are everywhere: soil, water, air, inside our bodies. They are useful because they **grow very fast** and are **chemical factories**. Under good conditions a bacterium divides into two every 20 to 60 minutes: one cell becomes 2, 4, 8... after `$n$` divisions there are `$N_0 \times 2^n$` cells. Try the growth curve of a culture.

## 2. Microbes in household products

**Curd.** A little curd (the **starter** or inoculum) added to warm milk has millions of **lactic acid bacteria (LAB)** such as *Lactobacillus*. They grow and turn the sugar of milk (lactose) into lactic acid. The acid coagulates and partly digests the milk protein (casein). Curd also has more **vitamin B₁₂** and the LAB in our stomach protect against disease-causing microbes. Milk sets best at about 35-40 °C: in cold weather it takes longer, which is why we keep it somewhere warm.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-microbe-curd"><figcaption>Change the milk temperature and the amount of curd added. Watch the pH fall to 4.6, where the milk sets. Very hot milk kills the bacteria.</figcaption></figure>

**Dough.** The dough used for idli and dosa (a batter of rice and black gram) rises because bacteria produce **CO₂**, which puffs up the batter. The dough for bread, naan, bhatura and the batter of some other foods rises through **baker's yeast** (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*), which also makes CO₂ and gives a light texture. **Toddy**, a traditional drink in southern India, is made by fermenting the sap of palms. Many foods are made by microbes: some **cheeses** are ripened by bacteria and fungi, such as the large holes of **Swiss cheese**, due to CO₂ from *Propionibacterium shermanii* (NCERT: *sharmanii*), and the flavour of **Roquefort** from a fungus; **fermented fish, bamboo shoots and soybean** are made in Indian and Asian homes.

## 3. Microbes in industrial products

Large industries grow microbes in huge steel tanks called **fermentors (bioreactors)** and collect what they make. The word "fermentation" comes from the bubbling of yeast.

### Fermented beverages

**Yeast** (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, "brewer's yeast") ferments sugars. **Wine** and **beer** are made without distilling: wine from grape or other fruit juice, beer from malted barley and other cereals. **Whisky, brandy and rum** are also **distilled**, so they have more alcohol. ### Antibiotics

**Antibiotics** are chemicals produced by one microbe that kill or stop the growth of other (disease-causing) microbes. *Anti* means against and *bios* means life. In **1928 Alexander Fleming** noticed, while working with *Staphylococcus*, that a mould (*Penicillium notatum*) that had accidentally grown on a plate had killed the bacteria around it. He named the chemical **penicillin**. Its full potential as a medicine was established by **Ernst Chain and Howard Florey**, who made it in quantity for the soldiers wounded in the Second World War; Fleming, Chain and Florey got the Nobel Prize in 1945. Penicillin was the first antibiotic; we now have many (streptomycin, tetracycline, and others), used against plague, whooping cough, diphtheria and leprosy.

<!--fig:tool antibiotic-zones-->

Antibiotics must be used with care. If you take them when you do not need them, or stop the course early, the sensitive bacteria die and the **resistant** ones are left to multiply. This is natural selection (Chapter 6) in your own body. Press Play and compare a short course with a full one.

## 4. Microbes in sewage treatment

Cities make a huge amount of **sewage** (waste water from homes, toilets and industries) every day, rich in organic matter and disease germs. It must not go into rivers raw. In India, sewage is treated in **sewage treatment plants (STPs)**, and schemes like the Ganga Action Plan and the Yamuna Action Plan aim to clean rivers. Treatment has two main stages, then a digester.

**Primary treatment** is physical: filtration (floating debris is removed by **screens**), then sedimentation in settling tanks (grit and then solids settle as **primary sludge**). The liquid on top is the **primary effluent**.

**Secondary (biological) treatment.** The primary effluent goes to large **aeration tanks**, where air is pumped in. Useful **aerobic microbes** grow into **flocs** (masses of bacteria associated with fungal filaments), and digest the organic matter. This lowers the **biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)**: the amount of oxygen that bacteria would take from a litre of water to break down all the organic matter in it. The higher the BOD, the more polluted the water. When the BOD has fallen enough, the effluent goes to a **settling tank**, where the flocs settle as **activated sludge**. A small part of it is pumped back to the aeration tank as an inoculum, and the rest goes to the **anaerobic sludge digesters**, where **anaerobic bacteria** digest it and make **biogas** (a mixture of methane, hydrogen sulphide and CO₂). The clear effluent is released into a river or stream.

## 5. Microbes in the production of biogas

**Biogas** is a mixture of gases, mainly **methane (CH₄, about 55 to 65 %)** with CO₂ and a little H₂ and H₂S, produced by the microbial breakdown of organic matter without oxygen. It is a clean fuel for cooking and lighting. The bacteria that make methane are the **methanogens**, such as ***Methanobacterium***. They are found in the **anaerobic sludge** of sewage plants and in the **rumen of cattle**, where they help to digest cellulose of the fodder. For the same reason, the dung of cattle (**gobar**) makes a good raw material, and the gas is also called **gobar gas**.

In a **biogas plant**, a pit (the **digester**) is filled with a slurry of dung and water. The slurry is fermented by the bacteria, and the gas collects in a floating gas holder. Gas goes through a pipe to the kitchen. The leftover slurry is taken out and used as **manure**. The technology was developed in India by the **Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI)** and the **Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC)**. A family plant of a few cattle is enough for cooking.

## 6. Microbes as biocontrol agents

**Biocontrol** is using living organisms to control pests and diseases of crops. Chemical pesticides and insecticides are toxic, kill useful insects, pollute soil and water and leave residues in food, and pests become resistant. In **integrated pest management (IPM)** farmers use biological and cultural methods first and chemicals least.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-microbe-pest"><figcaption>Compare no control, chemical spraying and a natural enemy. Each spray kills fewer pests and also the beneficial insects; biocontrol holds the pest low with no poison.</figcaption></figure>

- The **ladybird** (a beetle) eats **aphids**; **dragonflies** eat mosquitoes.
- ***Bacillus thuringiensis*** (**Bt**) forms spores with a protein toxin in a crystal. Dried spores are sprayed on crops such as brassicas and fruit; caterpillars (butterfly larvae) eat it, the toxin dissolves in their alkaline gut and kills them, while other organisms are unharmed. Bt genes are also put in plants (Bt cotton, Chapter 10).
- ***Trichoderma*** is a fungus living in the root zone that controls several plant diseases.
- **Baculoviruses** (genus *Nucleopolyhedrovirus*) attack insects and other arthropods. They are **species-specific, narrow-spectrum**, with no negative effect on plants, mammals, birds, fish or even non-target insects, which makes them good in IPM.

## 7. Microbes as biofertilisers

**Biofertilisers** are organisms that improve the nutrient content of soil. Chemical fertilisers cost money and pollute water, so biofertilisers are a part of **organic farming**.

- **Bacteria:** *Rhizobium* lives in nodules of the roots of **legumes** (pea, gram, beans) and fixes atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia. Free-living bacteria in soil, such as ***Azospirillum*** and ***Azotobacter***, also fix nitrogen and enrich the soil.
- **Fungi:** **mycorrhiza**, a symbiotic fungus such as *Glomus*, grows in the roots of many plants; the fungal threads absorb **phosphorus** from the soil and pass it to the plant, and also give resistance to root pathogens, tolerance to salt and drought, and plant growth.
- **Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae):** *Anabaena*, *Nostoc*, *Oscillatoria* are free-living and fix nitrogen. In **paddy fields** they are an important biofertiliser. Blue-green algae also add organic matter to the soil and increase its fertility.

<div class="keypoints">

- Microbes grow fast: N = N₀ × 2ⁿ. Growth curve: lag, log, stationary, decline.
- Curd: *Lactobacillus* (LAB), lactic acid, vitamin B₁₂. Dough: CO₂ from bacteria (idli, dosa) or yeast (bread). Swiss cheese: *Propionibacterium shermanii*. Toddy: palm sap.
- Beverages: *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. Wine, beer (not distilled); whisky, brandy, rum (distilled).
- Antibiotics: penicillin from *Penicillium notatum* (Fleming 1928; Chain and Florey). Misuse causes resistance.
- Acids and drugs: citric (*Aspergillus*), acetic (*Acetobacter*), butyric (*Clostridium*), lactic (*Lactobacillus*); streptokinase, cyclosporin A, statins (*Monascus*).
- Sewage: primary (physical), secondary (aeration, flocs, BOD falls, activated sludge), anaerobic digester gives biogas.
- Biogas: methanogens (*Methanobacterium*) from cattle dung; mostly methane; IARI and KVIC.
- Biocontrol: ladybird, Bt, *Trichoderma*, baculovirus (IPM). Biofertilisers: *Rhizobium*, *Azospirillum*, *Azotobacter*, mycorrhiza, cyanobacteria.

</div>
