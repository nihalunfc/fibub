**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 11 · about 85 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how organisms cope with their environment, describe the attributes of a population, use the equations for exponential and logistic growth, read an age pyramid, and classify the interactions between species.

<div class="bigidea">Every organism lives inside a set of conditions it can tolerate, and every population is a group that grows until its surroundings say stop. Ecology is the study of those limits: how a single body copes with heat and water, how a population grows and levels off, and how populations of different species help or harm one another.</div>

## 1. The organism and its environment

**Ecology** is the study of the relationships between living organisms, and between organisms and their physical (abiotic) environment. It works at four levels: **organisms, populations, communities and biomes**. This chapter is about the first two (and the interactions between populations). An **organism** is one individual; a **population** is a group of individuals of the same species living in one place at one time; the **habitat** is the place where an organism lives, and its **niche** is the role it plays there: what it eats, who eats it, where and when it is active.

### 1.1 Major abiotic factors

- **Temperature** affects enzymes and so the rate of every process. It varies with latitude, altitude and season, from the 50 °C of the Thar summer to −50 °C in Dras and Leh winter. A species that tolerates a **wide** temperature range is **eurythermal**; one that tolerates only a narrow range is **stenothermal**.
- **Water** is the basis of life. Organisms in the sea, in fresh water and in dry deserts face different problems. The **salinity** of water (salt concentration) is under 5 parts per thousand in inland waters, 30 to 35 in the sea and over 100 in hypersaline lagoons. A species that tolerates a wide range of salinity is **euryhaline**; one that tolerates a narrow range is **stenohaline**. The hilsa (Ilish) and salmon move between salt and fresh water.
- **Light.** Plants need it for photosynthesis; for many animals it sets the clock for migration, breeding and activity (photoperiod). Light changes with depth in water and in a forest canopy.
- **Soil.** Its composition, grain size, aggregation, pH, mineral content and water-holding capacity decide the plants that grow in it and the animals that burrow in it.

### 1.2 Responses to abiotic factors

When conditions change, an organism can do one of four things.

1. **Regulate.** Maintain a **constant internal environment** (homeostasis) whatever the outside. Birds and mammals regulate body temperature by sweating, shivering and changing blood flow. It costs energy, but allows life in widely different habitats.
2. **Conform.** Let the internal state follow the outside. Most fish, amphibians, reptiles, invertebrates and plants are conformers. Cheap, but they depend on the surroundings.
3. **Migrate.** Move away **temporarily** from a stressful habitat to a more suitable one and return when conditions improve: Siberian cranes coming to Keoladeo National Park in Bharatpur for the winter.
4. **Suspend.** Escape in **time**: bears hibernate in winter; snails and fish **aestivate** in summer to avoid heat and dryness; bacteria, fungi and lower plants form **spores**; higher plants make **seeds**; zooplankton in lakes and ponds enter **diapause**, a stage of suspended development.

<div class="myth">

**“Warm-blooded and cold-blooded are the only two choices.”** Those names are old and misleading. Regulating (keeping a fixed internal state), conforming, migrating and suspending are four *strategies*, and many organisms use more than one: a bear regulates in summer and suspends in winter.

</div>

### 1.3 Adaptations

An **adaptation** is any attribute of the organism (morphological, physiological or behavioural) that enables it to survive and reproduce in its habitat. Many adaptations have evolved over a long evolutionary time and are genetically fixed. Examples:

- **Desert.** The **kangaroo rat** in North American deserts never needs to drink: it makes water by oxidising fat and passes very concentrated urine. Desert plants such as *Opuntia* have thick cuticles, sunken stomata, and leaves reduced to spines, with the stem doing photosynthesis.
- **Cold.** In cold climates, mammals have shorter ears and limbs to minimise heat loss (**Allen's rule**). Seals have a thick layer of fat (blubber) under the skin.
- **Water.** Many aquatic animals live in water with little oxygen or high pressure, and have adaptations for them.
- **Altitude.** Above about 3500 m, people suffer **altitude sickness**: nausea, fatigue and palpitations, because there is less oxygen in the air. The body adjusts by raising red blood cell production, decreasing the binding capacity of haemoglobin, and increasing breathing rate.
- **Behaviour.** The desert lizard basks in the sun when its body temperature is low and moves into the shade when the ambient temperature starts to increase.

## 2. Populations

A **population** has attributes that individuals do not: **birth rate, death rate, sex ratio and age distribution**. An individual has a birth and a death; a population has a *rate* of each.

### 2.1 Population size and density

**Population density** (N) is the number of individuals per unit area (or volume), such as 40 neem saplings per hectare or 2 million yeast cells in a millilitre of dough. Counting heads is not always possible or fair: **percent cover** suits plants where one water hyacinth mat covers a whole pond corner while one banyan covers the same area on land, **biomass** (total dry weight) suits big trees, and the **number of fish caught per hour** is used in fisheries. Four basic processes change the size of a population in a given time:

1. **Natality (B)**: the number of births during a given period.
2. **Mortality (D)**: the number of deaths in the same period.
3. **Immigration (I)**: individuals of the same species that have come into the habitat.
4. **Emigration (E)**: individuals that have left the habitat.

```math
N_{t+1} = N_t + [B + I] - [D + E]
```

<dl>

<dt>D, E</dt><dd>individuals lost: deaths and emigrants</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Think of a school: students joining (new admissions, transfers in) add, those leaving (passing out, transfers out) subtract. Natality and immigration increase the population; mortality and emigration decrease it. In a simple pond, I and E are often small, so growth depends on B − D.</dd>
</dl>

### 2.2 Age distribution and sex ratio

The individuals of a population fall in three age groups: **pre-reproductive, reproductive and post-reproductive**. The **age pyramid** plots the proportion of each: for a **growing** population the base is broad (many young); for a **stable** one it looks like a bell; for a **declining** population the base narrows (fewer young). It helps predict whether a population will grow, stay stable or decline. The **sex ratio** (females : males) also matters for how fast a population grows.

### 2.3 Population growth

The size of a population changes with the **availability of food, predation pressure and weather**. It is a balance of the four processes above.

**Exponential growth.** When resources (food and space) are **unlimited**, a population grows in proportion to its size. With **r**, the **intrinsic rate of natural increase** (births minus deaths per individual per unit time),

```math
\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \qquad N_t = N_0 e^{rt}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>N<sub>0</sub></dt><dd>the population at time 0</dd>
<dt>r</dt><dd>intrinsic rate of natural increase = (births − deaths) per individual per unit time</dd>
<dt>e</dt><dd>the base of natural logarithms, 2.718</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Like compound interest: the growth added each year is itself added to the principal. In a plot of N against time the curve is **J-shaped**. Bacteria dividing every half hour are a standard example. Each species has its own r: tiny for elephants, very large for microbes.</dd>
</dl>

**Logistic growth (Verhulst-Pearl).** No habitat has unlimited resources. Sooner or later the food, space and other resources become **limiting**. Individuals compete, and the population stabilises at the **carrying capacity K**, the maximum number that the habitat can sustain. The curve is **S-shaped (sigmoid)**.

```math
\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\frac{K-N}{K}\right)
```

The bracket (K − N)/K is the *fraction of resources still free*: it is 1 when the population is tiny (so growth is almost exponential) and 0 when N = K (growth stops).

**Life history variation.** Populations have evolved their life histories to maximise their reproductive fitness (**Darwinian fitness**) in a habitat. Some organisms breed only once in their lifetime (Pacific salmon, bamboo); others breed many times (most birds and mammals). Some produce many small-sized offspring (oysters, pelagic fishes); others produce a small number of large-sized offspring (birds, mammals).

<div class="myth">

**“A population always settles at K.”** The logistic curve is a model. Real populations overshoot, crash and cycle, because K itself changes with the seasons and because there are delays. The model shows the *direction* of the forces, not a guarantee.

</div>

## 3. Population interactions

In nature, animals, plants and microbes do not live in isolation. They **interact** in many ways. These interactions are shown by their effect on the two species: **+** (benefits), **−** (harmed) or **0** (not affected).

### 3.1 Predation

A **predator** kills and eats another organism, the **prey**. Predation is an important way of transferring **energy** to higher trophic levels. Predators also **keep prey populations under control**: when the prickly pear cactus (*Opuntia*) was introduced to Australia and spread, it was brought under control only after a cactus-feeding predator, a moth (*Cactoblastis*), was introduced. Predators also help maintain **species diversity** in a community by reducing the intensity of competition among competing prey species.

**Prey defences.** Prey species have evolved many defences. Some are **camouflaged** (cryptic colouration); some are poisonous or distasteful, like the **monarch butterfly**, whose body is poisonous because it feeds on a poisonous weed as a caterpillar; many plants have **morphological defences**: thorns (acacia, cactus) and chemical defences, such as **nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine and opium** that make the plant unpalatable or toxic. *Calotropis* produces cardiac glycosides, so goats and cattle do not eat it.

### 3.2 Competition

Competition occurs between individuals of different species that use the **same resources**, even if the resource is not short. It is a negative effect for both (−, −). **Gause's competitive exclusion principle** states that two closely related species competing for the **same limited resources** cannot coexist indefinitely, and the competitively inferior one is eventually eliminated, as in his *Paramecium* flasks. In nature, species avoid this by **resource partitioning**: MacArthur showed that five closely related warblers on the same tree avoid competition by feeding on different parts of the tree. In **competitive release**, a species whose distribution is restricted by a superior competitor expands once that competitor is removed: Connell's study of two barnacles on the Scottish coast.

### 3.3 Parasitism

A **parasite** lives on or in another organism, the **host**, and gets food from it (+, −). Parasites are often **host-specific** and evolve **together** with their hosts (co-evolution): if the host evolves to resist, the parasite must evolve to overcome it. Many parasites have simple bodies, hooks and suckers, a loss of digestive system, and a high **reproductive potential** with complex life cycles involving two or more hosts (liver fluke, malarial parasite).

- **Ectoparasites** live on the surface: lice on humans, ticks on dogs, copepods on fish, *Cuscuta* (a plant parasite) on hedge plants.
- **Endoparasites** live inside: tapeworm, liver fluke, *Plasmodium*.
- **Brood parasitism** in birds: the **cuckoo** lays eggs in the nest of a crow. The eggs of the parasite resemble the host's eggs in size and colour, so the host does not recognise and throw them out.

### 3.4 Commensalism and mutualism

**Commensalism** (+, 0): one species benefits and the other is neither harmed nor benefited. An **orchid** growing as an epiphyte on a mango branch; **barnacles** growing on the back of a whale gain a place to live while the whale is not affected; the **cattle egret** and grazing cattle; a **sea anemone** with stinging tentacles and the clown fish that lives among them for protection.

**Mutualism** (+, +): both species benefit. **Lichens** are an alga (or cyanobacterium) with a fungus; the alga makes food, the fungus takes up water and minerals and protects. **Mycorrhiza** are fungi that live in association with the roots of higher plants: the fungus gets sugars; the plant gets phosphorus. In plant-animal mutualism, flowering plants offer nectar and fruit to pollinators and seed dispersers. The **fig tree** and its **wasp** (each fig species has its own wasp that pollinates it, and lays eggs in the fruit); the Mediterranean orchid *Ophrys* shows a case of **sexual deceit**: one petal resembles a female bee in size, colour and markings, so the male bee pseudo-copulates and pollen sticks to him.

<div class="keypoints">

- Ecology works at four levels: organism, population, community, biome. Habitat is where, niche is the role.
- Abiotic factors: temperature, water, light, soil. Wide tolerance = eury-; narrow = steno-.
- Responses: regulate, conform, migrate, suspend (hibernation, aestivation, spores, seeds, diapause).
- Population attributes: birth rate, death rate, sex ratio, age distribution; size or density.
- N(t+1) = N(t) + [B + I] − [D + E].
- Exponential: dN/dt = rN, N = N₀e^(rt), J-shaped. Logistic: dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K, S-shaped; fastest growth at N = K/2.
- Age pyramid: broad base = growing, bell = stable, narrow base = declining.
- Interactions: mutualism (+,+), competition (−,−), predation (+,−), parasitism (+,−), commensalism (+,0), amensalism (−,0).
- Gause: competitive exclusion; resource partitioning (MacArthur's warblers); competitive release (Connell).

</div>
