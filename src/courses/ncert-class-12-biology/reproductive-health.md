**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 3 · about 70 minutes.** By the end you will be able to define reproductive health and list the ways to improve it, explain why populations grow and how to measure it, describe each type of contraceptive and say how it works, state the rule for medical termination of pregnancy, name the common sexually transmitted infections and how to prevent them, and explain the techniques that help couples who cannot have children.

<div class="bigidea">Reproductive health is more than being free from disease: it means a healthy body, mind and social life in everything to do with reproduction. A healthy society gives people knowledge, safe ways to plan the size of a family, protection from infection and help when they cannot conceive. This chapter is about how to look after your own health and how a country’s population is shaped by millions of such choices.</div>

## 1. Reproductive health: problems and strategies

The **World Health Organization (WHO)** defines **reproductive health** as total well-being in all aspects of reproduction: **physical, emotional, behavioural and social**. A society has good reproductive health when its people:

- have **reproductive organs that work normally** and no disease of them;
- can have **safe and satisfying** reproductive lives, free from fear of unwanted pregnancy or infection;
- are **free to decide** whether and when to have children, and how many;
- have **access to** knowledge, safe contraception, medical help in pregnancy and childbirth, and care of the newborn.

India was one of the first countries in the world to plan the size of its population: **family planning programmes** began in **1951**. They have since grown into the **Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) programmes**, which also cover care of mothers and babies, and many government and non-government agencies run them.

**Strategies** to improve reproductive health include:

- **Awareness and education**: schools and the media give correct information about the changes of adolescence, the reproductive organs, safe and hygienic practices, sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and AIDS. This removes myths and builds responsible choices.
- **Safe care**: trained attendants at birth, **antenatal check-ups** and **postnatal care**, immunisation, and care for the baby and the mother. Safe, hygienic birth reduces deaths of mothers and babies.
- **Contraception**: easy access to a **variety of methods** to choose from.
- **Prevention and early treatment** of STIs.
- **Preventing misuse**: for example, **amniocentesis**, a test of the fluid around the foetus, is meant to find genetic disorders (such as Down syndrome), but it was misused to find the sex of the foetus so that female foetuses could be aborted. This is a crime in India (the PCPNDT Act), and sex determination before birth is banned. A healthy society values girls and boys equally.

## 2. Population explosion and birth control

The world’s population was about **2 billion in 1900** and crossed **8 billion in 2022**. India’s census counted about **36 crore (361 million) in 1951** and **121 crore (1.21 billion) in 2011**. This quick rise is called the **population explosion**. It happened mainly because:

- the **death rate fell** (better food, clean water, medicines, vaccines);
- **infant and maternal mortality** fell, and life expectancy rose;
- the **birth rate** stayed high for many years, as people did not yet expect all their children to live, and as many young people grew into parents (large numbers of women in the child-bearing age).

**Population growth** is the net result of births, deaths and migration. When births and deaths alone matter, the **growth rate** r is the (birth rate − death rate) divided by 1000. A population growing at 2 % a year doubles in about 35 years.

Too fast a rise strains food, water, housing, schooling and jobs, so people try to plan family size. Another useful number is the **total fertility rate (TFR)**, the average number of children a woman will have. A TFR of about **2.1** is **replacement level**: a couple is replaced by two children, with a little extra for the ones who die young. India’s TFR has fallen to about 2.0 in the most recent national survey (NFHS-5, 2019-21), below replacement level, though the population keeps growing for some years because so many people are young.

## 3. Birth control: contraception

**Contraception** means methods that help a couple avoid a pregnancy when they do not want one. An **ideal contraceptive** should be user-friendly, easily available, effective, **reversible**, with no or few **side effects**, and it should not interfere with sexual drive, desire or the act. People choose a method with a doctor’s advice, because the right method depends on health and wishes. The main types are in the figure.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-rhealth-methods"><figcaption>Pick a type of method. The bars show how many in 100 women become pregnant in a year of ordinary use (data from the United States; real numbers vary). Lower is better.</figcaption></figure>

### Natural methods

These try to avoid the meeting of sperm and egg without drugs or devices.

- **Periodic abstinence**: no intercourse from about day 10 to day 17 of the cycle, when ovulation is likely. Because the cycle can vary, this fails often.
- **Withdrawal (coitus interruptus)**: the male withdraws before ejaculation, so semen does not enter the vagina. It is unreliable.
- **Lactational amenorrhea**: during full breast-feeding in the first six months after birth, ovulation and periods usually do not return, so the chance of pregnancy is low. This works only for a limited period.

### Barrier methods

A barrier physically stops sperm from reaching the egg. **Condoms** (for males, and also a female version) are thin covers; **diaphragms, cervical caps and vaults** are rubber caps fitted by a woman over the cervix. Condoms are also the best way (apart from not having sex) to lower the risk of STIs. Spermicidal creams or jellies are often used with diaphragms and caps.

### Intrauterine devices (IUDs)

A doctor or nurse puts the IUD in the uterus through the vagina. There are three kinds: **non-medicated** (Lippes loop), **copper releasing** (CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375) and **hormone releasing** (such as LNG-20). Copper ions reduce sperm movement and their fertilising ability; the hormone types also make the uterus unsuitable for implantation and the cervix unfriendly to sperm. IUDs are very effective, last for years and are ideal for women who want to **delay** or **space** children.

### Hormonal methods

Oral pills, injections and implants contain **progestogen**, alone or with **oestrogen**. They stop **ovulation** and **implantation**, and make the cervical mucus thick so sperm cannot pass. A woman takes the common pill daily for 21 days, starting within the first five days of her period, with a break of 7 days. **Saheli**, a non-steroidal pill developed at the Central Drug Research Institute, Lucknow, is taken once a week and has few side effects. Injections and implants work for months or years. Pills used within 72 hours of unprotected intercourse can work as **emergency contraceptives**, but they are not meant for regular use, and they may cause nausea and other effects.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-rhealth-pill"><figcaption>The pill changes the hormone pattern. Compare a natural cycle with a pill cycle: with the pill, LH does not surge and no egg is released.</figcaption></figure>

### Surgical methods (sterilisation)

For people who are sure they want no more children. In **vasectomy** the vas deferens are cut and tied; in **tubectomy** the fallopian tubes are cut and tied. Both are very effective, but poorly reversible, so they should be chosen with care.

**“Contraceptives are all the same, so any one will do.”** They are very different: a condom protects against many STIs, the pill and IUDs do not; the pill can have side effects and needs a doctor’s advice. Using any method wrongly (late pills, a torn condom) makes it fail. Always seek correct information from a qualified doctor.

## 4. Medical termination of pregnancy (MTP)

**MTP** is the voluntary ending of a pregnancy by a qualified doctor. It is **legal in India** under the **MTP Act of 1971**, amended in 2021, in a government hospital or a registered centre, and only to protect the woman’s health, when a pregnancy results from rape or contraceptive failure, or when the foetus has serious abnormalities. MTP is **safest in the first trimester (up to 12 weeks)** and gets riskier as pregnancy advances. It is **not** a method of birth control, and must never be used to choose the sex of a child. The rules on weeks and doctors’ opinion have changed over time, so always check the current law from an official source.

## 5. Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

**Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)**, also called venereal diseases (VD) or reproductive tract infections (RTI), spread through sexual contact. The common ones are:

- **Gonorrhoea** and **syphilis** (bacteria, curable with antibiotics),
- **Chlamydiasis** (bacteria), **trichomoniasis** (a protozoan),
- **Genital herpes** and **genital warts** (viruses), **hepatitis B** and **HIV/AIDS** (viruses).

Hepatitis B and HIV also spread by **sharing contaminated needles or surgical instruments**, by **blood transfusion** with infected blood, and from an infected **mother to her baby**. They do **not** spread by shaking hands, hugging or sharing a meal. Many STIs show no early symptoms or only mild ones (itching, pain, swelling, a discharge), so people may ignore them and spread them. Untreated, they can cause **pelvic inflammatory disease**, **infertility**, ectopic pregnancy, and problems in the newborn. **HIV** destroys the body’s immune cells and leads to **AIDS**; there is no cure but good treatment lets people live long lives.

**Prevention**: avoid sex with unknown or multiple partners; use **condoms** in every contact; avoid sharing needles and razors; have blood and instruments checked and sterilised; and, above all, **go for early testing and treatment** and complete the whole course of medicine. Hepatitis B can be prevented by a vaccine.

## 6. Infertility and assisted reproduction

**Infertility** is the inability to have children even after a year or more of regular unprotected living together. It may be due to physical, congenital, disease, drug, immunological or even psychological causes, in the man, in the woman or both. Many couples can be helped by **assisted reproductive technologies (ART)**.

- **In vitro fertilisation (IVF)** with **embryo transfer (ET)**: eggs and sperm are put together in a dish in the laboratory. The resulting embryo is placed in the woman’s body. The baby is called a **test-tube baby**, though only fertilisation and the first days of growth happen in the lab.
- **ZIFT** (zygote intra-fallopian transfer): a zygote or an early embryo of up to 8 blastomeres goes into the **fallopian tube**.
- **IUT** (intra-uterine transfer): an embryo with **more than 8 blastomeres** goes into the **uterus**.
- **GIFT** (gamete intra-fallopian transfer): eggs from a donor or the woman are put into the fallopian tube, where fertilisation takes place **inside the body**.
- **ICSI** (intra-cytoplasmic sperm injection): a single sperm is injected into an egg in the laboratory, then the embryo is transferred. Used when the male has very few sperm.
- **Artificial insemination (AI) / intra-uterine insemination (IUI)**: semen from the husband or a donor is put into the vagina or uterus, used when the male cannot inseminate or has a low sperm count.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-rhealth-ivf">

<div class="step"><b>Collect.</b> Eggs are collected with a fine needle under ultrasound; semen is collected.</div>

<div class="step"><b>Transfer.</b> An embryo of up to 8 cells goes into the fallopian tube (ZIFT), a bigger one into the uterus (IUT).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pregnancy.</b> The embryo implants and the pregnancy goes on as normal.</div>

These methods are expensive and need trained doctors, and they may not always succeed. They also bring up social and ethical questions (the cost, the rights of donors and of the child, **surrogacy**), so the law controls them. Couples who cannot have children by any method can also consider **adoption**, which gives a home to a child who needs one. There is a strong social wish for a child of one’s own, but a family can be happy in many ways.

<div class="keypoints">

- Reproductive health (WHO): total well-being in physical, emotional, behavioural and social aspects of reproduction. India’s family planning began in 1951 (now RCH programmes). Sex-determination tests are banned; amniocentesis is only for genetic disorders.
- Population explosion: death rate and infant and maternal mortality fell while the birth rate stayed high. Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate in %. Replacement-level fertility ≈ 2.1 children per woman.
- Ideal contraceptive: user-friendly, effective, reversible, few side effects. Types: natural (periodic abstinence, withdrawal, lactational amenorrhea), barrier (condom, diaphragm, caps), IUDs (non-medicated, copper, hormone releasing), hormonal (pills, Saheli, injections, implants), surgical (vasectomy, tubectomy).
- MTP: legal in India under the MTP Act (1971, amended 2021); safest in the first trimester; not for sex selection.
- STIs: gonorrhoea, syphilis, chlamydiasis, trichomoniasis, genital herpes, genital warts, hepatitis B, HIV. Prevent by condoms, no needle sharing, early testing and treatment.
- ART: IVF + ET (more than 8 blastomeres into the uterus = IUT; up to 8 into the tube = ZIFT), GIFT, ICSI, AI/IUI.

</div>
