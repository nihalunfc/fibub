**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 6 · about 80 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe how life may have begun, list the evidence for evolution, explain adaptive radiation and Darwin's natural selection, use the Hardy-Weinberg principle for numbers, name the factors that change allele frequency, and trace human evolution.

<div class="bigidea">Living things are related. Individuals vary, some leave more offspring than others, and when the differences are inherited the population changes over many generations. Given enough time, one population can split into many species. Hardy-Weinberg gives the baseline: if nothing acts on a population, its gene frequencies stay put. Evolution is what happens when something does act.</div>

## 1. Origin of life

The universe is about 14 billion years old (the Big Bang), the Earth about 4.6 billion years. The early Earth was hot, with no free oxygen and an atmosphere of water vapour, methane (CH₄), ammonia (NH₃) and hydrogen (H₂). As it cooled the water vapour condensed into oceans.

**Chemical evolution.** In the 1920s Oparin (Russia) and Haldane (England) proposed that simple molecules combined, in the warm oceans and with energy from lightning and ultraviolet light, to make more complex ones, and that these gathered into the first living things.

In 1953 **Stanley Miller**, in the laboratory of Harold Urey, tested this. Follow the steps.

<div class="step">A condenser cools the mixture; the liquid collects in a trap and returns to the flask.</div>
<div class="step">After about a week the liquid contains amino acids and other organic molecules.</div>

Later experiments found that sugars and the bases of nucleic acids could form in similar ways. Another idea, **panspermia**, says that the "seeds" of life came from outer space; it only moves the question of origin elsewhere. The first forms were probably **RNA-based**, in cells with membranes, **heterotrophs that lived without oxygen**. When food ran out, **autotrophs** appeared; photosynthesis then filled the air with oxygen (about 2.5 billion years ago), which made aerobic life and an ozone layer possible.

## 2. Evolution of life forms: ideas about it

Many cultures believed in **special creation**: that species were created as they are today and do not change. The voyages and fossils of the 1800s did not agree. Two theories tried to explain the change:

- **Lamarck** (1809) said organs used often grow stronger and those not used shrink (**use and disuse**), and that acquired changes are passed on: the giraffe stretching its neck, so each generation's neck is longer. This is not supported: a body-builder's muscles are not inherited.
- **Charles Darwin** (1859) said: organisms make more offspring than can survive (an idea from Thomas Malthus), they **vary**, the variations are **heritable**, and the ones best suited to the environment survive and reproduce more: **natural selection**. Alfred Russel Wallace, working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently.

On the 5-year voyage of HMS Beagle (1831-1836) Darwin was struck by the animals of the Galapagos Islands, especially the finches (see section 4). Darwin's **fitness** does not mean strength: it means the **number of offspring that survive and reproduce**. Hugo de Vries later proposed large sudden changes, **mutations**, as the source of the variation (modern theory combines both).

<div class="myth">

**“Survival of the fittest means the strongest wins.”** Fitness is about leaving descendants. A small, well-camouflaged moth that raises many young is "fitter" than a big, conspicuous one that is eaten. Individuals do not evolve either: populations change, over generations.

</div>

## 3. What are the evidences?

1. **Fossils** are remains or impressions of organisms of the past in rocks. Older rocks lie below younger ones, so the order of layers shows a story: fossils change from simple to complex, and some (such as the lobe-finned fish and early amphibians) show forms between two groups. The **age** of a fossil is found by radioactive dating: for example carbon-14 (half-life 5730 years) for younger remains and uranium series for rocks that are millions of years old.
2. **Comparative anatomy and morphology.** **Homologous organs** have the same basic structure and origin but different functions (forelimbs of whale, bat, cheetah and human; the thorn of Bougainvillea and the tendril of Cucurbita). They show **divergent evolution** from a common ancestor. **Analogous organs** have different structure and origin but the same function (wings of a butterfly and of a bird; the flippers of a penguin and a dolphin). They show **convergent evolution**: similar environments selected similar solutions.
3. **Embryology.** The embryos of vertebrates look alike in early stages (all have gill slits at some stage), which supports common ancestry. Ernst Haeckel's claim that the embryo repeats its adult ancestors ("ontogeny repeats phylogeny") was later shown to be wrong.
4. **Biochemical and molecular similarity.** All living things use DNA or RNA, the same genetic code and the same cell machinery. The more closely related two species are, the more alike their DNA and proteins are.
5. **Adaptive evidence**, in which evolution is seen happening: **industrial melanism** (below), **resistance to antibiotics** in bacteria, and resistance to pesticides in insects and herbicides in weeds, all of which arose within a few years.

### The peppered moth

Before the Industrial Revolution in England, most peppered moths were white-winged, camouflaged on lichen-covered tree trunks. After the factories blackened the bark with soot, the dark (melanic) moths became common, since birds picked out the light ones. When the air was cleaned, light moths became common again. The same species, the same gene, and a population changed by natural selection within a few decades.

## 4. Adaptive radiation

When one ancestral form spreads out and gives rise to many species, each adapted to a different habitat, we call it **adaptive radiation**.

- **Darwin's finches.** On the Galapagos islands, one ancestral seed-eating finch from the mainland gave about 13 species with different beaks: some for seeds, some for insects, one for cactus, one that uses a twig as a tool. A 30-year study by Peter and Rosemary Grant found that in the 1977 drought, with only large hard seeds left, birds with deeper beaks survived and the next generation had deeper beaks: natural selection seen in action.
- **Australian marsupials.** From one ancestor many marsupials (kangaroo, koala, wombat, Tasmanian wolf, marsupial mole) evolved in Australia. Placental mammals on other continents evolved similar-looking forms (wolf, mole, anteater, flying squirrel), a case of **convergent evolution** inside adaptive radiation.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-evo-speciation">
<div class="step">One population: genes flow among all the animals.</div>
<div class="step">A barrier (river, mountain, sea) splits the population: no gene flow.</div>
<div class="step">Each side faces its own conditions; selection and drift make the two groups different.</div>
<div class="step">The barrier goes and they meet again. Matings fail: reproductive isolation.</div>
<div class="step">Two species now live side by side. This is how adaptive radiation produces new species.</div>

## 5. Biological evolution and its mechanism

Evolution works on **populations** through **variation**. Variation comes from **mutation** (a change in DNA) and **recombination** (new combinations of existing alleles from crossing over and independent assortment in meiosis, and from sexual reproduction). These create the material; the forces below decide which variants spread.

### Natural selection acts in three ways

- **Stabilising selection:** the middle value is favoured and the extremes are removed (for example human birth weight: very small and very large babies have higher death rates). The curve gets narrower.
- **Directional selection:** one extreme is favoured; the mean moves (dark moths in sooty woods; bacteria becoming resistant to an antibiotic).
- **Disruptive selection:** both extremes are favoured and the middle is lost; the population may split into two forms.

### The Hardy-Weinberg principle

In 1908 G. H. Hardy (a mathematician) and Wilhelm Weinberg (a doctor) showed independently that **allele frequencies in a population are stable and stay the same from one generation to the next, if nothing disturbs them.** The population is then in **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium**.

For one gene with two alleles A and a, let the frequency of A be p and of a be q. Since there are only two alleles, `$p + q = 1$`. With random mating the genotypes are in the proportions

```math
(p + q)^2 = p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>p, q</dt><dd>frequency of allele A and allele a in the gene pool</dd>
<dt>p²</dt><dd>frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype AA</dd>
<dt>2pq</dt><dd>frequency of the heterozygous genotype Aa</dd>
<dt>q²</dt><dd>frequency of the homozygous recessive genotype aa</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>It is like a pack of cards dealt at random: if 70 % of the cards in the shuffled pack are red and 30 % are black, the chance of getting two reds in a hand of two is 0.7 × 0.7, and one of each is 2 × 0.7 × 0.3. Random shuffling does not change the proportion of red cards in the pack.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

**A worked example.** Albinism (aa) affects 1 person in 10 000. Then `$q^2 = 0.0001$`, so `$q = 0.01$` and `$p = 0.99$`. The carriers are `$2pq = 2 \times 0.99 \times 0.01 = 0.0198$`, about 2 in 100, which is about 200 times the number of people with the disease. Most copies of a rare recessive allele are hidden in carriers.

The equilibrium holds only if there is a **large population, random mating, and no mutation, migration or selection**. When allele frequencies **do** change, the population is evolving. The five factors that disturb the equilibrium:

1. **Gene flow (migration):** individuals move in or out and carry alleles with them. Gene flow can add new alleles; if it is repeated it makes two populations more alike.
2. **Genetic drift:** change in allele frequency by **chance**, strong in small populations. If a few individuals start a new colony (the **founder effect**) or a population is cut down by a disaster (a **bottleneck**), some alleles may be lost and others fixed, even if they are not useful.
3. **Mutation:** new alleles arise by chance; they are the original source of variation, but the rate is low, so a single mutation changes frequencies slowly.
4. **Genetic recombination:** shuffles alleles into new combinations, giving new phenotypes for selection.
5. **Natural selection:** differences in survival and reproduction change the frequencies; the only force that is directional toward better adaptation.

Try each one yourself: begin at p = 0.5 and switch on one force at a time.

<div class="myth">

**“Hardy-Weinberg means populations never evolve.”** It describes a population with no forces acting, as a baseline. Real populations are always being pushed by mutation, drift, selection or migration, and a change from the Hardy-Weinberg proportions tells us that evolution is happening.

</div>

## 6. A brief account of evolution

About **2000 million years ago** the first cellular forms appeared. Over time the first organisms with a nucleus (eukaryotes) arose, then multicellular organisms. About **500 million years ago** (the Cambrian) invertebrates were common and the first jawless fish appeared (about 350 mya). The lobe-finned fish (coelacanth, once thought extinct but found alive in 1938) gave rise to the first amphibians that could live on land and in water, and from them the reptiles. The **age of reptiles** followed: giant ferns (which formed our coal) and, from about 200 mya, dinosaurs; they died out around **65 million years ago** (the best-known cause is an asteroid impact). Mammals, which were small and mostly lived at night, then spread into the empty habitats.

## 7. Origin and evolution of humans

About **15 million years ago** *Dryopithecus* and *Ramapithecus* were ape-like primates that were more man-like than ape-like in some features (Ramapithecus' teeth were more human, an idea since revised). Then:

- ***Australopithecus*** (about 3.5 million years ago, East Africa): walked upright on two legs, ate fruit, brain about 450 cm³.
- ***Homo habilis*** (about 2 million years ago): the first human-like; brain 650 to 800 cm³; probably did not eat meat.
- ***Homo erectus*** (about 1.5 million years ago): brain about 900 cm³; ate meat.
- ***Neanderthals*** (about 100 000 to 40 000 years ago): brain about 1400 cm³; used hides to protect their bodies, buried their dead.
- ***Homo sapiens*** (about 300 000 years ago in Africa): modern humans. Cave art arose 18 000 years ago and agriculture 10 000 years ago, civilisations later.

The trend: a bigger brain, an upright posture, a smaller face and jaws, and the use of tools, fire, language and culture. Genetic evidence agrees with the fossils that modern humans arose in Africa and then spread across the world.

**Beyond the syllabus.** Because DNA changes at a fairly steady rate, the number of differences between two species' DNA can be used like a clock to estimate when they split (the **molecular clock**). <div class="keypoints">

- Life likely began by chemical evolution (Oparin-Haldane; Miller-Urey made amino acids from CH₄, NH₃, H₂ and water vapour). The earliest cells were anaerobic heterotrophs.
- Lamarck said use and disuse; Darwin said variation plus natural selection (fitness = reproductive success). Mutation (de Vries) is a source of variation.
- Evidence: fossils, homologous organs (divergent evolution) and analogous organs (convergent evolution), embryology, molecular similarity, adaptive cases (peppered moth, antibiotic resistance).
- Adaptive radiation: Darwin's finches, Australian marsupials. New species arise by isolation.
- Selection: stabilising, directional, disruptive.
- Hardy-Weinberg: p + q = 1; p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Frequencies change by gene flow, drift (founder effect, bottleneck), mutation, recombination, natural selection.
- Human evolution: Australopithecus (450 cm³), H. habilis (650-800), H. erectus (900), Neanderthal (1400), H. sapiens (about 1350).

</div>
