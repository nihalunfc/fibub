**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 1 · about 85 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name every part of a flower and say what it does, follow pollen and egg from their mother cells to the seed, give the chromosome number of every cell on the way, explain every kind of pollination, double fertilisation and apomixis, and solve the counting questions the board paper asks.

<div class="bigidea">A flower is a factory for two tiny plants: the pollen grain (the male plant, at most 3 cells) and the embryo sac (the female plant, 7 cells). One pollen tube brings two male gametes and both are used: one makes the embryo and the other makes its food store. After that the ovule becomes the seed and the ovary becomes the fruit.</div>

## 1. The flower: a reproductive organ

Flowers look pretty so that insects, birds and bats will visit them, but their real job is **sexual reproduction**: making seeds by joining male and female gametes. Every flower sits on the **thalamus**, the swollen tip of the flower stalk, and has four whorls (rings of parts), one inside the other.

- **Calyx**: the sepals, usually green; they protect the bud.
- **Corolla**: the petals, often coloured and scented; they attract pollinators.
- **Androecium**: all the stamens, the male whorl. A stamen has a **filament** (stalk) and an **anther** that makes pollen.
- **Gynoecium**: the pistil or pistils (the female whorl). A pistil has a **stigma** (the landing pad for pollen), a **style** and an **ovary** containing one or more **ovules**.

Tap each whorl below. Only the stamens and the pistil take part in reproduction. A flower with both is **bisexual** (hibiscus, mustard); a flower with only one kind is **unisexual** (papaya, cucumber, maize).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-flower-parts"><figcaption>A flower cut in half. Pick a whorl to highlight it; the other whorls fade. The thalamus holds all four whorls.</figcaption></figure>

Flowers are written in short form with the **floral formula**, and drawn as a **floral diagram**. Practise on the [Floral formula tool](/learn/floral-formula/).

The whole life of a flowering plant is an alternation of two generations. The big plant you see is the **sporophyte** (2n). The tiny **gametophytes** (n) live inside the flower: the pollen grain is the male gametophyte and the embryo sac is the female gametophyte. The [Life cycle wheel](/learn/life-cycle-wheel/) shows the switch between 2n and n.

## 2. Before fertilisation: making the male gametophyte

### The stamen and the pollen sac

A typical anther is **bilobed** (two lobes) and **dithecous** (two chambers in each lobe), so it holds four **microsporangia**, also called **pollen sacs**. In cross-section each sac is round and has a wall of layers around a core of **sporogenous tissue**.

- **Epidermis**: the protective outer layer.
- **Endothecium**: below the epidermis; its cells dry and pull, so the anther splits open when it is ripe.
- **Middle layers**: one to three layers that crush as the anther grows.
- **Tapetum**: the innermost layer. It feeds the developing pollen and supplies the material for its wall.

### Microsporogenesis: from mother cell to pollen grain

Every cell of the sporogenous tissue is a potential **pollen mother cell** (also called a microspore mother cell, 2n). Each one divides by **meiosis** to make a **tetrad** of four **microspores** (n). This is called **microsporogenesis**. When the anther matures, the microspores separate and each grows into a **pollen grain**.

### The pollen grain

A pollen grain is a **male gametophyte**, usually spherical and 25 to 50 micrometres across. It has two walls.

- **Exine**: the hard outer wall, made of **sporopollenin**, one of the most resistant organic materials known. It is not broken down by strong acids, alkalis or enzymes, which is why pollen is found as fossils. The exine has patterns (spikes, ridges) that identify the plant, and thin spots called **germ pores** where sporopollenin is missing.
- **Intine**: the thin inner wall of cellulose and pectin.

Inside is a large **vegetative cell** full of food and with an irregular nucleus, and a small, spindle-shaped **generative cell** that floats in it. In about 60 % of flowering plants the grain is shed at this **2-celled** stage. In the rest the generative cell divides before shedding, so the grain is shed with the vegetative cell and **two male gametes** (the **3-celled** stage).

Pollen is rich in nutrients (protein, sugars), so pollen tablets and syrups are sold as food supplements, and pollen from some plants (Parthenium, the "carrot grass") causes allergies. Pollen grains stay alive for very different times: rice and wheat for about 30 minutes after shedding, but members of the Rosaceae, Leguminosae and Solanaceae for months. Pollen can be stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C in **pollen banks** for use in breeding.

<div class="myth">

**“A pollen grain is a male gamete.”** It is not: it is a whole male gametophyte with a vegetative cell and a generative cell. The **male gametes** are the two cells made when the generative cell divides.

</div>

## 3. Before fertilisation: making the female gametophyte

### The pistil and the ovule

The **pistil** (carpel) has a stigma, a style and an ovary. A flower may have one pistil (**monocarpellary**) or many; if many are free from one another the gynoecium is **apocarpous** (lotus, rose), and if they are fused it is **syncarpous** (mustard, tomato). Inside the ovary is a cavity, the **locule**, with a **placenta** to which the ovules are attached.

An **ovule** (megasporangium) is a small structure attached to the placenta by a stalk, the **funicle**. The point where the body joins the funicle is the **hilum**. Its parts are:

- **Integuments**: two protective coats around the nucellus; they leave a small opening at the tip, the **micropyle**.
- **Nucellus**: a mass of cells with stored food, inside the integuments.
- **Chalaza**: the base of the ovule, opposite the micropyle.
- **Embryo sac**: the female gametophyte, formed inside the nucellus.

### Megasporogenesis and the embryo sac

One cell of the nucellus near the micropyle becomes the **megaspore mother cell (MMC)**, large and diploid (2n). It divides by meiosis into **four megaspores** (n). In most flowering plants **three degenerate** and only one is **functional**. This making of megaspores is **megasporogenesis**.

The functional megaspore grows into the female gametophyte by three rounds of **mitosis**, with **no cytokinesis** at first, so all the nuclei lie in one big cell. The nucleus divides into 2, which go to opposite poles; each divides again (4), and again (8): **four nuclei at each pole**. Then walls form.

The **synergids** have a thickened tip, the **filiform apparatus**, which helps guide the pollen tube. The **egg** is the female gamete. The central cell, with its two polar nuclei, will join a male gamete to start the endosperm. The antipodals have no known job after the sac forms.

<div class="myth">

**“The embryo sac has 8 cells.”** It has **8 nuclei** but only **7 cells**, because the two polar nuclei share the one large central cell. Count 3 + 3 + 1.

</div>

## 4. Pollination

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma. It comes before fertilisation and can be of three kinds.

- **Autogamy**: pollen goes from the anther to the stigma of the **same flower**. It needs the anther and stigma to be ripe at the same time and close together. Some flowers are **cleistogamous**: they never open, so they are always self-pollinated (Viola, Oxalis, Commelina) and always set seed. Flowers that open to expose the anthers and stigma are **chasmogamous**.
- **Geitonogamy**: pollen goes to a **different flower of the same plant**. It looks like cross-pollination because an agent is needed, but genetically it is self-pollination, because the pollen and the stigma come from one plant (as in a maize or a cucumber plant).
- **Xenogamy**: pollen goes to a flower of a **different plant**. This is the only kind that brings genetically different pollen. It gives variety.

### Agents of pollination

Plants cannot walk, so they use the **wind, water or animals**. Each agent has shaped the flowers it serves.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-flower-agents"><figcaption>Choose wind, insect or water and watch the pollen move. Each agent goes with its own type of flower.</figcaption></figure>

- **Wind (anemophily)**: light, non-sticky pollen in huge numbers; well-exposed anthers; a large, often feathery stigma; flowers often in clusters or tassels (maize cobs, grasses).
- **Water (hydrophily)**: rare, in only about 30 genera, mostly monocots (Vallisneria, Hydrilla). The pollen is covered by a waxy coat so it does not get wet. Most water plants (water hyacinth, water lily) are pollinated by insects or wind.
- **Animals (zoophily)**: insects (most common: bees, butterflies, moths, beetles, flies), but also birds, bats and some other animals. Flowers are showy, scented, with nectar; pollen is sticky. In return for pollen and nectar the animal brings pollen. Some flowers are specialised: a Yucca plant and a moth depend on each other, and in the Amorphophallus (the largest flower cluster in the world) the flower smells like rotting meat to attract flies and beetles.

Animals that visit flowers get a **reward**: nectar, pollen or a place to lay eggs. Some orchids cheat: the flower looks and smells like a female bee, so the male bee tries to mate and gets covered in pollen.

### Outbreeding devices

Continued self-pollination makes a population less varied and weaker (inbreeding depression), so many plants have ways of encouraging cross-pollination.

1. Pollen release and stigma ripeness are not synchronised (**dichogamy**).
2. Anther and stigma are at different positions (**herkogamy**), so pollen cannot reach the stigma of its own flower.
3. **Self-incompatibility**: the pistil recognises pollen from the same plant and stops its tube from growing.
4. **Unisexual flowers**: if male and female flowers are on the same plant (**monoecious**: maize, castor) geitonogamy is possible but autogamy is not; if they are on separate plants (**dioecious**: papaya, date palm) even geitonogamy is impossible.

### Pollen-pistil interaction

A stigma is a chemical laboratory, not just a landing pad. When pollen lands, the pistil checks whether it is the **right kind**. If it is, the pollen is **compatible**: it absorbs water, **germinates** and makes a **pollen tube** through a germ pore. If not, the pistil stops it. The tube grows through the **style** (down the hollow canal or through the tissue), carrying the tube nucleus ahead and the two male gametes behind, and reaches the ovary.

### Artificial hybridisation

Breeders want to cross chosen parents to combine good traits, so they must stop unwanted pollen. The steps are:

1. **Emasculation**: remove the anthers of the female parent in the bud stage, before they burst, with forceps. (Not needed if the flowers are unisexual female.)
2. **Bagging**: cover the flower with a bag of fine paper or muslin so no foreign pollen lands on it.
3. When the stigma is ripe, **dust** it with pollen from the chosen male parent using a brush, and **bag** the flower again.
4. Let the fruit develop, and collect the seeds, which are the hybrid.

## 5. Double fertilisation

The pollen tube enters the ovule through the **micropyle** (rarely through the chalaza or the side). It reaches the embryo sac, enters a **synergid** and bursts, releasing the two male gametes. Then:

- **Syngamy**: one male gamete (n) fuses with the egg (n) to make the **zygote** (2n).
- **Triple fusion**: the other male gamete (n) fuses with the two polar nuclei (n + n) to make the **primary endosperm nucleus** (PEN, 3n).

Because two fusions happen in one sac, the process is **double fertilisation**, found only in flowering plants. Triple fusion is part of it, since three nuclei fuse.

## 6. After fertilisation

**Post-fertilisation events** are everything that follows: development of the endosperm and embryo, maturation of the ovule into a seed and of the ovary into a fruit. Slide through time to see what each part becomes.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-flower-fate"><figcaption>Move the slider from flower to ripe fruit. Each pair on the right turns on when it happens. Petals, sepals, stamens, style and stigma wither.</figcaption></figure>

### Endosperm

The endosperm develops **before** the embryo, so that food is ready. The PEN divides repeatedly. In **free-nuclear endosperm** (the common type) many nuclei form in the central cell before walls appear. The **tender coconut water** is free-nuclear endosperm with thousands of free nuclei; the white kernel is cellular endosperm formed later. In cellular endosperm each division is followed by a wall. The endosperm feeds the growing embryo. In some seeds all of it is used up (pea, bean: non-albuminous) and in others it stays in the mature seed (wheat, maize, castor, coconut: albuminous).

### Embryo

The embryo forms at the micropylar end of the embryo sac, once the endosperm has begun. In a dicot the zygote divides to make a small ball of cells, the **proembryo**, then a **globular** embryo, a **heart-shaped** embryo (cotyledons start), and finally a mature embryo with:

- an **embryonal axis**: the **plumule** (future shoot) above the cotyledons on the **epicotyl**, and the **radicle** (future root) below, on the **hypocotyl**;
- **cotyledons** (two in dicots, one in monocots), which store food or absorb it from the endosperm.

### Seed and fruit

A **seed** is the fertilised ovule. Its **integuments** harden into the **seed coat** (a tough testa outside and a thin tegmen inside), and the micropyle remains as a tiny pore that lets in water and oxygen at germination. The **ovary** grows into the **fruit**, and its wall becomes the **pericarp**, which may be dry (pea pod) or fleshy (mango: skin, pulp and stone). A fruit that develops from only the ovary is a **true fruit**. In apple and strawberry the thalamus swells too and forms the edible part; these are **false fruits** (pseudocarps).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-flower-seed"><figcaption>Cut open a bean (dicot) and a maize grain (monocot). Notice where each stores its food.</figcaption></figure>

Many fruits are formed without fertilisation, as in banana, and are called **parthenocarpic**. They are seedless; farmers can also get them by spraying growth hormones.

Seeds are the most valuable products of a flowering plant. They let a plant spread (wind, water, animals), survive bad seasons in a dormant state, and give **new combinations of genes** to cope with change. Food stored in seeds feeds the seedling until it can make its own. The seed is also what we eat in rice, wheat and pulses. **Seed dormancy** can last a very long time: a seed of the Judean date palm, around 2000 years old and found in an excavation at Masada in Israel, germinated and grew into a healthy plant.

## 7. Apomixis and polyembryony

**Apomixis** is the formation of seeds **without fertilisation**. It looks like sexual reproduction but is really asexual, because the embryo develops from a cell of the mother plant (an unreduced egg or a cell of the nucellus), so **the seed is a clone of its mother**. It occurs in some grasses and in the Asteraceae. This matters in farming: a hybrid crop with high yield loses its good traits in the next generation, because the genes are shuffled again. If a hybrid made seeds by apomixis, farmers could save and sow the seeds year after year without losing the hybrid vigour, so scientists are trying to put apomixis into crops.

**Polyembryony** is the presence of **more than one embryo in a seed** (citrus, mango, groundnut). The extra embryos often come from nucellus cells, so they are clones of the mother. A seed of orange can be cut and show several embryos.

<div class="keypoints">

- Flower whorls: calyx, corolla, androecium (stamens: filament + anther), gynoecium (pistil: stigma, style, ovary, ovules). Anther: 4 microsporangia, wall layers epidermis, endothecium, middle layers, tapetum.
- Pollen mother cell (2n) → meiosis → tetrad of 4 microspores (n) → 4 pollen grains. Pollen grain: exine (sporopollenin) + intine; vegetative cell + generative cell; generative cell → 2 male gametes.
- Megaspore mother cell (2n) → meiosis → 4 megaspores, 3 degenerate. Functional megaspore → 3 mitoses → 8 nuclei, 7 cells: egg + 2 synergids, 3 antipodals, central cell with 2 polar nuclei.
- Pollination: autogamy (same flower; cleistogamous flowers are always self-pollinated), geitonogamy (same plant), xenogamy (different plants). Agents: wind, water, animals. Outbreeding devices: dichogamy, herkogamy, self-incompatibility, unisexual flowers.
- Artificial hybridisation: emasculate, bag, dust with chosen pollen, bag again.
- Double fertilisation: syngamy (male gamete + egg → zygote 2n) and triple fusion (male gamete + 2 polar nuclei → PEN 3n). Only in angiosperms.
- Endosperm (3n) develops first and feeds the embryo. Embryo (2n): plumule, radicle, cotyledons (2 in dicots, 1 in monocots).
- Ovule → seed, integuments → seed coat, ovary → fruit, ovary wall → pericarp. Apomixis: seeds without fertilisation, clones of the mother. Polyembryony: more than one embryo in a seed.

</div>
