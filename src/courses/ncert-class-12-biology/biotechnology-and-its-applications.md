**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 10 · about 80 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe how genetic engineering is used in farming and medicine, explain the Bt toxin, RNA interference, recombinant insulin, gene therapy and PCR and ELISA diagnosis, and discuss ethical issues and biopiracy.

<div class="bigidea">Once a gene can be moved between species, the same tool solves very different problems: a bacterial gene that protects cotton, a human gene that makes insulin in bacteria, a working copy of a gene for a child who lacks it. The same power needs rules: who checks safety, who owns the knowledge, and what should not be done.</div>

## 1. Applications in agriculture

Farming has three ways to raise food: use better seeds, use more chemicals and water, or grow plants that need less. Green revolution gave India higher yields, but the cost of fertiliser, pesticide and irrigation is high. **Genetically modified organisms (GMOs)** are plants, animals, bacteria or fungi whose genes have been altered by manipulation. A **GM crop** can be made to:

- tolerate **abiotic stresses** (cold, drought, salt, heat);
- need **fewer chemical pesticides** (pest-resistant crops);
- lose **less food after harvest**;
- use **minerals** better, so less fertiliser is needed;
- have **better nutrition**, for example **Golden Rice**, rich in vitamin A;
- make **tailor-made** oils, starch or even medicines.

### 1.1 Bt cotton and pest control

*Bacillus thuringiensis* (**Bt**) is a soil bacterium. It makes protein crystals during a particular phase of its growth, and these crystals contain a toxic insecticidal protein. The gene that codes for it is a **cry** gene. Different cry genes kill different insects: **cryIAc** and **cryIIAb** control cotton bollworms, **cryIAb** controls corn borer.

**How the toxin works.** The crystal is an **inactive protoxin**. It becomes active only in the **alkaline pH of the insect gut**, which dissolves the crystals. Gut proteases cut the protoxin to the active toxin. The toxin binds to the surface of the cells that line the gut and makes **pores**, so the cells swell and burst. The insect dies.

Why is the bacterium itself safe? The Bt protein only dissolves in alkaline guts and acts on specific receptors that are in the insects. Our guts are acidic, and we do not have those receptors.

<div class="step"><b>The protoxin.</b> The cry gene makes a crystal of inactive protein, in the bacterium and in Bt cotton cells.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The larva eats.</b> A bollworm chews the cotton leaf or boll and takes in the crystals.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Activation.</b> The alkaline gut dissolves the crystals and gut proteases change protoxin into active toxin.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Binding.</b> The toxin sticks to receptors on the cells that line the gut.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pores.</b> Toxin molecules make holes in the gut wall. The cells swell and burst.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Death.</b> The insect stops feeding and dies. The toxin does not act in humans.</div>
</div>

India approved Bt cotton for farming in 2002, after safety review by a government committee, the **Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC)**. Bt cotton lets farmers spray fewer insecticides on bollworm. Other Bt crops are Bt corn, rice, tomato, potato and soybean.

### 1.2 Pest-resistant plants by RNA interference

A nematode, *Meloidogyne incognita*, infects the roots of tobacco plants and reduces the yield a great deal. The solution uses **RNA interference (RNAi)**, a method of **cellular defence** found in all eukaryotes that **silences** a gene using RNA.

In the tobacco plant, genes were introduced that make **sense** and **antisense** RNA of a gene needed by the nematode. The two RNAs are complementary and form **double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)**. This dsRNA **binds to and silences** the nematode's matching **mRNA**: the mRNA is not translated, the protein is not made and the parasite cannot survive in the host. The host plants thus become resistant. (In nature, dsRNA often comes from RNA viruses or from mobile genetic elements called transposons.)

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-bta-rnai">
<div class="step"><b>The problem.</b> A nematode feeds in tobacco roots and cuts the yield.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Add genes.</b> A vector brings genes that make both sense and antisense RNA of a nematode gene.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pair.</b> The two RNAs are complementary, so they stick into dsRNA.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Silence.</b> The dsRNA makes the cell destroy the matching mRNA of the nematode as it enters the roots.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Resistance.</b> No protein is made, the nematode cannot grow, and the plant is protected.</div>
</div>

## 2. Applications in medicine

### 2.1 Genetically engineered insulin

Adult-onset diabetes is treated with **insulin**. Earlier, insulin was taken from the pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs, which could cause an **allergy** or other reactions because it is a foreign protein. **Recombinant** human insulin has none of those problems.

**Structure of insulin.** Insulin has two short polypeptide chains: **chain A (21 amino acids)** and **chain B (30 amino acids)**, joined by **disulphide bridges**. In humans it is made first as **pro-hormone (proinsulin)** with a third piece, the **C peptide**, between A and B. The C peptide is removed during maturation to give active insulin.

**The challenge.** Making human insulin in bacteria was hard because bacteria cannot remove the C peptide. In 1983 the US company **Eli Lilly** solved it by preparing the DNA sequences for chains A and B separately, putting them in **plasmids of *E. coli***, and making the chains in different bacteria. The chains were extracted and joined by creating **disulphide bonds**. Later methods make proinsulin in bacteria and cut off the C peptide in the laboratory.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-bta-insulin">
<div class="step"><b>Proinsulin.</b> In the body, insulin is first made as one chain: B chain, C peptide and A chain.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Maturation.</b> The C peptide is cut out. The A and B chains stay joined by disulphide bridges.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Two genes.</b> DNA for the A chain and the B chain is put in two different plasmids.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Two cultures.</b> One set of <i>E. coli</i> makes only A chains and another set only B chains.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Join.</b> The chains are extracted, mixed and joined by disulphide bonds: human insulin.</div>
</div>

### 2.2 Gene therapy

**Gene therapy** is an attempt to **correct a gene defect** in a child or embryo. A normal (working) gene is put into the person's cells so that it makes the missing protein.

The first gene therapy was done in **1990**, on a four-year-old girl with **adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency**. ADA is needed for the immune system to work. The deficiency is caused by the **deletion of the gene for ADA** and leaves the child with severely weak defences (SCID). Treatment options were a bone-marrow transplant, or enzyme replacement by injections of functional ADA, which is not completely curative.

In gene therapy, **lymphocytes** from the patient's blood are grown in a culture outside the body. A functional **ADA cDNA** (using a **retroviral vector**) is introduced into these lymphocytes, which are then returned to the patient. The cells are **not immortal**, so the patient needs periodic infusions. If the gene from marrow cells that produce lymphocytes is introduced into **early embryonic stages**, it could be a permanent cure.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-bta-gene">
<div class="step"><b>The defect.</b> Without ADA, the child's lymphocytes die and the immune system fails.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Take out cells.</b> Blood is drawn and lymphocytes are grown in a flask outside the body.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Add the gene.</b> A disarmed retrovirus carries a working ADA cDNA into the lymphocytes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Give them back.</b> The treated cells go back into the patient and make ADA.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Limits.</b> The cells die in time, so infusions are repeated unless the gene is placed in marrow cells.</div>

### 2.3 Molecular diagnosis

Standard diagnosis relies on symptoms, and symptoms appear only after the disease is well established, perhaps too late. Early detection of the **pathogen** needs more sensitive methods:

- **PCR (polymerase chain reaction).** A tiny amount of the pathogen's DNA (or RNA, after making DNA from it) is **amplified**, so even a few copies can be seen. It is used to detect **HIV** in suspected AIDS patients and mutations in genes in suspected cancer patients.
- **ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay)** is based on the principle of **antigen-antibody interaction**. Infection by a pathogen is detected by either the **presence of antigens** (proteins, glycoproteins and so on) or by detecting the **antibodies** synthesised against the pathogen. An enzyme-linked second antibody gives a colour, so a coloured well means a positive result.
- **A probe.** A single-stranded DNA or RNA, tagged with a radioactive molecule (or a fluorescent dye), is used as a **probe**. It is allowed to hybridise to its complementary DNA in a cell clone, and the clone with the mutated gene will not appear on the photographic film (autoradiography), because the probe does not pair with it.

### 2.4 Transgenic animals

**Transgenic animals** are animals whose DNA has been manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene. Over 95 per cent of transgenic animals are mice. Their uses:

1. **Normal physiology and development.** Studying how genes regulate growth, e.g. insulin-like growth factor.
2. **Study of disease.** Models for cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's.
3. **Biological products.** Medicines made by transgenic animals: for example human **alpha-1-antitrypsin** for emphysema, and **Rosie**, a transgenic cow of 1997, whose milk contained human **alpha-lactalbumin** (2.4 grams per litre) and was a more balanced product for babies.
4. **Vaccine safety.** Transgenic mice are being used to test the **polio vaccine**; if successful, it could reduce the use of monkeys.
5. **Chemical safety testing (toxicity).** Transgenic animals are made more sensitive to toxic substances, so the effects of a chemical show up sooner.

## 3. Ethical issues, biosafety and patents

**Ethics.** Humans have a right to change nature for their needs, but the effects on other species and on future generations must be considered. Moral standards decide what is right in treating living beings. The Indian government has set up the **Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC)** to make decisions on the validity of GM research and the **safety of introducing GM organisms for public services**.

**Biosafety.** GM organisms could harm natural ecosystems (for example, a gene spreading to wild relatives), affect human health through **allergens**, or reduce biodiversity. Testing before release is needed.

**Biopiracy** is the use of **bioresources by multinational companies and other organisations without proper authorisation** from the countries and people concerned, and **without compensatory payment**. Industrialised nations are rich in **financial resources** but poor in biodiversity and traditional knowledge. Developing nations are rich in biodiversity and traditional knowledge related to bioresources. Such knowledge can be used to develop modern applications and could also be exploited to **save time, effort and expenditure** during their commercialisation.

A **patent** is the right granted by a government to an inventor to prevent others from commercial use of the invention. In the late 1990s a US company got patents on **basmati rice**, and patents related to the **turmeric and neem** were also granted to foreign firms. These were challenged and revised, because the basic knowledge was old and part of Indian farming and medicine. The **Indian Parliament** has since passed amendments to the **Indian Patents Bill** to look after such issues, including patent terms and **emergency provisions**.

<div class="keypoints">

- GM crops: stress tolerance, fewer pesticides, less post-harvest loss, better minerals use, better nutrition (Golden Rice), tailor-made products.
- Bt cotton: the cry gene (cryIAc, cryIIAb) makes a protoxin crystal; in the alkaline insect gut it is activated, binds gut receptors, makes pores and kills the larva.
- RNAi: sense and antisense RNA make dsRNA, which silences the matching mRNA (tobacco and *Meloidogyne incognita*).
- Insulin: A chain (21 aa) and B chain (30 aa) joined by disulphide bridges; proinsulin has a C peptide. Eli Lilly (1983) made the chains separately in *E. coli*.
- Gene therapy: ADA deficiency treated first in 1990 by putting a functional ADA cDNA into lymphocytes with a retroviral vector.
- Diagnosis: PCR (early, tiny amounts of DNA/RNA), ELISA (antigen-antibody), DNA probes.
- Transgenic animals: disease models, human proteins (alpha-1-antitrypsin, Rosie's alpha-lactalbumin), vaccine and toxicity testing.
- GEAC checks safety of GM organisms in India. Biopiracy is using bioresources and knowledge without authorisation or payment; patents on basmati, turmeric and neem were challenged.

</div>
