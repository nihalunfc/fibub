**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 13 · about 85 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the three levels of biodiversity, read the species-area relationship and the latitudinal gradient, explain why biodiversity matters and why it is being lost, and describe how it is conserved in situ and ex situ.

<div class="bigidea">Life on Earth is not just plentiful, it is varied, and that variety does work for us: food, medicines, clean water, stable harvests. More area gives more species; the tropics hold the most. Humans are removing species far faster than they naturally disappear, mostly by destroying habitats. The aim of conservation is to keep the variety, on the spot where it lives wherever possible, and in safe stores for what cannot be kept there.</div>

## 1. What is biodiversity?

**Biodiversity** is the variety of life at all levels: genes, species and ecosystems. The word was popularised by the sociobiologist Edward Wilson. It has three levels.

- **Genetic diversity.** The variation of genes within a species. The medicinal plant *Rauwolfia vomitoria* grows in different Indian regions, and plants from different regions differ in the amount and potency of the active chemical reserpine they make. India has tens of thousands of traditional rice varieties and many hundreds of mango varieties.
- **Species diversity.** The number and variety of species in a region. The Western Ghats have more amphibian species than the Eastern Ghats.
- **Ecological diversity.** The variety of ecosystems. India, with its deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, alpine meadows and estuaries, has a much greater ecosystem diversity than a country such as Norway.

### 1.1 How many species are there?

The **IUCN** (International Union for Conservation of Nature) lists over **1.5 million species** that have been described. Most of the unknown species are likely to be small: insects, fungi and the animals of tropical forests and the deep sea. Robert May (1988) estimated that the total number of species on Earth could be about **7 million**, if we assume the tropics have more species and use what we know of the ratios of different groups. More than 70 per cent of the species recorded are animals, and among animals the insects are by far the largest group. Plants are fewer in number; fungi outnumber the species of vertebrates put together.

## 2. Patterns of biodiversity

### 2.1 Latitudinal gradients

Species diversity is not evenly spread over the Earth. It shows a **latitudinal gradient**: **diversity falls from the equator to the poles** for most groups (birds, butterflies, trees and so on). The tropics (23.5° N to 23.5° S) have more species than the temperate or polar regions; the Amazon rain forest has the greatest biodiversity on Earth. Three explanations are given:

1. **Evolutionary time.** Temperate areas were repeatedly covered by glaciers, which wiped out many species. Tropical areas stayed relatively undisturbed for millions of years, so species had a long time to **evolve and diversify**.
2. **A stable climate.** Tropical environments are relatively more constant and predictable, without strong seasons. This **promotes niche specialisation**: species can divide the habitat into many narrow niches.
3. **More solar energy.** Higher **productivity** supports more species.

### 2.2 Species-area relationships

During his explorations in the forests of South America, **Alexander von Humboldt** observed that **within a region, species richness increases with increasing explored area**, but only up to a limit. A graph of species richness against area is a **rectangular hyperbola**. On a **logarithmic scale** it is a straight line:

```math
\log S = \log C + Z \log A \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad S = C\,A^{Z}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>S</dt><dd>species richness (number of species)</dd>
<dt>A</dt><dd>area</dd>
<dt>Z</dt><dd>slope of the line (regression coefficient)</dd>
<dt>C</dt><dd>the Y-intercept: a constant that depends on the region and taxon</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Bigger areas have more habitats, more niches and bigger populations, so they hold more species. The slope Z is remarkably similar in different taxa and regions: **0.1 to 0.2**. But for very large areas, such as a whole continent, the slope is **steeper (0.6 to 1.2)**. A larger Z means richness rises faster with area. Ten times the area gives 10^Z times the species.</dd>
</dl>

<div class="myth">

**“Doubling the area doubles the species.”** If that were true, Z would be 1. On a small to medium scale Z is only 0.1 to 0.2, so doubling the area adds only about 7 to 15 per cent more species. Species do not grow in proportion to area.

## 3. The importance of species diversity to the ecosystem

Does a richer ecosystem work better? Two ideas have been used, and some experiments help.

- **David Tilman's grassland plots (long-term field experiments):** plots with more species showed **higher total biomass** and **less year-to-year variation** in it. So a diverse community is more productive and more **stable**, with more resistance to drought or invaders.
- **The rivet-popper hypothesis (Paul Ehrlich).** In an aeroplane, all parts are joined by thousands of rivets. If every passenger pops a few rivets to take home as souvenirs, the plane may fly for a while, but when a **key rivet** goes the wing falls off. Likewise, every species may be a rivet: the loss of one may not matter now, but we cannot tell which loss will collapse the system.
- The opposite, **redundant species** view, says that many species do the same job, so the loss of a few is made up by others, but the system fails when too many are lost.

## 4. Loss of biodiversity

The IUCN Red List groups species by their risk of extinction: extinct, extinct in the wild, critically endangered, endangered, vulnerable and so on. **Extinction** is the complete loss of a species. Species go extinct naturally, but over the last five centuries humans have raised the **rate of extinction** hundreds of times above the natural rate: the **dodo** (Mauritius), the **quagga** (Africa), the thylacine (Australia), the Steller's sea cow (Russia) and three subspecies of tiger (Bali, Javan and Caspian) are lost. The cheetah was declared extinct in India in 1952. Tens of thousands of species are now threatened. Besides the loss of species, ecosystems become less productive and more variable, and there is loss of ecosystem services.

The **"evil quartet"** is the name given to the four major causes of the loss.

1. **Habitat loss and fragmentation.** The most important. Tropical rain forests, which once covered 14 per cent of the land, now cover less than 6. Habitats are also broken into small patches, so migratory animals cannot move and populations that need large ranges (tigers, elephants) decline. Pollution damages habitats too.
2. **Over-exploitation.** Overuse of natural resources by humans: hunting, over-fishing, collecting. Many marine fish stocks and the passenger pigeon (lost to hunting) are examples.
3. **Alien species invasions.** Species brought from elsewhere may become invasive and cause the decline or extinction of native species. The **Nile perch** introduced into Lake Victoria led to the extinction of more than 200 species of cichlid fish. The **water hyacinth** (*Eichhornia*) clogs ponds, and *Parthenium* weed and *Lantana* have spread across India. Some alien fish, like *Clarias gariepinus* in Indian rivers, threaten native catfish.
4. **Co-extinctions.** When a species becomes extinct, the species that are obligately dependent on it (a host-specific parasite, a plant and its only pollinator) also die.

## 5. Why conserve biodiversity?

The reasons fall into three groups.

- **Narrowly utilitarian:** humans get direct economic benefits: food, firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins, perfumes) and **medicines** (about a quarter of the drugs in use come from plants, and many more are being found, e.g. the anticancer drug vinblastine from the rosy periwinkle).
- **Broadly utilitarian:** ecosystem services: forests store carbon and shape rainfall; ecosystems pollinate our crops, purify water, control floods and erosion, and give aesthetic pleasure.
- **Ethical:** every species has an intrinsic value even if it gives no benefit to us, and we have a moral duty to care for the species and pass on the beauty of nature to the next generation.

## 6. How do we conserve biodiversity?

**In situ conservation** is protection **on site**, in the natural habitat, so that the whole ecosystem is protected and species continue to evolve with their surroundings. **Ex situ conservation** is protection **off site**, outside the natural habitat, for species that are so threatened that they need special care.

### 6.1 In situ

- **Biodiversity hotspots.** Norman Myers proposed protecting regions with very high species richness, many **endemic** species (found nowhere else) and a large loss of habitat, to get the most for the effort. There are about 36 hotspots in the world; India has parts of three: the **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka**, the **Himalaya** and **Indo-Burma**. All the hotspots together cover only about 2 per cent of the land but hold a very large share of the species.
- **Protected areas.** **National parks**, **wildlife sanctuaries** and **biosphere reserves**. India has over a hundred national parks, more than five hundred sanctuaries and eighteen biosphere reserves, such as Nilgiri and Sundarbans. (Biosphere reserves have a core, a buffer and a transition zone.)
- **Sacred groves.** Forest patches protected by religious tradition and local people, found in Khasi and Jaintia Hills (Meghalaya), the Western Ghats of Karnataka and Maharashtra and the Aravalli Hills of Rajasthan. They protect rare plants as well.

### 6.2 Ex situ

- **Zoological parks, botanical gardens and wildlife safari parks** keep and breed threatened animals and plants.
- **Cryopreservation:** gametes of threatened species are stored in viable and fertile condition at a very low temperature, in **liquid nitrogen (−196 °C)**.
- **In vitro fertilisation, tissue culture** and **seed banks**, for storing the seeds of different genetic strains of crops and wild relatives.

### 6.3 People and agreements

Conservation also works through people and law. In **1992** the **Convention on Biological Diversity** at the Rio **Earth Summit** called on all nations to take appropriate measures for conservation and for the sustainable use of its benefits. In 2002 at the **World Summit on Sustainable Development** in Johannesburg, 190 countries pledged to reduce the current rate of biodiversity loss. Local communities and **traditional knowledge** (as in the sacred groves) are often the best protectors.

<div class="keypoints">

- Three levels: genetic, species and ecological diversity. About 1.5 million species described; May estimated about 7 million.
- Latitudinal gradient: richness falls from the equator to the poles (evolutionary time, stable climate, more solar energy).
- Species-area: S = C·A^Z, log S = log C + Z log A; Z is 0.1 to 0.2 for small areas, 0.6 to 1.2 for continents.
- Tilman: more species give more biomass and more stable yields. Ehrlich: the rivet-popper hypothesis.
- Evil quartet: habitat loss and fragmentation, over-exploitation, alien species invasions, co-extinctions.
- Reasons to conserve: narrowly utilitarian, broadly utilitarian, ethical.
- In situ: hotspots, national parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, sacred groves. Ex situ: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation (−196 °C), tissue culture.
- India has parts of three hotspots: Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Himalaya, Indo-Burma.
