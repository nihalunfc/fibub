**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 4 · about 100 minutes.** By the end you will be able to state Mendel’s laws and use a Punnett square for crosses of one or two genes, deal with incomplete dominance, codominance and multiple alleles, explain linkage and recombination, work out how sex is determined, read a pedigree, and describe the common genetic disorders and the mistakes in chromosomes that cause them.

<div class="bigidea">Children look like their parents because parents pass on genes in gametes, one copy of each pair. The copies separate at random when gametes are made and meet at random at fertilisation, so ratios like 3 : 1 and 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 appear. Genes sit on chromosomes, so genes that are close together travel together, and a change in a gene or in the number of chromosomes can cause a disorder.</div>

## 1. Mendel and his experiments

**Inheritance** is the passing of traits from parents to offspring; **variation** is the differences between them. The monk **Gregor Mendel** (1822-1884) did the first careful experiments, with the garden **pea** (*Pisum sativum*) in a monastery garden over about seven years. Pea was a good choice: it has many **clear contrasting traits**, it grows fast and makes many seeds, and it can be **self-pollinated** or **cross-pollinated** at will. Mendel studied seven pairs of traits: seed shape (round or wrinkled), seed colour (yellow or green), pod shape (inflated or constricted), pod colour (green or yellow), flower colour (violet or white), flower position (axial or terminal) and stem height (tall or dwarf).

He began with **true-breeding** (pure) lines, which give the same trait generation after generation. Crossing a tall and a dwarf plant gave a first generation (**F1**) that was **all tall**. Letting the F1 self-pollinate gave a second generation (**F2**) with **tall and dwarf plants in about 3 : 1**: the dwarf trait came back after skipping a generation. Counting thousands of plants was a key part of his method: for seed shape he got 5474 round and 1850 wrinkled seeds, which is 2.96 : 1.

### Some words you need

- **Gene**: a unit of inheritance; a stretch of DNA that codes for a trait. **Allele**: one of the alternative forms of a gene (T for tall and t for dwarf).
- **Genotype**: the alleles an individual has (TT, Tt, tt). **Phenotype**: what it looks like (tall, dwarf).
- **Homozygous**: two identical alleles (TT or tt). **Heterozygous**: two different alleles (Tt).
- **Dominant** allele: shows itself even with one copy. **Recessive** allele: shows only when there is no dominant allele.
- **P, F1, F2**: parent, first and second filial generations.

## 2. Inheritance of one gene (monohybrid cross)

A **Punnett square** is a simple way to find the offspring of a cross. Write the gametes of one parent along the top, the gametes of the other down the side, and fill each box with the two alleles that meet. Every box is equally likely.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-inherit-segregation">
<div class="step"><b>Parents.</b> A true-breeding tall plant (TT) crossed with a true-breeding dwarf plant (tt).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Gametes.</b> The tall parent makes only T gametes; the dwarf parent makes only t gametes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>F1.</b> Every F1 plant is Tt and looks tall, because T is dominant over t.</div>
<div class="step"><b>F1 gametes.</b> A Tt plant makes T gametes and t gametes in equal numbers: the two alleles separate at meiosis.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Fusion.</b> Any sperm can meet any egg: TT, Tt, tT and tt are equally likely.</div>
<div class="step"><b>F2.</b> Genotypes 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, which gives 3 tall : 1 dwarf.</div>
</div>

From such crosses Mendel drew his laws.

1. **Law of dominance**: when two different alleles are present, one (the dominant one) shows and the other (recessive) is hidden. It explains why F1 is all tall and why a 3 : 1 ratio appears in F2.
2. **Law of segregation** (the law of purity of gametes): the two alleles of a gene **separate** when gametes are formed, so each gamete carries **only one** of them. The alleles stay unchanged and unmixed, and join again at random at fertilisation.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Genotype ratio 1 : 2 : 1</dt><dd>One in four offspring is TT, two in four are Tt, one in four is tt.</dd>
<dt>Phenotype ratio 3 : 1</dt><dd>TT and Tt both look tall, so three in four are tall, one in four dwarf.</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Each parent hands over one of its two alleles by the toss of a coin (half T, half t). Two coin tosses give HH, HT, TH or TT in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1, exactly the Punnett square.</dd>
</dl>

### The test cross

How can you tell whether a tall plant is TT or Tt? Cross it with a **homozygous recessive** (tt) plant, the **test cross**. If all the offspring are tall, the plant was TT. If tall and dwarf appear in a 1 : 1 ratio, it was Tt, because the recessive parent gives only t gametes and so shows the gametes of the other parent.

### When the rule of dominance is not complete

- **Incomplete dominance**: the heterozygote is **in between**. In snapdragon (dog flower), red × white gives **pink** F1, and F2 is 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. Here the phenotype ratio equals the genotype ratio, 1 : 2 : 1. The allele for red does not fully hide the white allele: the dose is not enough.
- **Codominance**: **both** alleles show in the heterozygote. In human **ABO blood groups**, IA and IB are both expressed in an IAIB person (group AB). In a roan cow both red and white hairs appear.
- **Multiple alleles**: some genes have more than two alleles in the population. The ABO gene has three: IA, IB and i. A person has only two of them. IA and IB make different sugars on the surface of red blood cells; i makes none. IA and IB are both dominant over i.

| Blood group | Genotypes | Sugar on red cells |
|---|---|---|
| A | IAIA or IAi | A |
| B | IBIB or IBi | B |
| AB | IAIB | A and B |
| O | ii | none |

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-blood-groups"><figcaption>Pick the genotype of each parent and see the possible blood groups of their children. This is multiple alleles and codominance together.</figcaption></figure>

Blood group is also a sample of **pleiotropy**: one gene having more than one effect. For example, the single gene whose faulty form causes **phenylketonuria** (PKU) affects both brain function and hair and skin colour.

<div class="myth">

**“A dominant trait is the commoner or the stronger one.”** Dominant only means it shows when only one copy is present. Many harmful conditions are dominant but rare, and many common traits are recessive. Dominance says nothing about how common or how good a trait is.

</div>

## 3. Inheritance of two genes (dihybrid cross)

Mendel then followed **two traits at a time**: seed shape and seed colour. Round (R) and yellow (Y) are dominant; wrinkled (r) and green (y) are recessive. A cross of a round yellow (RRYY) and a wrinkled green (rryy) plant gave F1 all round yellow (RrYy). Selfing the F1 gave four types in F2: **round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green = 9 : 3 : 3 : 1**.

Think of two separate 3 : 1 ratios multiplied: round to wrinkled is 3 : 1 and yellow to green is 3 : 1, so round yellow is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16, round green is 3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16, and so on. Because the ratio of one trait does not change with the other trait, the two genes are **independent**. This gives the third law.

3. **Law of independent assortment**: when two pairs of traits are followed in a cross, the alleles of one gene separate into gametes **independently** of the alleles of the other (provided the genes are on different chromosomes or far apart). An RrYy plant therefore makes four types of gamete (RY, Ry, rY, ry) in equal numbers.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-inherit-di"><figcaption>The 16-box dihybrid square. Choose a cross and click a class under Highlight to see where each phenotype falls. The test cross gives 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.</figcaption></figure>

The reason for independent assortment is in meiosis: at metaphase I the chromosome pairs line up on the plate and each pair faces the poles in either direction by chance, independently of the other pair.

## 4. Polygenic inheritance

Some traits, such as **human height and skin colour**, do not fall into two or three neat classes. They are controlled by **several genes** acting together, each allele adding a little. This is **polygenic inheritance**. If skin colour were controlled by three genes (AaBbCc), the number of "dark" (capital) alleles in a child would range from 0 to 6, and a cross between two AaBbCc parents would give seven classes in the ratio 1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1. Most children are in the middle and few are at the extremes, a bell-shaped (**normal**) distribution. The environment also affects such traits (for example, nutrition affects height).

## 5. Chromosomal theory of inheritance

Mendel’s work was ignored for 35 years. In 1900 three scientists (de Vries, Correns and Tschermak) rediscovered it independently. In 1902 **Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri** saw that chromosomes **behave like Mendel’s factors**: they occur in pairs, the pair separates in meiosis, each gamete gets one of the pair and the pairs assort independently. This gave the **chromosomal theory of inheritance**: genes are carried on chromosomes. Sutton united the knowledge of chromosomes with Mendel’s ideas, and later the work of **Thomas Hunt Morgan** with the fruit fly *Drosophila melanogaster* proved it. Fruit flies are good for genetics: they are small, breed in two weeks, make many offspring, have clear sex differences and have only four pairs of chromosomes.

<div class="steps" data-fig="@bio-meiosis-steps">
<div class="step"><b>Prophase I.</b> Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) into bivalents.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Crossing over.</b> Non-sister chromatids swap pieces at chiasmata: new combinations of genes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Metaphase I.</b> The pairs line up on the plate, each pair facing opposite poles at random.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Anaphase I and telophase I.</b> Homologues separate (not sister chromatids): two cells with n = 2, the reduction division.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Meiosis II.</b> Like mitosis in each cell: centromeres divide and sister chromatids separate.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Four haploid cells.</b> Each has n = 2 and is genetically different from the others and from the parent.</div>

## 6. Linkage and recombination

Morgan crossed a fly with yellow body and white eyes with a normal fly (grey body, red eyes). The F2 did **not** follow 9 : 3 : 3 : 1: the two traits tended to be inherited **together**. He explained that the genes for yellow body and white eyes lie **on the same chromosome**, so they are **linked** and are separated only rarely. A few **recombinants** (new combinations) did appear, which he explained by **crossing over**, an exchange of segments between homologous chromosomes in prophase I.

The strength of linkage depends on the distance: the **farther apart** two genes are on a chromosome, the more likely a crossover falls between them. So the **recombination frequency** is a measure of distance. Morgan’s students found it was 1.3 % for yellow body and white eyes (very close) and 37.2 % for white eyes and miniature wings (much farther). **Alfred Sturtevant** used such numbers to make the first **genetic map**, putting genes in order along a chromosome. A recombination frequency of 1 % equals **one map unit** (one **centimorgan**).

## 7. Sex determination

In many animals one pair of chromosomes, the **sex chromosomes**, decides the sex; the others are **autosomes**.

- **XX–XY (humans, fruit flies)**: the female is **XX** and makes only X eggs; the male is **XY** and makes half X and half Y sperm. So the **sperm decides** the sex: X gives a girl, Y a boy. Each child has a 50 % chance of each sex.
- **XX–XO (grasshopper)**: the male has no Y chromosome (XO), so he makes half X sperm and half sperm with no sex chromosome.
- **ZW–ZZ (birds)**: the **female** is ZW and the **male** ZZ. So the **egg decides** the sex of the chick.
- **Haplodiploidy (honeybee)**: an **unfertilised** egg develops into a haploid male (**drone**, n = 16); a fertilised egg into a diploid female (**queen or worker**, 2n = 32). The drone has no father.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-inherit-sex"><figcaption>Choose human, grasshopper or bird. The square shows each possible child; press the button to produce ten random children and watch the tally approach half and half.</figcaption></figure>

In humans there are **23 pairs** of chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes (XX or XY).

## 8. Mutation

A **mutation** is a sudden change in the DNA: a change in one base pair (**point mutation**), the loss or addition of bases (**frame shift**), or a change in a whole chromosome or in the number of chromosomes. Mutations are the **source of new alleles**, and so of variation. They can be caused by **mutagens** such as ultraviolet light, X-rays and some chemicals.

**Chromosomal aberrations**: whole chromosomes can be lost or gained when they fail to separate during meiosis. This failure is called **non-disjunction**. It gives gametes with an extra chromosome (n + 1) or a missing one (n − 1). **Aneuploidy** is the gain or loss of single chromosomes; **polyploidy** is a whole extra set of chromosomes (3n, 4n), common in plants.

## 9. Genetic disorders and pedigrees

### Pedigree analysis

A **pedigree** is a family tree that follows one trait: squares for males, circles for females, filled shapes for the affected. Reading it tells you the mode of inheritance, and the chance of a trait in a child. Clues:

- Two **unaffected** parents with an **affected** child: the trait is **recessive**.
- Affected people in **every generation** and an affected parent for every affected child: **dominant**.
- Mostly **sons** affected, and the trait passes through carrier mothers; an affected father cannot pass it to a son: **X-linked recessive**.

### Mendelian disorders

These are caused by a change in a single gene and follow Mendel’s laws.

- **Haemophilia** (X-linked recessive): blood does not clot properly because a clotting factor is missing. A heterozygous (carrier) female is usually normal, but her sons have a 50 % chance. A haemophilic girl is possible only if her mother is a carrier and her father is affected.
- **Colour blindness** (X-linked recessive): a defect in the red or green cone cells; it affects about 8 % of males and about 0.4 % of females.
- **Sickle-cell anaemia** (autosomal recessive): caused by a single base change (GAG → GTG) in the β-globin gene, which changes glutamic acid to valine and makes the red cells sickle-shaped in low oxygen. Carriers have some protection against malaria.
- **Phenylketonuria** (autosomal recessive): the enzyme that changes phenylalanine to tyrosine is missing, so phenylalanine builds up and harms the brain. It is controlled by diet.
- **Thalassaemia** (autosomal recessive): too little of the α or β globin chain, giving anaemia.
- **Cystic fibrosis** (autosomal recessive): thick mucus clogs the lungs and gut.

### Chromosomal disorders

- **Down syndrome**: an extra copy of **chromosome 21** (trisomy 21, 47 chromosomes). Short height, a round head, a furrowed tongue, delayed development.
- **Klinefelter syndrome**: **XXY** (47 chromosomes), a male with an extra X; he has some female features and is usually sterile.
- **Turner syndrome**: **XO** (45 chromosomes), a female with only one X; short, with undeveloped ovaries.

The chance of a chromosomal disorder like Down syndrome rises with the age of the mother. Tests in pregnancy can find them early.

<div class="keypoints">

- Laws: dominance, segregation (alleles separate in gametes; gametes are pure) and independent assortment (genes on different chromosomes). Monohybrid F2: 3 : 1 and 1 : 2 : 1. Dihybrid F2: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Test cross (with the recessive) gives 1 : 1 for a heterozygote, 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 for a dihybrid.
- Incomplete dominance (1 : 2 : 1 phenotype), codominance (both alleles show; ABO blood group), multiple alleles (IA, IB, i), pleiotropy, polygenic inheritance (bell curve).
- Chromosomal theory (Sutton and Boveri; Morgan, Drosophila). Linked genes travel together; crossing over makes recombinants; recombination % = map distance (1 % = 1 map unit).
- Sex determination: XX–XY humans (sperm decides), XX–XO grasshopper, ZW–ZZ birds (egg decides), honeybee haplodiploid.
- Mutation: point mutation, frame shift, chromosomal change; non-disjunction gives aneuploidy.
- Disorders: haemophilia, colour blindness (X-linked recessive); sickle-cell anaemia, PKU, thalassaemia, cystic fibrosis (autosomal recessive); Down (trisomy 21), Klinefelter (XXY), Turner (XO).

</div>
