<div class="bigidea">Two habits help here. In every Hardy-Weinberg question write p + q = 1 first, then decide whether the number you are given is q² (the disease) or q (the allele). In every "give an example" question, name the example and say what it shows (for instance: Darwin's finches, which show adaptive radiation).</div>

## How to set out answers

1. For Hardy-Weinberg numbers: state p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1, find q from q², then p, then the genotype asked.
2. For compare-and-contrast questions, use a two-column table.
3. For a theory, give the key points in order: variation, struggle, survival of the fittest, inheritance.
4. Draw simple labelled diagrams: the three selection curves, an adaptive radiation tree, the Miller apparatus.

## 1-mark questions

1. Name the scientist who showed that amino acids can form from simple gases.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Stanley Miller (with Harold Urey), 1953.</details>

2. What is the term for the change in allele frequency caused by chance in a small population?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Genetic drift.</details>

3. Give one example of analogous organs.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Wings of a butterfly and of a bird (or flippers of a penguin and a dolphin).</details>

4. What does "fitness" mean in Darwin's theory?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The ability to survive and leave more offspring than others.</details>

5. Name the hominid that had a brain capacity of about 900 cm³.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Homo erectus.</details>

## 2-mark questions

6. Distinguish homologous and analogous organs with one example each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Homologous: same origin and structure, different function (forelimbs of a human and a bat); they show divergent evolution. Analogous: different origin and structure, same function (wings of a bird and an insect); they show convergent evolution.</details>

7. Why did the dark moths increase near factories in the nineteenth century?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Soot darkened the tree trunks. Dark moths were camouflaged and escaped birds, while light moths were easily seen and eaten, so the dark allele increased by natural selection.</details>

8. State the Hardy-Weinberg principle in one or two lines.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection, allele frequencies and genotype frequencies stay the same from generation to generation (p² + 2pq + q² = 1).</details>

9. What is adaptive radiation? Give an example.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The evolution of many species, adapted to different habitats, from one ancestral form in a geographical area. Example: Darwin's finches of the Galapagos, or the Australian marsupials.</details>

10. How does the Lamarckian idea differ from Darwin's on the long neck of the giraffe?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Lamarck: the neck lengthened by stretching and this acquired change was passed on. Darwin: giraffes varied in neck length; the longer-necked ones reached more food and left more offspring, so the heritable trait spread.</details>

## 3-mark questions

11. Describe the Miller-Urey experiment and its conclusion.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A closed apparatus held CH₄, NH₃, H₂ and water vapour, with electric sparks passed through the heated mixture. After a week amino acids had formed. It showed that organic molecules could form from inorganic gases in conditions like those of the early Earth, supporting chemical evolution.</details>

12. Name the factors that disturb Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Which of them can bring in new alleles?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Gene flow (migration), genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination and natural selection. Mutation produces new alleles and gene flow can bring in alleles from other populations.</details>

13. Explain stabilising, directional and disruptive selection with one example each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Stabilising: the middle form is favoured (human birth weight). Directional: one extreme is favoured and the mean shifts (dark peppered moths in sooty areas, antibiotic resistance). Disruptive: both extremes are favoured and the middle is selected against, which may split the population.</details>

14. List three lines of evidence for evolution from fossils, comparative anatomy and molecular biology.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Fossils: layers show a sequence of forms, such as the transition from lobe-finned fish to amphibians. Comparative anatomy: homologous organs such as the forelimbs of vertebrates. Molecular: DNA and proteins are more alike in closely related species, and all organisms share the same genetic code.</details>

## 5-mark questions

15. Trace the evolution of modern humans, naming the stages and their brain sizes.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Dryopithecus and Ramapithecus (about 15 million years ago) were ape-like. Australopithecus (about 3.5 million years ago, East Africa) walked upright and had a brain of about 450 cm³. Homo habilis (about 2 million years ago) was the first human-like form, with a brain of 650 to 800 cm³, using stone tools. Homo erectus (about 1.5 million years ago) had a brain of about 900 cm³ and ate meat. Neanderthals (about 100 000 to 40 000 years ago) had a brain of about 1400 cm³. Homo sapiens arose in Africa about 300 000 years ago. The brain became larger, the face and jaws smaller, and tools, language and culture developed.

</details>

16. Explain how a new species can form when a population is divided by a barrier.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A mountain, river or sea splits one population into two (geographical isolation), so there is no gene flow. Each part faces different conditions and chance events: natural selection, mutation and genetic drift change the two gene pools in different ways. After a long time the two groups differ so much that, even if the barrier disappears, they cannot interbreed or produce fertile offspring: reproductive isolation. They are now separate species. Darwin's finches arose in this way on the Galapagos islands.

</details>

## Case-based question

**Read this.** Sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a recessive allele (s). In one region of India the frequency of the normal allele S is 0.9 in a large, randomly mating population. Heterozygotes (Ss) are protected from severe malaria.

17. (i) What is the frequency of the allele s? (ii) What fraction of the population is expected to be Ss? (iii) What fraction has sickle-cell anaemia (ss)? (iv) Why does the s allele stay in malaria regions even though ss is harmful?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(i) q = 1 − 0.9 = 0.1. (ii) 2pq = 2 × 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.18, or 18 %. (iii) q² = 0.01, or 1 %. (iv) Heterozygotes survive malaria better than SS people, so natural selection keeps the s allele at a moderate frequency (a balance between the harm to ss and the benefit to Ss).

</details>

## Assertion-reason

Choose: (A) both true and R explains A; (B) both true but R does not explain A; (C) A true, R false; (D) A false, R true.

18. **A:** Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is rarely found in nature. **R:** Real populations are acted upon by mutation, migration, drift and selection.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) Both true and R explains A.</details>

19. **A:** Birds of one island with very different beaks are one species. **R:** Darwin's finches show adaptive radiation.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(D) A is false: the different beak forms are different species. R is true.</details>

20. **A:** Wings of a bird and of an insect are analogous organs. **R:** They perform the same function but differ in structure and origin.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) Both true and R explains A: that is the definition of analogous organs, and they show convergent evolution.</details>
