**Biology · Class 12 · Chapter 12 · about 90 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the structure of an ecosystem, measure productivity, explain decomposition, follow energy through food chains with the 10 per cent law, draw and explain ecological pyramids, describe succession, and explain the carbon and phosphorus cycles and ecosystem services.

<div class="bigidea">An ecosystem is a living machine run on sunlight. Energy comes in once, flows one way through producers, consumers and decomposers, and leaves as heat. Matter, on the other hand, goes round and round: carbon, phosphorus and the other nutrients are used again and again. Understanding that one-way flow and those loops explains why food chains are short, why pyramids have their shapes, and why forests and soil are valuable.</div>

## 1. Structure of an ecosystem

An **ecosystem** is a functional unit of nature where living organisms interact among themselves and with the surrounding physical environment. Ecosystems vary in size from a small pond to a large forest or a sea. Many ecologists regard the entire biosphere as a global ecosystem, a composite of all local ecosystems on Earth.

An ecosystem has **structure**: its species composition and **stratification** (vertical layering: in a forest the trees occupy the top layer, shrubs the second, herbs and grasses the bottom), and its **function**: productivity, decomposition, energy flow and nutrient cycling. The components are:

- **abiotic**: sunlight, temperature, water, soil, minerals;
- **biotic**: **producers** (green plants, algae, some bacteria), **consumers** (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, parasites) and **decomposers** (bacteria, fungi).

A **pond** is a simple example: the water and sediment with dissolved gases and nutrients, floating phytoplankton and rooted plants as producers, zooplankton, insects and fish as consumers, and bacteria and fungi on the bottom as decomposers. A pond is a fairly self-sustaining unit: it needs only sunlight from outside.

## 2. Productivity

Primary production is the amount of **biomass or organic matter produced per unit area over a time period** by plants during photosynthesis. It is expressed as weight (g m⁻²) or energy (kcal m⁻²). The **rate** of biomass production is called **productivity**, expressed as g m⁻² yr⁻¹ or kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹, so that different ecosystems can be compared.

- **Gross primary productivity (GPP)**: the rate of production of organic matter during photosynthesis.
- A considerable part of GPP is used by the plants in **respiration (R)**.
- **Net primary productivity (NPP)** is what is left: `$\text{NPP} = \text{GPP} - R$`. It is the available biomass for the **consumers**.
- **Secondary productivity** is the rate of formation of new organic matter by the **consumers**.

</div>

Primary productivity depends on the plant species in the area, their photosynthetic capacity, and on **environmental factors** (light, temperature, rainfall), the availability of **nutrients**, and the soil. It therefore varies from one ecosystem to another. The annual net primary productivity of the whole biosphere is about **170 billion tonnes (dry weight)** of organic matter, of which the oceans, though they cover most of the Earth, produce only about 55 billion tonnes, because open ocean water is poor in nutrients.

## 3. Decomposition

**Decomposers** break down complex organic matter into inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide, water and nutrients. The raw material is **detritus**: dead plant remains (leaves, bark, flowers) and dead animal remains, together with faecal matter. Decomposition proceeds through five steps:

1. **Fragmentation**: **detritivores** such as earthworms break detritus into smaller particles.
2. **Leaching**: water-soluble inorganic nutrients go down into the soil horizon and are precipitated as unavailable salts.
3. **Catabolism**: bacterial and fungal enzymes degrade the detritus into simpler inorganic substances.
4. **Humification**: formation of **humus**, a dark-coloured amorphous substance resistant to microbial action, that decomposes extremely slowly. It acts as a reservoir of nutrients.
5. **Mineralisation**: humus is further degraded by some microbes and **inorganic nutrients** are released.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-eco-decompsteps">
<div class="step"><b>Fragmentation.</b> Earthworms and termites break the litter into small pieces. More surface means faster microbes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Leaching.</b> Rain dissolves water-soluble nutrients and moves them into the soil.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Catabolism.</b> Bacteria and fungi make enzymes that break the pieces into simpler substances.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Humification.</b> What remains becomes humus, which decays very slowly and stores nutrients.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Mineralisation.</b> Microbes slowly break humus down, releasing nitrate, phosphate and other minerals for plants.</div>
</div>

Decomposition is an **oxygen-requiring process**. Its rate is controlled by the **chemical composition of the detritus** and by **climatic factors**. If the detritus is rich in **lignin and chitin**, decomposition is slow; if it is rich in **nitrogen and water-soluble substances like sugars**, it is faster. **Temperature and soil moisture** are the most important climatic factors: warm and moist environments favour decomposition, and low temperature and anaerobiosis (little oxygen) inhibit it, resulting in a build-up of organic materials.

## 4. Energy flow

The **sun** is the only source of energy for all ecosystems on Earth, except the deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystems. Of the incident solar radiation, **less than 50 per cent is photosynthetically active radiation (PAR)**. Plants capture only about **2 to 10 per cent of the PAR**, and this small amount of energy sustains the entire living world. So it is very important to know how the solar energy captured by plants flows through different organisms of an ecosystem.

All organisms are dependent for their food on producers, either directly or indirectly. So we have a **grazing food chain (GFC)**, which starts with living plants (producers), and a **detritus food chain (DFC)**, which starts from **dead organic matter**, taken by **detritivores and decomposers**. In an **aquatic ecosystem**, the GFC is the major conduit for energy flow. On land, a much larger fraction of energy flows through the DFC.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12b-eco-chains"><figcaption>Compare a grazing chain with a detritus chain in water and on land. The bar shows the share of energy going each way (illustrative).</figcaption></figure>

**Trophic levels.** The place an organism occupies in a food chain is its **trophic level**: producers are the first, herbivores (primary consumers) the second, carnivores that eat them (secondary consumers) the third, and so on. An organism can occupy more than one trophic level (a sparrow eats seeds and insects), so in nature chains join into a **food web**. The mass of living material at each trophic level at a given time is the **standing crop** (or standing state).

**The 10 per cent law.** When energy passes from one trophic level to the next, about **10 per cent** is transferred and the rest is lost: most is used in **respiration**, as heat, in movement, and part goes unused to the decomposers. This was stated by Lindeman (1942). So energy decreases at every step and the food chain stays short.

<div class="step"><b>PAR.</b> Less than half (50 units) is the kind of light plants can use.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Capture.</b> Plants fix 2 to 10 % of PAR. Here 5 % gives 2.5 units: GPP.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Respiration.</b> The plants burn part of it themselves: 1.5 units remain as NPP.</div>

## 5. Ecological pyramids

An **ecological pyramid** shows the relationship between trophic levels, with the producers at the base. There are three kinds:

- **Pyramid of number:** the number of individuals at each level. In a grassland it is upright (many grasses, fewer grasshoppers, fewer frogs). It can be **inverted** when one big producer supports many consumers: one tree supports thousands of insects and birds.
- **Pyramid of biomass:** the total mass (dry weight) at each level at a time. It is usually upright, but in the **sea it is inverted**: the phytoplankton are tiny with a short life and fast turnover, so at any moment their biomass is less than that of the zooplankton and fish that depend on them.
- **Pyramid of energy:** the energy flow at each level in a given time. It is **always upright**, never inverted, because energy is lost at each step.

Pyramids do not account for the same species at two trophic levels, they assume a simple food chain and not a web, and they do not include decomposers. Even so, they are a useful tool.

<div class="myth">

**“Fewer animals at the top mean the pyramid of energy can be inverted.”** The pyramid of *numbers* can be inverted and so can *biomass* in some water bodies, but the pyramid of *energy* cannot: you can never get more energy from a level than was passed to it.

## 6. Ecological succession

Ecological succession is the gradual and fairly predictable change in the species composition of a given area. During succession some species colonise an area and their populations become more numerous, whereas the populations of other species decline and even disappear. The entire sequence of communities that successively change in a given area is called a **sere**, the individual transitional communities **seral stages**, and the first species to colonise **pioneer species**. The process ends in a stable **climax community** that is in equilibrium with the environment (rain, temperature and soil). Over time, **species diversity** and **biomass** increase, as does the **complexity of the food web**.

- **Primary succession** starts where no living organism was ever present: bare rock, a new lava flow, a newly formed pond. It is slow, taking hundreds to thousands of years, because soil must form.
- **Secondary succession** begins in an area that lost its community but has soil or seeds left: abandoned farmland, a burnt or flooded forest. It is faster.

**Hydrarch (water to land):** phytoplankton → submerged plants → free-floating plants → reed-swamp → marsh-meadow → scrub → forest. The pond fills with silt and becomes land.

**Xerarch (dry to wet):** on bare rock, **crustose lichens** secrete acids that weather rock and help soil to form; then foliose lichens, **mosses**, herbs, shrubs and finally trees.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12b-eco-succession">
<div class="step"><b>Pioneers.</b> Phytoplankton in water, or crustose lichens on rock.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Second stage.</b> Submerged plants, or foliose lichens, change the place a little.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Third stage.</b> Free-floating plants, or mosses, trap more silt or dust and build soil.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Fourth stage.</b> Reeds in shallow water, or herbs and grasses on land.</div>

<div class="step"><b>Sixth stage.</b> Shrubs and small trees take over.</div>

Succession is also happening in many places now, because humans change ecosystems (clearing forests, building dams). In a polluted pond, succession can be slowed or reversed.

## 7. Nutrient cycling

**Nutrient cycles** (biogeochemical cycles) move nutrients between the living and non-living parts of an ecosystem. The amount of nutrients such as carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and calcium in the soil at a given time is the **standing state**. Cycles are of two types: **gaseous** (the reservoir is the atmosphere or hydrosphere: carbon and nitrogen) and **sedimentary** (the reservoir is the Earth's crust: phosphorus, sulphur). Environmental factors such as soil, moisture, pH and temperature regulate the rate at which nutrients are released.

### 7.1 Carbon cycle

Living organisms contain carbon in all organic compounds. The carbon cycle: **producers** take CO₂ from the air (or dissolved in water) by photosynthesis; carbon passes to **consumers** along food chains; **respiration** of all organisms, and **decomposition** of dead matter, return CO₂ to the air; some carbon is locked up for a long time in **fossil fuels**, and the oceans hold a great deal as dissolved carbon. Human activities have changed the balance: burning **fossil fuels** and **deforestation** add carbon dioxide faster than it can be removed, which increases the greenhouse effect.

### 7.2 Phosphorus cycle

Phosphorus is a major constituent of **biological membranes, nucleic acids and cellular energy transfer systems** (ATP). The natural reservoir is **rock**, which contains phosphorus in the form of phosphates. When rocks are weathered, small amounts of phosphates dissolve in soil water and are absorbed by the roots of plants. **Herbivores and other animals** get phosphorus from plants. Wastes and dead remains are broken down by **decomposers**, which release phosphorus back to the soil. Unlike carbon, there is **no gaseous exchange** of phosphorus with the atmosphere (only small amounts come in through rain). Respiration, which returns CO₂ to the air in the carbon cycle, does not return phosphorus to the air.

## 8. Ecosystem services

Healthy ecosystems are the base for a wide range of economic, environmental and aesthetic goods and services. The products of ecosystem processes are named **ecosystem services**. For example, healthy forest ecosystems **purify air and water**, **mitigate droughts and floods**, **cycle nutrients**, **generate fertile soils**, provide **wildlife habitat**, **maintain biodiversity**, **pollinate crops**, provide storage sites for carbon, and also provide **aesthetic, cultural and spiritual values**. Robert Costanza and colleagues estimated in 1997 that the value of these services, for the whole Earth, was around **33 trillion US dollars per year**, about twice the global gross national product at the time. Climate regulation (forests storing carbon) is one large part of that value. The estimate is only a rough guide, but it shows that nature does work for us that we cannot easily replace.

<div class="keypoints">

- Ecosystem: abiotic and biotic components; structure = species composition and stratification; function = productivity, decomposition, energy flow, nutrient cycling.
- GPP − R = NPP. Secondary productivity is made by consumers.
- Decomposition: fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification, mineralisation. Fast in warm, moist, nitrogen-rich detritus; slow with lignin and chitin or in the cold.
- Energy flows in one direction. PAR is under 50 % of sunlight; plants capture 2-10 % of PAR. About 10 % passes between trophic levels.
- GFC starts with living plants; DFC starts with dead matter. Aquatic: GFC is the main route; land: DFC.
- Pyramid of energy is always upright; numbers (tree) and biomass (sea) can be inverted.
- Succession: pioneer, seral stages, climax; primary (no soil) is slow, secondary is fast. Hydrarch and xerarch.
- Carbon cycle is gaseous; phosphorus cycle is sedimentary with no gas phase.
- Ecosystem services: air and water purification, soil, pollination, climate regulation; estimated at about 33 trillion US dollars per year (1997).

</div>
