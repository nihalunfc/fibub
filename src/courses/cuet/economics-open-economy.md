**Module 11 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will place transactions in the balance of payments and explain rupee movements.

<div class="bigidea">The balance of payments records every transaction between a country and the rest of the world. Goods, services, income and transfers go in the current account; investment and loans in the capital account.</div>

<div class="steps" data-title="Where does it go?">
<div class="step"><b>Current account:</b> exports and imports of goods (the trade balance) and services, income such as interest and dividends, and transfers such as remittances.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Capital account:</b> foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, external loans and banking flows.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Official reserves:</b> when the two do not balance, the RBI's foreign exchange reserves make up the difference.</div>
</div>

## Exchange rates

Under a **flexible** rate, demand and supply of foreign currency set the price. Higher imports or capital outflows raise demand for dollars and **depreciate** the rupee; higher exports or inflows **appreciate** it. Under a **fixed** rate, the government holds the rate; under a **managed float** (India's system), the market sets it but the RBI steps in to calm sharp swings.

<div class="myth">

**"A falling rupee is always bad."** Depreciation makes imports dearer, but it also makes exports cheaper for foreign buyers, which can help exporters and jobs.

</div>
