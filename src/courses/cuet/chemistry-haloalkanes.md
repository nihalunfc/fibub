**Module 7 · about 22 minutes.** By the end you will predict SN1 or SN2, the product of elimination, and why haloarenes behave differently. This starts the organic run of Module 7: alcohols, carbonyls and amines build on it.

<div class="bigidea">The carbon attached to a halogen is slightly positive, so electron-rich nucleophiles attack it and push the halide out. Whether this happens in one step (SN2) or two (SN1) depends on how crowded that carbon is and how stable a carbocation it would make.</div>

**Everyday picture:** a seat on a crowded bus. If the seat is in the open (a methyl carbon), a new passenger can sit as the old one stands (SN2, one step). If the seat is boxed in (a tertiary carbon), the old passenger must leave first, and then anyone can take it from either side (SN1, two steps).

## 1. SN1 versus SN2

<div class="steps" data-title="Two ways to swap a halogen">
<div class="step"><b>SN2:</b> the nucleophile attacks from the back as the halide leaves, in one step. Rate = k[RX][Nu⁻].</div>
<div class="step"><b>SN2 result:</b> inversion of configuration, like an umbrella turning inside out in the wind.</div>
<div class="step"><b>SN1 step 1 (slow):</b> the halide leaves on its own, giving a flat carbocation. Rate = k[RX].</div>
<div class="step"><b>SN1 step 2 (fast):</b> the nucleophile attacks either face, giving a racemic mixture.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Which one?</b> Primary favours SN2; tertiary favours SN1 (stable carbocation); polar protic solvents help SN1.</div>
</div>

| Factor | SN2 faster with | SN1 faster with |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | CH₃X &gt; 1° &gt; 2° &gt; 3° | 3° &gt; 2° &gt; 1° (and benzylic, allylic) |
| Leaving group | I⁻ &gt; Br⁻ &gt; Cl⁻ &gt; F⁻ (both) | same |
| Nucleophile | strong, high concentration | does not matter (not in the slow step) |

**Elimination:** with alcoholic KOH, H and X leave from neighbouring carbons to give an alkene. Saytzeff's rule: the more substituted alkene is the major product.

## 2. Haloarenes and useful compounds

Haloarenes undergo nucleophilic substitution only under harsh conditions (or with −NO₂ groups ortho or para to the halogen). Halogen is ortho-para directing in electrophilic substitution but slightly deactivating. Grignard reagents (RMgX) and the Wurtz, Wurtz-Fittig and Fittig reactions join carbon chains.

| Compound | Use and concern |
|---|---|
| CH₂Cl₂ | paint stripper; harms the nervous system |
| CHCl₃ | once an anaesthetic; forms poisonous phosgene in air and light, so stored in dark bottles |
| CCl₄ | was used in fire extinguishers; damages the ozone layer |
| Freons (CFCs) | fridges, aerosols; break down ozone in the stratosphere |
| DDT | insecticide that fought malaria; persists and builds up in food chains |

<div class="myth">

**"Tertiary halides are the most reactive in every substitution."** Only in SN1. In SN2 they are the slowest, because the bulky groups block the back-side attack.

<div class="keypoints">

- SN2: one step, back attack, inversion; CH₃ &gt; 1° &gt; 2° &gt; 3°.
- SN1: carbocation, racemisation; 3° &gt; 2° &gt; 1°.
- Leaving group ability I &gt; Br &gt; Cl &gt; F.
- Alcoholic KOH eliminates (Saytzeff); aqueous KOH substitutes.
- Haloarenes resist substitution (partial double bond).
