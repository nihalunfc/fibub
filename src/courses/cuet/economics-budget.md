**Module 11 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will classify receipts and spending and compute the three deficits.

<div class="bigidea">A budget is a plan of receipts and spending. The deficits measure how much the government must borrow, and for what: day-to-day spending, everything, or everything except old interest.</div>

<div class="steps" data-title="Revenue or capital?">
<div class="step"><b>Revenue receipts</b> neither create a liability nor reduce assets: taxes, fees, dividends and interest received.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Capital receipts</b> do one of the two: borrowing, recovery of loans, disinvestment.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Revenue expenditure</b> creates no asset: salaries, subsidies, interest payments.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Capital expenditure</b> creates assets or cuts liabilities: building roads, buying machinery, repaying loans.</div>
</div>

| Deficit | Formula | It shows |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue deficit | revenue expenditure − revenue receipts | borrowing to meet day-to-day spending |
| Fiscal deficit | total expenditure − revenue receipts − non-debt capital receipts | total borrowing needed |
| Primary deficit | fiscal deficit − interest payments | borrowing for current needs, apart from past debt |

<div class="problem" data-problem="fiscal-deficit"></div>

<div class="myth">

**"All borrowing is bad."** Borrowing to build assets that raise future output can pay for itself. Borrowing to cover a revenue deficit adds debt without adding assets, which is why the revenue deficit is watched closely.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Capital receipts create liabilities or reduce assets; revenue receipts do neither.
- Fiscal deficit = total borrowing needed; primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest.
- Revenue deficit means borrowing for consumption, not investment.
- Budget aims: allocation, redistribution, stabilisation, growth.
