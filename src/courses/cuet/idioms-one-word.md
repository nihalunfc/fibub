**Module 2 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will solve idiom, phrasal verb and one-word questions, including ones you have never seen, from context.

<div class="bigidea">An idiom's meaning is not the meaning of its words. When you don't know one, the sentence around it and the "feel" of the picture it paints usually give it away.</div>

## 1. Idioms: think of the picture

<div class="steps" data-title="Cracking an idiom you have not met">
<div class="step"><b>Picture it.</b> "To add fuel to the fire": adding fuel makes a fire bigger. So the idiom means making a bad situation worse.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Use the sentence.</b> "His rude reply only added fuel to the fire, and the argument got louder." The result confirms it.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Check the charge.</b> Most idioms are clearly positive or negative. Rule out options with the wrong charge.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Don't read it literally.</b> An option that repeats the literal words ("to put petrol on a fire") is almost always the trap.</div>
</div>

## 2. Phrasal verbs: the small word matters

One verb changes meaning with its particle: **put off** (postpone), **put out** (extinguish), **put up with** (tolerate), **put across** (explain clearly). **Turn down** (refuse), **turn up** (arrive), **turn out** (prove to be). Learn them in groups by verb.

## 3. One-word substitution

Many come from roots (Lesson 2.2): a person who loves books is a **bibliophile**; the killing of a king is **regicide**; something that can be read is **legible**; a speech made without preparation is **extempore**. Learn them in families: -cide (killing), -phile (lover), -cracy (rule by), -logy (study of), omni- (all).

<div class="myth">

**"Similar-looking words mean similar things."** Incorrigible, invincible and infallible all start with "in-" but mean beyond correction, unbeatable and never wrong. One-word questions are built on these look-alikes.

</div>
