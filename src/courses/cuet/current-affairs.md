**Module 5 · about 10 minutes.** By the end you will have a daily habit and a revision sheet for current affairs that takes 15 minutes a day.

<div class="bigidea">Current affairs cannot be crammed in a week, but they also do not need hours. Fifteen minutes a day with one reliable source, written as five-line notes, covers what the test asks.</div>

## 1. The daily 15 minutes

<div class="steps" data-title="15 minutes a day">
<div class="step"><b>5 minutes: scan.</b> One national newspaper's front page and national pages, or the Press Information Bureau's daily releases.</div>
<div class="step"><b>7 minutes: note.</b> For two or three events that could be asked (new schemes, awards, appointments, summits, sports results, science missions), write: what, who, where, when, why it matters.</div>
<div class="step"><b>3 minutes: revise.</b> Read yesterday's notes once. That one look doubles what you remember a month later.</div>
</div>

## 2. The monthly sheet

On the first of each month, copy the month's notes into one page sorted by type: government and schemes, economy, science and space, sports, awards and books, international. Twelve pages make your whole current-affairs revision for the last week before the exam.

## 3. What to skip

Political opinion pieces, crime stories and gossip are rarely asked. Focus on facts with a name, a number and a date.

<div class="myth">

**"I can do current affairs from a monthly magazine in the last week."** Last-minute compilations are long and fade fast. Short notes written yourself through the year are faster to revise and stick better.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- 15 minutes a day: scan, note two or three events, revise yesterday.
- Note what, who, where, when and why it matters.
- Use official sources such as the Press Information Bureau.
- One page per month; revise the twelve pages in the last week.

</div>
