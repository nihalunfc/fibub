**Module 7 · about 24 minutes.** By the end you will predict carbonyl reactions, tell aldehydes from ketones by test, and rank acid strengths. Read first: [alcohols, phenols and ethers](/learn/courses/cuet/chemistry-alcohols/).

<div class="bigidea">The C=O carbon is electron-poor, so nucleophiles add to it. The hydrogen next to it (the α-hydrogen) is slightly acidic, which opens a second family of reactions. And when the carbonyl carries an O-H, you get a carboxylic acid.</div>

**Everyday picture:** vinegar is ethanoic acid in water; the smell of almonds is benzaldehyde; nail-polish remover is propanone (acetone).

## 1. Telling them apart

| Test | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|
| Tollens' (ammoniacal AgNO₃) | silver mirror | no change |
| Fehling's (Cu²⁺ in tartrate) | red Cu₂O (aliphatic aldehydes) | no change |
| 2,4-DNP | orange-yellow precipitate | orange-yellow precipitate |
| Iodoform (I₂ + NaOH) | only ethanal | methyl ketones (CH₃CO−) |

## 2. Key reactions

<div class="steps" data-title="Reactions to know by name">
<div class="step"><b>Nucleophilic addition:</b> HCN gives cyanohydrins; NaHSO₃ gives crystalline adducts; NH₂-Z gives oximes, hydrazones, semicarbazones.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Aldol condensation:</b> two carbonyls with α-H join in dilute base to a β-hydroxy carbonyl, which loses water on heating.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Cannizzaro:</b> no α-H + conc. base: one molecule becomes the alcohol, one the acid salt.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Reductions:</b> Clemmensen (Zn-Hg/HCl) or Wolff-Kishner (NH₂NH₂/KOH) turn C=O into CH₂.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Acids:</b> HVZ (Cl₂/red P) puts Cl on the α-carbon; decarboxylation with soda lime gives an alkane with one carbon fewer.</div>
</div>

Preparation in brief: Rosenmund (acid chloride + H₂/Pd-BaSO₄ → aldehyde), Etard (toluene + CrO₂Cl₂ → benzaldehyde), Stephen (nitrile + SnCl₂/HCl), ozonolysis of alkenes, and Friedel-Crafts acylation for aromatic ketones.

## 3. Acid strength

Carboxylic acids are stronger than phenols because the carboxylate's charge is shared equally by two oxygens. Electron-pulling groups (−Cl, −NO₂, −F) near the COOH raise strength; electron-pushing alkyl groups lower it. Order: HCOOH &gt; CH₃COOH; FCH₂COOH &gt; ClCH₂COOH.

<div class="problem" data-problem="weak-acid-ph"></div>

<div class="myth">

**"Ketones are always less reactive than aldehydes for no clear reason."** There are two reasons: two alkyl groups push electrons onto the C=O carbon (less positive) and they crowd it (harder to reach). That is why aldehydes are more reactive in nucleophilic addition.

</div>
