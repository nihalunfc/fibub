**Chemistry · Class 12 · about 40 minutes.** By the end you will be able to make aldehydes, ketones and acids, predict addition products and their order of reactivity, use the tests that tell them apart, apply the aldol, Cannizzaro and haloform reactions, and rank acids by strength.

<div class="bigidea">The C=O group is polar: carbon slightly positive, oxygen slightly negative. Nucleophiles attack the carbon; that is almost the whole chemistry of aldehydes and ketones. Hydrogens next to C=O (α-hydrogens) are slightly acidic, which gives aldol reactions. In carboxylic acids the C=O sits next to an OH, and the charge of the carboxylate ion spreads over two oxygens: that is why they are true acids.</div>

## 1. Structure, names and making them

The carbonyl carbon is sp², trigonal planar, with bond angles near 120°. Names: methanal HCHO (formaldehyde), ethanal CH₃CHO, propanone CH₃COCH₃ (acetone), benzaldehyde C₆H₅CHO, acetophenone C₆H₅COCH₃; methanoic (formic), ethanoic (acetic), benzoic acid.

**Aldehydes and ketones:**
- Oxidise alcohols: 1° → aldehyde (PCC, or distil it off), 2° → ketone; or dehydrogenate over hot Cu.
- **Ozonolysis** of alkenes; **hydration of alkynes** (HgSO₄/H₂SO₄: ethyne gives ethanal, others give ketones).
- **Rosenmund reduction:** acid chloride + H₂ over Pd/BaSO₄ (poisoned) → aldehyde. **Stephen reaction:** nitrile + SnCl₂/HCl, then water → aldehyde. **DIBAL-H** reduces nitriles and esters to aldehydes.
- **Etard reaction:** toluene + CrO₂Cl₂ → benzaldehyde. **Gattermann-Koch:** benzene + CO + HCl (AlCl₃/CuCl) → benzaldehyde.
- Ketones from acid chlorides with dialkylcadmium, from nitriles with Grignard reagents, and by **Friedel-Crafts acylation** (benzene + CH₃COCl/AlCl₃ → acetophenone).

**Carboxylic acids:** oxidise 1° alcohols or aldehydes (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇); oxidise alkylbenzenes with hot KMnO₄ (any side chain with a benzylic H becomes COOH); hydrolyse nitriles or amides; Grignard reagent + CO₂ (dry ice), then acid.

## 2. Nucleophilic addition

A nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon, the carbon becomes sp³, and the oxygen picks up H⁺. **Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones** for two reasons: fewer electron-pushing alkyl groups (more positive carbon) and less crowding. Order: HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃COCH₃; aromatic aldehydes and ketones are slower still (the ring shares electrons with C=O).

- **HCN** (with a trace of base to make CN⁻) → **cyanohydrins**; hydrolysis gives α-hydroxy acids.
- **NaHSO₃** → crystalline bisulphite compounds (aldehydes and small methyl ketones); used to purify them.
- **Grignard reagents** → alcohols after hydrolysis (HCHO gives 1°, RCHO 2°, ketones 3°).
- **Alcohols** (dry HCl) → hemiacetals then **acetals**; with ethane-1,2-diol, cyclic ketals (used to protect C=O).
- **Ammonia derivatives** (addition then loss of water, Z = OH, NH₂, NHC₆H₅, NHCONH₂): hydroxylamine → oxime, hydrazine → hydrazone, phenylhydrazine → phenylhydrazone, **2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNP)** → orange-red precipitate, semicarbazide → semicarbazone.

## 3. Reduction, oxidation and tests

**Reduction:** to alcohols with NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄; all the way to CH₂ with **Clemmensen** (Zn-Hg/conc. HCl, for acid-stable compounds) or **Wolff-Kishner** (NH₂NH₂, then KOH in ethylene glycol, heat; for base-stable compounds).

**Oxidation tells aldehydes from ketones.** Aldehydes have an H on the carbonyl carbon and are easily oxidised; ketones need strong oxidisers and break C-C bonds (Popoff's rule: the C=O stays with the smaller group).

- **Tollens reagent** ([Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺): silver mirror with all aldehydes.
- **Fehling's** (Cu²⁺ tartrate, alkaline) and **Benedict's** solutions: red Cu₂O with aliphatic aldehydes, not with benzaldehyde.
- **Iodoform (haloform) test:** I₂ + NaOH with any CH₃CO- compound (or an alcohol oxidised to one, CH₃CH(OH)-) gives yellow CHI₃ and the acid with one carbon fewer.

## 4. Reactions of α-hydrogens and the Cannizzaro reaction

**Aldol reaction:** in dilute NaOH, an aldehyde or ketone with α-H forms an enolate that attacks a second molecule: 2CH₃CHO → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO (aldol, 3-hydroxybutanal), which loses water on heating to give but-2-enal (**aldol condensation**). **Crossed aldol** with two different compounds that both have α-H gives four products; with one lacking α-H (benzaldehyde + ethanal, the Claisen-Schmidt reaction) the mixture is cleaner.

**Cannizzaro reaction:** an aldehyde **without** α-H in concentrated NaOH disproportionates: one molecule is reduced to the alcohol, another oxidised to the acid salt. 2HCHO → CH₃OH + HCOO⁻. In a **crossed Cannizzaro** with HCHO, methanal is always the one oxidised (it is the most reactive), so the other aldehyde becomes the alcohol.

**Halogenation at α-carbon:** for acids, the **Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky (HVZ)** reaction (Cl₂ or Br₂ with red P) gives α-halo acids.

<div class="myth">

**"Any aldehyde in NaOH gives the Cannizzaro reaction."** Only aldehydes with no α-hydrogen (HCHO, C₆H₅CHO, (CH₃)₃CCHO). Ethanal in NaOH gives the aldol product instead, because pulling off an α-H is much faster than the hydride transfer that Cannizzaro needs.

</div>

## 5. Carboxylic acids: acidity and derivatives

Carboxylic acids (pKa about 4 to 5) are stronger than phenols (10) and alcohols (16), because the carboxylate charge is shared **equally** by two oxygens. They dissolve in NaHCO₃ with fizzing.

**Substituent effects:** electron-withdrawing groups strengthen the acid, more so when closer to COOH: Cl₃CCOOH > Cl₂CHCOOH > ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH; FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > BrCH₂COOH; 2-chlorobutanoic > 3-chlorobutanoic. Electron-pushing groups weaken it: HCOOH (3.75) > CH₃COOH (4.76). Benzoic acid (4.20) is stronger than ethanoic; p-nitrobenzoic is stronger still, p-methylbenzoic weaker. **Ortho effect:** almost any ortho-substituted benzoic acid is stronger than benzoic acid.

**Derivatives** (reactivity to nucleophiles): acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide. Acid chlorides come from SOCl₂, PCl₅ or PCl₃. **Esterification** (acid + alcohol, conc. H₂SO₄) is reversible; removing water or using excess alcohol pushes it forward.

<div class="problem" data-problem="ak-ester-eq"></div>

**Other reactions:** LiAlH₄ reduces acids to 1° alcohols (NaBH₄ does not). **Decarboxylation:** sodium salt heated with soda lime gives an alkane with one carbon fewer (CH₃COONa → CH₄). **Kolbe electrolysis** of a carboxylate gives an alkane with twice the alkyl group (2CH₃COO⁻ → C₂H₆ + 2CO₂). In benzoic acid, COOH is deactivating and meta-directing; benzoic acid does not undergo Friedel-Crafts reactions.

<div class="myth">

**"Methanoic acid is just a carboxylic acid, so it fails the Tollens test."** Methanoic acid, H-COOH, has a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon, like an aldehyde. It reduces Tollens and Fehling's reagents (being oxidised to CO₂ and water). It is the classic odd one out in "which acid gives a silver mirror?"

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Nucleophilic addition: HCHO > other aldehydes > ketones; aromatic ones slower.
- 2,4-DNP: any aldehyde or ketone. Tollens: aldehydes (and HCOOH). Fehling: aliphatic aldehydes only.
- Iodoform: CH₃CO- or CH₃CH(OH)-; gives CHI₃ and an acid with one carbon fewer.
- Aldol needs α-H (dilute base); Cannizzaro needs no α-H (concentrated base); crossed Cannizzaro oxidises HCHO.
- Clemmensen (acid) and Wolff-Kishner (base) reduce C=O to CH₂.
- Acid strength rises with nearby electron-withdrawing groups; HCOOH > CH₃COOH; ortho effect in benzoic acids.
- Derivative reactivity: acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide.

</div>
