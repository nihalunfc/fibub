**Chemistry · Class 12 · about 36 minutes.** By the end you will be able to write configurations of transition atoms and ions, explain their colours, magnetism and variable oxidation states, write the key reactions of dichromate and permanganate, and explain the lanthanoid contraction and its effects.

<div class="bigidea">Transition metals fill an inner shell (3d, 4d, 5d) while the outer s shell stays the same. Because the d and s energies are so close, these atoms can lose different numbers of electrons (many oxidation states), their partly filled d levels absorb visible light (colour), their unpaired d electrons make them magnetic, and their ability to switch states makes them good catalysts.</div>

## 1. Electron configurations

The 3d series runs from Sc (3d¹ 4s²) to Zn (3d¹⁰ 4s²). Two exceptions: **Cr is 3d⁵ 4s¹** and **Cu is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹**, because half-filled and completely filled d sets have extra stability (exchange energy).

When the atom forms an ion, **4s electrons leave first**, even though 4s filled first. Fe is 3d⁶ 4s²; Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶; Fe³⁺ is 3d⁵.

A **transition element** has a partly filled d subshell in the atom or a common ion. Zn, Cd and Hg (d¹⁰ in atom and ions) are d-block but not true transition elements. Cu counts, because Cu²⁺ is d⁹.

## 2. Physical trends

- **Size** falls from Sc to about Fe, then levels off (extra d electrons shield the extra nuclear charge). 4d and 5d atoms are almost the **same size** because of the lanthanoid contraction (section 6).
- **Melting points** are high, peaking in the middle (many unpaired d electrons join metallic bonding). Zn, Cd, Hg are low; Hg is a liquid.
- **Ionisation energies** rise slowly across. Irregular jumps match stable configurations: Mn²⁺ (d⁵) and Zn²⁺ (d¹⁰) are hard to oxidise further.
- **E°(M²⁺/M)** is negative for most (they displace H₂ from acid) except **Cu (+0.34 V)**, which is why copper does not dissolve in dilute HCl. Mn (d⁵ ion), Zn (d¹⁰ ion) and Ni (high hydration enthalpy) are more negative than the trend.
- **E°(M³⁺/M²⁺)**: Mn³⁺/Mn²⁺ is very positive (+1.57 V), because Mn²⁺ is the stable d⁵; Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ is modest (+0.77 V) because Fe³⁺ is d⁵. Cr²⁺ is a strong reducing agent (it wants to become d³ Cr³⁺, which has a half-filled t₂g set).

## 3. Oxidation states, colour, magnetism, catalysis

**Oxidation states.** The most states are in the middle: Mn shows +2 to +7. The highest state equals the number of 3d + 4s electrons up to Mn (+7 in MnO₄⁻). High states are found in oxides and fluorides (O and F are small and very electronegative): Mn₂O₇, CrO₃, VF₅. Low states appear in carbonyls like Ni(CO)₄ (0).

**Colour** comes from **d-d transitions**: in a complex, the d orbitals split into two energy levels, and an electron absorbs visible light to jump between them. We see the complementary colour. d⁰ and d¹⁰ ions are colourless. MnO₄⁻ (d⁰) and Cr₂O₇²⁻ (d⁰) are coloured for another reason: **charge transfer** from oxygen to the metal.

**Magnetism.** Count unpaired electrons and use μ = √(n(n + 2)): 1.73, 2.83, 3.87, 4.90 and 5.92 BM for n = 1 to 5.

**Catalysis.** Variable oxidation states and surfaces that adsorb reactants: Fe (Haber), V₂O₅ (contact), Ni (hydrogenation of oils), Pt/Rh (Ostwald, car exhausts), TiCl₄ with Al(C₂H₅)₃ (Ziegler-Natta polythene). Fe³⁺ catalyses I⁻ + S₂O₈²⁻ by cycling between Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺.

**Interstitial compounds** (TiC, Fe₃C, Mn₄N) trap small atoms in the metal lattice: hard, high melting, still conducting. **Alloys** form easily because the atoms are of similar size (brass: Cu-Zn; bronze: Cu-Sn; stainless steel: Fe-Cr-Ni).

<div class="myth">

**"Transition metal ions lose their d electrons first because 3d is higher in energy."** In the neutral atom 4s fills first, but once electrons are removed, 3d drops below 4s. So 4s empties first: Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶, not 3d⁴ 4s². Writing [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² for Fe²⁺ is a classic lost mark.

</div>

## 4. Potassium dichromate, K₂Cr₂O₇

**Made from chromite ore** FeCr₂O₄: fuse with Na₂CO₃ in air → Na₂CrO₄ (yellow); acidify → Na₂Cr₂O₇ (orange); add KCl → K₂Cr₂O₇ (less soluble, crystallises out).

**Chromate-dichromate equilibrium** depends on pH: 2CrO₄²⁻ (yellow) + 2H⁺ ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻ (orange) + H₂O. Acid gives orange, alkali yellow. Cr is +6 in both.

**Oxidising agent in acid:** Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ (green) + 7H₂O. It oxidises Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺, I⁻ to I₂, H₂S to S, Sn²⁺ to Sn⁴⁺, SO₂ to SO₄²⁻ (the green colour test for SO₂), and ethanol in the old breath test. Structure: two CrO₄ tetrahedra sharing one oxygen.

## 5. Potassium permanganate, KMnO₄

**Made from pyrolusite** MnO₂: fuse with KOH and an oxidiser (air or KNO₃) → K₂MnO₄ (green manganate); then oxidise it (Cl₂, O₃, or electrolysis), or disproportionate it in neutral or acid solution: 3MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂ + 2H₂O.

**Its oxidising power depends on the medium:**

| Medium | Change | Electrons gained |
|---|---|---|
| Acid | MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ (colourless) | 5 |
| Neutral or weakly alkaline | MnO₄⁻ → MnO₂ (brown) | 3 |
| Strongly alkaline | MnO₄⁻ → MnO₄²⁻ (green) | 1 |

In acid it oxidises Fe²⁺, oxalate (warm, autocatalysed by Mn²⁺), I⁻, H₂S, SO₂ and NO₂⁻. In titrations it is its own indicator: the first lasting pink means the end point. **HCl is not used** to acidify, because KMnO₄ oxidises Cl⁻ to Cl₂; dilute H₂SO₄ is used.

## 6. The f-block: lanthanoids and actinoids

**Lanthanoids** (Ce to Lu) fill 4f. Their common state is **+3**. Some show +2 or +4 when that gives an empty, half-filled or full 4f set: Ce⁴⁺ (4f⁰, a strong oxidiser), Eu²⁺ (4f⁷) and Yb²⁺ (4f¹⁴, reducing agents), Tb⁴⁺ (4f⁷).

**Lanthanoid contraction:** across the series, atomic and ionic radii shrink steadily, because 4f electrons shield each other poorly from the growing nuclear charge. Results: (i) the 4d and 5d elements of each group are nearly the same size (Zr/Hf, Nb/Ta, Mo/W); (ii) the lanthanoids themselves are hard to separate; (iii) basic strength of Ln(OH)₃ falls from La(OH)₃ to Lu(OH)₃. **Misch metal** (lanthanoids with iron) is used in lighter flints.

**Actinoids** (Th to Lr) fill 5f. They show more oxidation states (U: +3 to +6; Np, Pu up to +7) because 5f, 6d and 7s are close in energy. All are radioactive; the later ones are made in reactors. The **actinoid contraction** is larger than the lanthanoid contraction, because 5f shields even worse than 4f.

**Try it:** slide from La to Lu, then switch to "Zr and Hf twins" and compare the 4d and 5d metals.

<div class="myth">

**"Lanthanoids show many oxidation states, like transition metals."** Most stick to +3. The 4f orbitals are buried deep inside the atom and hardly take part in bonding. It is the actinoids, with their more exposed 5f orbitals, that show a wide range.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Cr 3d⁵ 4s¹, Cu 3d¹⁰ 4s¹; ions lose 4s electrons first.
- Colour from d-d transitions (d¹ to d⁹); d⁰ and d¹⁰ colourless; MnO₄⁻ and Cr₂O₇²⁻ coloured by charge transfer.
- μ = √(n(n + 2)) BM: 1.73, 2.83, 3.87, 4.90, 5.92 for n = 1 to 5.
- Mn shows +2 to +7; highest states in oxides and fluorides.
- Dichromate in acid gains 6 e⁻ (→ 2Cr³⁺); permanganate gains 5 (acid), 3 (neutral), 1 (strong alkali).
- Chromate (yellow) ⇌ dichromate (orange) with pH; Cr stays +6.
- Lanthanoid contraction: poor 4f shielding; Zr and Hf the same size; Ce⁴⁺ oxidises, Eu²⁺ reduces.

</div>
