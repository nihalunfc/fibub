**Chemistry · Class 12 · about 34 minutes.** By the end you will be able to draw and explain glucose and fructose, tell reducing from non-reducing sugars, explain mutarotation, describe amino acids and protein structure, work out a peptide's mass, and recall the key facts about enzymes, vitamins, nucleic acids and hormones.

<div class="bigidea">Life runs on four kinds of large molecules built from small repeating units: sugars joined by glycosidic links (carbohydrates), amino acids joined by peptide bonds (proteins), nucleotides joined by phosphate links (nucleic acids), and fats. Every link forms by losing water and breaks by adding it back (hydrolysis). Shape decides function: a cooked egg has the same sequence but a ruined shape.</div>

## 1. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that give them on hydrolysis. **Monosaccharides** (glucose, fructose) cannot be hydrolysed further; **oligosaccharides** give 2 to 10 units (sucrose, maltose, lactose are disaccharides); **polysaccharides** give many (starch, cellulose, glycogen). A sugar with a CHO group is an **aldose**, with C=O inside the chain a **ketose**. **Reducing sugars** reduce Tollens' and Fehling's reagents: all monosaccharides, maltose and lactose. Sucrose is non-reducing.

**Glucose** C₆H₁₂O₆ (an aldohexose). Evidence for the open-chain structure: HI gives n-hexane (straight chain of six carbons); NH₂OH gives an oxime and HCN a cyanohydrin (C=O present); bromine water gives **gluconic acid** (the C=O is a CHO); acetic anhydride gives a **pentaacetate** (five OH groups); nitric acid gives **saccharic acid** (both ends oxidised, so one end was CH₂OH). Natural glucose is the **D** form (OH on the last chiral carbon on the right) and is dextrorotatory.

**Cyclic structure.** Glucose fails some aldehyde tests (Schiff's, NaHSO₃) and its pentaacetate does not react with NH₂OH. The CHO reacts with the OH on C-5 to form a six-membered ring (**pyranose**, a hemiacetal). C-1 becomes a new chiral centre: **α-D-glucose** (OH on C-1 down in the Haworth ring) and **β-D-glucose** (up). These two are **anomers**.

**Mutarotation.** Pure α-glucose has specific rotation +112°, β-glucose +19°. Either one in water slowly changes to an equilibrium mixture (about 36% α, 64% β, a trace open chain) with +52.5°, through the open-chain form.

**Fructose** is a ketohexose (C=O at C-2), laevorotatory (−92°), and forms a five-membered ring (**furanose**). It still reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents in alkaline solution.

**Disaccharides:**

| Sugar | Units and link | Reducing? |
|---|---|---|
| Sucrose | α-glucose C-1 to β-fructose C-2 | no |
| Maltose | two α-glucose, C-1 to C-4 | yes |
| Lactose (milk) | β-galactose C-1 to glucose C-4 | yes |

**Invert sugar:** sucrose (+66.5°) hydrolysed by acid or the enzyme invertase gives equal amounts of glucose (+52.5°) and fructose (−92°), a laevorotatory mixture: the sign of rotation inverts.

**Polysaccharides:** **starch** = amylose (unbranched α(1→4) glucose, water soluble, blue with iodine; 15 to 20%) + amylopectin (branched with α(1→6) links; 80 to 85%). **Glycogen** (animal starch) is like amylopectin but more branched. **Cellulose** is unbranched **β**(1→4) glucose; humans lack the enzyme to break β links, so it is fibre for us.

## 2. Amino acids and proteins

**α-Amino acids** have NH₂ and COOH on the same carbon, plus a side chain R. Twenty are common; all but glycine are chiral and natural ones are **L**. **Essential amino acids** (valine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, phenylalanine, threonine, methionine, tryptophan, histidine, arginine) must come from food.

In water, an amino acid exists as a **zwitterion**, ⁺H₃N-CHR-COO⁻: the acid group gives its H⁺ to the amine group. So amino acids are crystalline, high melting and water soluble, unlike typical organic compounds. They are **amphoteric**. At the **isoelectric point (pI)** the net charge is zero and the molecule does not move in an electric field.

**Peptides** are named from the free NH₂ end (N-terminal) to the free COOH end (C-terminal): Gly-Ala means glycine supplies the NH₂ end. Two different amino acids can make 2 dipeptides (Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly); three different ones make 6 tripeptides.

**Levels of protein structure:**
- **Primary:** the sequence of amino acids. One change can cause disease (sickle cell anaemia: one glutamic acid replaced by valine in haemoglobin).
- **Secondary:** **α-helix** (right-handed coil held by hydrogen bonds between C=O and N-H four residues apart) or **β-pleated sheet** (side-by-side chains, as in silk).
- **Tertiary:** the overall 3D folding: **fibrous** (keratin, myosin; insoluble) or **globular** (insulin, albumin; soluble). Held by hydrogen bonds, disulphide bridges, ionic and van der Waals forces.
- **Quaternary:** several chains together (haemoglobin has four).

**Denaturation:** heat, pH change or chemicals destroy the 2°, 3° and 4° structure without breaking peptide bonds. Boiled egg white and curdled milk are examples; the protein loses its function.

<div class="myth">

**"Denaturation breaks a protein down into amino acids."** Denaturation only unfolds the chain: the peptide bonds and the primary sequence stay intact. Breaking peptide bonds is **hydrolysis**, which needs acid, alkali or digestive enzymes.

</div>

## 3. Enzymes, vitamins and hormones

**Enzymes** are biological catalysts, mostly globular proteins. They are highly **specific** (lock and key), work best at about body temperature and an optimum pH, and lower activation energy enormously (for example, invertase: sucrose → glucose + fructose; maltase; zymase: glucose → ethanol + CO₂). Many need a non-protein helper (**cofactor**: a metal ion or a coenzyme, often a vitamin).

**Vitamins:** fat-soluble **A, D, E, K** (stored in the body); water-soluble **B group and C** (must be eaten regularly; B₁₂ is stored in the liver).

| Vitamin | Deficiency disease |
|---|---|
| A | night blindness, xerophthalmia |
| B₁ (thiamine) | beriberi |
| B₂ (riboflavin) | cracked lips, cheilosis |
| B₆ (pyridoxine) | convulsions |
| B₁₂ | pernicious anaemia |
| C (ascorbic acid) | scurvy (bleeding gums) |
| D | rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults |
| E | muscle weakness, fragile red blood cells |
| K | slow blood clotting |

**Hormones** are chemical messengers: **steroids** (oestrogen, testosterone, cortisol), **polypeptides** (insulin and glucagon control blood glucose), **amino acid derivatives** (adrenaline, thyroxine; iodine deficiency causes goitre).

## 4. Nucleic acids

A **nucleoside** = base + sugar; a **nucleotide** = base + sugar + phosphate. Nucleotides join by **phosphodiester** links between C-5′ of one sugar and C-3′ of the next.

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | 2-deoxy-D-ribose | D-ribose |
| Bases | A, G, C, **T** | A, G, C, **U** |
| Structure | double helix (Watson and Crick, 1953) | usually single strand |
| Role | stores genetic information, replicates | protein synthesis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) |

In the DNA double helix, **A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds)** and **G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds)**, so A = T and G = C in amount (Chargaff's rule). Adenine and guanine are **purines** (two rings); cytosine, thymine and uracil are **pyrimidines** (one ring). One turn of the helix is about 10 base pairs and 3.4 nm.

**Try it:** drag the helix to look along it, then pick AT-rich and GC-rich pieces and raise the temperature to melt them.

<div class="myth">

**"All sugars are sweet and all reduce Fehling's solution."** Starch and cellulose are not sweet and not reducing; sucrose is sweet but not reducing. Reducing power needs a free anomeric carbon (a ring that can open to a CHO or α-hydroxy ketone).

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Glucose: open chain shows CHO, 5 OH, straight 6-carbon chain; ring (pyranose) explains failed Schiff's and NaHSO₃ tests.
- α (+112°) and β (+19°) anomers mutarotate to +52.5°.
- Sucrose (C-1 to C-2) is non-reducing; maltose and lactose are reducing. Invert sugar is laevorotatory.
- Starch: α links (amylose + amylopectin); cellulose: β(1→4) links.
- Amino acids are zwitterions; pI is where net charge is zero.
- Peptide mass = sum of amino acids − 18 × (number of peptide bonds).
- Protein structure: primary sequence, secondary helix or sheet, tertiary fold, quaternary assembly; denaturation keeps the sequence.
- Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K; DNA has T and deoxyribose, RNA has U and ribose; A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3).

</div>
