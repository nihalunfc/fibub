**Chemistry · Class 11 and 12 laboratory · about 36 minutes.** By the end you will be able to make a standard solution, choose an indicator, do the calculations for acid-base and redox titrations, identify the anion and cation in a salt from test results, detect N, S and halogens in an organic compound, and recall the key preparations and functional group tests.

<div class="bigidea">Practical chemistry asks two questions: "how much?" (titrations, where equal equivalents react at the end point) and "what is it?" (qualitative analysis, where each ion gives a colour, gas or precipitate you can see). Behind every test is a reaction you already know: solubility, complex formation or redox.</div>

## 1. Standard solutions and titration basics

A **primary standard** is pure, stable, not hygroscopic and has a high molar mass, so it can be weighed exactly: **oxalic acid dihydrate** (126), **Mohr's salt** FeSO₄·(NH₄)₂SO₄·6H₂O (392), anhydrous Na₂CO₃, K₂Cr₂O₇. NaOH (absorbs CO₂ and water) and KMnO₄ (slowly decomposes, contains MnO₂) are **secondary standards**: they are standardised by titration against a primary one.

At the end point, **equivalents of titrant = equivalents of titrand**: N₁V₁ = N₂V₂, where normality N = molarity × n-factor. Using moles and the balanced equation gives the same answer.

## 2. Acid-base titrations and indicators

An indicator is a weak acid whose two forms have different colours. It changes over about 2 pH units around its pKa. Choose one whose range lies inside the steep jump at the equivalence point.

| Titration | pH at equivalence | Indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | 7 | methyl orange or phenolphthalein |
| Weak acid + strong base | above 7 | phenolphthalein (colourless to pink, 8.3 to 10) |
| Strong acid + weak base | below 7 | methyl orange (red to yellow, 3.1 to 4.4) |
| Weak acid + weak base | no sharp jump | not done with a simple indicator |

## 3. Redox titrations

- **KMnO₄** in dilute H₂SO₄ (5 electrons per MnO₄⁻) against Mohr's salt or oxalic acid. **No indicator** is needed: the first excess drop turns the solution pale pink. Oxalic acid titrations are done warm (about 60 °C), and they speed up once a little Mn²⁺ forms (autocatalysis). HCl is never used: KMnO₄ would oxidise Cl⁻.
- **K₂Cr₂O₇** (6 electrons) against Fe²⁺, with **diphenylamine** as an internal indicator.
- **Iodometry:** an oxidiser frees I₂ from excess KI, then the I₂ is titrated with sodium thiosulphate using **starch** (blue-black disappears at the end point).

## 4. Salt analysis: anions

**Preliminary dry tests:** colour (blue/green suggests Cu²⁺ or Ni²⁺; pink Co²⁺ or Mn²⁺), **flame test** (Na golden yellow, K lilac, Ca brick red, Sr crimson, Ba apple green, Cu blue-green), **borax bead** (blue for Co, green for Cr, violet for Mn, brown for Ni in the oxidising flame).

| Anion | Test | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| CO₃²⁻ | dilute H₂SO₄ | colourless gas, turns lime water milky (CO₂) |
| S²⁻ | dilute H₂SO₄ | rotten-egg smell; lead acetate paper turns black |
| SO₃²⁻ | dilute H₂SO₄ | SO₂ (burning smell), turns acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ green |
| NO₂⁻ | dilute H₂SO₄ | brown fumes of NO₂; KI-starch paper turns blue |
| CH₃COO⁻ | dilute H₂SO₄ | vinegar smell; deep red with neutral FeCl₃ |
| Cl⁻ | conc. H₂SO₄; AgNO₃ | HCl fumes; white AgCl, soluble in NH₄OH; **chromyl chloride test**: heated with K₂Cr₂O₇ and conc. H₂SO₄, orange-red CrO₂Cl₂ vapours, giving yellow chromate in NaOH |
| Br⁻ | conc. H₂SO₄; AgNO₃ | brown Br₂ vapour; pale yellow AgBr, partly soluble in NH₄OH |
| I⁻ | conc. H₂SO₄; AgNO₃ | violet I₂ vapour; yellow AgI, insoluble in NH₄OH |
| NO₃⁻ | FeSO₄ then conc. H₂SO₄ down the side | **brown ring** of [Fe(H₂O)₅NO]²⁺ |
| SO₄²⁻ | BaCl₂ | white BaSO₄, insoluble in HCl |
| PO₄³⁻ | ammonium molybdate + HNO₃ | canary-yellow precipitate |

## 5. Salt analysis: cation groups

| Group | Ions | Group reagent | Precipitate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | NH₄⁺ | NaOH, heat | NH₃ gas (turns red litmus blue; brown with Nessler's reagent) |
| I | Pb²⁺ (Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺) | dilute HCl | white chlorides (PbCl₂ dissolves in hot water) |
| II | Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, Cd²⁺, As³⁺ | H₂S in dilute HCl | sulphides (CuS black, CdS yellow, As₂S₃ yellow) |
| III | Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Cr³⁺ | NH₄OH with NH₄Cl | hydroxides (Fe(OH)₃ reddish-brown, Al(OH)₃ white gelatinous, Cr(OH)₃ green) |
| IV | Zn²⁺, Mn²⁺, Ni²⁺, Co²⁺ | H₂S in NH₄OH | sulphides (ZnS white, MnS buff, NiS and CoS black) |
| V | Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺ | (NH₄)₂CO₃ with NH₄OH | white carbonates |
| VI | Mg²⁺ | Na₂HPO₄ with NH₄OH | white Mg(NH₄)PO₄ |

**Why the order works:** NH₄Cl in group III holds down [OH⁻] (common ion), so only the least soluble hydroxides precipitate and Mg(OH)₂ stays in solution. Confirmatory tests: Cu²⁺ deep blue with excess NH₄OH; Fe³⁺ Prussian blue with K₄[Fe(CN)₆] and blood red with KSCN; Ni²⁺ red with dimethylglyoxime; Pb²⁺ yellow PbCrO₄ with K₂CrO₄ (and yellow PbI₂ with KI).

<div class="myth">

**"If a gas turns lime water milky, the anion must be carbonate."** SO₂ from a sulphite does the same. Check with acidified dichromate paper: only SO₂ turns it green. Many exam questions are built on this pair.

</div>

## 6. Organic analysis

**Lassaigne's test:** fuse the compound with sodium metal, which turns N, S and halogens into NaCN, Na₂S and NaX. Boil the fused mass in water (sodium extract).
- **N:** FeSO₄, boil, then FeCl₃ and acid: **Prussian blue** Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃.
- **S:** sodium nitroprusside gives **violet**; lead acetate gives black PbS.
- **N and S together:** blood red with FeCl₃ (NaSCN).
- **Halogens:** boil with HNO₃ first (to destroy CN⁻ and S²⁻, which would also precipitate with Ag⁺), then AgNO₃.

**Functional group tests:** unsaturation (bromine water or Baeyer's reagent decolourised); alcohols (Na gives H₂; ceric ammonium nitrate gives red); phenols (violet with FeCl₃); aldehydes and ketones (2,4-DNP orange; Tollens' silver mirror and Fehling's red for aldehydes); carboxylic acids (NaHCO₃ fizz); primary amines (carbylamine smell; azo dye test for aryl amines).

**Preparations you should know:** Mohr's salt and potash alum (crystallise from mixed sulphate solutions, with a little H₂SO₄ to stop hydrolysis); acetanilide (aniline + ethanoic anhydride); p-nitroacetanilide (nitrate acetanilide in cold conc. H₂SO₄); aniline yellow (benzenediazonium chloride coupled with aniline); iodoform (ethanol or acetone with I₂ and NaOH).

**Chromatography:** components move up a paper or thin-layer plate at different rates. **Rf** = distance of spot / distance of solvent front. Less polar compounds usually move further on silica or paper.

<div class="myth">

**"In the Lassaigne halogen test, add AgNO₃ straight to the sodium extract."** Cyanide and sulphide in the extract would also give precipitates with silver, faking a halogen result. Boiling with dilute HNO₃ first drives them off as HCN and H₂S.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Primary standards: oxalic acid dihydrate (126), Mohr's salt (392), Na₂CO₃, K₂Cr₂O₇; NaOH and KMnO₄ must be standardised.
- N₁V₁ = N₂V₂; n-factor: KMnO₄ 5 in acid, K₂Cr₂O₇ 6, oxalate 2, Fe²⁺ 1.
- Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein. Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange.
- KMnO₄ is its own indicator; titrate in dilute H₂SO₄, never HCl.
- Anions: dilute H₂SO₄ group, conc. H₂SO₄ group (chromyl chloride for Cl⁻, brown ring for NO₃⁻), BaCl₂ for sulphate.
- Cations: 0 NH₄⁺; I HCl; II H₂S/HCl; III NH₄OH/NH₄Cl; IV H₂S/NH₄OH; V (NH₄)₂CO₃; VI Na₂HPO₄.
- Lassaigne: Prussian blue (N), violet with nitroprusside (S), blood red (N + S); boil with HNO₃ before testing halogens.

</div>
