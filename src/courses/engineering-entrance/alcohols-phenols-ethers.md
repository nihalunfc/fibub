**Chemistry · Class 12 · about 38 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name and make alcohols, phenols and ethers, explain their boiling points and acidity, predict products of dehydration, oxidation and substitution, and use the tests that tell them apart.

<div class="bigidea">All three families have an oxygen with two lone pairs. In alcohols and phenols that oxygen also holds a hydrogen, so they form hydrogen bonds and can give up H⁺. What sits on the other side decides the rest: an alkyl group (alcohol), a benzene ring that shares the oxygen's lone pair (phenol), or another carbon group (ether, no O-H at all).</div>

## 1. Classes, names and physical properties

Alcohols are 1°, 2° or 3° by the carbon holding OH (ethanol 1°, propan-2-ol 2°, 2-methylpropan-2-ol 3°). Phenols have OH on a benzene ring (phenol, cresols, catechol, resorcinol, quinol). Ethers are R-O-R′ (methoxymethane, ethoxyethane, methoxybenzene = anisole). The C-O-C angle in dimethyl ether is about 111°, a bit more than tetrahedral, because the two bulky groups repel.

**Boiling points.** Alcohols boil far higher than ethers or alkanes of similar mass (ethanol 78 °C, dimethyl ether −24 °C) because of hydrogen bonding. Among isomeric alcohols, more branching lowers the boiling point (less surface contact). Small alcohols mix with water through hydrogen bonds; solubility falls as the carbon chain grows.

## 2. Making alcohols and phenols

- **Hydration of alkenes:** dilute H₂SO₄, Markovnikov (propene → propan-2-ol). **Hydroboration-oxidation** (B₂H₆ then H₂O₂/OH⁻): anti-Markovnikov (propene → propan-1-ol).
- **Reduction:** aldehydes give 1°, ketones 2° alcohols (H₂/Pd, NaBH₄, LiAlH₄); acids and esters need LiAlH₄.
- **Grignard reagents:** with HCHO give 1° alcohols, other aldehydes 2°, ketones 3°.
- **Phenol** from chlorobenzene (NaOH, 623 K, 300 atm), from benzenesulphonic acid (fused NaOH), from diazonium salts (warm water), and from **cumene** (isopropylbenzene, oxidised with air, then dilute acid: phenol + acetone).
- **Ethanol** by fermentation of sugars (yeast enzymes invertase and zymase); **methanol** from CO + 2H₂ (ZnO-Cr₂O₃ catalyst). Methanol is highly toxic: it is oxidised in the body to methanal and methanoic acid, causing blindness.

## 3. Acidity

Alcohols are weaker acids than water; they react with sodium (2R-OH + 2Na → 2R-ONa + H₂) but not with NaOH. Electron-pushing alkyl groups make the alkoxide less stable, so acidity runs **1° > 2° > 3°**.

**Phenols are much more acidic** (phenol pKa 10) because the phenoxide ion spreads its negative charge into the ring by resonance. Phenol dissolves in NaOH but not in NaHCO₃ (it is weaker than carbonic acid); carboxylic acids dissolve in both. **Electron-withdrawing groups** (NO₂, Cl) increase acidity, most strongly at ortho and para; **electron-donating groups** (CH₃, OCH₃) decrease it.

## 4. Reactions of alcohols

**Breaking O-H:** with Na (H₂ given off), **esterification** with acids (conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst) or faster with acid chlorides and anhydrides.

**Breaking C-O:** with HX (reactivity 3° > 2° > 1°; the basis of the **Lucas test**), PCl₃, PCl₅, SOCl₂.

**Dehydration** with conc. H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄ at 443 K gives alkenes (ease 3° > 2° > 1°), via a carbocation. **Saytzeff** product is major, and the carbocation can **rearrange** to a more stable one. At 413 K with excess ethanol, ethoxyethane forms instead.

**Oxidation:** 1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ or KMnO₄); stop at the aldehyde with **PCC** or by distilling it off. 2° → ketone. 3° resist (only cleave under harsh conditions). Passing vapour over hot copper (573 K) dehydrogenates 1° to aldehydes and 2° to ketones, while 3° alcohols dehydrate to alkenes.

<div class="myth">

**"Dehydrating 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol gives 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene."** The secondary carbocation formed first shifts a methyl group to become tertiary, and the major product is **2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene**. Always check whether a hydride or methyl shift can give a more stable cation before writing the alkene.

</div>

## 5. Reactions of phenol

The OH donates electrons into the ring, so phenol undergoes electrophilic substitution far more easily than benzene, at **ortho and para**.

- **Bromine water** gives a white precipitate of **2,4,6-tribromophenol** at once; in CS₂ at low temperature, mainly 4-bromophenol.
- **Dilute HNO₃** gives o- and p-nitrophenol (separated by steam distillation: the ortho isomer is volatile thanks to **intramolecular** hydrogen bonding). Concentrated HNO₃ gives **picric acid** (2,4,6-trinitrophenol).
- **Kolbe reaction:** sodium phenoxide + CO₂ (4 to 7 atm, 400 K), then acid → **salicylic acid** (o-hydroxybenzoic acid), used for aspirin.
- **Reimer-Tiemann reaction:** phenol + CHCl₃ + NaOH → **salicylaldehyde** (o-hydroxybenzaldehyde), via dichlorocarbene.
- With **zinc dust**, phenol is reduced to benzene. With **neutral FeCl₃** it gives a violet colour (test for phenols).
- Oxidation with chromic acid gives **benzoquinone**.

## 6. Ethers

**Williamson synthesis:** R-O⁻Na⁺ + R′-X → R-O-R′ + NaX (an SN2 reaction). Use a **primary** halide; with a tertiary halide, the alkoxide acts as a base and gives an alkene. So tert-butyl methyl ether is made from sodium tert-butoxide and **methyl** bromide, not from sodium methoxide and tert-butyl bromide. For aryl ethers, the phenoxide must be the nucleophile (aryl halides do not do SN2).

**Cleavage by HI** (HI > HBr ≫ HCl): the oxygen is protonated, then I⁻ attacks. With primary or methyl groups, I⁻ attacks the **less hindered** carbon (SN2): CH₃-O-C₂H₅ gives CH₃I + C₂H₅OH. With a tertiary group, the tertiary carbocation forms (SN1) and takes the iodide. **Anisole** with HI gives **phenol + CH₃I**, never iodobenzene (the O-aryl bond has partial double-bond character).

Anisole's OCH₃ group activates the ring and directs ortho and para: bromination in ethanoic acid gives mainly 4-bromoanisole; Friedel-Crafts reactions give o- and p-substituted products.

<div class="myth">

**"Ethers are inert, so they are safe to store forever."** Ethers like ethoxyethane slowly form explosive **peroxides** in air and light. Old ether bottles are tested before distilling. Their low reactivity toward bases, metals and Grignard reagents is why they are used as solvents.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Hydrogen bonding: alcohols boil far above ethers of the same mass; branching lowers boiling points.
- Acidity: carboxylic acid > phenol > water > alcohol; o/p-NO₂ raises phenol acidity, CH₃ lowers it.
- Lucas test: 3° at once, 2° in about 5 minutes, 1° no reaction at room temperature.
- Dehydration via carbocations: Saytzeff product, watch for methyl and hydride shifts.
- Oxidation: 1° → aldehyde (PCC) → acid; 2° → ketone; 3° resists; hot Cu dehydrates 3°.
- Phenol: o/p substitution, tribromophenol with bromine water, Kolbe (salicylic acid), Reimer-Tiemann (salicylaldehyde), violet with FeCl₃.
- Williamson needs a primary halide; HI cleaves ethers at the less hindered carbon (SN2) or the tertiary one (SN1); anisole gives phenol + CH₃I.

</div>
