**Chemistry · Class 11 · about 34 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain where hydrogen fits, predict how each alkali and alkaline earth metal reacts, order their compounds by stability and solubility, and recall the key industrial compounds with their reactions.

<div class="bigidea">The s-block metals have one or two loosely held outer electrons, which they give away easily. That makes them strong reducing agents and gives them ionic compounds. Almost every trend in the block follows from one thing: the ion gets bigger down the group, and bigger ions hold water and small anions less tightly.</div>

## 1. Hydrogen

Hydrogen (1s¹) is unique. Like the alkali metals it can lose its electron to form H⁺; like the halogens it can gain one to form H⁻ and it exists as diatomic molecules. So it is placed on its own at the top of group 1. Its isotopes are **protium** ¹H (no neutron), **deuterium** ²H or D (one neutron, used as heavy water D₂O in nuclear reactors) and radioactive **tritium** ³H.

Hydrogen is made in the laboratory from zinc and dilute acid, and industrially by steam reforming of methane (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂) followed by the water-gas shift (CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂), or by electrolysis of water.

**Hydrides.** **Ionic** (saline) hydrides form with s-block metals (NaH, CaH₂) and react with water to give H₂. **Covalent** (molecular) hydrides form with p-block elements: electron-deficient (B₂H₆), electron-precise (CH₄) and electron-rich (NH₃, H₂O, HF, with lone pairs). **Metallic** (interstitial) hydrides of d- and f-block metals are often non-stoichiometric (such as LaH₂.₈₇) and can store hydrogen.

**Water and hardness.** Water has a bent shape, strong hydrogen bonding and is amphoteric. **Temporary hardness** comes from Ca and Mg hydrogencarbonates and is removed by boiling (or by Clark's method with lime). **Permanent hardness** comes from chlorides and sulfates and is removed by washing soda, ion exchange resins or calgon.

**Hydrogen peroxide** (H₂O₂) has a non-planar "open book" shape. It acts as an oxidising and a reducing agent and disproportionates (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂), so it is stored in dark plastic bottles with a little stabiliser. Its strength is often given as **volume strength**: "10 volume" means 1 L releases 10 L of O₂ at STP. Volume strength = 11.2 × molarity.

## 2. Group 1 and group 2: trends

| Property | Down the group | Group 1 vs group 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic and ionic radius | increases | group 2 smaller |
| Ionisation enthalpy | decreases | group 2 higher (IE₁); group 1 IE₂ is huge |
| Hydration enthalpy of the ion | less negative | group 2 far more negative (2+ charge) |
| Reducing power | increases (Li is special in water) | group 1 stronger |
| Melting point | decreases (group 1) | group 2 much higher |

## 3. Reactions of the metals

- **With water**: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂, more violent down group 1 (Li calm, K catches fire). Group 2: Be does not react, Mg only with hot water or steam, Ca to Ba with cold water.
- **With air**: Li forms Li₂O (and Li₃N, the only alkali metal that reacts with N₂), Na forms Na₂O₂, K, Rb and Cs form superoxides MO₂. Group 2 forms MO; Ba also forms BaO₂. Mg burns with a dazzling white light giving MgO and Mg₃N₂.
- **Storage**: alkali metals are kept under kerosene (Li is wrapped in paraffin wax because it floats).
- **In liquid ammonia**: dilute solutions are **blue**, conducting and paramagnetic (ammoniated electrons); concentrated solutions are bronze and diamagnetic as electrons pair up.
- **Flame colours**: Li crimson red, Na golden yellow, K lilac, Rb red-violet, Cs blue, Ca brick red, Sr crimson, Ba apple green. Heat excites the outer electron and it emits visible light as it falls back. Cs and K are used in **photoelectric cells** because their electrons leave so easily.

## 4. Lithium and beryllium: the odd ones out

Li⁺ and Be²⁺ are tiny and highly polarising, so their compounds are more covalent. Lithium: hardest and highest-melting alkali metal, forms a nitride, its carbonate and nitrate decompose on heating like those of Mg (Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂; 4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂), LiCl is deliquescent and soluble in ethanol. These are the features of its **diagonal relationship with magnesium**. Beryllium: its oxide and hydroxide are **amphoteric**, BeCl₂ is covalent and polymeric in the solid, it is not attacked by water and is made passive by nitric acid, all like **aluminium**. Be cannot exceed 4 bonds (no d orbitals in its valence shell).

## 5. Stability and solubility trends

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Carbonates and nitrates: stability rises down the group.</b> A small cation distorts the big CO₃²⁻ ion and pulls it apart into O²⁻ and CO₂. So BeCO₃ decomposes easily and BaCO₃ needs strong heating. In group 1, only Li₂CO₃ decomposes; Na₂CO₃ and K₂CO₃ are stable. Group 1 nitrates (except Li) give nitrites and O₂; LiNO₃ and group 2 nitrates give oxides, NO₂ and O₂.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Group 2 sulfates: solubility falls down the group.</b> BeSO₄ and MgSO₄ dissolve well (big hydration energy), CaSO₄ is slightly soluble and BaSO₄ is practically insoluble (used as the "barium meal" for X-rays).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Group 2 hydroxides: solubility and basic strength rise down the group.</b> Be(OH)₂ is amphoteric, Mg(OH)₂ weakly basic, Ba(OH)₂ a strong, soluble base. Here the lattice energy falls faster than the hydration energy.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Group 1 hydrogencarbonates exist as solids</b> (NaHCO₃, KHCO₃); group 2 ones are known only in solution (they cause temporary hardness).</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Since the metals get more reactive down the group, all their compounds get less stable."** Often the opposite. Carbonates, nitrates and peroxides become more stable down the group, because a large cation distorts a large anion less. Reactivity of the metal and stability of its salts are different questions.

</div>

## 6. Important compounds

- **Sodium carbonate (Solvay process)**: brine saturated with NH₃ is carbonated: NaCl + NH₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → NaHCO₃↓ + NH₄Cl. NaHCO₃ is less soluble and crystallises; heating gives Na₂CO₃. NH₃ is recovered with Ca(OH)₂ from limestone, leaving CaCl₂. The process cannot make K₂CO₃ because KHCO₃ is too soluble. Na₂CO₃·10H₂O is washing soda.
- **Sodium hydroxide**: by electrolysis of brine (chlor-alkali process), with H₂ at the cathode and Cl₂ at the anode.
- **Sodium hydrogencarbonate**: baking soda, antacid, fire extinguishers.
- **Calcium compounds**: quicklime CaO (from heating limestone), slaked lime Ca(OH)₂ (milk of lime, used to whitewash; lime water turns milky with CO₂), **plaster of Paris** CaSO₄·½H₂O made by heating gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O to about 393 K; with water it sets back into gypsum (casts for bones, statues). Above 393 K anhydrous "dead burnt plaster" forms, which does not set. In **cement**, a little gypsum is added to slow the setting.

## 7. Biological roles

Na⁺ (mostly outside cells) and K⁺ (mostly inside) carry nerve signals and are pumped across cell membranes by the sodium-potassium pump, which uses ATP. Mg²⁺ is at the centre of chlorophyll and is needed by enzymes that use ATP. Ca²⁺ builds bones and teeth (as calcium phosphate) and is needed for muscle contraction and blood clotting.

<div class="myth">

**"Lithium is the most reactive alkali metal in water because it has the most negative E°."** E° is about the energy change, not the speed. Lithium releases more energy per mole, but it reacts gently because it is hard, melts high (so it stays solid and does not spread out) and its reaction is slow. Potassium, with a less negative E°, reacts violently.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Hydrogen is placed alone: like group 1 (H⁺) and group 17 (H⁻, H₂). Volume strength of H₂O₂ = 11.2 × M.
- Down both groups: radius up, IE down, hydration enthalpy less negative.
- Air: Li → Li₂O (+Li₃N), Na → Na₂O₂, K/Rb/Cs → MO₂.
- Dilute Na in liquid NH₃: blue, conducting, paramagnetic.
- Diagonal pairs: Li-Mg, Be-Al; BeO and Be(OH)₂ amphoteric.
- Carbonate and nitrate stability rises down the groups; group 2 sulfate solubility falls, hydroxide solubility rises.
- Solvay: NaHCO₃ precipitates; no K₂CO₃ by this route. Plaster of Paris = CaSO₄·½H₂O.

</div>
