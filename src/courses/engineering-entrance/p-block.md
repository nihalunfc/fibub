**Chemistry · Class 11 and 12 · about 40 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain the trends in groups 13 to 18, predict shapes and oxidation states, write the key preparations and industrial processes, and rank oxoacids and hydrides by acidity, stability and reducing power.

<div class="bigidea">Two ideas run through the whole p-block. First, the top element of each group is small, highly electronegative and has no d orbitals, so it behaves differently (N, O and F form hydrogen bonds and multiple bonds; their heavier cousins form more bonds). Second, going down a group, the ns² pair gets lazier (the inert pair effect), so lower oxidation states become more stable.</div>

## 1. Trends that apply to every group

- **Oxidation states.** The group oxidation state is the number of valence electrons (group 13: +3, group 15: +5). Down the group, the state two lower becomes more stable: Tl⁺ over Tl³⁺, Pb²⁺ over Pb⁴⁺, Bi³⁺ over Bi⁵⁺. So PbO₂ and NaBiO₃ are strong oxidising agents.
- **Anomalous first member.** Small size, high electronegativity, no d orbitals. Nitrogen forms N≡N (a gas) while phosphorus forms P₄ (a solid); carbon forms long chains (catenation); fluorine shows only −1.
- **Hydrides.** Down a group, thermal stability falls, reducing power rises, bond angle falls toward 90°, and (for groups 16 and 17) acid strength rises. Boiling point rises down the group, except the first hydride, which is lifted by hydrogen bonding.

## 2. Group 13: boron and aluminium

Boron is a non-metal; the rest are metals. With only 6 electrons around it, boron in BF₃ is **electron deficient** and a **Lewis acid** (BF₃ < BCl₃ < BBr₃ in strength, because back bonding from F's small p orbital is strongest).

- **Borax** Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O gives a glassy bead on heating that turns colours with metal oxides (borax bead test). In water it is alkaline: B₄O₇²⁻ + 7H₂O → 2OH⁻ + 4H₃BO₃.
- **Boric acid** H₃BO₃ is a weak **monobasic Lewis acid**: it does not give H⁺ itself but takes OH⁻ from water, B(OH)₃ + H₂O → [B(OH)₄]⁻ + H⁺.
- **Diborane** B₂H₆ has two **3-centre-2-electron (banana) bonds**: two B-H-B bridges holding one electron pair each, plus four ordinary terminal B-H bonds.
- **Aluminium** is protected by a thin oxide layer; it is amphoteric and dissolves in both acid and NaOH (giving H₂). AlCl₃ exists as the dimer Al₂Cl₆ in the vapour.

## 3. Group 14: carbon and silicon

Carbon's allotropes: **diamond** (sp³, hard, insulator), **graphite** (sp², layers, conducts along the layers, a lubricant), **fullerenes** (C₆₀, sp²), **graphene**. **CO** is a toxic neutral oxide that binds haemoglobin far more strongly than O₂; **CO₂** is acidic. **SiO₂** is a giant network (quartz), not a gas, because Si does not form stable π bonds with O.

**SiCl₄ hydrolyses** easily (Si has empty d orbitals for water to attack), while CCl₄ does not. **Silicones** are (R₂SiO)ₙ chains made by hydrolysing R₂SiCl₂: water repellent and heat stable. **Silicates** are built from SiO₄⁴⁻ tetrahedra; **zeolites** are aluminosilicates used as shape-selective catalysts and water softeners.

## 4. Group 15: nitrogen and phosphorus

- **Ammonia** by the **Haber process**: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔH negative; iron catalyst with molybdenum promoter, about 200 atm and 700 K (a compromise between yield and speed).
- **Nitric acid** by the **Ostwald process**: 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O (Pt/Rh gauze), 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂, 3NO₂ + H₂O → 2HNO₃ + NO. Dilute HNO₃ with copper gives NO; concentrated gives NO₂. Gold and platinum dissolve only in **aqua regia** (3 HCl : 1 HNO₃).
- **Phosphorus**: white P₄ (reactive, glows in air, stored under water), red (polymeric, safer), black (most stable). **PH₃** forms from white P and hot NaOH. **PCl₅** is trigonal bipyramidal as a gas but [PCl₄]⁺[PCl₆]⁻ as a solid.

**Oxoacids of phosphorus.** Basicity = number of P-OH groups; P-H bonds make the acid a reducing agent.

| Acid | P oxidation state | Basicity | P-H bonds |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₃PO₂ (hypophosphorous) | +1 | 1 | 2 |
| H₃PO₃ (phosphorous) | +3 | 2 | 1 |
| H₃PO₄ (orthophosphoric) | +5 | 3 | 0 |
| H₄P₂O₇ (pyrophosphoric) | +5 | 4 | 0 |

## 5. Group 16: oxygen and sulphur

**Ozone** O₃ is bent, a powerful oxidising agent (O₃ → O₂ + [O]); it turns moist KI paper blue-black with starch by freeing iodine, used to measure it. Oxides are acidic (SO₂, CO₂), basic (Na₂O, CaO), amphoteric (Al₂O₃, ZnO) or neutral (CO, N₂O).

**Sulphuric acid** by the **contact process**: S → SO₂; 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (V₂O₅ catalyst, about 720 K, 2 bar); SO₃ is absorbed in concentrated H₂SO₄ to give **oleum** H₂S₂O₇, then diluted. Concentrated H₂SO₄ is a strong dehydrating agent (chars sugar) and an oxidising agent when hot. **SO₂** is a reducing agent: it decolourises acidified KMnO₄ and turns dichromate paper green.

## 6. Group 17: the halogens

All are oxidising agents; F₂ is the strongest (high hydration enthalpy of F⁻ and a weak F-F bond). Each halogen displaces the ones below it from their salts: Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂.

- **Chlorine** from MnO₂ + 4HCl, or KMnO₄ + HCl; industrially by electrolysing brine. With **cold dilute** NaOH: NaCl + NaOCl; with **hot concentrated** NaOH: 5NaCl + NaClO₃ (a **disproportionation**). With dry slaked lime: **bleaching powder** Ca(OCl)Cl.
- **Oxoacids of chlorine:** HOCl < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄ in acid strength (more O atoms spread the negative charge of the anion), but oxidising power runs the other way: HOCl is the best oxidiser.
- **Interhalogens** XX′ₙ (n = 1, 3, 5, 7), with the larger halogen in the centre: ClF₃ is T-shaped, BrF₅ square pyramidal, IF₇ pentagonal bipyramidal. They are more reactive than the halogens themselves (except F₂).

## 7. Group 18: noble gases

Full shells make them inert, but xenon (large, lowest ionisation energy) reacts with fluorine and oxygen. **Bartlett** made the first noble-gas compound in 1962 after noticing that O₂ and Xe have nearly the same ionisation energy.

| Compound | Xe lone pairs | Steric number | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| XeF₂ | 3 | 5 | linear |
| XeF₄ | 2 | 6 | square planar |
| XeF₆ | 1 | 7 | distorted octahedral |
| XeO₃ | 1 | 4 | trigonal pyramidal |
| XeOF₄ | 1 | 6 | square pyramidal |

XeF₆ hydrolyses completely to XeO₃ (explosive). Helium is used in diving gas and to cool superconducting magnets; argon in light bulbs and welding.

<div class="myth">

**"Fluorine has the most negative electron gain enthalpy, since it is the most electronegative."** Chlorine does (−349 vs −333 kJ/mol). Fluorine's 2p shell is so small that an incoming electron is repelled by the electrons already there. Electronegativity (pull on shared electrons in a bond) and electron gain enthalpy (energy released by a free atom) are different things.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"More oxygen atoms make an oxoacid a stronger oxidising agent."** For chlorine it is the reverse: HOCl oxidises faster and more strongly than HClO₄. Perchlorate is very stable because its charge is spread over four oxygens. Acid strength and oxidising power do not go together.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Inert pair effect: lower oxidation state more stable down the group (Tl⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺).
- First member is anomalous: H-bonding hydrides, multiple bonds, no d orbitals.
- Down groups 15 to 17: hydride stability falls, reducing power and acid strength rise, bond angle falls.
- Boric acid: monobasic Lewis acid. Diborane: two 3-centre-2-electron bonds.
- Haber (Fe, Mo), Ostwald (Pt/Rh), contact (V₂O₅): know the catalyst and conditions.
- Basicity of oxoacids = number of O-H; P-H bonds mean reducing power (H₃PO₂, H₃PO₃).
- Xenon shapes: count lone pairs = (8 − F atoms − 2 × O atoms)/2, then VSEPR.

</div>
