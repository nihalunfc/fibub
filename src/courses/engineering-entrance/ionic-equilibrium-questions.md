**Chemistry · Class 11 · about 27 minutes.** Read [Ionic equilibrium: acids, bases, pH and buffers](/learn/courses/engineering-entrance/ionic-equilibrium/) first. Here are the question types, worked examples and practice.

<div class="bigidea">Before any formula, decide what is actually in the beaker after everything has reacted: a strong acid, a weak acid, a salt, a buffer, or a saturated solution. Each one has its own short formula; using the wrong one is the commonest mistake.</div>

## The question types

<div class="steps" data-ms="8000">
<div class="step"><b>1. Theories and conjugate pairs.</b> Brønsted, Lewis, amphiprotic. <i>Trap:</i> the conjugate base of H₂PO₄⁻ is HPO₄²⁻, not PO₄³⁻.</div>
<div class="step"><b>2. pH of strong acids and bases.</b> [H⁺] = n × C. <i>Trap:</i> 10⁻⁸ M HCl is not pH 8; dilution by 10 raises pH by 1 only for strong acids.</div>
<div class="step"><b>3. Weak acids and bases.</b> √(K<sub>a</sub>C); Ostwald α = √(K<sub>a</sub>/C). <i>Trap:</i> check α &lt; 5% before using the shortcut.</div>
<div class="step"><b>4. Mixing.</b> Count millimoles; neutralise; see what is left. <i>Trap:</i> divide by the total volume; a weak acid half neutralised is a buffer.</div>
<div class="step"><b>5. Salt hydrolysis.</b> Identify the weak parent. <i>Trap:</i> CH₃COONH₄ pH does not depend on concentration.</div>
<div class="step"><b>6. Buffers.</b> Henderson; after adding acid, convert salt to acid by the moles added. <i>Trap:</i> use moles (the volume cancels), and update both terms.</div>
<div class="step"><b>7. Titration curves and indicators.</b> Equivalence pH from hydrolysis; indicator range in the jump. <i>Trap:</i> phenolphthalein for weak acid-strong base, not methyl orange.</div>
<div class="step"><b>8. Ksp and solubility.</b> Ksp = (xs)ˣ(ys)ʸ. <i>Trap:</i> compare s, not K<sub>sp</sub>, across salt types.</div>
<div class="step"><b>9. Common ion and precipitation.</b> Q<sub>sp</sub> after mixing &gt; K<sub>sp</sub>. <i>Trap:</i> mixing two solutions halves both concentrations.</div>
<div class="step"><b>10. Selective precipitation and pH (JEE Advanced).</b> The ion needing less precipitant comes out first; hydroxides start at a pH set by K<sub>sp</sub>. <i>Trap:</i> square or cube roots for 1:2 or 1:3 salts.</div>
</div>

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (level 3, buffer after acid).** A buffer has 0.1 mol CH₃COOH and 0.1 mol CH₃COONa in 1 L (pK<sub>a</sub> 4.74). Find the pH after adding 0.02 mol HCl. *Solution.* H⁺ turns acetate into acid: acid 0.12, salt 0.08. pH = 4.74 + log(0.08/0.12) = 4.74 − 0.176 = **4.56**. The same HCl in pure water would give pH 1.7.

**Example 2 (level 4, salt hydrolysis).** Find the pH of 0.1 M NH₄Cl (pK<sub>b</sub> of NH₃ = 4.74). *Solution.* Strong acid + weak base: pH = 7 − ½pK<sub>b</sub> − ½log C = 7 − 2.37 − ½(−1) = **5.13**.

**Example 3 (level 4, very dilute acid).** Find the pH of 10⁻⁷ M HCl. *Solution.* Let [H⁺] = x; from water, [OH⁻] = x − 10⁻⁷. x(x − 10⁻⁷) = 10⁻¹⁴ gives x = 10⁻⁷ × (1 + √5)/2 = 1.62 × 10⁻⁷. pH = **6.79**.

**Example 4 (level 5, selective precipitation).** A solution is 0.01 M in Cl⁻ and 0.01 M in CrO₄²⁻. Ag⁺ is added slowly. K<sub>sp</sub>: AgCl 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰, Ag₂CrO₄ 1.1 × 10⁻¹². Which precipitates first, and what is [Cl⁻] left when the second starts? *Solution.* AgCl needs [Ag⁺] = 1.8 × 10⁻⁸ M; Ag₂CrO₄ needs √(1.1 × 10⁻¹²/0.01) = 1.05 × 10⁻⁵ M. **AgCl first.** When Ag₂CrO₄ starts, [Cl⁻] = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰/1.05 × 10⁻⁵ = **1.7 × 10⁻⁵ M**, so over 99.8% of the chloride is already out. (This is how Mohr's method works.)

<div class="myth">

**"At the equivalence point the pH is always 7."** Only for a strong acid with a strong base. At the equivalence point of acetic acid with NaOH the flask holds sodium acetate, which is basic (pH near 8.7 for 0.05 M).

</div>

## Practise

<div class="qbank" data-bank="entrance-chemistry" data-topics="ionic-equilibrium" data-mode="practice"></div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Work out what is in the beaker after reaction, then pick the formula.
- Very dilute strong acid: solve x(x − C) = K<sub>w</sub>.
- Mixing: millimoles, then divide by the total volume.
- NH₄Cl pH = 7 − ½pK<sub>b</sub> − ½log C; CH₃COONa pH = 7 + ½pK<sub>a</sub> + ½log C.
- Buffer after adding acid: salt down, acid up by the same moles.
- Selective precipitation: the smallest required precipitant concentration goes first.

</div>
