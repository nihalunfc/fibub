**Chemistry · Class 11 · about 32 minutes.** By the end you will be able to place any element from its configuration, predict the order of radius, ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy and electronegativity for any set of elements, explain every common exception and work out an effective nuclear charge.

<div class="bigidea">Every periodic trend comes from a tug of war: the nucleus pulls the outer electrons in, and the inner electrons shield them. Across a period the pull grows while the shielding barely changes. Down a group a new shell is added each time, so the outer electrons sit further away.</div>

## 1. From triads to the modern periodic law

Chemists looked for order long before anyone knew about electrons. **Döbereiner** (1829) noticed **triads** such as Li, Na, K, where the middle atomic mass is roughly the average of the other two. **Newlands** (1865) saw that every eighth element (by mass) resembled the first, his **law of octaves**, which worked only up to calcium. **Mendeleev** (1869) arranged about 60 elements by atomic mass and similar properties, left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties. His "eka-aluminium" turned out to be gallium, and "eka-silicon" germanium.

Mendeleev had to swap a few pairs (such as Te and I) to keep families together. **Moseley** (1913) showed with X-ray spectra that the square root of the X-ray frequency rises in a straight line with the **atomic number** Z, not the mass. So the **modern periodic law** says: the properties of elements are a periodic function of their **atomic numbers**.

## 2. Periods, groups and blocks

The long form table has **7 periods** (rows) and **18 groups** (columns). The **period** is the highest principal quantum number n in the configuration. The number of elements in a period equals twice the number of orbitals being filled: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32.

| Block | Last electron enters | Groups | Group number from the configuration |
|---|---|---|---|
| s | ns | 1, 2 | number of s electrons |
| p | np | 13 to 18 | 10 + s electrons + p electrons |
| d | (n−1)d | 3 to 12 | d electrons + s electrons |
| f | (n−2)f | lanthanoids, actinoids (group 3) | placed below the main table |

**Naming new elements.** Until IUPAC gives a permanent name, an element above Z = 100 gets a temporary name from its digits: 0 nil, 1 un, 2 bi, 3 tri, 4 quad, 5 pent, 6 hex, 7 sept, 8 oct, 9 enn, ending in -ium. Z = 120 is unbinilium, symbol Ubn.

## 3. Shielding and effective nuclear charge

An outer electron does not feel the full nuclear charge Z, because the electrons between it and the nucleus cancel part of it. What it feels is the **effective nuclear charge**, Z<sub>eff</sub> = Z − S, where S is the **shielding constant**. **Slater's rules** (for an s or p electron) estimate S: each other electron in the same shell adds 0.35, each electron in the shell just inside adds 0.85, and each electron deeper adds 1.00.

## 4. Atomic and ionic radius

**Across a period** the radius falls (Z<sub>eff</sub> rises, same shell). **Down a group** it rises (a new shell each time). A **cation** is smaller than its atom (fewer electrons, sometimes a whole shell gone); an **anion** is larger (more electron repulsion, same nuclear charge).

**Isoelectronic species** have the same number of electrons. Among them, the one with more protons is smaller: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺ (all have 10 electrons, Z = 8 to 13).

**Lanthanoid contraction.** The 4f electrons shield very poorly, so across the lanthanoids the size falls steadily. As a result, elements of the 5th and 6th periods in the same d-block group are almost the same size (Zr 160 pm and Hf 159 pm) and are hard to separate.

## 5. Ionisation enthalpy

**Ionisation enthalpy** (IE) is the energy needed to remove the most loosely held electron from one mole of isolated gaseous atoms: M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻. It is always positive. Size, nuclear charge, shielding and the stability of the subshell all matter. IE rises across a period and falls down a group, with two dips per period: **Be > B** and **N > O** (the reasons are in the figure note).

**Successive ionisation enthalpies** always increase (IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃), because each electron leaves a more positive ion. A **huge jump** shows that a noble gas core has been broken into. Magnesium: 738, 1451, then 7733 kJ/mol, so Mg has 2 valence electrons and sits in group 2.

## 6. Electron gain enthalpy

**Electron gain enthalpy** (EGE) is the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms each gain an electron: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). It is **negative** when energy is released. Halogens have the most negative values; noble gases, Be, Mg and N have positive or near-zero values because the electron must enter a new subshell or spoil a half-filled one. Adding a **second** electron (O⁻ → O²⁻) is always endothermic, since the electron is pushed onto a negative ion.

<div class="myth">

**"Fluorine has the most negative electron gain enthalpy because it is the most electronegative."** Electronegativity is about shared electrons in a bond; electron gain is about a lone gaseous atom. Fluorine's 2p shell is so compact that the newcomer is strongly repelled, so chlorine releases more energy. The same thing happens in group 16: S is more negative than O.

</div>

## 7. Electronegativity, metallic character and oxides

**Electronegativity** (EN) is the pull of an atom on the shared electrons of a bond. It is not measured directly. **Pauling's scale** comes from bond energies (F = 4.0, the highest). **Mulliken's** is the average of IE and electron affinity (in eV); dividing it by about 2.8 gives a number close to Pauling's. EN rises across a period and falls down a group.

<div class="problem" data-problem="pt-mulliken"></div>

**Metallic character** (the ease of losing electrons) falls across a period and rises down a group. So oxides move from **basic** (Na₂O, MgO) through **amphoteric** (Al₂O₃) to **acidic** (SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₃, Cl₂O₇) across period 3. A few oxides are **neutral**: CO, NO, N₂O. For one element, a higher oxidation state gives a more acidic oxide (Mn₂O₇ is acidic, MnO basic).

## 8. Valence, the inert pair and diagonal pairs

The **valence** of s- and p-block elements is usually the number of valence electrons or 8 minus it. Down groups 13 to 15 the lower oxidation state becomes more stable (Tl⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺): the ns² pair is reluctant to take part, the **inert pair effect**.

The **first element of each group** (Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F) is unusual: it is small, highly electronegative and has no d orbitals, so it cannot exceed four bonds. It often resembles the element diagonally below it in the next group: **Li and Mg, Be and Al, B and Si** (the **diagonal relationship**), because their charge-to-size ratios are similar.

<div class="myth">

**"Down a group, ionisation enthalpy always falls."** Usually, but group 13 breaks the rule: Ga (579 kJ/mol) is about equal to Al (578), and Tl is above In. The filled 3d and 4f subshells shield poorly, so the outer electrons feel a larger pull than expected.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Modern periodic law: properties repeat with atomic number (Moseley), not atomic mass.
- Period = highest n; group = s electrons (s-block), 10 + s + p (p-block), d + s (d-block).
- Z<sub>eff</sub> = Z − S; Slater: 0.35 same shell, 0.85 shell inside, 1.00 deeper.
- Radius falls across, rises down; isoelectronic: more protons, smaller ion.
- IE rises across with dips at Be > B and N > O; a big jump in successive IE marks the group.
- Most negative electron gain enthalpy: Cl (not F). Highest EN: F.
- Oxides: basic on the left, amphoteric Al₂O₃, acidic on the right; CO, NO, N₂O neutral.
- Diagonal pairs Li-Mg, Be-Al, B-Si; inert pair effect makes lower states stable down groups 13 to 15.

</div>
