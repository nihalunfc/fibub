**Module 1 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how we found that the Sun is far from the centre of the Milky Way, what the 1920 "Great Debate" was about, and how one kind of star settled it.

<div class="bigidea">A hundred years ago, many astronomers thought the Milky Way was the whole universe. Within five years they learned it is one galaxy among countless others.</div>

## 1. Where are we in the Milky Way?

On a dark night the Milky Way is a pale band across the sky: the disc of our own galaxy seen from inside. (Its shape and parts are covered in the Astronomy course: [the Milky Way](/learn/courses/astronomy/milky-way/).) Finding where we are in it turned out to be surprisingly hard. Step through it:

<div class="steps" data-fig="milky-way-maps">
<div class="step"><p><b>Herschel, 1785.</b> William and Caroline Herschel counted stars in hundreds of directions. They found about the same number all round the band, so they drew a flat disc with the Sun near the middle.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>The trap: dust.</b> The disc is full of dust that blocks starlight. From inside, we only see stars a few thousand light-years away in any direction along the band, so every direction looks alike and we seem to sit at the centre of a bubble.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Shapley, 1918.</b> Harlow Shapley mapped globular clusters, huge balls of old stars that sit above and below the dusty disc. They are bunched towards the constellation Sagittarius, and form a ball around a centre far from us. The galaxy's centre is where they are centred.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Today.</b> The Milky Way is a barred spiral about 100,000 light-years across. The Sun lies about 26,000 light-years from the centre (Shapley's first estimate was too big, because dust made his clusters look farther away).</p></div>
</div>

## 2. The Great Debate, 26 April 1920

Shapley had made the Milky Way enormous. That raised a second question. Telescopes showed thousands of faint, fuzzy **spiral nebulae**, like the one in Andromeda. Were they small clouds inside our huge galaxy, or whole galaxies far outside it, "island universes" as the philosopher Kant had guessed in 1755?

At the National Academy of Sciences in Washington, Shapley and Heber Curtis argued it out:

| | Shapley | Curtis |
|---|---|---|
| Size of the Milky Way | huge, about 300,000 light-years | small, about 30,000 light-years |
| Sun's position | far from the centre | near the centre |
| Spiral nebulae | clouds inside the Milky Way | separate galaxies far away |

Each was partly right. **Shapley was right about our place** in the Milky Way. **Curtis was right about the spiral nebulae.** Both had evidence, and both had mistakes. Nobody could settle it, because nobody could measure the distance to a nebula.

<div class="meaning">

**Why distance is everything.** A small cloud nearby and a giant galaxy far away can look exactly the same on a photograph. Without a distance you cannot tell size from closeness. Almost every big leap in cosmology has come from a new way to measure distance, which is why Module 2 is all about distances.

</div>

## 3. Hubble settles it

In 1923, using the new 2.5-metre telescope on Mount Wilson, Edwin Hubble found a **Cepheid** star in the Andromeda nebula. Cepheids pulse with a period that reveals their true brightness (Henrietta Leavitt's discovery; see [variable stars](/learn/courses/astronomy/variable-stars/) in the Astronomy course). Comparing that true brightness with how faint it looked, Hubble put Andromeda about 900,000 light-years away, far beyond even Shapley's giant Milky Way. Today's value is about 2.5 million light-years. Lesson 2.3 walks through the measurement.

The universe suddenly became millions of times bigger. The Milky Way was not the universe: it was one galaxy among, as we now estimate, hundreds of billions to around two trillion.

<div class="myth">

**"Hubble discovered that galaxies exist."** Others, from Kant to Curtis, had argued for "island universes". Hubble's contribution was the measurement that proved it. Science often works like this: an idea floats for a long time until someone finds the measurement that settles it.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- From inside the dusty disc, every direction looks alike, which fooled Herschel into putting the Sun near the centre.
- Shapley used globular clusters to show the centre lies far away, towards Sagittarius; today we put the Sun about 26,000 light-years out.
- The 1920 Great Debate asked whether spiral nebulae are inside the Milky Way or separate galaxies.
- Hubble's Cepheids in Andromeda (1923-25) proved it is a separate galaxy, millions of light-years away.

</div>
