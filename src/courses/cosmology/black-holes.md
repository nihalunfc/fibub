How stars collapse into black holes is told in the [Astronomy course](/learn/courses/astronomy/stellar-remnants/); here we look at what general relativity says a black hole is, and why cosmologists care. By the end you will be able to work out the size of any black hole's event horizon.

<div class="bigidea">Squeeze enough mass into a small enough space and spacetime curves so steeply that nothing, not even light, can climb back out.</div>

## 1. A dark star, then a hole in spacetime

In 1783 the English clergyman John Michell reasoned with Newton's gravity that a star big and dense enough would have an escape speed above the speed of light, so it would be dark. In 1916, months after Einstein published general relativity, Karl Schwarzschild, serving in the German army, found the first exact solution of Einstein's equation: the spacetime around a single round mass. It contained a strange boundary that later became known as the **event horizon**.

## 2. The event horizon

The horizon of a non-spinning black hole is a sphere of radius:

```math
r_{s} = \frac{2 G M}{c^{2}} \approx 2.95\ \text{km} \times \frac{M}{M_{\odot}}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>r<sub>s</sub></dt><dd>the Schwarzschild radius: the size of the event horizon</dd>
<dt>M</dt><dd>the black hole's mass; M<sub>⊙</sub> is one Sun's mass</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Size grows in step with mass: double the mass, double the radius. Squash the Sun to 3 km or the Earth to 9 mm and they would become black holes.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

The horizon is not a surface you could stand on. It is a one-way boundary. Outside it, a rocket can escape. Inside, space and time are so bent that every possible future path leads further in, as surely as every path today leads to tomorrow.

## 3. Seeing the unseeable

A black hole gives out no light of its own, but it shows itself in three ways:

- **Orbits.** For over 25 years, teams led by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez tracked stars circling the centre of the Milky Way. The star S2 goes round every 16 years, coming within about 120 times the Earth-Sun distance of an invisible object of about **4.3 million Suns**: Sagittarius A*. They shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics with Roger Penrose, who showed that black holes follow from general relativity.
- **Hot gas.** Gas spiralling in heats up and glows brightly in X-rays.
- **Shadows.** In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope, radio dishes across the Earth linked into one Earth-sized telescope, published the first image of a black hole's shadow: **M87***, 6.5 billion Suns, 55 million light-years away. In 2022 it imaged Sagittarius A*.

<div class="history">

**Why the shadow is bigger than the horizon.** Light passing close to a black hole is bent so strongly it can loop round. The dark shadow in the images is about 2.6 times wider than the event horizon, ringed by light from hot gas bent around the hole.

</div>

## 4. Why cosmologists care

Almost every big galaxy has a black hole of millions to billions of Suns at its centre. When gas falls in, it can shine brighter than all the galaxy's stars together: a **quasar**, visible across billions of light-years. Quasars light up the distant universe for us, and their light can be lensed (next lesson).

How did such giants grow so early? The James Webb Space Telescope has found black holes of millions of Suns less than a billion years after the Big Bang (Lesson 12.4). It is one of today's open questions.

<div class="myth">

**"Black holes suck everything in."** Far away, a black hole pulls just like any other object of the same mass. If the Sun became a black hole of the same mass, the Earth would keep orbiting exactly as now. Only very close in does the extreme curving matter.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- A black hole is a region where spacetime is so curved that nothing can escape from inside its event horizon.
- Horizon radius r = 2GM/c², about 2.95 km for each Sun of mass.
- We know them from orbiting stars (4.3 million Suns at our galaxy's centre), glowing gas and the Event Horizon Telescope's images of M87* (2019) and Sagittarius A* (2022).
- Supermassive black holes power quasars, and their early growth is an open question for cosmology.

</div>
