<div class="bigidea">Mass is energy packed very tightly. A raisin's worth of mass, fully released, would match a large power station running for about half a day.</div>

## 1. One more surprise from 1905

A few months after his paper on time and space, Einstein added a short note. Following his own rules, he found that a body which gives out energy E as light must lose mass E/c². Turned round: **every mass has an energy locked inside it.**

```math
E = m c^{2}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>E</dt><dd>energy, in joules</dd>
<dt>m</dt><dd>mass, in kilograms</dd>
<dt>c²</dt><dd>the speed of light squared, about 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s²: the "exchange rate" between mass and energy</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Because c² is so enormous, a tiny mass is a huge energy. And it works both ways: enough energy in one place can become new mass, as new particles.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

## 2. How the Sun shines

The Sun gives out about 3.8 × 10²⁶ joules every second. Divide by c² and you find it loses about **4 million tonnes of mass every second** as sunlight. It can afford it: at that rate it would take about 15 trillion years to lose its whole mass, and it will stop fusing long before.

The mass goes in **nuclear fusion**. In the Sun's core, four hydrogen nuclei join step by step into one helium nucleus. The helium weighs about 0.7% less than the four hydrogens. That missing mass leaves as energy. (How stars shine is covered in the [Astronomy course](/learn/courses/astronomy/how-stars-shine/); here we only need the bookkeeping.)

<div class="myth">

**"E = mc² is about nuclear bombs."** It is true for everything. A hot cup of tea weighs a tiny bit more than the same tea cold, and a stretched spring a little more than a slack one. The difference is far too small to weigh in everyday life, but in 2005 a direct test using atoms and gamma rays confirmed E = mc² to better than one part in a million.

</div>

## 3. Why cosmologists care

Run the expanding universe backwards (Module 6) and it gets hotter and hotter. When the light was energetic enough, a pair of photons could turn into a **particle and its antiparticle**, and such pairs could turn back into light. For a while the universe was a soup in which energy and matter changed into each other all the time.

As the universe cooled, the light could no longer make pairs. Nearly all particles met their antiparticles and turned back into light, leaving a tiny excess of matter: about one extra particle in every billion. That small leftover is every star, planet and person. E = mc² is the rule behind it.

<div class="history">

**Most of your mass is energy.** Protons and neutrons are made of quarks, but the quarks' own masses add up to only about 1% of a proton's mass. The rest is the energy of the quarks moving and the force fields holding them, appearing as mass through E = mc².

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- E = mc²: mass and energy are two forms of one thing, exchanged at the rate c² ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ J per kg.
- The Sun turns about 4 million tonnes of mass into light every second, by fusing hydrogen into helium (0.7% of the mass is released).
- It works both ways: enough energy can become new particles.
- In the hot early universe, light and matter changed into each other; the matter we are made of is the small leftover.

</div>
