**Module 5 · about 15 minutes.** Read first: [5.8 Distances in an expanding universe](/learn/courses/cosmology/cosmological-distances/). Light has a finite speed and the universe has a finite age, so there must be a limit to what we can see. The surprise is how far away that limit is, and that a second limit, set by dark energy, cuts us off from much of the universe forever. By the end you will be able to explain the size of the observable universe.

<div class="bigidea">We see light from regions that are now 46 billion light-years away, more than three times 13.8, because space kept stretching behind the light. And because the expansion is speeding up, most of the universe is already out of reach forever.</div>

## 1. Three distances on one map

<div class="steps" data-fig="horizons">
<div class="step">A map that stretches with the universe: across is distance from us today, up is time. The curve is our past light cone: the path of every bit of light reaching us now. Light from a nearby galaxy left recently; from far away, long ago.</div>
<div class="step">Follow the light cone back to the start. The farthest place it reaches, about 46 billion light-years from us today, is the particle horizon: the edge of the observable universe. Nothing beyond it has had time to send us light.</div>
<div class="step">The Hubble distance, c / H₀ ≈ 14 billion light-years, is where galaxies recede at the speed of light today. It is not a horizon: we see plenty of galaxies beyond it, whose light set out long ago.</div>
<div class="step">The event horizon, about 16.6 billion light-years: light we send today will never reach galaxies beyond it, and theirs will never reach us. Dark energy's speeding up causes it.</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Particle horizon (46 billion ly)</dt><dd>the radius of the observable universe: how far, today, is the farthest place whose light could have reached us since the start</dd>
<dt>Hubble distance (c/H<sub>0</sub>, 14 billion ly)</dt><dd>where Hubble's law gives the speed of light. A useful scale, not a boundary of sight</dd>
<dt>Event horizon (16.6 billion ly)</dt><dd>the farthest place a signal sent today can ever reach. A consequence of accelerating expansion; a slowing universe has none</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>What we can see grows with time; what we can ever reach shrinks. The universe beyond the observable part is not empty: we just cannot see it yet, or ever.</dd>
</dl>

## 2. Why 46 and not 13.8

Picture an ant walking at a steady pace along a rubber band that is being stretched. The ant gets there in the end, if the stretching is not too fast, but the band behind it has grown. Measure the distance from where the ant started to where it ended up, at the end, and it is far longer than "pace × time". Light crossing the universe is the ant; the stretching band is space. The light that left the region now 46 billion light-years away has been travelling for almost 13.8 billion years.

## 3. A shrinking future

Because the expansion is accelerating, galaxies beyond the event horizon are slowly fading from our future. We keep seeing their old light, ever redder and dimmer, frozen in time, but nothing they do from now on will reach us. In a hundred billion years or more, astronomers in the merged Milky Way and Andromeda would see no other galaxies at all, and might never learn that the universe is expanding.

<div class="history">

**Naming the horizons.** The physicist Wolfgang Rindler gave the particle horizon and the event horizon their precise meanings in 1956. In 2007 Lawrence Krauss and Robert Scherrer worked out the lonely far future: a static-looking universe, with the evidence for the Big Bang gone over the horizon.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Nothing can recede faster than light."** Relativity forbids passing something nearby faster than light. Galaxies far away do recede faster than light today, because the space between is growing, and we see many of them. Davis and Lineweaver's paper on this is listed below.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- The observable universe is a sphere about 46 billion light-years in radius (93 billion across), though it is 13.8 billion years old, because space stretched while light travelled.
- The Hubble distance c/H<sub>0</sub> ≈ 14 billion light-years is where recession reaches light speed; we see beyond it.
- Accelerating expansion creates an event horizon, about 16.6 billion light-years: beyond it, galaxies are cut off from us forever.
- In the far future, only our own merged galaxy group will be visible.

</div>
