**Module 2 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain what a Type Ia supernova is, why it makes a good standard candle, how astronomers correct ("standardise") its brightness, and how far it can reach.

<div class="bigidea">A Type Ia supernova shines as brightly as a whole galaxy for a few weeks. That makes it a lighthouse visible across billions of light-years.</div>

## 1. A star that explodes at a fixed weight

A **white dwarf** is the hot, dense core left over when a Sun-like star dies: about the mass of the Sun packed into a ball the size of the Earth. (How stars end up as white dwarfs is covered in the [Astronomy course](/learn/courses/astronomy/stellar-remnants/).)

On its own, a white dwarf just cools for ever. But if it has a close companion star, or merges with another white dwarf, it can gain mass. As it approaches about **1.4 times the Sun's mass** (the Chandrasekhar limit), carbon in its core ignites all at once. In a few seconds the whole star is torn apart in a thermonuclear explosion.

Because these explosions happen in similar stars near a similar mass, they release similar amounts of energy. At peak, a Type Ia supernova reaches an absolute magnitude of about **−19.3**: roughly 5 billion times the Sun's power, enough to outshine its whole galaxy.

<div class="meaning">

**Why "Ia"?** Astronomers sort supernovae by their spectra. Type I have no hydrogen lines; Type Ia also show strong silicon lines. Type II (and Ib, Ic) are different beasts: massive stars whose cores collapse. Only Type Ia are good standard candles.

</div>

## 2. Nearly standard, then standardised

Type Ia supernovae are not perfectly identical. Their peaks vary by a factor of 2 or so. In 1993 Mark Phillips found the key: **brighter ones fade more slowly**. Measure how quickly a supernova fades in the first weeks, and you can work out how bright it really was.

After this correction (and another for colour, which deals with dust), the peaks agree to within about 0.1-0.15 magnitudes. That means each supernova gives a distance good to about **5-7%**, from a single explosion seen across the universe.

## 3. Reaching across the universe

Cepheids can be seen in galaxies up to about 100 million light-years away. Type Ia supernovae can be seen out to **billions** of light-years. In a large galaxy, one goes off only every century or so, but there are so many galaxies that modern surveys find thousands a year.

Two of the most important results in cosmology came from them:

- **The rate of expansion today** (the Hubble constant, Module 4 and Lesson 12.2), measured by linking nearby supernovae to Cepheids.
- **The accelerating universe** (1998, Module 9): distant supernovae looked about 25% fainter than expected, meaning the expansion is speeding up. That discovery won the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

<div class="myth">

**"A supernova is a star burning up its fuel very fast."** A Type Ia is a white dwarf that has no fuel burning at all, until gaining mass triggers carbon fusion everywhere at once. It is less a fire than a detonation. It leaves no remnant star behind.

</div>

<div class="history">

**A supernova in 1572**, seen by Tycho Brahe, appeared where no star had been and outshone Venus. It showed that the "unchanging heavens" of Aristotle could change. Modern study of its light echoes (light reflected off dust and arriving centuries late) confirmed it was a Type Ia.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- A Type Ia supernova is the thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf near 1.4 solar masses.
- At peak it reaches absolute magnitude about −19.3, bright enough to see across billions of light-years.
- Brighter ones fade more slowly (the Phillips relation), so their peaks can be corrected to give distances good to about 5-7%.
- They measured today's expansion rate and revealed in 1998 that the expansion is accelerating.

</div>
