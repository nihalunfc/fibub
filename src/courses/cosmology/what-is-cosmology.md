**Module 1 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will be able to say what cosmology studies, how it differs from the rest of astronomy, and why looking far away means looking back in time.

<div class="bigidea">Cosmology is the science of the universe as one whole thing: its size, its age, what it is made of, how it began and how it will end.</div>

## 1. The biggest questions there are

Every culture has asked the same questions. Has the universe always existed? Does it have an edge? What is it made of? What happens to it in the end?

For most of history these were questions for stories and philosophy. In the last hundred years they became questions you can **measure**. Today we know, with numbers and error bars, that:

- the universe is about <span class="count" data-to="13.8"></span> billion years old;
- it is **expanding**, and the expansion is **speeding up**;
- everything we can see (stars, planets, gas, you) is only about <span class="count" data-to="5"></span>% of what is out there;
- it began hot and dense, and the leftover heat still glows at <span class="count" data-to="2.7"></span> degrees above absolute zero.

This course shows you how each of those numbers was found, step by step, and where scientists still disagree.

## 2. Astronomy and cosmology: what is the difference?

**Astronomy** studies objects in the sky: the Sun, planets, stars, nebulae and galaxies. **Cosmology** zooms out until galaxies are just dots, and asks about the whole pattern: how the dots are spread out, how they move apart, and what the universe was like before there were any dots at all.

| | Astronomy | Cosmology |
|---|---|---|
| Studies | single objects and groups | the universe as a whole |
| Typical question | How does a star shine? | How old is the universe? |
| Biggest distances | inside and near our galaxy | billions of light-years |
| Key tools | telescopes, spectra, orbits | the same, plus Einstein's gravity and the physics of the early universe |

The two overlap, and cosmology stands on astronomy's shoulders. When this course needs a piece of astronomy (how light carries information, how planets orbit, how we measure the distance to nearby stars), it links to fibub's free [Astronomy course](/learn/courses/astronomy/) instead of repeating it.

## 3. All of history in one year

Big numbers are hard to feel. A good trick is to squeeze the whole 13.8 billion years into one calendar year. Step through it:

<div class="steps" data-fig="cosmic-calendar">
<div class="step"><p><b>1 January, midnight.</b> The universe begins hot and dense. On this calendar, one day stands for about 38 million years.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>January.</b> The first stars and galaxies switch on within the first two weeks. Our Milky Way's disc settles by spring.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>September.</b> Only around 1 September does a cloud of gas in the Milky Way collapse to make the Sun and Earth. Life appears a few weeks later.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>December.</b> Animals with shells and bones appear on 17 December. Dinosaurs rule from Christmas Day and die out on 30 December.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>31 December, the last day.</b> Our species appears at about 11:48 at night. Everything humans have ever done fits in the last few minutes.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>The last minute.</b> Farming begins about 30 seconds before midnight. The telescope, invented in 1609, appears in the last second of the year.</p></div>

## 4. How can anyone study the past of the whole universe?

There is one wonderful fact that makes cosmology possible: **light is not instant**. It travels at about 300,000 km every second, which is fast but finite.

So when you look at the Moon, you see it as it was 1.3 seconds ago. The Sun: 8 minutes ago. The Andromeda galaxy: 2.5 million years ago. The farthest galaxies seen by the James Webb Space Telescope: more than 13 billion years ago.

<div class="meaning">

**The universe is its own history book.** The farther away you look, the older the page you read. Cosmologists don't need to guess what young galaxies looked like: they photograph them. Lesson 2.1 explores this properly.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Cosmology is just speculation."** Some parts are (and this course labels them clearly). But the core of modern cosmology rests on measurements that many teams with different instruments have repeated and checked: the expansion of the universe, the leftover heat of the Big Bang, and the amounts of hydrogen and helium made in the first minutes. Few ideas in science are tested as hard.

</div>

## 5. The road ahead

This course follows the real story, roughly in the order people worked it out:

1. **Part A, Looking up:** the oldest ideas about the universe, and how we learned the distances to other galaxies.
2. **Part B:** Einstein's gravity, which turned space and time into something that can bend and stretch.
3. **Part C:** the discovery that the universe is expanding, and the equations that describe it.
4. **Part D:** the hot Big Bang, the first three minutes and the glow left over from them.
5. **Part E:** dark matter and dark energy, the unseen 95%.
6. **Parts F to H:** how galaxies grew, what happened in the first instant, and the open questions of today.

You need no physics to start. Every equation comes with a **physical meaning** box that says what it means in words, and the maths is always optional.

<div class="keypoints">

- Cosmology is the science of the universe as a whole: its size, age, contents, beginning and future.
- Its big claims rest on measurements: the universe is about 13.8 billion years old, expanding, and mostly made of things we cannot see.
- Light takes time to travel, so looking far away means looking into the past.
- On a one-year cosmic calendar, the Sun forms in September and all of human history fits in the last minute.

</div>
