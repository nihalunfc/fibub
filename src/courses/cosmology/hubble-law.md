**Module 4 · about 15 minutes.** Read first: [4.1 Slipher's discovery](/learn/courses/cosmology/redshift-galaxies/) for speeds and [2.6 The distance ladder](/learn/courses/cosmology/distance-ladder/) for distances. Put the two together and a law appears. By the end you will be able to use Hubble's law to turn a galaxy's speed into its distance.

<div class="bigidea">Plot galaxies' speeds against their distances and they fall on a straight line: twice as far, twice as fast. The slope, the Hubble constant, is the expansion rate of the universe.</div>

## 1. Speeds plus distances

By the late 1920s astronomers had two lists: Slipher's speeds and, after Hubble's [Cepheid work on Andromeda](/learn/courses/cosmology/hubble-andromeda/), rough distances to a few dozen galaxies. In 1927 the Belgian priest and physicist **Georges Lemaître** put them together, found that speed grows with distance, and explained it as the expansion of space in Einstein's theory (Lesson 4.6). His paper was in French in a little-read Belgian journal.

In 1929 **Edwin Hubble** published a plot of 24 galaxies, speed against distance. The points were scattered, but they rose along a line. With Milton Humason he extended it to much greater distances by 1931. The pattern became famous as Hubble's law. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union recommended calling it the **Hubble-Lemaître law**.

## 2. The law

```math
v = H_0 \, d
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>v</dt><dd>how fast a galaxy moves away from us, in km/s (from its redshift: v ≈ cz for nearby galaxies)</dd>
<dt>d</dt><dd>its distance, in megaparsecs (1 Mpc = 3.26 million light-years)</dd>
<dt>H<sub>0</sub></dt><dd>the Hubble constant: about 70 km/s for every Mpc of distance. The 0 means "today"</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Every megaparsec of space between us and a galaxy adds about 70 km/s to its speed. It is a rate of stretching: about 0.007% per million years.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<div class="steps" data-fig="hubble-diagram">
<div class="step">A plot like Hubble's 1929 one (made-up galaxies that behave like the real ones). Nearby galaxies, all within 2 Mpc by the distances of the day. A rough line, slope about 500 km/s per Mpc.</div>
<div class="step">The same galaxies with today's distances, about 7 times bigger. The slope drops to about 70. The scatter is the galaxies' own motions, a few hundred km/s, which matter a lot this close.</div>
<div class="step">Supernovae out to 400 Mpc. Here expansion speeds are tens of thousands of km/s and the own motions hardly matter: the straight line is unmistakable.</div>
<div class="step">Today two careful methods give 67 (from the cosmic microwave background plus the standard model) and 73 (from the distance ladder). Small on this plot, but far bigger than either method's error bars.</div>
</div>

**Worked example.** A galaxy's lines show a redshift z = 0.02, so v ≈ 0.02 × 299,792 ≈ 6,000 km/s. With H<sub>0</sub> = 70, its distance is d = 6,000 ÷ 70 ≈ **86 Mpc**, about 280 million light-years. Once H<sub>0</sub> is known, a single spectrum gives a distance. This is how 3-D maps of millions of galaxies are made.

## 3. Why the first value was seven times too big

Hubble's slope was about **500 km/s per Mpc**. The trouble was the distances. In 1952 Walter Baade showed there are two kinds of Cepheid star with different brightnesses, and Hubble's calibration had mixed them up. Allan Sandage then found that some "brightest stars" Hubble had used in far galaxies were really glowing gas clouds. Each fix pushed the distances out and the slope down.

<div class="history">

**A factor of two, for decades.** From the 1960s to the 1990s, one school (Sandage) argued for H<sub>0</sub> ≈ 50 and another (Gérard de Vaucouleurs) for about 100. The Hubble Space Telescope was built partly to settle it. Its Key Project, led by Wendy Freedman, reported **72 ± 8** in 2001.

</div>

## 4. The Hubble tension

Now the error bars are a few per cent, and they do not overlap. The distance ladder (Cepheids, then Type Ia supernovae) gives about **73**. The Planck satellite's map of the cosmic microwave background, read with the standard model of cosmology, gives about **67**. If both are right, something may be missing from the standard model, perhaps in the early universe. We return to it in Module 12.

<div class="myth">

**"Galaxies are flying away from us because we are in the middle."** The law looks exactly the same from every galaxy. That is the surprise of the next lesson: a universe that stretches evenly has no centre.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance: v = H<sub>0</sub> d (the Hubble-Lemaître law).
- Lemaître found it first (1927) and explained it as expanding space; Hubble's plot (1929) made it famous.
- H<sub>0</sub> ≈ 70 km/s per Mpc. Hubble's 500 was wrong because his distances were about 7 times too small.
- With H<sub>0</sub> known, a redshift gives a distance: d = v ÷ H<sub>0</sub>.
- Today's two best methods give 67 and 73: the Hubble tension, an open question.

</div>
