**Module 1 · about 13 minutes.** By the end you will be able to read and write numbers in powers of ten, picture the main steps of scale from a person to the observable universe, and convert between kilometres, light-years and light-travel times.

<div class="bigidea">The universe spans about 27 powers of ten in size, from you to its edge. Learning to hop between them is the cosmologist's most basic skill.</div>

## 1. Writing big numbers

Cosmology deals with numbers too big to write out. The trick is to count the zeros.

| Written out | Power of ten | Said as |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 10<sup>1</sup> | ten |
| 1,000 | 10<sup>3</sup> | a thousand |
| 1,000,000 | 10<sup>6</sup> | a million |
| 1,000,000,000 | 10<sup>9</sup> | a billion |
| 1 followed by 12 zeros | 10<sup>12</sup> | a trillion |

So 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s (the speed of light) means 3 followed by 8 zeros: 300,000,000 metres per second. Small numbers use negative powers: 10<sup>−3</sup> = 0.001.

<div class="meaning">

**Each step of ten is a real jump.** 10<sup>9</sup> is not "a bit more" than 10<sup>6</sup>: it is a thousand times more. Counting a million seconds takes about 12 days; counting a billion takes about 32 years.

</div>

## 2. Zoom out

<figure class="fig" data-fig="powers-of-ten"><figcaption>Drag the slider to zoom out. Each circle is an object drawn at its true size compared with the view. As you zoom, objects shrink to dots and the next level up appears. The line under the picture tells you how long light takes to cross the view.</figcaption></figure>

Some milestones on the way:

- **10<sup>7</sup> m**: the Earth, 12,742 km across.
- **10<sup>9</sup> m**: the Sun, 109 Earths wide. Light crosses it in under 5 seconds.
- **10<sup>11</sup> m**: Earth's orbit. Light from the Sun takes 8.3 minutes to reach us.
- **10<sup>16</sup> m**: about one light-year. The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is 4.2 light-years away.
- **10<sup>21</sup> m**: the Milky Way's disc, about 100,000 light-years across.
- **10<sup>23</sup> m**: the Local Group, our galaxy's neighbourhood with Andromeda.
- **10<sup>24.7</sup> m**: Laniakea, the supercluster of 100,000 galaxies we belong to, about 520 million light-years across.
- **10<sup>26.9</sup> m**: the observable universe, about 93 billion light-years across.

## 3. The light-year: a distance, not a time

A **light-year** is how far light travels in one year: about 9.46 × 10<sup>12</sup> km, nearly ten trillion kilometres. It is a distance, but it has a handy built-in meaning: something 1,000 light-years away is seen as it was 1,000 years ago. Astronomers also use the **parsec** (3.26 light-years); its origin in parallax is explained in the [Astronomy course](/learn/courses/astronomy/parallax/). Cosmologists most often use the **megaparsec** (Mpc), a million parsecs or 3.26 million light-years.

## 4. The universe is mostly empty

If the Sun were a grapefruit in your hand, the Earth would be a grain of sand 13 metres away, and the nearest star another grapefruit about 3,500 km away. Galaxies are packed much more closely compared with their size: Andromeda is only about 25 Milky Way widths away. That's why stars almost never collide, but galaxies often do.

<div class="myth">

**"The observable universe is 13.8 billion light-years in radius, because it is 13.8 billion years old."** Light has travelled for 13.8 billion years, but space has been expanding while it travelled. The places that sent that light are now about 46 billion light-years away. Lesson 5.8 shows how distances work in an expanding universe.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- A power of ten counts zeros: 10<sup>9</sup> is a billion, 10<sup>−3</sup> is a thousandth.
- From a person (10<sup>0</sup> m) to the observable universe (about 10<sup>27</sup> m) is about 27 powers of ten.
- A light-year (about 9.46 trillion km) is a distance, and also tells you how old the light is.
- Stars are very far apart compared with their size; galaxies are relatively close, so galaxies collide but stars rarely do.

</div>
