**Module 10 · about 15 minutes.** Read first: [10.3 Dark matter halos](/learn/courses/cosmology/dark-matter-halos/). Halos are the knots. Between them, on scales of tens to hundreds of megaparsecs, matter is arranged in a pattern that looks like a sponge, or a spider's web. By the end you will be able to name its parts, explain why gravity makes sheets and threads rather than balls, and place the Milky Way in it.

<div class="bigidea">Gravity does not gather matter into neat balls. It squeezes it first into sheets, then threads, then knots, leaving most of space as nearly empty voids: the cosmic web.</div>

## 1. Four kinds of place

Map where galaxies are, out to a few hundred megaparsecs, and four kinds of place appear:

- **Nodes:** galaxy clusters, up to about 10¹⁵ suns, where several threads meet.
- **Filaments:** threads of galaxies, gas and dark matter, tens of megaparsecs long and a few across, along which matter flows towards the nodes.
- **Walls (sheets):** thin, flattened layers of galaxies, the faces between neighbouring voids.
- **Voids:** nearly empty regions tens of megaparsecs across, filling most of space.

In one careful simulation study (Marius Cautun and colleagues, 2014), filaments hold about half of all the mass in only about 6% of the volume, while voids fill more than three-quarters of space with only about a seventh of the mass. Beyond a few hundred megaparsecs the pattern repeats without any bigger structure, and the universe becomes smooth on average, as the [cosmological principle](/learn/courses/cosmology/cosmological-principle/) assumes.

## 2. Why sheets and threads?

In 1970 Yakov Zel'dovich found a beautifully simple way to see it. In the early stages, every bit of matter keeps moving in a straight line in the direction it first started to fall, with a distance that grows as the lumps grow:

```math
\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{q} + D(t)\, \boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{q})
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>q</dt><dd>where a bit of matter started, on a smooth grid</dd>
<dt>ψ(q)</dt><dd>the direction and amount it is pushed, fixed once and for all by the starting ripples (it points downhill in gravity, towards denser places)</dd>
<dt>D(t)</dt><dd>the growth factor of Lesson 10.2: how much the ripples have grown (D ∝ a while matter rules)</dd>
<dt>x(t)</dt><dd>where that bit of matter is at time t</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Each region is squeezed at different rates along three directions. It collapses first along the fastest one, flattening into a "pancake": a wall. Later a second direction collapses (a filament), and in a few places all three (a node). Matter streams out of the regions squeezed along no direction at all: the voids.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

A random lump is never perfectly round, so a ball is the last thing it becomes. Zel'dovich called the first structures pancakes. Dick Bond, Lev Kofman and Dmitri Pogosyan showed in 1996 that the strongest filaments link the rare high peaks of the starting ripples, which become clusters; they named the whole pattern the **cosmic web**.

## 3. An almost frozen pattern

Galaxies move through the web at a few hundred km/s on top of the expansion. That sounds fast, but even over the whole age of the universe it only takes them a few megaparsecs, small next to the web itself. So the web we map today is, on the largest scales, still the pattern drawn by the first ripples: a fossil of the early universe, which is why maps of it can measure the starting conditions.

## 4. Where we live

The Milky Way and Andromeda form the Local Group, sitting in a thin wall at the edge of the **Local Void**, a region some tens of megaparsecs across with hardly any galaxies. Our wall is part of a filament system that flows towards the Virgo cluster and, on a larger scale, towards the Great Attractor region. In 2014 Brent Tully and colleagues mapped the motions of galaxies to outline our home supercluster, **Laniakea** ("immense heaven" in Hawaiian), about 160 Mpc across and holding some 10¹⁷ suns ([beyond our galaxy](/learn/courses/astronomy/beyond-our-galaxy/)).

Filaments are not only galaxies. Most of the universe's ordinary matter is thin gas at hundreds of thousands to millions of degrees, strung along the filaments between galaxies. It is very hard to see, and for years was the "missing baryons"; it has now been weighed, by the way it delays radio bursts from distant galaxies (2020) and by its faint X-ray glow.

<div class="myth">

**"Voids are empty holes in space."** Voids hold dark matter, thin gas and a few faint galaxies, at perhaps 10 to 20% of the average density in their middles. Space in a void is the same space as anywhere else, with the same dark energy. Voids are not holes but the thinnest parts of the sponge, and they expand slightly faster than average, which makes them useful tests of gravity.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- On scales of tens to hundreds of Mpc, matter forms a web: nodes (clusters), filaments, walls and voids.
- Voids fill most of the volume; filaments hold about half the mass in a few percent of the space.
- Zel'dovich: x = q + Dψ. Lumps collapse first along one direction (walls), then two (filaments), then three (nodes).
- Galaxies drift only a few Mpc in the age of the universe, so the web is a near-fossil of the first ripples.
- We live in a wall beside the Local Void, inside the Laniakea supercluster.

</div>
