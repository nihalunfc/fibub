This lesson is different from the others. Much of it is **speculation**: ideas that follow from theories but have no direct evidence. Each claim is labelled with how solid it is. By the end you will be able to explain what scientists mean by a multiverse, why some take it seriously, and why others think it is not science at all.

<div class="bigidea">If inflation, once started, never stops everywhere, it keeps making new regions where it ends, "bubble universes", for ever. If string theory is right, each bubble could have different laws. That might explain the strange smallness of dark energy, but it may also be impossible to test.</div>

## 1. Four meanings of "multiverse"

The word is used for very different ideas. From most solid to least:

1. **Space beyond our horizon** (very likely). The observable universe is a sphere 46 billion light-years in radius ([Lesson 5.9](/learn/courses/cosmology/cosmic-horizons/)). Space is flat to 0.2%, so it is probably much larger, perhaps infinite, with more galaxies under the same laws. Few dispute this, though some do not call it a multiverse.
2. **Bubble universes from eternal inflation** (speculative, follows from many inflation models). Regions where inflation ended at different places and times, beyond any horizon we could ever reach.
3. **Different laws in different bubbles** (speculative, needs the string landscape). Different particle masses, forces or dark energy in each.
4. **Quantum many-worlds** (a separate idea about quantum measurement, not cosmology). Not covered here.

## 2. Eternal inflation

In the slow roll of Lesson 11.2, the inflaton creeps downhill. But it also gets quantum kicks, about H/2π each Hubble time (Lesson 11.3), up or down at random. High on the hill the slope is so gentle that the kicks beat the roll. In the regions kicked uphill, inflation goes faster and lasts longer, and since each Hubble time multiplies an inflating region's volume by e³ ≈ 20, those regions soon outweigh everything else. Alexander Vilenkin (1983) and Andrei Linde (1986) showed that, in many models, **inflation ends in more and more places, but never everywhere**. Each place where it ends becomes a hot Big Bang, a bubble; ours would be one.

## 3. The landscape and the anthropic argument

String theory has extra dimensions curled up in many possible shapes, threaded by fields. Each choice gives a different "vacuum", a different kind of empty space with its own particle masses, forces and vacuum energy. Estimates made in 2003-04 gave around **10<sup>500</sup>** such vacua: the **string landscape**. Combined with eternal inflation, every vacuum could be realised in some bubble.

That links to the worst puzzle of [Lesson 9.3](/learn/courses/cosmology/vacuum-energy/): why is dark energy about 10<sup>120</sup> times smaller than the natural estimate? In 1987 Steven Weinberg argued that if it were much bigger, the expansion would have sped up before galaxies could form, so no one would be around to measure it. In a landscape where the value differs from bubble to bubble, most bubbles have huge vacuum energies and are empty; observers find themselves only in the rare ones with a small value. This is the **anthropic argument**. Weinberg used it to predict a small, non-zero value a decade before supernovae found one.

## 4. Can it be tested?

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Bubble collisions.</b> If our bubble formed near another, the collision could leave a round disc-shaped mark on the CMB. In 2011 Stephen Feeney and colleagues searched the WMAP maps: no convincing collision was found. Absence does not rule the idea out, since collisions may be rare or out of view.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The sign of curvature.</b> In the simplest bubble pictures, the inside of a bubble is slightly open (negatively curved). Matthew Kleban and Marjorie Schillo showed in 2012 that clearly measuring a closed universe (Ω slightly above 1) would rule out this kind of eternal inflation. So far curvature is consistent with zero.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Predictions from the landscape.</b> If we knew how likely each vacuum was, we could predict the typical properties of our bubble. But in an eternally inflating universe there are infinitely many of everything, and how to count them (the "measure problem") is unsolved. Different counting rules give different answers.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Indirect support.</b> If inflation and string theory gained strong independent evidence, their consequences, including a multiverse, would be taken seriously, as we accept that galaxies exist beyond our horizon because our theories of space say so.</div>
</div>

## 5. The debate

In 2014 George Ellis and Joe Silk wrote in Nature, "Scientific method: Defend the integrity of physics", warning that some physicists were proposing to relax the requirement that theories be testable, to accommodate the multiverse and string theory. Supporters reply that science has always accepted unseen consequences of well-tested theories, and that the multiverse is a prediction of theories, not an extra assumption. Paul Steinhardt, one of inflation's founders, has gone further: he argues that eternal inflation can produce any outcome and so predicts nothing, and favours bouncing alternatives. In 2017 more than 30 physicists, including Guth and Linde, replied that inflation has made and passed real tests. Meanwhile, some string theorists have questioned whether the landscape's vacua with positive vacuum energy exist at all.

What we can say firmly: our observable universe is very likely a small part of something larger. Whether that larger thing contains other bubbles with other laws is not known and may never be known for certain.

<div class="myth">

**"Scientists have discovered parallel universes."** No observation has detected another universe. What exists are theories with good support (inflation) or deep mathematical appeal (string theory), some versions of which imply a multiverse. When you read a headline about other universes, ask which of the four meanings it uses and what, if anything, was measured.

<div class="keypoints">

- "Multiverse" has several meanings: space beyond our horizon (very likely), bubbles from eternal inflation and different laws in each (speculative), quantum many-worlds (separate idea).
- In many inflation models quantum kicks keep some regions inflating; their volume grows faster than bubbles form, so inflation never ends everywhere (Vilenkin 1983, Linde 1986).
- The string landscape may hold around 10<sup>500</sup> vacua; with eternal inflation, observers would find themselves only where dark energy is small enough for galaxies (Weinberg 1987).
- Possible tests: bubble-collision marks in the CMB (none found), a closed curvature would rule out simple versions; the measure problem blocks firm predictions.
- Ellis and Silk (2014) and others argue it is not testable science; supporters call it a consequence of tested theories. The question is open.
