**Module 5 · about 14 minutes.** Read first: [5.3 Critical density and Omega](/learn/courses/cosmology/critical-density/) and [3.5 Curved spacetime](/learn/courses/cosmology/curved-spacetime/). The curvature term in the Friedmann equation is the shape of space itself. This lesson shows what that means and how it was measured. By the end you will be able to explain how a triangle could reveal curved space.

<div class="bigidea">Space can be curved as a whole, like the surface of a ball or a saddle. We tested it with the biggest triangle possible, across the whole observable universe, and found it flat.</div>

## 1. Geometry you can test from inside

An ant living on a ball cannot step outside to see the curve. But it can still find out: draw a big triangle and add up its angles. On a flat sheet they make 180°. On a ball they make more. On a saddle, less. The same tests work for three-dimensional space.

<div class="steps" data-fig="triangles">
<div class="step">Flat space: a triangle's angles add to exactly 180°, and two parallel lines stay the same distance apart forever. This is the geometry you learned at school (Euclid's).</div>
<div class="step">Closed space, like a ball's surface: the angles add to more than 180°, and lines that start parallel meet, like lines of longitude at the poles. A closed universe is finite, with no edge.</div>
<div class="step">Open space, like a saddle: the angles add to less than 180°, and parallel lines spread apart. An open universe goes on forever.</div>
<div class="step">The cosmic test. Spots in the cosmic microwave background have a size we can calculate. Curved space would act like a lens across the universe: closed space makes them look bigger, open space smaller. They look about 1° across: flat.</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>

## 2. Measuring the shape

The hot and cold spots in the cosmic microwave background are the imprint of sound waves in the young universe (Module 7). Physics tells us how far a sound wave could travel before the light was released: that is a ruler of known length. Comparing it with how big the spots look gives the geometry.

In 2000 the **BOOMERanG** balloon experiment, flown over Antarctica, mapped the spots and found them about 1° across: the size expected for flat space. The WMAP satellite and then the Planck satellite sharpened it. Planck, combined with maps of galaxies, finds **Ω<sub>k</sub> = 0.001 ± 0.002**: flat to within about 0.2%.

<div class="history">

**Gauss and the mountains.** A popular story says the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss, surveying Germany in the 1820s, measured the triangle between three mountain tops to test whether space was curved. Historians doubt he meant it as a test, but the idea is right. His triangle, about 100 km a side, was billions of billions of times too small to show anything.

</div>

## 3. Flat, but how big?

Flat means the geometry is Euclid's on all the scales we can test. It does not by itself say whether space is infinite. A flat space could, in principle, wrap round, like a video game screen where walking off one side brings you back on the other. If our universe wrapped round on a scale smaller than the observable universe, we would see repeated patterns in the microwave sky. Planck looked and found none. So the universe is at least far bigger than what we can see, and may be infinite.

<div class="myth">

**"A flat universe is flat like a pancake."** Flat here means the geometry of space has no curvature: triangles make 180° in every direction. The universe is fully three-dimensional; "flat" is about angles, not thickness.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Space can be closed (triangle angles over 180°), flat (exactly 180°) or open (under 180°).
- Curvature can be measured from inside by big enough triangles or by how the sizes of distant things look.
- Spots in the cosmic microwave background, a ruler of known size, look about 1° across: flat space.
- Planck plus galaxy maps: Ω<sub>k</sub> = 0.001 ± 0.002. The universe is flat to about 0.2%, and at least far bigger than the part we see.

</div>
