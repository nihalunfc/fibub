**Module 4 · about 14 minutes.** Read first: [4.3 What expansion means](/learn/courses/cosmology/expanding-universe/). We now look again at redshift, the measurement that started it all, and see what it really tells us. By the end you will be able to turn any redshift into how much the universe has grown.

<div class="bigidea">Light crossing an expanding universe is stretched along with space. A galaxy's redshift is a direct record of how much the universe has grown since the light left it.</div>

## 1. Stretching on the way

Picture a light wave leaving a far galaxy. On its long trip, the space it is crossing grows. The wave's crests are carried apart with the stretching, so the wavelength grows by exactly the same factor as every other distance.

```math
1 + z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{seen}}}{\lambda_{\text{sent}}} = \frac{a_{\text{now}}}{a_{\text{then}}} = \frac{1}{a_{\text{then}}}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>z</dt><dd>the redshift we measure from the spectrum</dd>
<dt>λ<sub>sent</sub>, λ<sub>seen</sub></dt><dd>the wavelength when the light left, and when it arrives</dd>
<dt>a<sub>then</sub></dt><dd>the scale factor when the light left (a<sub>now</sub> = 1)</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Redshift is a size ratio. z = 1 means the universe has doubled in size since the light left; z = 9 means it has grown tenfold.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="stretched-wave"><figcaption>The grid lines are marks fixed in space. As they spread out, the wave crossing them stretches by the same amount. Slide how much the universe grows during the trip and watch the light go from blue, through red, into infrared.</figcaption></figure>

**Worked example.** Hydrogen sends out ultraviolet light at 121.6 nm. A galaxy shows this line at 851 nm, in the infrared. Then 1 + z = 851 ÷ 121.6 = 7.0, so z = 6. When the light left, every distance between galaxies was **one-seventh** of today's.

## 2. Not quite a Doppler shift

For nearby galaxies, the speed from v = cz (the Doppler formula of [Lesson 4.1](/learn/courses/cosmology/redshift-galaxies/)) and the stretching picture give the same answer. Far away they part company. The Doppler formula would say a galaxy with z = 2 moves at twice the speed of light, which sounds impossible. In the stretching picture there is no problem: z = 2 simply means the universe has tripled in size while the light travelled.

Physicists also show that cosmological redshift can be thought of as many small Doppler shifts added up along the path, between neighbours that are each moving apart slowly. Both descriptions are correct; the stretching picture is simpler.

<div class="history">

**Light and time both stretch.** Not only the waves: everything that happens in a far galaxy looks slowed down by the same factor 1 + z. A Type Ia supernova at z = 1 seems to brighten and fade over twice as long as a nearby one. Astronomers saw exactly this in the 1990s, strong evidence that the redshift really comes from expansion and not from "tired light" losing energy on the way.

</div>

## 3. Redshift as a time machine

Because redshift tells us the size of the universe when the light left, astronomers use it as a clock. "z = 1" means "when the universe was half its present size", about 8 billion years ago. Some landmarks:

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>z ≈ 0.003</b> Sombrero galaxy: the universe was 0.3% smaller.</li>
<li><b>z ≈ 1</b> universe half today's size: about 8 billion years ago.</li>
<li><b>z ≈ 6</b> the first galaxies have cleared the fog of neutral hydrogen.</li>
<li><b>z ≈ 14</b> the most distant galaxies confirmed by the James Webb telescope (2024).</li>
<li><b>z ≈ 1,100</b> the cosmic microwave background: the universe was 1,100 times smaller and glowing hot.</li>
</ul>

This is why the [James Webb Space Telescope](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/) works mainly in the infrared. Young galaxies shine brightest in ultraviolet and visible light, but by the time that light reaches us from z = 10 it has been stretched 11 times, far into the infrared.

<div class="myth">

**"Redshift is light getting tired and losing energy."** Each photon does arrive with less energy, but because the wave was stretched, not because it got tired. The time-stretch of distant supernovae and the perfect blackbody spectrum of the cosmic microwave background both rule out "tired light".

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Light crossing expanding space is stretched by the same factor as space: 1 + z = 1 / a<sub>then</sub>.
- So redshift measures how much the universe has grown since the light left (z = 1: doubled).
- For nearby galaxies it matches v = cz; far away, only the stretching picture makes sense, and z can exceed 1.
- Distant events are slowed down by 1 + z too, as seen in supernovae.
- Webb looks in infrared because the light of the first galaxies is stretched there.

</div>
