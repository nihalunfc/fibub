**Module 9 · about 14 minutes.** Read first: [5.5 How each ingredient thins out](/learn/courses/cosmology/matter-radiation-dark-energy/) and [9.3 The vacuum energy puzzle](/learn/courses/cosmology/vacuum-energy/). Lesson 5.5 noticed in passing that matter and dark energy happen to be about equal today. This last lesson of the module takes that seriously: how odd is it, and what might it mean? By the end you will be able to work out when the two were equal and weigh the main explanations.

<div class="bigidea">One ingredient thins out a thousand-fold every time the universe grows ten-fold; the other never changes. Yet we live just when the two are about the same size. Either we are lucky, or our existence is tied to the timing, or dark energy knows about matter.</div>

## 1. A brief overlap

Matter density falls as 1/a³ while a cosmological constant stays put, so their ratio grows as a³:

```math
\frac{\rho_\Lambda}{\rho_m} = \frac{\Omega_\Lambda}{\Omega_m}\,a^3 = \frac{\Omega_\Lambda}{\Omega_m}\,\frac{1}{(1+z)^3}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Ω<sub>Λ</sub>/Ω<sub>m</sub></dt><dd>today's ratio: 0.69 / 0.31 ≈ 2.2</dd>
<dt>a³</dt><dd>the volume of the universe compared with today. Ten times the size, a thousand times the ratio</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>When the CMB was released (a ≈ 1/1100) dark energy was a billionth of matter. When the universe is ten times bigger it will be two thousand times matter. "About equal" is a passing moment.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

Two dates are worth keeping apart:

- **Speeding up began** at z ≈ 0.65, about **6 billion years ago**, when matter had thinned to twice Λ's density (Λ pushes with 2ρ, matter pulls with ρ; Lesson 9.1).
- **Equal densities** came later, at z ≈ 0.3, about **3.6 billion years ago**.

The Sun and Earth formed 4.6 billion years ago, between the two. Life on Earth has run its whole course in the era of acceleration.

<div class="problem" data-problem="coincidence-problem-ratio"></div>

## 2. How strange is it?

It depends on how you count time, and it is fair to say so. On the log axis of the figure, from a century after the Big Bang to a trillion years, the shaded band is under a tenth of the width. Measured against an endless future, it is vanishingly short. But measured in ordinary years since the Big Bang, the band runs from 3.6 to 22 billion years, which includes most of the past. So the puzzle is not that "now" is a strange moment, but that the value of dark energy, set by physics nobody understands (Lesson 9.3), happens to make the hand-over fall within a few billion years of when stars, planets and astronomers appear.

## 3. Proposed answers

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>Selection: we could only be here about now.</b> Observers need heavy elements, stars and planets, which take billions of years to build. In 2007 Charles Lineweaver and Chas Egan used the ages of Earth-like planets in the universe to argue that observers like us would most likely find dark energy and matter comparable.</li>
<li><b>Anthropic Λ.</b> Weinberg's 1987 argument (Lesson 9.3): if Λ were much larger, galaxies would never have formed. Observers then find a Λ that took over at roughly the time galaxies finish forming, which is not long before now.</li>
<li><b>Tracker fields.</b> In 1999 Ivaylo Zlatev, Limin Wang and Paul Steinhardt showed that some quintessence fields "track" the dominant ingredient for most of cosmic history, then take over late, whatever they start with. This removes the need to fine-tune the start, though not the timing of the take-over itself.</li>
<li><b>Dark energy talks to dark matter.</b> If the two exchange energy, their densities could stay linked. Such models are tightly limited by the CMB and galaxy maps, but not ruled out.</li>
<li><b>Just chance.</b> Some cosmologists argue a factor of two today is not unlikely enough to need an explanation.</li>
</ul>

None of these is accepted as the answer. The DESI hint (Lesson 9.6), if it grows, would favour dynamical ideas over a pure constant.

## 4. Dark energy: what we know, what we don't

**Known, from many independent methods:** the expansion has been speeding up for about 6 billion years; something smooth with strongly negative pressure makes up about 69% of the universe's energy; it behaves within a few per cent like Einstein's cosmological constant (w ≈ −1).

**Not known:** what it is. Why its density is 10<sup>120</sup> times smaller than the simplest estimate. Whether it changes with time. How the universe will end. Why it took over just about now. These open questions return in Module 12, where Lesson 12.1 sets out ΛCDM, the standard model built on Λ.

<div class="history">

**Naming the puzzle.**

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>1987</b> Weinberg's anthropic bound links the size of Λ to the time galaxies form.</li>
<li><b>1998</b> The measured value makes matter and Λ comparable today.</li>
<li><b>1999</b> Zlatev, Wang and Steinhardt name it the "cosmic coincidence" problem and propose tracker quintessence; Steinhardt often put it as "why now?".</li>
<li><b>2007</b> Lineweaver and Egan argue it is a selection effect from when Earth-like planets form.</li>
</ul>

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Dark energy switched on about 6 billion years ago."** It did not switch on. A cosmological constant has had the same density in every cubic metre since the beginning. Early on, matter was far denser and simply outweighed it. What changed 6 billion years ago is that matter thinned enough for dark energy's push to win.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- ρ<sub>Λ</sub>/ρ<sub>m</sub> = (Ω<sub>Λ</sub>/Ω<sub>m</sub>) a³: a billionth at the CMB, about 2.2 today, thousands in the far future.
- Acceleration began at z ≈ 0.65 (about 6 billion years ago, when ρ<sub>m</sub> = 2ρ<sub>Λ</sub>); equal densities at z ≈ 0.3 (about 3.6 billion years ago).
- The two are within a factor of 10 only from about 3.6 to 22 billion years after the Big Bang, and we live in that window: the coincidence, or "why now?", problem.
- Proposed answers: a selection effect (observers need billions of years), anthropic Λ, tracker quintessence, linked dark energy and dark matter, or chance. None is settled.
- Dark energy is 69% of the universe and close to a cosmological constant; what it is remains one of the biggest open questions in physics.

</div>
