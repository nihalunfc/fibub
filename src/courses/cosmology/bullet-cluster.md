**Module 8 · about 14 minutes.** Read first: [3.8 Gravitational lensing](/learn/courses/cosmology/gravitational-lensing/) and [8.2 Rotation curves](/learn/courses/cosmology/rotation-curves/). Speeds of galaxies and stars say there is extra mass. But could it be that gravity itself works differently far out, with no extra mass at all? A collision between two clusters gave the clearest answer so far. By the end you will be able to explain why the Bullet Cluster is such strong evidence that dark matter is a real substance.

<div class="bigidea">When two clusters crashed, their gas got stuck in the middle but the mass, seen by lensing, carried on. The mass is not where most of the atoms are, so most of the mass is not atoms.</div>

## 1. Three ways to see a cluster

A galaxy cluster has three ingredients, and we see each one differently:

- **Galaxies**, seen in ordinary light. Their stars are only about 2% of the cluster's mass.
- **Hot gas** at about 100 million degrees, seen in X-rays (for example by NASA's Chandra telescope). It holds several times more mass than the stars: most of the cluster's ordinary matter.
- **All the mass**, whatever it is made of, seen by **gravitational lensing**. Mass bends the light of galaxies far behind the cluster, so their images are slightly stretched (weak lensing) or turned into arcs (strong lensing). Mapping the stretching across the sky maps the mass ([Lesson 3.8](/learn/courses/cosmology/gravitational-lensing/)).

In a quiet cluster all three sit on top of each other, so you cannot tell whether the extra mass is extra matter, or ordinary matter with gravity that works differently. You need something to pull them apart.

## 2. A cosmic car crash

The **Bullet Cluster**, 1E 0657-558, whose light set out about 3.5 billion years ago (redshift 0.296), is two clusters that passed through each other some 150 million years before the light we see left them. Step through what happened:

<div class="steps" data-fig="bullet-cluster">
<div class="step">Two clusters head towards each other, a big one and a smaller one (the "bullet"). In each, galaxies, hot gas and the mass seen by lensing are centred together.</div>
<div class="step">They close in at thousands of kilometres per second, pulled by each other's gravity.</div>
<div class="step">They pass through each other. The galaxies are tiny compared with the gaps between them, so they almost never hit: they sail through. The two gas clouds cannot pass through each other: they collide, slow down and heat up, like two crowds trying to cross a narrow bridge.</div>
<div class="step">Today the galaxies have moved on to either side, and the gas lags behind in the middle. The smaller gas cloud is swept into a bullet shape with a shock front ahead of it, like the bow wave of a boat. The lensing maps show the mass is with the galaxies, not with the gas.</div>
<div class="step">If there were no dark matter, most of the mass would be the gas, so lensing would be strongest on the gas (dashed red). It is not. The mass went with something that, like the galaxies, passed through without colliding.</div>
</div>

## 3. What the maps showed

In 2006 Douglas Clowe and his colleagues combined weak and strong lensing from Hubble and ground telescopes with Chandra's X-ray picture. The two peaks of the mass map sit on the two groups of galaxies, offset from the two gas clouds at a significance of 8 standard deviations: a chance of it being a fluke of far less than one in a trillion. Their paper was boldly titled *A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter*.

Why is this so hard to explain without dark matter? In a theory where gravity is simply stronger than Newton's far out, extra pull should still be centred on where most of the ordinary mass is, and that is the gas. A modified law of gravity alone cannot move the pull away from the mass that causes it. Something unseen, with plenty of mass, moved with the galaxies.

## 4. Dark matter does not collide much

The collision also tested how dark matter behaves. The two dark matter clumps passed through each other with little sign of being slowed or scattered. That sets a limit on how much dark matter particles can bump into each other: their **self-interaction cross-section** (the target area they present, per gram of dark matter) must be less than about 1 cm² per gram. It almost ignores itself, as well as ordinary matter.

The Bullet Cluster is no longer alone. Other colliding clusters, such as MACS J0025.4-1222, show the same split between gas and mass. A few are puzzling: in Abell 520, the "Train Wreck", some studies find a lump of mass with the gas and few galaxies. Messy collisions are hard to map, and astronomers keep studying them.

<div class="history">

**Making the case.** The cluster was first spotted in 1995 in X-rays. In 2002 Maxim Markevitch's team used Chandra to find its spectacular bow shock: the gas is moving faster than sound in the cluster gas, with the shock front racing at about 4,700 km/s (later work suggests the dark matter clumps themselves moved somewhat slower). In 2004 they used it to limit dark matter's self-interaction, and in 2006 Clowe's lensing map made headlines worldwide.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The Bullet Cluster proves what dark matter is."** It shows that most of the mass is something other than the hot gas and stars, that it is not strongly colliding, and that it behaves like a cloud of particles that hardly ever collide. It says nothing about which particle it is (Lessons 8.5 and 8.6). Supporters of modified gravity reply that their theories need some unseen matter in clusters anyway (Lesson 8.7), but that matter then has to do almost everything dark matter does.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Clusters have galaxies (about 2%), hot X-ray gas (most of the atoms) and much more mass seen by lensing.
- In the Bullet Cluster, two clusters passed through each other: the gas collided and stayed in the middle, the galaxies sailed through.
- Lensing shows the mass went with the galaxies, offset from most of the atoms at 8 standard deviations (Clowe et al. 2006).
- So most of the mass is not ordinary matter, and dark matter barely collides with itself (less than about 1 cm² per gram).

</div>
