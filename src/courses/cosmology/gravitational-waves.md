By the end you will be able to explain what a gravitational wave is, how it was first detected, and how it gives a new way to measure the universe.

<div class="bigidea">Spacetime can ripple. Whirl two huge masses round each other and waves of stretching and squeezing spread out at the speed of light.</div>

## 1. Ripples in the fabric

If mass curves spacetime, then moving masses should make the curves move. In 1916 Einstein showed that his equations allow **waves**: travelling ripples of curvature that move at the speed of light. As one passes, space is stretched in one direction and squeezed in the other, then the reverse, over and over.

Einstein doubted they could ever be measured. The ripples from even the most violent events in the universe are tiny by the time they reach us.

## 2. The first clue: a shrinking orbit

In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor found a pulsar orbiting another neutron star every 7.75 hours. Over the following years they watched the orbit shrink, exactly as fast as general relativity says it should if the pair is losing energy as gravitational waves. It won them the 1993 Nobel Prize, and it was strong evidence that the waves are real, though no one had felt one directly yet.

## 3. LIGO hears two black holes

LIGO uses two huge L-shaped detectors in the USA, each with arms 4 km long. Laser light runs up and down both arms and is recombined. A passing wave stretches one arm and squeezes the other, changing the light pattern.

On **14 September 2015**, both detectors caught the same signal, 7 milliseconds apart. Two black holes of about 36 and 29 Suns, 1.3 billion light-years away, had spiralled together and merged into one of about 62 Suns. The missing 3 Suns of mass became gravitational-wave energy in a fraction of a second (E = mc², Lesson 3.3).

The size of the effect is measured as **strain**, h: the fraction by which a length changes.

```math
h = \frac{\Delta L}{L}
```

Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish and Kip Thorne won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics for LIGO. Since then, LIGO, Virgo in Italy and KAGRA in Japan have recorded hundreds of mergers. A third LIGO detector, **LIGO-India**, approved in 2023, is being built in Maharashtra to help pinpoint where signals come from.

## 4. Standard sirens: a new ruler for the universe

On 17 August 2017 LIGO and Virgo caught two **neutron stars** merging, and 1.7 seconds later telescopes saw a burst of gamma rays from the same patch of sky. Dozens of observatories then found the glow in a galaxy 130 million light-years away.

Two big results followed:

- Gravitational waves travel at the speed of light, to better than one part in a thousand trillion.
- The wave's shape and strength reveal how far away the source really is, with no distance ladder. Such a source is called a **standard siren**. With the host galaxy's redshift, it gave an expansion rate of about 70 km/s per megaparsec: a brand-new, independent way to tackle the Hubble tension (Lesson 12.2).

<div class="myth">

**"We hear gravitational waves."** They are not sound and cannot travel through air as sound does; they are ripples of space itself. But many of them fall in the same frequency range as sound, so scientists often turn the signal into audio, and the "chirp" is a real description of its shape.

</div>

<div class="history">

**Waves from the beginning.** In 2023 several pulsar timing arrays, which use dozens of pulsars across our galaxy as clocks, reported evidence of a slow background of gravitational waves, probably from pairs of supermassive black holes across the universe. A space detector, LISA, is planned for the 2030s, and some cosmologists hope to one day detect waves left over from the very early universe (Module 11).

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Gravitational waves are ripples of spacetime curvature that travel at the speed of light, stretching and squeezing space.
- A shrinking pulsar orbit (1974 onwards) gave the first evidence; LIGO first detected them directly on 14 September 2015.
- Merging black holes make a "chirp": the wave rises in pitch and strength, then rings down.
- A merger's wave gives its true distance, a "standard siren", an independent way to measure the expansion of the universe.

</div>
