**Module 6 · about 15 minutes.** Read first: [6.1 Gamow, Alpher and Herman](/learn/courses/cosmology/gamow-alpher-herman/) and [4.8 The cosmological principle](/learn/courses/cosmology/cosmological-principle/). An expanding universe does not have to have a beginning, if it can make new matter as it grows. For seventeen years that idea was a serious rival to the Big Bang. By the end you will be able to explain how each picture made its own predictions and which observations decided between them.

<div class="bigidea">The Steady State universe expands but never changes, because new matter fills the gaps. It made one clear promise: the distant past looks like today. Observations of the distant past showed it does not.</div>

## 1. A universe with no beginning

In 1948, in Cambridge, three young scientists, **Hermann Bondi**, **Thomas Gold** and **Fred Hoyle**, disliked the idea of a beginning. A creation event cannot be studied, and in the 1940s the Big Bang also had an embarrassing problem: Hubble's expansion rate, about 500 km/s per megaparsec, made the universe only about 2 billion years old, younger than the Earth's rocks.

They proposed the **perfect cosmological principle**: the universe is the same not only everywhere ([Lesson 4.8](/learn/courses/cosmology/cosmological-principle/)) but also at every time. It expands, so galaxies drift apart; and new matter appears, very slowly, so that new galaxies form in the gaps. A fountain in a park looks the same all day though its water keeps changing: the Steady State universe is a fountain, not a firework.

## 2. How much new matter?

To keep the density fixed while every cubic metre of space grows, new matter must appear at a steady rate. A cube of space of side L grows in volume as L³, and L grows at the rate H, so the volume grows at the rate 3H:

```math
\frac{\text{new atoms}}{\text{volume} \times \text{time}} = 3 H_0\, n
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>n</dt><dd>atoms per cubic metre, which in the Steady State never changes (Hoyle took the critical density, about 5 hydrogen atoms per m³)</dd>
<dt>3H<sub>0</sub></dt><dd>how fast each cubic metre of space grows in volume: three times the expansion rate, because space grows in three directions</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>About one new hydrogen atom per cubic metre every billion years: one atom in a large room every few tens of millions of years. Far too slow to notice, so the theory could not be tested by looking for creation directly.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<div class="problem" data-problem="big-bang-vs-steady-state-creation"></div>

Creating matter from nothing breaks the law of conservation of energy, and critics said so. Hoyle answered that a Big Bang creates the whole universe at once, which is no better. The argument could only be settled by looking.

## 3. Four tests

The two pictures differ most in what they say about the past. In a Big Bang universe, looking far away ([Lesson 2.1](/learn/courses/cosmology/lookback-time/)) shows a younger, denser, different universe. In a Steady State universe, the far past must look like today.

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>The age (1952 onwards).</b> Walter Baade found that Hubble's distances were too small: galaxies were about twice as far away, and later work by Allan Sandage pushed the age up again. By the late 1950s the Big Bang no longer looked younger than the Earth. The Steady State's first advantage was gone.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Counting radio sources (1955 to 1961).</b> Martin Ryle's team in Cambridge counted radio galaxies by brightness. In a universe the same everywhere and at all times, the number brighter than a flux S goes as S<sup>−3/2</sup> nearby, and expansion makes the faint end fall below that. Ryle found the opposite: far more faint, distant sources than the rule allows. The young universe held more of them.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Quasars (1963).</b> Maarten Schmidt found that the star-like radio source 3C 273 has a redshift of 0.158: an extremely bright object far away. Quasars turned out to be common at large distances, long ago, and rare nearby. A universe that never changes should have them everywhere.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Helium and the glow (1964 to 1965).</b> Every old star and gas cloud is about 25% helium by mass, too much for stars to have made. Then in 1965 a cold glow was found filling the sky, just as a hot beginning predicts (<a href="/learn/courses/cosmology/cmb-discovery/">Lesson 7.1</a>). The Steady State had no natural way to make either.</div>
</div>

## 4. After the argument

Most astronomers accepted the hot Big Bang after 1965. Hoyle never did. With Geoffrey Burbidge and the Indian astrophysicist **Jayant Narlikar**, a former student of Hoyle's who later founded the Inter-University Centre for Astronomy and Astrophysics (IUCAA) in Pune, he proposed a **quasi-steady state** model in 1993, with matter created in bursts. It never matched the precise measurements of the microwave background, and few cosmologists follow it today.

The argument was good for science. Because the Steady State made firm predictions, it pushed astronomers to count radio sources, hunt for quasars and measure helium, and in the end it was decided by observation, not by taste.

<div class="history">

**A rivalry with a famous name.** On 28 March 1949, explaining the two theories on BBC radio, Hoyle called the rival idea a "big bang", and the name stuck ([Lesson 4.4](/learn/courses/cosmology/not-an-explosion/)). Hoyle and Ryle, both in Cambridge, argued in public for years, and Ryle's first radio counts (1955) turned out to be partly wrong, which made Hoyle's camp distrust the later, better ones. Science settled it anyway.

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>1948</b> Bondi and Gold; Hoyle: the Steady State, with continuous creation.</li>
<li><b>1949</b> Hoyle says "big bang" on the radio.</li>
<li><b>1952</b> Baade doubles the distance scale; the age problem eases.</li>
<li><b>1961</b> Ryle and Clarke: too many faint radio sources for a Steady State.</li>
<li><b>1963</b> Schmidt: quasars are far away; they belong to the past.</li>
<li><b>1965</b> The cosmic microwave background is found.</li>
<li><b>1993</b> Hoyle, Burbidge and Narlikar's quasi-steady state model.</li>
</ul>

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The Steady State was a silly idea that only stubborn people believed."** It was a serious, elegant theory held by excellent scientists, and it was more testable than the early Big Bang: it allowed no change at all, so any sign of change anywhere could disprove it. That is exactly what happened.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Steady State (Bondi, Gold and Hoyle, 1948): the universe expands but looks the same at all times; new matter appears at 3H<sub>0</sub>n, about one atom per cubic metre per billion years.
- It solved the 1940s age problem, which went away when distances were corrected in the 1950s.
- Radio source counts (more faint sources than allowed) and quasars (common only far away, long ago) showed the universe changes with time.
- 25% helium everywhere and the cosmic microwave background (1965) fit a hot beginning and settled the argument.
- Hoyle, Burbidge and Narlikar's quasi-steady state (1993) kept the idea alive but does not fit the CMB.

</div>
