**Module 2 · about 12 minutes.** By the end you will be able to work out how old the light from an object is, explain why a deep image of the sky is a slice through history, and say why huge distances need extra care.

<div class="bigidea">Every time you look up, you look into the past. The farther you look, the older the light, until you are seeing the universe as a baby.</div>

## 1. Old light

Light travels at 299,792 km every second. That's fast enough to go round the Earth seven times in a second, but the universe is big. So:

- the Moon you see is the Moon of **1.3 seconds** ago;
- the Sun is the Sun of **8.3 minutes** ago (if it vanished, you'd have 8 minutes of sunshine left);
- the bright red star Betelgeuse is seen as it was about **640 years** ago;
- the Andromeda galaxy as it was **2.5 million years** ago, before our species existed.

## 2. Lookback time

The time a galaxy's light has spent travelling is called its **lookback time**. Because the universe is 13.8 billion years old, no light can have travelled longer than that. So lookback time runs from zero (here and now) to 13.8 billion years (the beginning).

<div class="meaning">

**A deep image is a history book.** In one long-exposure picture of a dark patch of sky, nearby galaxies appear as they were a few hundred million years ago, and the faintest red smudges as they were more than 13 billion years ago. Cosmologists can literally line up galaxies by age and watch how they grew. No other science can see its own past directly.

</div>

## 3. What we have seen so far back

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>1990s</b> The Hubble Space Telescope's deep fields show galaxies whose light left about 12 billion years ago, small and messy compared with today's spirals.</li>
<li><b>2016</b> Hubble spots a galaxy (GN-z11) seen about 400 million years after the beginning.</li>
<li><b>2022 onwards</b> The James Webb Space Telescope, which sees in infrared, finds galaxies from about 300 million years after the beginning, earlier than many expected (Lesson 12.4).</li>
<li><b>The limit</b> Before about 380,000 years, the universe was a glowing fog that light could not cross. Its afterglow, the cosmic microwave background, is the oldest light there is (Module 7).</li>
</ul>

<div class="myth">

**"A galaxy 13 billion light-years away is 13 billion light-years away now."** For nearby objects, light-travel distance and real distance are the same. For very distant galaxies they are not, because space kept stretching while the light was on its way. Such a galaxy is now about 30 billion light-years away. That's why cosmologists usually describe the farthest objects by their **redshift** or their lookback time rather than by "light-years away" (Lessons 4.5 and 5.8).

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Light takes time to travel, so we always see things as they were when the light left them.
- The lookback time of a galaxy is how long its light has travelled, from 0 up to 13.8 billion years.
- Deep images show galaxies at many ages at once, so cosmologists can watch the universe grow up.
- For very distant galaxies, the expansion of space makes "light-years away" ambiguous; redshift is used instead.

</div>
