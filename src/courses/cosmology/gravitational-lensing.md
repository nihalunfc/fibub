<div class="bigidea">Every mass bends the light that passes it. A galaxy or a cluster of galaxies can bend light from far behind it into arcs and rings, like a lens made of gravity.</div>

## 1. A lens made of mass

The 1919 eclipse showed that the Sun bends starlight (Lesson 3.6). Put a much bigger mass, a galaxy, between us and a more distant galaxy or quasar. Light from the background source can now reach us along more than one path: one passing each side of the lens. We see **more than one image** of the same object, each bent and stretched.

## 2. Two images, or a ring

<figure class="fig" data-fig="lens-ring"><figcaption>What a telescope sees when a single compact mass lenses a background source. Off to one side, there are two images on opposite sides of the lens. Slide the source to line it up exactly and the images merge into an Einstein ring.</figcaption></figure>

When source, lens and observer line up perfectly, the light is bent equally round every side, and the image becomes a ring. The radius of that ring, the **Einstein radius**, depends on the mass of the lens and the distances:

```math
\theta_{E} = \sqrt{\frac{4 G M}{c^{2}}\;\frac{D_{LS}}{D_{L}\, D_{S}}}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>θ<sub>E</sub></dt><dd>the angular radius of the Einstein ring</dd>
<dt>M</dt><dd>the mass of the lens inside the ring</dd>
<dt>D<sub>L</sub>, D<sub>S</sub>, D<sub>LS</sub></dt><dd>distances to the lens, to the source, and from lens to source</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Measure the size of a ring and the distances, and you get the mass of the lens directly, from geometry and general relativity alone.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

Real lenses are not single points, so astronomers also see **arcs**, crosses of four images ("Einstein crosses") and partial rings.

## 3. Three kinds of lensing

- **Strong lensing:** clear multiple images, rings and arcs. The first example, found in 1979, was a "twin quasar": two images of one quasar, 6 arcseconds apart, split by a galaxy in between. Galaxy clusters make spectacular arcs, and they **magnify** galaxies behind them, letting telescopes such as Hubble and JWST see galaxies that would otherwise be too faint.
- **Weak lensing:** the shapes of millions of background galaxies are very slightly stretched by mass along the way. No single galaxy shows it, but averaged together they map the mass of the universe. This is one of the main tools for mapping **dark matter** (Module 8).
- **Microlensing:** a star passing in front of another briefly brightens it. It has found planets around other stars, and tested whether dark matter could be made of dark compact objects.

<div class="myth">

**"A gravitational lens works like a glass lens."** A glass lens bends light more at its edges to bring it to one focus. A gravitational lens bends light *less* the farther out it passes, so it never makes one sharp focus. That is why it makes arcs and rings rather than a neat magnified picture.

</div>

<div class="history">

**Einstein thought it would never be seen.** In 1936 a Czech amateur, Rudi Mandl, persuaded Einstein to publish the idea that a star could lens another. Einstein wrote that there was "no great chance of observing this phenomenon". He was thinking of stars. Fritz Zwicky pointed out in 1937 that whole galaxies would make lenses big enough to see. He was right.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Mass bends light, so a galaxy or cluster can show us several images of a single background source.
- A perfectly lined-up source becomes an Einstein ring, whose size gives the lens's mass.
- Strong lensing makes arcs and rings and magnifies faint distant galaxies; weak lensing maps mass, including dark matter; microlensing finds planets.
- Lensing weighs everything, shining or not, which makes it central to cosmology.

</div>
