**Module 4 · about 13 minutes.** Read first: [4.3 What expansion means](/learn/courses/cosmology/expanding-universe/) and [1.4 The Copernican revolution](/learn/courses/cosmology/copernican-revolution/). We have seen that every galaxy sees itself at the centre. This lesson turns that into the founding assumption of modern cosmology, and shows how we test it. By the end you will be able to say on what scale the universe looks the same everywhere.

<div class="bigidea">Look at a big enough piece of the universe and it is much like any other big piece, in any direction. That one idea, the cosmological principle, lets a handful of numbers describe the whole universe.</div>

## 1. From Copernicus to cosmology

Copernicus moved the Earth from the centre. Later astronomers moved the Sun from the centre of the Milky Way, and the Milky Way from the centre of anything. The lesson each time: **we are not in a special place**. The cosmological principle takes it to its limit: no place in the universe is special.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Homogeneous</dt><dd>the same everywhere: a big box here holds about as many galaxies as a big box anywhere else</dd>
<dt>Isotropic</dt><dd>the same in every direction: looking left or right, up or down, shows the same kind of universe</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Only true on average, over big scales. Up close there are planets, stars, galaxies and empty voids; far enough out the averages match. A sandy beach is like this: lumpy grain by grain, smooth from a cliff.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

## 2. Lumpy up close, smooth far out

Galaxies are not spread evenly. They gather in groups and clusters, strung along filaments around huge empty **voids**, a pattern called the cosmic web (Module 10). So how can the universe be "the same everywhere"? Count galaxies in boxes. In small boxes the counts swing wildly; in big boxes they come out almost the same.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="homogeneity"><figcaption>Made-up galaxies in clumps and strands. Slide the box size: small boxes hold very different numbers (orange: more than average, blue: fewer); large boxes hold almost the same number. That is how real surveys test homogeneity.</figcaption></figure>

Real surveys do exactly this in 3-D. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey (2005) and the WiggleZ survey (2012) found that the counts even out in boxes above roughly **300 to 400 million light-years** across. The observable universe is about 300 times wider than that, so there is plenty of room for averaging.

## 3. The same in every direction

The best test of isotropy is the cosmic microwave background, the leftover glow of the hot early universe. Its temperature is 2.7255 K in every direction, the same to about **1 part in 100,000** once our own motion is taken out (Module 7). Radio galaxies and distant supernovae are also spread evenly across the sky.

If the universe looks the same in every direction from here, and we are not in a special place, then it must look the same in every direction from everywhere, and that makes it homogeneous too.

<div class="history">

**An assumption first, a fact later.** Einstein assumed the universe was evenly filled in 1917 simply because it made the equations solvable, long before anyone could check. The British cosmologist Edward Milne named it the "cosmological principle" in the 1930s. Only the big galaxy maps of the 2000s showed directly where the lumpiness ends.

</div>

## 4. Why it matters so much

If every place is alike, then the whole universe can be described by one **scale factor** a(t) and one **Hubble rate** H(t), both depending only on time. Einstein's equations, which are fiendish in general, then shrink to one simple equation, the Friedmann equation. Module 5 builds it from a thrown ball.

<div class="myth">

**"If the universe is the same everywhere, then the Earth is nothing special."** The principle is about the universe on huge scales. On small scales, the Earth is very special: it is where we live. The principle only says our location gives us an ordinary view of the large-scale universe.

</div>

Astronomers keep testing the principle: some claim giant structures, such as rings and arcs of galaxies over a billion light-years long, may be bigger than it allows. So far none has overturned it, but it is a lively question.

<div class="keypoints">

- The cosmological principle: on large scales the universe is homogeneous (same everywhere) and isotropic (same in every direction).
- Galaxies clump in a cosmic web; counts even out in boxes above about 300 to 400 million light-years.
- The cosmic microwave background is the same in all directions to 1 part in 100,000.
- With the principle, one scale factor a(t) and one Hubble rate H(t) describe the whole universe, which makes the equations of Module 5 possible.

</div>
