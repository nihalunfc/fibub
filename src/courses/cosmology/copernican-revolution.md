**Module 1 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain why the planets seem to loop backwards, why two different pictures could explain it, and which observation decided between them.

<div class="bigidea">Moving the Earth out of the centre was the first step in a long series of discoveries that we are not special: not our planet, not our Sun, not even our galaxy.</div>

## 1. The puzzle of the wandering planets

Most nights, Mars drifts slowly eastward against the stars. But every two years or so it stops, moves backwards (westward) for a couple of months, stops again and carries on. Jupiter and Saturn do the same. This backward loop is called **retrograde motion**, and any picture of the universe had to explain it.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="two-pictures"><figcaption>Play the motion in both pictures. In the Sun-centred one, Earth on the inside lane overtakes Mars, so Mars seems to slip backwards. In the Earth-centred one, Mars rides a small circle (an epicycle) on a big one. The loop drawn on the sky (bottom) comes out exactly the same.</figcaption></figure>

## 2. Two pictures, one sky

Ptolemy (about 150 CE) explained the loop with an **epicycle**: Mars moves on a small circle whose centre travels around the Earth on a big circle. Copernicus (1543) explained it by putting the Sun in the middle: the Earth moves faster on its inner orbit and simply overtakes Mars.

Here is the surprise: the two pictures predict the **same loop**. Mars's epicycle in Ptolemy's model is just a copy of the Earth's own orbit, flipped around. So for nearly 70 years after Copernicus, the evidence could not decide.

<div class="meaning">

**Same predictions, different stories.** When two models make identical predictions, you need a new observation where they disagree. Finding that "crucial test" is one of the most important skills in science. In cosmology today, the Hubble tension (Lesson 12.2) is exactly this kind of hunt.

</div>

Copernicus's picture was simpler in one way: it explained naturally why Mercury and Venus never stray far from the Sun, and why the loops happen when a planet is opposite the Sun in our sky. (Kepler later replaced the circles with ellipses, which you can explore in the Astronomy course: [Kepler's laws](/learn/courses/astronomy/keplers-laws/).)

## 3. Galileo's test: the phases of Venus

In 1610 Galileo turned one of the first telescopes on Venus. He saw it go through **phases like the Moon**, and he saw that it looked **small when nearly full and large when a thin crescent**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="venus-phases"><figcaption>Switch between the pictures. In Ptolemy's, Venus is always between us and the Sun, so we only ever see its night side or a thin crescent. In the Sun-centred one, Venus swings behind the Sun and shows its whole lit face, but it is then far away and looks small.</figcaption></figure>

Ptolemy's model simply cannot produce a full Venus. Galileo's observation broke it. (A third picture, Tycho Brahe's, in which the planets go round the Sun and the Sun goes round the Earth, could also explain the phases. It took Kepler's ellipses and Newton's gravity to finish the argument.)

<div class="history">

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>1543</b> Copernicus publishes <i>On the Revolutions</i>, putting the Sun at the centre.</li>
<li><b>1609</b> Kepler shows that Mars moves on an ellipse.</li>
<li><b>1610</b> Galileo sees the moons of Jupiter (not everything circles the Earth) and the phases of Venus.</li>
<li><b>1687</b> Newton's <i>Principia</i> explains why: one law of gravity for everything.</li>
<li><b>1838</b> Friedrich Bessel finally measures the tiny shift of a nearby star as the Earth moves (stellar parallax): direct proof that the Earth orbits the Sun.</li>
</ul>

</div>

## 4. The Copernican principle

Copernicus's idea grew into a guiding principle of cosmology: **we should not assume we are in a special place**. It has been confirmed again and again:

1. The Earth is not the centre of the Solar System.
2. The Sun is not the centre of the Milky Way (Lesson 1.7): it sits about 26,000 light-years out in a spiral arm.
3. The Milky Way is one ordinary galaxy among hundreds of billions (Lesson 1.7).
4. On the largest scales, every place in the universe looks roughly the same (the cosmological principle, Lesson 4.8).

<div class="myth">

**"Copernicus proved the Earth goes round the Sun."** He proposed it and showed it could work. The proof came bit by bit over the next three centuries: Galileo's phases of Venus, Kepler's ellipses, Newton's gravity, the aberration of starlight (1729) and stellar parallax (1838).

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Planets sometimes loop backwards (retrograde motion) against the stars.
- Ptolemy's epicycles and Copernicus's moving Earth both reproduce the loops, because an epicycle is just Earth's orbit seen in reverse.
- Galileo's 1610 view of Venus's full set of phases ruled out Ptolemy's arrangement.
- The Copernican principle, "we are not in a special place", is still a foundation of cosmology.

</div>
