By the end you will be able to explain the equivalence principle and why it leads straight to bent light and curved spacetime.

<div class="bigidea">Someone falling freely feels no weight at all. So gravity and acceleration must be two faces of the same thing.</div>

## 1. Everything falls the same way

Galileo argued that, without air, a heavy ball and a light ball fall together. In 1971 astronaut David Scott tested it on the airless Moon: he dropped a hammer and a falcon feather at the same moment, and they hit the ground together.

This is odd, if you think about it. A heavier object is pulled harder by gravity, but it is also harder to get moving. The two effects cancel **exactly**. Space experiments such as MICROSCOPE (finished 2022) found them equal to better than a few parts in a thousand trillion. Einstein decided this could not be a coincidence.

## 2. Einstein's lift

In 1907 Einstein had what he called "the happiest thought of my life": a person falling from a roof would not feel their own weight. He imagined a closed lift with no windows.

<div class="steps" data-fig="elevator">
<div class="step">Standing in a lift on the ground, you feel your weight and a dropped ball falls to the floor.</div>
<div class="step">Now the lift is a rocket far out in space, speeding up at 9.8 m/s². You feel exactly the same weight and the ball "falls" the same way. No experiment inside can tell the two apart.</div>
<div class="step">Cut the lift's cable. You and the ball fall together, and you both float. Inside, gravity seems to have vanished. That is why astronauts in orbit float: they are falling round the Earth all the time.</div>
<div class="step">Shine a torch across the speeding-up rocket. While the light crosses, the floor rushes up, so inside the beam curves downwards. If gravity is the same as acceleration, gravity must bend light too.</div>
</div>

**The equivalence principle:** in a small enough room, the effects of gravity cannot be told apart from the effects of acceleration.

## 3. How much does the light fall?

Very little over a lift, because light crosses it so quickly.

```math
d = \tfrac{1}{2}\, g \left(\frac{L}{c}\right)^{2}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>d</dt><dd>how far the beam drops while crossing</dd>
<dt>g</dt><dd>gravity's acceleration, 9.81 m/s² on Earth</dd>
<dt>L / c</dt><dd>the time the light takes to cross a room of width L</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Light falls like everything else; it just goes so fast that it has almost no time to fall. Over the huge distances near the Sun or round a galaxy, the bending becomes measurable (Lessons 3.6 and 3.8).</dd>
</dl>

## 4. From a lift to the universe

The equivalence principle hints that gravity is not a force like the others. If everything falls the same way, whatever its mass or what it is made of, perhaps falling is just the natural path through space and time, and it is *standing still on the ground* that needs a push (from the floor). Einstein spent eight more years turning this hint into general relativity, the theory of curved spacetime in the next lesson. Every model of the universe in this course is built on it.

<div class="myth">

**"There is no gravity in space."** At the height of the International Space Station, gravity is about 90% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because they are in free fall, moving sideways so fast (7.7 km/s) that they keep missing the Earth.

</div>

<div class="history">

**Einstein's two clocks.** The same lift also shows that clocks run at different rates in gravity. Light sent up from the floor of the speeding-up rocket arrives at the ceiling slightly stretched (redshifted). So clocks lower in gravity run slower than clocks higher up. It was measured in 1959 in a 22.5 m tower at Harvard (Lesson 3.5).

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- All objects fall the same way in gravity, whatever their mass, tested to a few parts in a thousand trillion.
- The equivalence principle: in a small room, gravity and acceleration cannot be told apart. Free fall feels weightless.
- So gravity must bend light, and clocks lower down in gravity must run slower.
- These hints led Einstein to general relativity, the basis of all modern cosmology.

</div>
