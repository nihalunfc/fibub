**Module 1 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe four ancient pictures of the universe, say what evidence each was based on, and explain which ideas survived into modern science.

<div class="bigidea">Ancient cosmologies were not just myths. The best of them were models built to match careful observations, and some of their tools are still used today.</div>

## 1. Watching before explaining

Long before anyone had a telescope, people noticed that the sky is regular. The stars turn together every night. The Sun drifts through the same constellations every year. The Moon repeats its phases every 29.5 days. Five bright "wandering stars", the planets, move among the fixed stars in more complicated ways.

The first job of any cosmology is to explain that order. Step through four very different answers:

<div class="steps" data-fig="world-models">
<div class="step"><p><b>Babylon, about 1000 BCE onwards.</b> The Earth is a flat disc surrounded by water, under a solid dome that carries the stars. The picture was simple, but the records were superb: scribes logged the Moon and planets night after night for centuries and found the cycles that let them predict eclipses.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Greece, 350 BCE to 150 CE.</b> Aristotle put a round, still Earth at the centre of nested crystal spheres, one for each planet, with the stars on the outermost sphere. Ptolemy turned this into a working machine of circles on circles that predicted planet positions for 1,400 years.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Aristarchus of Samos, about 270 BCE.</b> From shadows and angles (Lesson 1.3) he worked out that the Sun is far bigger than the Earth, and suggested the Earth goes round the Sun. Almost nobody followed him for 1,800 years.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Aryabhata, India, 499 CE.</b> In his book the <i>Aryabhatiya</i> he wrote that the Earth spins once a day and the stars stay still, "as a man in a boat moving forward sees the stationary objects on the bank moving backwards". He also gave a good value for the length of the day and explained eclipses by shadows, not demons.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>The Islamic world, 800 to 1500 CE.</b> Astronomers in Baghdad, Damascus, Maragha and Samarkand translated and checked the Greek books, built large observatories and fixed Ptolemy's flaws. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi found that two circles, one rolling inside the other, can make straight-line motion. Copernicus later used the same "Tusi couple".</p></div>
</div>

## 2. Why the Greeks put the Earth in the middle

Today an Earth-centred universe sounds silly, but the Greeks had good reasons:

- **We don't feel the Earth move.** If the Earth spun or flew around the Sun, wouldn't we be thrown off, or feel a constant wind?
- **The stars don't shift.** If the Earth moved around the Sun, nearby stars should seem to shift against far ones during the year (parallax). The Greeks looked and saw no shift. They were right that there is a shift, but it is far too small to see without a telescope: it was first measured in 1838. (See [parallax](/learn/courses/astronomy/parallax/) in the Astronomy course.)
- **It worked.** Ptolemy's model predicted where the planets would be to within a degree or so, good enough for calendars and navigation.

<div class="meaning">

**A model is judged by its predictions, not by how it feels.** Ptolemy's model made good predictions with the wrong physical picture. That is a warning cosmologists still take seriously: a model that fits the data is not automatically the true story. Lesson 1.4 shows how the Sun-centred picture finally won, by making a prediction Ptolemy's could not.

</div>

## 3. What each culture got right

| Culture | Best idea | Still used? |
|---|---|---|
| Babylon | Long, written records and repeating cycles (such as the 18-year eclipse cycle) | Yes: data first, then patterns |
| Greece | The Earth is a sphere; geometry can measure the sky | Yes: Lesson 1.3 |
| Aristarchus | The Sun, being bigger, should be the centre | Yes |
| India (Aryabhata) | The daily turning of the sky is the Earth spinning | Yes |
| Islamic world | Large observatories, careful star catalogues, mathematical fixes to Ptolemy | Yes: observatories and new geometry |

<div class="history">

**The number zero and the decimal system** came from India, through Arabic scholars, to Europe. Without them the long calculations of later astronomers (Kepler spent years on Mars's orbit) would have been far harder. Cosmology owes a debt to arithmetic as well as to telescopes.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Medieval people thought the Earth was flat."** Educated people in Europe, the Islamic world and India knew the Earth was round from Greek times onwards. Eratosthenes even measured its size around 240 BCE, as the next lesson shows.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Ancient cosmologies were models built to fit what people saw: the turning stars, the yearly path of the Sun, the wandering planets.
- Babylon gave long records and cycles; Greece gave a round Earth and geometric models; Aristarchus suggested a Sun-centred system.
- Aryabhata (499 CE) taught that the Earth spins; Islamic astronomers built observatories and new geometry such as the Tusi couple.
- A model can predict well and still be physically wrong, which is why new tests matter.
