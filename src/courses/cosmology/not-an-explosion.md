**Module 4 · about 12 minutes.** Read first: [4.3 What expansion means](/learn/courses/cosmology/expanding-universe/). The name "Big Bang" sounds like a bomb. This lesson shows why that picture is wrong and gives you a better one. By the end you will be able to answer "where did the Big Bang happen?" correctly.

<div class="bigidea">An explosion throws things out from one centre into empty space. The Big Bang was different: all of space, everywhere, was hot and dense and began to stretch. There was no centre, and no empty room around it.</div>

## 1. Two pictures side by side

<div class="steps" data-fig="not-explosion">
<div class="step">An explosion. Debris flies out from one centre. There is a front edge, and empty space beyond it. Debris near the edge would see a lopsided sky: crowded on one side, empty on the other.</div>
<div class="step">Expansion. Galaxies fill all of space, past the edges of the frame too. Every gap grows by the same factor. No galaxy is the centre, and there is no edge to reach.</div>
<div class="step">The balloon picture. Paint dots on a balloon and blow it up: every dot moves away from every other. The dots live only on the rubber. The middle of the balloon is not on the surface, so the surface has no centre and no edge.</div>
<div class="step">Run the film backwards. The same patch of space gets more crowded and hotter, everywhere at once. Far back, the whole of space was hot and dense: not a tiny bomb inside a big dark room.</div>
</div>

## 2. So where was it?

Right where you are, and everywhere else. Every part of today's universe was part of the hot, dense early universe. If you could rewind, the atoms of your body would end up in that hot soup, but so would the atoms of a galaxy 10 billion light-years away. Neither place was the middle.

Our evidence agrees. The cosmic microwave background, light left over from the hot early universe, arrives with almost exactly the same temperature from every direction (Module 7). Debris near the edge of an explosion would see a hot side and an empty side.

## 3. The balloon, honestly

The balloon is a 2-D model of 3-D space. It is useful for one idea: dots on a stretching surface all move apart, with no centre on the surface. But two things are wrong with it:

- A real balloon has an inside and an outside. Our universe, as far as we know, has nothing outside it.
- Our universe may be infinite. A balloon is finite. A better model of an infinite universe is an endless sheet of rubber being stretched in all directions.

<div class="history">

**A name meant as a joke?** Fred Hoyle, who championed a rival "steady state" universe with no beginning, used the words "big bang" on BBC radio in 1949. He said later that he had only wanted a vivid phrase for listeners, not to mock the idea. The name stuck, and it has misled people ever since.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"If the universe was once smaller than an atom, it must have had a centre."** It is the *observable* universe, the part we can see today, that was once tiny. The whole universe may be infinite, and an infinite universe stays infinite as it expands, just less crowded. Either way, every place was part of the Big Bang.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- The Big Bang was not an explosion at a point: all of space was hot and dense and began to stretch.
- It happened everywhere; there is no centre and no edge.
- Evidence: the cosmic microwave background looks almost the same in every direction.
- The balloon model shows "no centre on the surface", but a real universe has no inside or outside and may be infinite.
- The theory describes the universe from very early on; what came before (if anything) is an open question.

</div>
