**Module 3 · about 13 minutes.** Read first: [1.5 Newton's universe](/learn/courses/cosmology/newtons-universe/). By the end you will be able to explain why the speed of light is special, how it was measured and tested, and why it forced Einstein to rethink space and time. To understand the expanding universe we need Einstein's ideas, and they start here.

<div class="bigidea">Everything else in nature can be caught up with. Light cannot: whoever you are and however you move, it always passes you at the same speed.</div>

## 1. Light takes time

For most of history people thought light arrived instantly. In 1676 the Danish astronomer **Ole Rømer** noticed that eclipses of Io, one of Jupiter's moons, came several minutes late when Jupiter was on the far side of its orbit from Earth. His explanation: the light had farther to travel. Light is fast, not instant.

Today the speed of light in empty space, called **c**, is fixed by definition at exactly **299,792,458 metres per second**. In fact the metre is defined from it: one metre is how far light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. Light could go round the Earth about 7½ times in one second.

## 2. Speeds usually add up

Throw a ball forward at 10 m/s from a train moving at 30 m/s, and someone standing by the track sees the ball go by at 40 m/s. That is everyday common sense, and Newton's physics agrees.

Now shine a torch forward from the train. Common sense says the person by the track should see the light at c + 30 m/s. **It does not.** They measure exactly c, the same as the passenger.

<div class="step">On a train going 30 m/s, a ball thrown forward at 10 m/s passes someone on the ground at 40 m/s. Speeds add.</div>
<div class="step">Light from a torch on the same train passes the ground at c, not c + 30 m/s. Light's speed does not add.</div>
<div class="step">In 1887 Michelson and Morley split a light beam into two arms at right angles and turned the apparatus every way. Earth moves round the Sun at 30 km/s, so one arm "should" have been slower. Both always matched.</div>
<div class="step">Einstein in 1905 took this as a law of nature. Speed is distance divided by time. If the speed of light is fixed for everyone, then distances and times must be what changes between observers.</div>

## 3. Why light, of all things?

In the 1860s James Clerk Maxwell found that electricity and magnetism together make waves, and worked out their speed from two numbers measured in the lab with magnets and charges. The answer matched the speed of light. **Light is an electromagnetic wave**, and Maxwell's equations give it one speed, with no mention of who is measuring.

Most physicists assumed light must travel through an invisible jelly, the "ether", and that c was its speed relative to the ether. Then Earth, moving through the ether, should feel an "ether wind". Michelson and Morley's experiment was built to measure that wind.

<div class="problem" data-problem="michelson-arm"></div>

They could compare the two arms to a small fraction of a wavelength of light. The ether wind should have shifted the pattern visibly. It never did.

## 4. Einstein's two rules

In 1905, at 26 and working in a patent office, Albert Einstein built a new physics on two simple rules:

1. The laws of physics are the same for everyone moving steadily (not speeding up or turning).
2. Everyone moving steadily measures the same speed of light, c.

```math
c = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>c</dt><dd>the speed of light (and of all electromagnetic waves) in empty space</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>c is not just the speed of light. It is the universe's speed limit for anything carrying matter, energy or information, and it links space and time: one second of time "is worth" 300,000 km of space.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

The ether was not needed. But the price was high: if c is the same for everyone, two observers moving past each other must disagree about lengths, times and even which events happen "at the same time". That is the next lesson.

<div class="myth">

**"Nothing in the universe can move apart faster than light."** Nothing can move *through space* faster than light. But in Module 4 you will see that space itself can stretch, and very distant galaxies are carried away from us faster than c. No rule is broken: nothing overtakes a light beam locally.

</div>

<div class="history">

**A light-speed cosmic clue.** Because light takes time, every look into space is a look into the past (Lesson 2.1). The finite speed of light is what lets cosmologists see the young universe directly.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Light takes time to travel: Rømer showed it in 1676 from Io's late eclipses.
- c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly; the metre is defined from it.
- Speeds of balls and trains add, but light's speed is the same for every steadily moving observer (Michelson and Morley, 1887).
- Einstein's two rules (1905): the same laws for everyone moving steadily, and the same c. The price: space and time are not the same for everyone.
