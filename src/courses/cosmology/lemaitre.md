**Module 4 · about 14 minutes.** Read first: [1.5 Newton's universe](/learn/courses/cosmology/newtons-universe/) and [3.5 Curved spacetime](/learn/courses/cosmology/curved-spacetime/). Here is the story of how Einstein's theory of gravity, at first, was made to describe a universe that stood still, and how a Belgian priest showed it should be moving. By the end you will be able to rewind the expansion to estimate when it began.

<div class="bigidea">Einstein's equations say a universe full of matter cannot stand still. Lemaître accepted what the equations said: the universe is expanding, so it was once far smaller, hotter and denser.</div>

## 1. Einstein's static universe (1917)

When Einstein applied general relativity to the whole universe in 1917, he hit Newton's old problem: gravity pulls everything together, so a still universe should collapse ([Lesson 1.5](/learn/courses/cosmology/newtons-universe/)). Like nearly everyone then, he believed the universe was eternal and unchanging. So he added a term, the **cosmological constant** Λ (lambda), a gentle push at large distances that exactly balanced gravity.

The balance was unstable, like a pencil standing on its point. Push it slightly and it would start to expand or collapse. Nobody noticed at first.

## 2. Universes that move

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>1917</b> Einstein: a static universe held up by Λ. Willem de Sitter finds another solution, empty but with strange redshifts.</li>
<li><b>1922</b> Alexander Friedmann, in Petrograd, shows the equations allow universes that expand or contract. Einstein first claims it is a mistake, then admits Friedmann is right but doubts it means anything physical.</li>
<li><b>1927</b> Georges Lemaître, a Belgian priest and physicist, finds expanding solutions independently and links them to Slipher's redshifts. He estimates the expansion rate from published speeds and distances.</li>
<li><b>1927</b> At a Solvay conference, Einstein tells Lemaître: "Your calculations are correct, but your physics is abominable."</li>
<li><b>1929</b> Hubble's plot shows speed rising with distance. Eddington, Lemaître's former teacher, spreads the word about his paper.</li>
<li><b>1931</b> Lemaître proposes the "primeval atom": the universe began from one very dense state.</li>
</ul>

## 3. The missing paragraph

Lemaître's 1927 paper, in French in a Belgian journal, contained what we now call Hubble's law, with a rate of roughly 600 km/s per megaparsec (close to Hubble's later 500, because the distances of the day had the same calibration problem). When it was translated into English in 1931, that paragraph was left out. For decades some suspected Hubble's supporters of hiding it. In 2011 the astrophysicist Mario Livio found Lemaître's letters: **Lemaître translated the paper himself** and dropped the old estimate because better data had appeared. He was not interested in claiming priority.

## 4. Rewinding the film

If galaxies are moving apart now, they were closer yesterday. Keep going back and everything crowds together: matter denser, light hotter. Lemaître, who knew some quantum physics, imagined the whole universe starting as a single "primeval atom" that broke apart, like a giant radioactive nucleus.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="rewind"><figcaption>Slide time back towards the start. Distances shrink by the scale factor a, matter gets denser by 1/a³, and the leftover light gets hotter, from 2.7 K today to thousands of degrees. The sizes come from today's standard model.</figcaption></figure>

How long ago did the rewind start? If each galaxy had always moved at today's speed, the time since we were all together is distance ÷ speed. Because speed is proportional to distance, the answer is the same for every galaxy.

<div class="history">

**Eddington spots the wobble.** In 1930 Arthur Eddington, Lemaître's former teacher in Cambridge, was puzzling over Hubble's results when Lemaître reminded him of the 1927 paper. Eddington saw at once that it solved the puzzle, and he also showed that Einstein's static universe could not last: the smallest nudge sets it expanding or collapsing. He arranged for the paper to appear in English in 1931.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Einstein called the cosmological constant his biggest blunder."** The quote comes from George Gamow's memories of a conversation, and historians are unsure Einstein ever said it. What is certain is that Einstein dropped Λ in 1931, once expansion was found.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Einstein (1917) added a cosmological constant to keep the universe static, but the balance was unstable.
- Friedmann (1922) and Lemaître (1927) showed his equations allow expanding universes.
- Lemaître linked expansion to galaxy redshifts and estimated the rate, two years before Hubble's plot.
- Rewinding the expansion leads to a hot, dense beginning: Lemaître's primeval atom (1931), the first Big Bang theory.
- Time since everything was together ≈ distance ÷ speed, the same for every galaxy: about 14 billion years.

</div>
