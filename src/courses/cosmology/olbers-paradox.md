**Module 1 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain why an endless, unchanging universe would have a bright night sky, and why the dark sky we actually see is evidence that the universe has an age.

<div class="bigidea">The darkness of the night sky is one of the most important observations in cosmology. You can make it yourself, tonight, with no telescope.</div>

## 1. The puzzle

Imagine standing in a huge forest. If the forest is small, you can see out between the trunks to the open fields. But if the forest goes on forever, every line of sight eventually hits a tree trunk, and all you see is bark in every direction.

Now replace the trees with stars. If the universe were **endless**, **unchanging** and **evenly filled with stars**, every line of sight would end on the surface of a star. The whole sky would be as bright as the surface of the Sun, day and night.

It isn't. That is **Olbers' paradox**, named after Heinrich Olbers who wrote about it in 1823, though Kepler and Halley had worried about it long before.

## 2. Why distance doesn't help

You might think distant stars are too faint to matter. Here's why they aren't. Cut the universe into thin shells around you, like the layers of an onion.

- A shell twice as far away has **4 times** the area, so it holds **4 times** as many stars.
- Each of those stars looks **1/4** as bright (light spreads out as 1/distance²; see [the inverse square law](/learn/courses/astronomy/flux-luminosity/) in the Astronomy course).
- So the whole shell sends us **exactly the same** light as the near one.

Every shell adds the same amount. Add up infinitely many shells and you get an infinitely bright sky (or, once nearer stars block farther ones, a sky as bright as a star's surface).

## 3. The answer: the universe has an age

The real answer was first guessed, remarkably, by the writer Edgar Allan Poe in 1848, and worked out properly by Lord Kelvin in 1901 and Edward Harrison in the 1960s-80s. Step through it with the forest of stars:

<div class="steps" data-fig="olbers-forest">
<div class="step"><p><b>Light needs time to travel.</b> If the universe has existed for a limited time, light can only have reached us from stars inside a certain distance: the dashed circle.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>A bigger circle</b> (an older universe) lets in more starlight. More sight lines end on a star.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Bigger still</b>, and the sky gets brighter. The trees of the forest start to fill in the gaps.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Forever:</b> if light had had forever to arrive, every sight line would end on a star. The sky would blaze.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Our universe:</b> stars are so far apart that you would need to see out to about 10<sup>23</sup> light-years before every line hit a star. Light has only had 13.8 billion years to travel. Most sight lines run out first, and the night is dark.</p></div>
</div>

You can also move the sliders under the picture at any step: one sets how far light has had time to travel, the other how tightly the stars are packed. The square on the right shows how bright your sky would be.

<div class="meaning">

**Two things keep the night dark.**
1. **A finite age** (the main reason): light from very far away hasn't arrived yet.
2. **Stars don't live forever, and the universe is expanding:** the light from distant galaxies is stretched to longer, weaker wavelengths (Lesson 4.5), and stars only shine for millions to billions of years.

Calculations by Harrison and others show the first effect matters most.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Dust blocks the light of distant stars."** Olbers himself suggested this. But dust that soaks up starlight for an endless time gets hot, and eventually glows as brightly as the stars it absorbs. Dust can only hide light for a while, so it can't explain a sky that has always been dark.

</div>

## 4. Why this matters

This simple observation rules out the universe Newton and most scientists believed in: endless, unchanging and full of stars forever. Something has to give. Either the universe has an age, or it is changing, or both. Modern cosmology says both: it began about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.

<div class="history">

**A night sky that does glow.** The sky is not perfectly dark. In every direction there is a faint glow of microwaves, the cooled light of the hot early universe, discovered in 1965 (Module 7). In a sense, if your eyes could see microwaves, Olbers' sky would be "bright": every line of sight ends on the hot gas of the young universe. It's just that the expansion has cooled that glow to 2.7 degrees above absolute zero.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- In an endless, unchanging universe full of stars, every sight line would end on a star, and the night sky would blaze.
- Distant shells don't fade away: they hold more stars in exact proportion to how much fainter each star is.
- The night is dark mainly because the universe has an age: light has only reached us from a limited distance.
- The expansion of the universe and the limited lives of stars help keep it dark too.

</div>
