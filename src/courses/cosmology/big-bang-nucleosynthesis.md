**Module 6 · about 17 minutes.** Read first: [6.4 The first second](/learn/courses/cosmology/first-second/) and [6.3 Hotter in the past](/learn/courses/cosmology/temperature-and-time/). We left the universe at a few seconds old, with about 1 neutron for every 6 protons and the neutrons slowly decaying. Now the universe becomes a nuclear reactor for about a quarter of an hour. By the end you will be able to work out the helium share yourself and explain how deuterium weighs all the atoms in the universe.

<div class="bigidea">For a few minutes the whole universe was a nuclear furnace. It turned almost every surviving neutron into helium, giving 25% helium by mass everywhere, and the traces it left behind measure how much ordinary matter there is.</div>

## 1. The bottleneck

Every nucleus starts with one step: a neutron and a proton stick together to make **deuterium** (D), heavy hydrogen, releasing 2.2 MeV. Deuterium is fragile: any photon with more than 2.2 MeV smashes it apart again. At 10<sup>10</sup> K the typical photon has only about 1 MeV, so you might expect deuterium to survive from then on. It does not, for a few more minutes.

The reason is the crowd. There are about **1.6 billion photons for every proton or neutron**. Even when only one photon in a billion is energetic enough, that is still more than one smasher for every deuterium nucleus. Deuterium survives only when the photons above 2.2 MeV become rarer than the nucleons, which takes kT down to about **0.08 MeV** (about 9 × 10<sup>8</sup> K), roughly **3 minutes** after the start. Everything waits for that moment: the **deuterium bottleneck**.

<div class="steps" data-fig="big-bang-nucleosynthesis-2">
<div class="step"><b>The bottleneck.</b> n + p → D happens all the time, but the swarm of photons breaks D apart at once. Meanwhile free neutrons keep decaying (Lesson 6.4).</div>
<div class="step"><b>About 3 minutes, kT ≈ 0.08 MeV.</b> Deuterium survives at last. It quickly grabs more particles: D + p → ³He, and D + D → T (tritium) + p or ³He + n.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Helium-4.</b> T + D → ⁴He + n and ³He + D → ⁴He + p. Helium-4 is extremely tightly bound, so nearly every neutron that still exists ends up inside it within minutes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Dead ends.</b> No stable nucleus has 5 or 8 particles, and the falling temperature makes it ever harder for charged nuclei to push past each other's repulsion. Only a trace of lithium-7 (from T + ⁴He) and beryllium-7 (from ³He + ⁴He, which later captures an electron and becomes lithium-7) gets through.</div>
<div class="step"><b>About 20 minutes: the furnace is out.</b> By about 3 × 10<sup>8</sup> K reactions have stopped. The result: about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium-4 by mass, plus a few deuterium and helium-3 nuclei per 100,000 hydrogen and a few lithium nuclei per 10 billion.</div>
</div>

## 2. Helium from one number

Because nearly every neutron ends up in helium-4, the helium share follows from counting. Take 2 neutrons and 14 protons (1 to 7). The 2 neutrons pair with 2 protons to make one helium nucleus of mass 4, out of a total mass of 16. Helium's share of the mass, called **Y**, is 4/16 = 25%. In general:

```math
Y_p \approx \frac{2\,(n/p)}{1 + n/p} \approx \frac{2 \times 1/7}{1 + 1/7} = 0.25
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Y<sub>p</sub></dt><dd>the primordial helium mass fraction: the share of the mass of ordinary matter in helium-4 straight after the Big Bang (the "p" means primordial)</dd>
<dt>n/p</dt><dd>neutrons per proton at the moment deuterium survives: about 1/7, after freezing at about 1/6 at one second and then decaying for a few minutes</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>The helium share is set by the weak force (which froze n/p), the neutron's lifetime and the expansion rate. A universe expanding faster (for example with extra kinds of neutrino) freezes earlier with more neutrons, and makes more helium.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

The detailed calculation gives Y<sub>p</sub> = 0.247. Astronomers measure helium in small, chemically young galaxies, whose gas has been barely touched by stars, and extrapolate to zero heavy elements: Y<sub>p</sub> = 0.245 ± 0.003. Stars have since added only a few percent more helium. The helium in a party balloon is mostly from the first minutes.

## 3. Deuterium weighs the atoms

How much deuterium survives depends on how crowded the nucleons were. With more nucleons per photon, deuterium meets other nuclei more often and is burned into helium more completely, so less is left. Cosmologists use **η**, the number of protons and neutrons per photon. The leftover deuterium falls steeply as η grows, roughly as η<sup>−1.6</sup>, which makes it a sensitive **baryometer**: a scale for ordinary ("baryonic") matter.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="big-bang-nucleosynthesis"><figcaption>The Schramm plot: what the Big Bang makes for each density of atoms (η, in units of 10<sup>−10</sup>). Top: helium-4 by mass, which barely changes. Bottom: deuterium, helium-3 and lithium-7 per hydrogen atom. Shaded bands are measurements; the grey line is the CMB's value. Deuterium and helium agree with the CMB; lithium-7 does not (Lesson 6.7). Curves are fits to detailed calculations.</figcaption></figure>

Deuterium is measured in clouds of nearly pristine gas lit from behind by distant quasars. The deuterium absorption line sits slightly beside hydrogen's (deuterium is twice as heavy, which shifts its lines). Ryan Cooke, Max Pettini and Charles Steidel found D/H = (2.53 ± 0.03) × 10<sup>−5</sup> in 2018. That means η ≈ 6.1 × 10<sup>−10</sup>: one proton or neutron for every 1.6 billion photons, and **ordinary matter is about 5% of the critical density**. The CMB measures the same number in a completely different way (Lesson 7.8). Two methods, one from 3 minutes and one from 380,000 years, agree to about a percent or two.

That 5% is the reason we need dark matter: the total matter is about 31% of the critical density, so most matter cannot be made of atoms (Module 8).

<div class="history">

**From a guess to a precision test.** After the CMB was found in 1965, Jim Peebles (1966) and then Robert Wagoner, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle (1967) calculated the light elements with a full network of nuclear reactions. In 1977 Gary Steigman, David Schramm and James Gunn used the helium share to argue there could be no more than about three or four kinds of light neutrino, because each extra kind would speed up the expansion and make more helium. In 1989 particle colliders at CERN and SLAC measured the decay of the Z boson and found exactly three. Since David Tytler, Scott Burles and colleagues measured deuterium in quasar absorption clouds in 1996 to 1998, Big Bang nucleosynthesis has been a precision test.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Most of the universe's helium was made in stars."** Stars do turn hydrogen into helium, but over 13.8 billion years they have added only a few percent to the helium share. Roughly nine-tenths of all helium is primordial, made in the first half-hour, which is why even the oldest stars are a quarter helium.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- The deuterium bottleneck: with 1.6 billion photons per nucleon, deuterium survives only below kT ≈ 0.08 MeV (about 9 × 10<sup>8</sup> K), about 3 minutes in.
- Then almost every neutron goes into helium-4: Y<sub>p</sub> ≈ 2(n/p)/(1 + n/p) ≈ 0.25 with n/p ≈ 1/7. Measured: 0.245 ± 0.003.
- No stable nuclei of mass 5 or 8: only traces of D, ³He and ⁷Li. The furnace is out by about 20 minutes.
- Leftover deuterium (D/H ≈ 2.5 × 10<sup>−5</sup>) weighs ordinary matter: η ≈ 6.1 × 10<sup>−10</sup>, about 5% of the critical density, matching the CMB.
- Helium also counts neutrino kinds: three, as particle colliders confirmed.

</div>
