**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 17 · about 65 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name the kinds of movement and muscle, describe the structure of a muscle fibre and sarcomere, explain the sliding filament theory step by step, count the bones of the skeleton, name the types of joint, and describe the common disorders of muscles and bones.

<div class="bigidea">Movement comes from a protein motor. In muscle, thin actin filaments are pulled past thick myosin filaments by tiny heads that grab, tilt and let go, using ATP, like a crowd pulling a rope hand over hand. Bones give the pull something to push against, and joints decide which way the bones can swing.</div>

## 1. Types of movement

All living things move, but not all movement is the same.

- **Amoeboid movement:** by false feet (**pseudopodia**), made by the flow of the cytoplasm with microfilaments of **actin and myosin**. Seen in *Amoeba*, and in our white blood cells (macrophages) as they crawl to a germ.
- **Ciliary movement:** by the beating of cilia. The cilia in the windpipe sweep dust and mucus out; the cilia in the female's oviduct help to push the ovum along.
- **Flagellar movement:** by a flagellum, as in a sperm cell, or the swimming of *Euglena*.
- **Muscular movement:** by contraction of muscles: limbs, jaws, tongue and the movement of food in the gut.

Moving the whole body from place to place is **locomotion** (walking, running, swimming, flying). Movement inside the body (blood flow, peristalsis) is movement without locomotion. All locomotion is movement, but not every movement is locomotion.

## 2. Muscle

A **muscle** is made of cells (fibres) that can shorten. About 40 to 50 per cent of an adult's body weight is muscle. Muscle has four main properties: **excitability** (reacts to a signal), **contractility** (shortens), **extensibility** (can stretch) and **elasticity** (returns to its shape). There are three kinds.

| Muscle | Where | Stripes (striated) | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Skeletal** | attached to bones | yes | voluntary |
| **Visceral (smooth)** | walls of the gut, vessels, bladder, uterus | no | involuntary |
| **Cardiac** | heart | yes, with branched cells and intercalated discs | involuntary |

Skeletal muscle is also called **striated** or **voluntary**: we decide when it moves. Visceral muscle works without our decision, through the autonomic nervous system. Cardiac muscle contracts all life long, beat after beat, and is also involuntary.

## 3. Structure of a skeletal muscle

A muscle such as the biceps is a bundle of bundles. The whole muscle is wrapped in a sheath of connective tissue called the **fascia**. Inside it are bundles called **fascicles**, and each fascicle is made of many muscle fibres. Each **muscle fibre** is a long, cylindrical, **multinucleate** cell. Its membrane is the **sarcolemma**, its cytoplasm is the **sarcoplasm**, and the endoplasmic reticulum is the **sarcoplasmic reticulum**, which stores **calcium ions (Ca²⁺)**.

A muscle fibre is full of thread-like **myofibrils**. Under the microscope a myofibril shows dark and light bands, which repeat along its length; that is why the muscle looks striped.

- **A bands** (anisotropic, dark): they hold the thick filaments of **myosin**.
- **I bands** (isotropic, light): they contain only the thin filaments of **actin**.
- A thin dark line, the **Z line**, runs in the middle of each I band and anchors the actin filaments.
- The **M line** is in the middle of the A band and holds the myosin filaments in place.
- The **H zone** is the lighter middle part of the A band, where there is only myosin (no actin).
- The part of a myofibril between **two successive Z lines** is the **sarcomere**, the **functional unit of contraction**.

### The contractile proteins

- **Actin** forms the **thin filaments**: two strands of beaded actin twisted like two strings of pearls. Along it lies a strand of **tropomyosin**, and at intervals a **troponin** complex. At rest the tropomyosin covers the sites on actin where myosin binds.
- **Myosin** forms the **thick filaments**. It is a bundle of many molecules; each has a **tail** and a **head** with a short arm. The heads, with their arms, are the **cross-bridges**. A head has a binding site for actin and an **ATPase** site that splits ATP.

## 4. Mechanism of muscle contraction: sliding filament theory

In 1954 Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke, and independently Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson, saw that during contraction the A band stays the same while the I band and the H zone shrink. They proposed the **sliding filament theory**: the thin filaments slide **inward** past the thick ones; neither shortens.

### What starts a contraction?

A muscle fibre contracts only when a **motor neuron** tells it to. The place where the neuron meets the fibre is the **neuromuscular junction (motor end plate)**. A motor neuron and all the fibres it controls form a **motor unit**.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-loco-trigger">
<div class="step">A motor neuron sends an action potential to the end of its axon.</div>
<div class="step">At the neuromuscular junction the neuron releases the neurotransmitter acetylcholine.</div>
<div class="step">Acetylcholine makes an action potential in the sarcolemma, which spreads into the fibre.</div>
<div class="step">The sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.</div>
<div class="step">Ca²⁺ binds troponin on the thin filaments; tropomyosin moves aside and uncovers the myosin-binding sites on actin.</div>
<div class="step">Myosin heads bind and pull: cross-bridges form and the sarcomere shortens, using ATP.</div>
<div class="step">When the signal stops, Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the reticulum; tropomyosin covers the sites again and the muscle relaxes.</div>
</div>

### The cross-bridge cycle

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-loco-xbridge">
<div class="step">A myosin head, "cocked" by the energy of ATP split into ADP and phosphate (Pi), waits near the thin filament.</div>
<div class="step">With the sites uncovered by Ca²⁺, the head binds actin and forms a cross-bridge.</div>
<div class="step">The head tilts: this power stroke pulls the actin filament toward the centre of the sarcomere. ADP and Pi are released.</div>
<div class="step">A new ATP molecule binds the head, which lets go of the actin.</div>
<div class="step">The ATP is split again, the head returns to its cocked position and the cycle can repeat.</div>
</div>

Each power stroke moves the filament only a few nanometres, but there are hundreds of heads, each repeating the cycle many times a second. The ATP in muscle runs out in seconds, so it is replaced from **creatine phosphate** and then by respiration. After death there is no ATP to release the heads, so the cross-bridges stay fixed and the body goes stiff: **rigor mortis**.

<div class="myth">

**“The filaments shorten when a muscle contracts.”** Neither the thick nor the thin filament changes in length. They slide past each other, and the sarcomere gets shorter because the overlap grows.

</div>

### Types of skeletal muscle fibre

Some fibres are **red** (**slow-twitch**): they have lots of **myoglobin** (an oxygen store) and many mitochondria, work for a long time by aerobic respiration and tire slowly, as in the muscles for standing and long-distance running. **White** (**fast-twitch**) fibres have little myoglobin and fewer mitochondria, and depend more on anaerobic respiration. They contract fast and tire quickly, as in a sprinter's muscles. Strained for long without enough oxygen, a muscle makes **lactic acid** and tires; that causes the cramp after a hard run. Rest and oxygen remove it.

## 5. Skeletal system

The skeletal system has **bones and cartilage**. An adult human skeleton has **206 bones** (a child has more, since some fuse). It is divided into the **axial** skeleton (80 bones) along the long axis of the body, and the **appendicular** skeleton (126 bones) that includes the limbs and girdles.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-loco-skeleton"><figcaption>Each dot is one bone. Choose a group to light it up: skull 29, vertebral column 26, ribs and sternum 25, girdles 6, limbs 120 bones. The axial group adds up to 80 and the appendicular group to 126.</figcaption></figure>

### Axial skeleton (80)

- **Skull (29):** the **cranium** with 8 bones protects the brain; the **face** has 14 bones; the **hyoid** (1) is a U-shaped bone in the neck; each middle ear has 3 tiny **ear ossicles** (malleus, incus, stapes), 6 in all. The skull joins the vertebral column through **two occipital condyles** (a dicondylic skull).
- **Vertebral column (26):** the backbone, made of 26 vertebrae: **7 cervical** (the first is the **atlas**), **12 thoracic**, **5 lumbar**, **1 sacral** (5 fused) and **1 coccygeal** (4 fused, the coccyx). It protects the spinal cord, supports the head and carries the trunk. Between the vertebrae are discs of cartilage.
- **Sternum (1)** is the flat breastbone in the middle of the chest.
- **Ribs (12 pairs = 24):** all join the thoracic vertebrae at the back. The first **7 pairs** are **true ribs**, joined to the sternum by hyaline cartilage. The next **3 pairs (8th, 9th, 10th)** are **false** (vertebrochondral) ribs, joined to the 7th rib's cartilage. The last **2 pairs (11th and 12th)** are **floating** ribs with no front attachment. Ribs, vertebrae and sternum form the **rib cage**, which protects the heart and lungs.

### Appendicular skeleton (126)

- **Each limb has 30 bones.** The **forelimb**: humerus (upper arm), radius and ulna (forearm), 8 carpals (wrist), 5 metacarpals (palm) and 14 phalanges (fingers). The **hindlimb**: femur (thigh, the longest bone), tibia and fibula (leg), patella (kneecap), 7 tarsals (ankle), 5 metatarsals and 14 phalanges (toes).
- **Pectoral girdle** (shoulder): a **clavicle** (collar bone) and a **scapula** (shoulder blade) on each side. It has a socket, the **glenoid cavity**, for the head of the humerus.
- **Pelvic girdle** (hip): two **hip bones**, each made of three fused bones (ilium, ischium, pubis). The socket for the femur is the **acetabulum**. The two halves meet in front at the **pubic symphysis**.

## 6. Joints

A **joint** is the place where two bones or a bone and cartilage meet. Without joints you could not bend or turn. By the material between the bones, there are three types.

1. **Fibrous joints:** the bones are fused by dense fibrous tissue and **do not move**, as in the **sutures** between the skull bones.
2. **Cartilaginous joints:** the bones are joined by cartilage and move **only a little**, as between the **vertebrae** and at the pubic symphysis.
3. **Synovial joints:** a fluid-filled space, the **synovial cavity**, lies between the bones, which allows a lot of movement. These joints are the ones that make locomotion possible.

The synovial joints are named by the kind of movement they allow.

## 7. Disorders of the muscular and skeletal system

- **Myasthenia gravis.** An autoimmune disorder that affects the neuromuscular junction. The body's own antibodies attack the acetylcholine receptors, so the signal from nerve to muscle is weak, and the person gets tired and weak, with drooping eyelids.
- **Muscular dystrophy.** A genetic disease that progressively destroys skeletal muscle.
- **Tetany.** Rapid, uncontrolled muscle spasms, caused by **low Ca²⁺ in the body fluids**.
- **Arthritis.** Inflammation of the joints.
- **Osteoporosis.** Bone mass falls and the bones turn fragile and break easily. It is more common in older age, especially in women after menopause because of the fall in **oestrogen**. Calcium, vitamin D and weight-bearing exercise help to prevent it.
- **Gout.** Uric acid crystals collect in a joint (often the big toe) and cause painful inflammation.

**Beyond the syllabus.** Bones also act as **levers**, with the joint as the fulcrum and the muscle supplying the effort. The forearm lifting a load is a third-class lever: the effort (biceps) lies between the fulcrum (elbow) and the load (hand). A long lever moves the load fast but needs more force.

<div class="keypoints">

- Movement types: amoeboid, ciliary, flagellar and muscular. Muscles: skeletal (striated, voluntary), visceral (smooth, involuntary), cardiac (striated, involuntary).
- Sarcomere = Z line to Z line. Actin (thin, with tropomyosin and troponin) and myosin (thick, with heads). A band = myosin, constant length; I band and H zone shorten.
- Sliding filament theory: Ca²⁺ binds troponin, tropomyosin uncovers actin, myosin heads bind, tilt (power stroke) and release with ATP.
- Red fibres: myoglobin, aerobic, slow; white fibres: anaerobic, fast. Lactic acid causes fatigue.
- 206 bones: axial 80 (skull 29, vertebrae 26, sternum 1, ribs 24), appendicular 126 (limbs 30 each, pectoral girdle 4, pelvic girdle 2).
- Joints: fibrous (fixed), cartilaginous (slightly movable), synovial (freely movable: ball and socket, hinge, pivot, gliding, saddle).
- Disorders: myasthenia gravis, muscular dystrophy, tetany, arthritis, osteoporosis, gout.

</div>
