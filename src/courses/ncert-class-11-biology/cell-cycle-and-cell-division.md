**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 10 · about 80 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the phases of the cell cycle, name and draw each stage of mitosis and meiosis, give the chromosome and DNA numbers at every stage, and explain why mitosis keeps the body going and meiosis creates variety.

<div class="bigidea">Every new cell comes from an old one. Before dividing a cell copies all its DNA, then shares the two copies equally. Mitosis does this to make two identical cells for growth and repair. Meiosis does it twice with only one copy, to make four different cells with half the chromosomes: eggs and sperm. Keep count of chromosomes (n) and DNA (C) at each step and nothing is confusing.</div>

## 1. The cell cycle

The sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its genome, makes other parts and divides is the **cell cycle**. A human cell in culture takes about **24 hours**, a yeast cell about 90 minutes; some cells, such as the *Allium* root tip, take 12 hours. The cycle has two main parts.

- **Interphase**, the long "resting" phase (really a busy one), is about 95 % of the time. It has three parts.
  - **G1 (gap 1):** the cell grows, makes proteins and organelles and is active in metabolism. DNA is not copied. A cell that leaves the cycle here and does not divide is in **G0** (quiescent): nerve and heart muscle cells are in G0 for life.
  - **S (synthesis):** the DNA is **replicated**, so the DNA content doubles from **2C to 4C**. The number of chromosomes does **not** change (2n), because the two new copies stay joined at the centromere as **sister chromatids**. In animal cells the centriole is also duplicated in the cytoplasm.
  - **G2 (gap 2):** the cell makes proteins for the spindle and gets ready to divide.
- **M phase (mitosis phase):** the nucleus divides (karyokinesis, mitosis) and then the cytoplasm divides (cytokinesis). It is only about 1 hour in a 24-hour cycle.

### Checkpoints and control

Special **checkpoints** stop a cell from moving on until it is safe. The **G1/S checkpoint** looks for DNA damage (the protein p53 stops the cell for repair), the **G2/M checkpoint** checks that all DNA is copied, and the **spindle checkpoint** in metaphase makes sure that every chromosome is attached to the spindle. Proteins called **cyclins** rise and fall during the cycle and switch on enzymes called **CDKs** (cyclin-dependent kinases) that push the cell forward. A cell that has lost its checkpoints (for instance by a mutation in p53) can divide without control: that is **cancer**.

## 2. Mitosis (equational division)

In **mitosis** one parent cell makes two daughter cells with exactly the same number of chromosomes (and the same genes) as the parent. It is called **equational division**. It happens in body (somatic) cells, in the root and shoot tips of plants, and also in haploid and diploid cells that make spores. The stages follow one another smoothly; we divide them for the study.

<div class="steps" data-fig="@bio-mitosis-steps">
<div class="step"><b>Prophase.</b> Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes, each of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The nucleolus fades. The spindle starts to form; in animals the two centrosomes move to opposite poles.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Metaphase.</b> The nuclear envelope has broken down. Spindle fibres attach to the kinetochore of each chromosome and line them up on the equator (metaphase plate). This is the best stage to count and photograph chromosomes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Anaphase.</b> The centromeres divide and the sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles by shortening spindle fibres. Each chromatid is now a chromosome, so the number of chromosomes doubles for a moment.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Telophase.</b> Chromosomes reach the poles and unwind; the nuclear envelope and nucleolus re-form: two nuclei.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Cytokinesis.</b> The cytoplasm divides: two identical cells, each with the full 2n.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“DNA doubles in prophase, when the chromosomes first appear.”** The DNA was copied earlier, in S phase. Prophase only condenses the already copied DNA into visible chromosomes. And "anaphase doubles the chromosomes" counts each separated chromatid as a chromosome, while the DNA per cell is unchanged.

### Cytokinesis

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-cycle-cytokinesis"><figcaption>Animal cells pinch in from the outside with a furrow; plant cells build a new wall (cell plate) from the middle outwards. Press Play.</figcaption></figure>

Sometimes the nucleus divides without cytokinesis. The result is a **syncytium**: a multinucleate cell (the liquid endosperm of the coconut). In animals and plants the furrow or cell plate also gives each daughter some of the organelles.

**Significance of mitosis:** growth of the whole organism, **cell repair** and replacement (skin, blood), keeping the **chromosome number constant**, and **asexual reproduction** in single-celled organisms. In plants the meristems divide by mitosis all through life.

## 3. Meiosis (reductional division)

**Meiosis** is the special cell division of the cells that make gametes (or spores). The chromosome number is **halved** (2n to n), so that when the egg and sperm fuse the 2n number is restored. It has **two divisions** (meiosis I and meiosis II) but only **one** DNA replication, so it yields **four haploid cells**. It is long: prophase I may last days, months or, in the human egg, years.

<div class="steps" data-fig="@bio-meiosis-steps">
<div class="step"><b>Prophase I.</b> Homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis) into bivalents.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Crossing over.</b> Non-sister chromatids swap pieces at chiasmata.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Metaphase I.</b> The pairs line up at the equator, with each pair facing the poles at random.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Anaphase I and telophase I.</b> Homologues (not sister chromatids) separate: n chromosomes per cell, each still with two chromatids.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Meiosis II.</b> Like mitosis in each cell: the centromeres divide and sister chromatids separate.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Four haploid cells.</b> Each is n and different from the others.</div>
</div>

### Prophase I in five parts

Prophase I is the longest stage and the most important. Follow it:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-cycle-prophase1">
<div class="step"><b>Leptotene.</b> The chromosomes condense into long thin threads.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Zygotene.</b> Homologues pair up (synapsis), held by the synaptonemal complex: bivalents (tetrads).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pachytene.</b> Crossing over: non-sister chromatids swap pieces at recombination nodules.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Diplotene.</b> The synaptonemal complex dissolves; homologues separate but remain joined at chiasmata.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Diakinesis.</b> Chiasmata slide to the ends (terminalisation), the envelope and nucleolus vanish and the spindle begins to form.</div>
</div>

**Metaphase I:** the bivalents are on the equator, each homologue joined to the spindle from one pole. **Anaphase I:** the homologues move to opposite poles, while the sister chromatids stay together (this is the difference from mitosis). **Telophase I:** two cells, each with n chromosomes (but 2 chromatids each); **cytokinesis** is called **dyad of cells**. **Interkinesis** is the short gap before meiosis II, with **no DNA replication**.

**Meiosis II** is like mitosis: prophase II, metaphase II (chromosomes on the equator), anaphase II (sister chromatids part), telophase II, and four **haploid** cells (a tetrad of cells).

### Mitosis and meiosis compared

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Where | body cells | germ cells making gametes or spores |
| Divisions | 1 | 2 |
| Daughter cells | 2, diploid (2n) | 4, haploid (n) |
| Pairing of homologues | none | yes, with crossing over |
| Genetic result | identical to the parent | different from each other and from the parent |
| Purpose | growth, repair, asexual reproduction | making gametes, keeping the chromosome number, variation |

**Significance of meiosis:** it keeps the chromosome number the same from one generation to the next, creates **variation** (independent assortment and crossing over) that is the raw material for evolution, and it makes gametes. Errors, such as chromosomes failing to separate (**non-disjunction**), cause conditions such as Down syndrome (an extra chromosome 21), covered in the chapters on genetics.

<div class="keypoints">

- Cell cycle: interphase (G1, S, G2) is about 95 %, M phase about 5 %. DNA doubles in S (2C to 4C); chromosome number stays 2n. G0 is a resting state.
- Checkpoints (G1/S, G2/M, spindle) stop the cycle; cyclins and CDKs drive it; failure of control can lead to cancer.
- Mitosis: prophase, metaphase, anaphase (centromeres divide), telophase, cytokinesis: 2 identical diploid cells.
- Animal cytokinesis: furrow; plant: cell plate.
- Meiosis: two divisions, one replication, 4 haploid cells. Meiosis I separates homologues (reductional), meiosis II separates sister chromatids.
- Prophase I: leptotene, zygotene, pachytene (crossing over), diplotene (chiasmata), diakinesis.
- Variation: independent assortment (2ⁿ) and crossing over.

</div>
