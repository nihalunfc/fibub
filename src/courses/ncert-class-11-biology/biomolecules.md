**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 9 · about 85 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name the main biomolecules and their parts, draw a peptide bond, explain the four levels of protein structure, describe carbohydrates, lipids and nucleic acids, and explain how enzymes work and what changes their rate.

<div class="bigidea">A living body is made of a few families of molecules: small ones like sugars, amino acids and fatty acids, and big ones built from them: proteins, polysaccharides, lipids and nucleic acids. Their shapes decide their jobs. Enzymes, which are proteins, speed up every reaction in the cell; they do it by shape, and anything that spoils the shape (heat, wrong pH, a poison) spoils the enzyme.</div>

## 1. What are we made of?

If you burn a piece of tissue, the ash is the minerals; what burns away is carbon compounds. Chemical analysis of living tissue shows carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen as the main elements, with smaller amounts of calcium, phosphorus, sulphur and others. By weight, **water** is about 70 to 90 % of the cell, **proteins** 10 to 15 %, **carbohydrates** about 3 %, **lipids** about 2 %, **nucleic acids** 5 to 7 % and a little minerals.

Compounds from living tissue are **biomolecules**. Those that make up the structure and the main reactions are **primary metabolites** (amino acids, sugars, nucleotides). The others, such as alkaloids (morphine, quinine), rubber, essential oils, antibiotics, pigments, drugs and toxins, are **secondary metabolites**; they have no known role in growth but help the organism in other ways, for example in defence, and many are useful to humans.

**Macromolecules** (biomacromolecules) have a molecular mass above 1000 Da: proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides. Lipids are not true polymers, but they are put in the acid-insoluble fraction because they are in membranes broken into vesicles.

## 2. Amino acids and proteins

**Amino acids** are the building blocks of proteins. Each has a central carbon (α-carbon) bound to an amino group (-NH₂), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen and a variable **R group** (side chain). There are **20 common amino acids** in proteins. The R group can be a hydrogen (glycine), a methyl (alanine) or a hydroxymethyl (serine), and so on. Amino acids can be acidic (glutamic acid), basic (lysine), neutral, aromatic (tyrosine) and so on. At a certain pH an amino acid has both charges and is called a **zwitterion**. **Essential** amino acids cannot be made by our body and must come from the food (lysine, tryptophan...); **non-essential** ones can be made.

A **protein** is a **polypeptide** (or several) folded into a definite shape. Its structure has four levels:

- **Primary:** the sequence of amino acids, like letters in a word (the left end is the N-terminus, the right end is the C-terminus). One change can spoil the protein (sickle-cell anaemia: one amino acid swapped in haemoglobin).
- **Secondary:** local coils (the right-handed **α-helix**) and zig-zag sheets (**β-pleated sheet**), held by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms.
- **Tertiary:** the whole chain folded into a compact 3D shape (globular, like myoglobin), held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulphide bridges and water-avoiding (hydrophobic) cores. The shape makes the active site.
- **Quaternary:** two or more chains joined together, such as haemoglobin (4 chains, 2α + 2β).

**What proteins do:** enzymes (trypsin), structure (collagen, keratin), transport (haemoglobin), movement (actin, myosin), defence (antibodies), hormones (insulin), receptors, storage (casein in milk), and signalling. Collagen is the most abundant protein in the animal world and **RuBisCO** the most abundant in the whole biosphere.

## 3. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are made of C, H and O, often in the ratio of `$C_n(H_2O)_n$`. They are sugars and their polymers.

- **Monosaccharides** (single sugars; one unit): glucose (blood sugar, the cell's main fuel), fructose (fruit sugar), galactose, ribose and deoxyribose (in RNA and DNA).
- **Disaccharides** (two units) join by a **glycosidic bond** (with loss of water): sucrose = glucose + fructose (table sugar); maltose = glucose + glucose; lactose = glucose + galactose (milk sugar).
- **Polysaccharides** (many units): **starch** (the store in plants, a coil of α-glucose, with iodine it turns blue-black), **glycogen** (animal store, more branched; "animal starch" in liver and muscle), **cellulose** (plant wall: straight chains of β-glucose that bundle into fibres; we cannot digest it), **chitin** (a modified sugar with nitrogen: the skeleton of insects and walls of fungi) and **inulin**.

<div class="myth">

**“Starch and cellulose are the same because both are glucose chains.”** The glucose units are joined in a different way (α-1,4 in starch, which coils and can be digested by us; β-1,4 in cellulose, which stays straight and makes strong fibres that we cannot digest). The shape of the bond changes the job.

</div>

## 4. Lipids

Lipids do not dissolve in water. **Fats and oils** are **triglycerides**: glycerol joined to three fatty acids by ester bonds. A fatty acid is a long chain of carbons with a -COOH group at the end. **Saturated** fatty acids have only single bonds (straight chains; solid fats such as ghee and butter), and **unsaturated** ones have one or more double bonds (kinked, liquid oils such as groundnut and sunflower).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-biomol-lipid"><figcaption>Slide the number of double bonds in an 18-carbon fatty acid. Each cis double bond puts a kink in the chain: the chains pack less tightly, and the melting point falls from 69.6 °C (stearic) to below room temperature.</figcaption></figure>

Other lipids: **phospholipids** (glycerol, two fatty acids and a phosphate head; the base of the cell membrane), **steroids** (cholesterol, hormones), **waxes** (a waterproof coat) and **glycolipids**. Fats store about twice as much energy as the same weight of carbohydrate (about 9 kcal against 4 kcal per gram).

## 5. Nucleic acids

Nucleic acids, **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) and **RNA** (ribonucleic acid), carry and use the genetic instructions. Their monomer is a **nucleotide**: a **sugar** (deoxyribose or ribose, 5 carbons) + a **phosphate** + a **nitrogenous base**. The sugar and base alone are called a **nucleoside**. The bases are **purines** (adenine A, guanine G; double ring) and **pyrimidines** (cytosine C, thymine T in DNA, uracil U in RNA; single ring). Nucleotides join into a chain by **phosphodiester bonds** between the phosphate of one and the sugar of the next, giving a sugar-phosphate backbone with a 5' end and a 3' end.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-biomol-dna"><figcaption>Switch between DNA and RNA, and between sequences. A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds) and G with C (3 bonds), so G-C-rich DNA melts at a higher temperature. In RNA, U takes the place of T.</figcaption></figure>

**Watson and Crick (1953)** proposed that DNA is a **double helix** of two antiparallel strands held by base pairing, 10 base pairs per turn, 3.4 nm per turn (0.34 nm between pairs). **Chargaff's rule**: A = T and G = C in any double-stranded DNA, so the purines equal the pyrimidines.

## 6. Metabolism: the dynamic state of the body

The molecules of the body are always being made and broken down. The sum of all these chemical reactions is **metabolism**. **Anabolic** pathways build (making a protein from amino acids, using energy); **catabolic** pathways break down (glucose to CO₂ and water, releasing energy). Living things are in a **steady state**, away from equilibrium, which needs a constant flow of energy (from ATP, the energy currency). When a cell reaches equilibrium it is dead. All the reactions are catalysed by enzymes.

## 7. Enzymes

**Enzymes** are biological catalysts, almost all of them proteins (a few RNA molecules, the **ribozymes**, also work). A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. Enzymes act on a **substrate** to give **products**; each has a groove, the **active site**, of a special shape. They are very specific: sucrase works only on sucrose.

### How an enzyme lowers the energy hill

In every reaction the substrate must pass through a high-energy **transition state**; the energy needed to climb it is the **activation energy**. An enzyme gives an easier path, lowering the hill. It does not change the energy of the substrate or of the products, nor the end balance of the reaction.

### Factors that change enzyme activity

**Temperature and pH.** Each enzyme has an optimum temperature and pH. Raise the temperature and molecules collide more, so the rate goes up; but beyond the optimum the protein unfolds (**denatures**) and the activity is lost. Cold only slows the enzyme, which recovers on warming. Enzymes from hot-spring bacteria (Taq polymerase) work at 72 °C; human enzymes are best at 37 °C. A pH far from the optimum changes the charges on the active site and its shape.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-biomol-envfactors"><figcaption>Pick an enzyme and slide the temperature and pH. See how the activity falls away on either side of the optimum, and fast above it (heat).</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c11b-biomol-q10"></div>

**Substrate concentration.** At low substrate the rate rises in proportion; at high substrate all the active sites are busy and the rate levels off at **Vmax**. The curve follows the Michaelis-Menten equation:

```math
v = \frac{V_{max}\,[S]}{K_m + [S]}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>v</dt><dd>rate of the reaction at a given substrate concentration</dd>
<dt>Vmax</dt><dd>highest rate, when every enzyme molecule is working all the time</dd>
<dt>[S]</dt><dd>substrate concentration</dd>
<dt>Km</dt><dd>the [S] at which v = Vmax/2 (the Michaelis constant). A small Km means the enzyme reaches half-speed at a very low [S]: it holds its substrate tightly.</dd>
<dt>Meaning</dt><dd>Think of a tea stall with one counter: when customers are few, the number served rises with the customers; when there is a long queue, the stall serves at its fastest speed and more customers do not speed it up.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

**Inhibitors.** A **competitive inhibitor** looks like the substrate and blocks the active site; it can be overcome with more substrate (succinate dehydrogenase and malonate; drugs such as statins are of this kind; sulpha drugs against bacteria). A **non-competitive inhibitor** binds elsewhere and changes the shape of the enzyme (cyanide on cytochrome oxidase). A product can feed back and block the first enzyme of its own pathway (**feedback inhibition**).

### Classification and cofactors

Enzymes are named by the reaction and put in six classes: **oxidoreductases** (electron or hydrogen transfer, e.g. dehydrogenases), **transferases**, **hydrolases** (break bonds with water, e.g. amylase), **lyases** (remove groups without water), **isomerases** and **ligases** (join molecules, using ATP).

Many enzymes need a non-protein partner, the **cofactor**. The protein part alone is the **apoenzyme**; with the cofactor it is the active **holoenzyme**. Cofactors are of three kinds: **prosthetic groups** (tightly bound, e.g. haem in catalase), **coenzymes** (loosely bound organic molecules, often made from vitamins: NAD, NADP from niacin) and **metal ions** (zinc in carbonic anhydrase, magnesium in many enzymes). Removing the cofactor kills the activity.

<div class="keypoints">

- Cells have water, proteins, carbohydrates, lipids and nucleic acids. Primary metabolites build the body; secondary metabolites (alkaloids, rubber, antibiotics) help in other ways.
- Amino acid: α-carbon with -NH₂, -COOH, H and R. Peptide bond: C-N, loses water; n amino acids make n - 1 bonds.
- Protein structure: primary (sequence), secondary (α-helix, β-sheet), tertiary (3D fold), quaternary (several chains).
- Carbohydrates: monosaccharides, disaccharides (glycosidic bond), polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin).
- Lipids: triglycerides, phospholipids, steroids; saturated fats are straight and solid, unsaturated are kinked and liquid.
- Nucleotide = sugar + phosphate + base; A pairs with T (U in RNA) and G with C; double helix antiparallel.
- Enzymes lower activation energy; they are specific; they have an optimum temperature and pH.
- v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]); Km is the [S] at half of Vmax. Competitive inhibitors raise Km, non-competitive lower Vmax.
- Cofactors: prosthetic groups, coenzymes, metal ions. Six classes of enzymes.

</div>
