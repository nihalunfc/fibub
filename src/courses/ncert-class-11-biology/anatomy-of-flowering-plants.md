**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 6 · about 75 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name and tell apart the plant tissues, describe the three tissue systems, draw and recognise dicot and monocot roots, stems and leaves, and explain how a tree grows thicker and makes annual rings and bark.

<div class="bigidea">A plant is built from a few kinds of cells, each shaped by its job. Dividing cells (meristems) make new cells, which become packing, support, a water pipe or a food pipe. Roots, stems and leaves are the same three tissue systems arranged in different patterns. Look at the pattern in a thin slice and you can tell the plant and the organ.</div>

## 1. Tissues

A **tissue** is a group of cells with a common origin that work together. Plant tissues are of two kinds: **meristematic** (dividing) and **permanent** (finished, not dividing).

### Meristematic tissue

Growth in plants happens only at certain places, the **meristems**. Their cells are small, round or cubical, thin-walled, packed tightly with a big nucleus and dense cytoplasm and almost no vacuole.

- **Apical meristems** sit at the tips of roots and shoots and make them longer (primary growth).
- **Intercalary meristems** are at the base of leaves or internodes (grasses regrow after the lawn is mown because of them).
- **Lateral meristems** are the vascular cambium and the cork cambium. They make the stem or root thicker (secondary growth).

### Permanent tissues

Cells that come from meristems and stop dividing become permanent tissues. **Simple** tissues have one kind of cell; **complex** tissues have more than one kind working together.

| Tissue | Cells | Wall | Living? | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parenchyma | round, with air spaces | thin, cellulose | yes | storage, photosynthesis (chlorenchyma), healing |
| Collenchyma | oval or angular | thick at the corners (cellulose, pectin) | yes | flexible support in young stems and petioles |
| Sclerenchyma | long fibres or short stone cells | very thick, lignin | no | strength (jute, coconut husk, gritty guava pulp) |

**Xylem** (complex) carries water and minerals upwards. It has **tracheids** (long, tapering), **vessels** (wide tubes made of cells joined end to end, with the end walls dissolved), **xylem fibres** (strength) and **xylem parenchyma** (the only living part, stores food). Gymnosperms have no vessels. In the first xylem to form (**protoxylem**) the cells are narrow; the later **metaxylem** is wider. In roots, protoxylem lies outside, and the arrangement is called **exarch**; in stems it lies towards the centre (**endarch**).

**Phloem** (complex) carries food, mostly sucrose. It has **sieve tube elements** (living but without a nucleus, joined end to end with perforated **sieve plates**), **companion cells** (living, with a nucleus; they run the sieve tube next to them), **phloem parenchyma** and **phloem fibres** (the only dead part; jute fibre comes from here). Gymnosperms have sieve cells and albuminous cells instead.

<div class="myth">

**“Xylem is dead, phloem is living, so every part follows that rule.”** Xylem parenchyma is living, and phloem fibres are dead. The rule holds for the conducting cells: mature xylem vessels and tracheids are dead hollow tubes, sieve tubes are alive.

</div>

## 2. The tissue system

All the tissues of a plant can be grouped into three systems that run through every organ.

- **Epidermal system:** the outer skin. The **epidermis** is one layer of cells with a waxy **cuticle** (thick in dry-climate plants). It has **stomata**: two bean-shaped **guard cells** (dumb-bell shaped in grasses) round a pore, each guard cell rich in chloroplasts. Pairs of neighbouring **subsidiary cells** may help. **Root hairs** are extensions of root epidermal cells and absorb water. **Trichomes** on the stem are hairs that cut water loss.
- **Ground tissue system:** everything that is not epidermis or vascular: the cortex, pericycle, pith and medullary rays in stems and roots, and the **mesophyll** of leaves.
- **Vascular tissue system:** the xylem and the phloem (with cambium in dicots), arranged in **vascular bundles**.

Vascular bundles come in three arrangements. Switch between them below.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-anat-bundle"><figcaption>Radial (roots), conjoint open and conjoint closed (stems) and the two concentric kinds. In a conjoint bundle the xylem faces the centre and the phloem faces the skin.</figcaption></figure>

### Stomata at work

A stoma opens when its guard cells fill with water and bend apart. Potassium ions rush in (in light), water follows by osmosis, the cell swells and the thick inner wall pulls it into a curve. In the dark or in drought the water leaves and the pore shuts.

## 3. Anatomy of dicot and monocot plants

### The dicot root

From outside in, a young dicot root has: the **epidermis** (with root hairs), the **cortex** (several layers of thin-walled parenchyma with air spaces), the **endodermis** (one layer of barrel-shaped cells with a waterproof **Casparian strip** of suberin on their sides that forces water to pass through the cells and be filtered), the **pericycle** (thin-walled; side roots and part of the cambium arise from it), then the **vascular bundles** (radial: 2 to 4 patches of xylem, alternate patches of phloem), a small **pith** or none, and **conjunctive tissue** between xylem and phloem. A **monocot root** looks like a dicot root but has more than 6 xylem arms (polyarch), a large pith, and no secondary growth.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-anat-root"><figcaption>Slide the number of xylem arms. 2 is diarch, 3 triarch, 4 tetrarch and so on. Dicot roots keep to 2 to 4; monocot roots go past 6 and have a big pith.</figcaption></figure>

### The dicot stem and the monocot stem

A young **dicot stem** has an epidermis with trichomes, a **hypodermis** of a few layers of collenchyma, a cortex, an endodermis (the starch sheath), the pericycle and a **ring** of **open** vascular bundles (cambium between the xylem and the phloem), with **medullary rays** (parenchyma between bundles) and a large pith in the middle.

A **monocot stem** has a sclerenchyma hypodermis, ground tissue not divided into cortex and pith, and **many scattered closed** bundles, each wrapped in a sclerenchyma sheath. There is no cambium, so there is no secondary growth, and each bundle has a large **water-containing cavity** (the protoxylem lacuna).

### The leaf

A leaf section has three parts. The **epidermis** (upper and lower, with cuticle and stomata) wraps the **mesophyll** and the **veins**. In a **dicot (dorsiventral) leaf** the mesophyll is split into tall, tightly packed **palisade parenchyma** below the upper skin (most photosynthesis) and loose **spongy parenchyma** with air spaces below it (gas exchange); the lower epidermis has more stomata. In a **monocot (isobilateral) leaf** the mesophyll is the same on both sides, stomata are on both skins, and the upper skin has large **bulliform cells**: they lose water in dry air, shrink and let the leaf roll up to save water. The veins are enclosed in a **bundle sheath** of parenchyma; a dicot leaf has a network of veins (reticulate venation), a monocot leaf has parallel veins.

## 4. Secondary growth

Roots and stems of dicots and gymnosperms grow thicker by **secondary growth**, using the two lateral meristems. Monocots do not have it. Follow it step by step in a dicot stem.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-anat-secondary">
<div class="step"><b>Primary stem.</b> A ring of bundles; each has a strip of cambium between its xylem and phloem.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Complete cambial ring.</b> Medullary-ray cells between the bundles start dividing, joining the strips into a full ring.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Wood and bast.</b> The ring cuts off more cells inward (secondary xylem) than outward (secondary phloem), so wood piles up and pushes the old tissues out.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Annual rings.</b> Spring wood is light with wide vessels; autumn wood is dark with narrow cells. One pair is one annual ring.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Cork cambium and bark.</b> The skin splits. The cork cambium (phellogen) makes dead, waterproof cork (phellem) outward and phelloderm inward.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Heartwood and sapwood.</b> Old inner xylem is darkened by resins and tannins (heartwood, no longer conducting). The young outer wood is sapwood.</div>
</div>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c11b-anat-rings"></div>

- **Spring wood** (early wood) forms in the growing season with plenty of water: lighter, wider vessels. **Autumn wood** (late wood) forms when growth slows: darker, denser. Counting rings tells the age of a tree (**dendrochronology**); wide rings mean good rain.
- **Heartwood** is dark, hard, durable and resists attack by insects (teak, rosewood). **Sapwood** is pale and conducts water.
- **Bark** is every tissue outside the vascular cambium: periderm, cortex, and old phloem. **Early** bark falls in flakes or strips; **late** bark is the inner bark. Periderm = phellogen + phellem (cork) + phelloderm.
- **Lenticels** are small gaps in the cork that let air in for the living cells under the bark (the raised dots on a cherry or guava twig).
- Secondary growth in **roots** is similar, with the cambium starting from the cells just inside the phloem and the pericycle. In monocots a different, anomalous pattern (as in *Dracaena*) is beyond this chapter.

<div class="myth">

**“Annual rings show the tree's age everywhere.”** Only where seasons are clear (a cold winter or a dry season). Many tropical trees with no season make no clear rings, or more than one a year after a drought.

</div>

Hardwood or softwood? Teak and sal are **hardwoods** from dicots (with vessels); pine and deodar are **softwoods** from gymnosperms (only tracheids), which are often easier to saw.

<div class="keypoints">

- Meristems (apical, intercalary, lateral) make new cells; permanent tissues are simple (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma) or complex (xylem, phloem).
- Xylem: tracheids, vessels, fibres, parenchyma (only parenchyma living). Phloem: sieve tubes, companion cells, parenchyma, fibres.
- Three systems: epidermal (stomata, root hairs, trichomes), ground (cortex, pith, mesophyll), vascular (bundles).
- Roots: radial bundles, endodermis with Casparian strip; dicot 2 to 4 xylem arms, monocot more than 6 with a big pith.
- Dicot stem: bundles in a ring, open, big pith. Monocot stem: bundles scattered, closed, sheathed, no pith or cambium.
- Dicot leaf: palisade and spongy, stomata mostly below. Monocot leaf: same on both sides, bulliform cells.
- Secondary growth: vascular cambium (wood inside, phloem outside) and cork cambium (cork outside). Rings, heartwood, sapwood, lenticels.

</div>
