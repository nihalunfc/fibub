**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 13 · about 70 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe how plants grow, calculate growth rates, name the plant hormones and what each does, and explain how day length and cold decide when a plant flowers.

<div class="bigidea">A plant grows for its whole life, from tips of shoots and roots that keep dividing. Hormones, made in small amounts in one place and acting in another, tell the cells whether to divide, stretch, stop, flower or sleep. Light, temperature and water change the hormone signals, so the same seed grows into a different plant on a different day.</div>

## 1. Growth

**Growth** is a permanent increase in size, mass or cell number. In plants it is **indeterminate** (open): a tree keeps growing as long as it lives because it keeps **meristems**, cells that can divide. **Apical meristems** (at the tips of roots and shoots) make the plant longer; **lateral meristems** (the vascular cambium and cork cambium) make it thicker (secondary growth). In an animal, growth stops in the adult (it is determinate).

Plant growth has three phases, best seen in a root tip.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-growth-zones"><figcaption>Choose each zone in turn: cells divide near the tip, then stretch by taking in water, then specialise.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Meristematic phase:** small, thin-walled cells with dense cytoplasm divide repeatedly.
2. **Elongation phase:** cells stop dividing and increase in size by absorbing water, forming a big vacuole. New wall material is added.
3. **Maturation phase:** cells reach their final size and become specialised (xylem, phloem, root hairs).

### Growth rates

The increase in size per unit time is the **growth rate**. It can be measured as the increase in length, area, volume, weight or cell number. Roots and shoots can be measured with an **auxanometer**, a lever whose pointer magnifies the tiny growth of a stem.

- **Absolute growth rate** is the total increase in a given time.
- **Relative growth rate** is the increase per unit of the starting size: it tells how fast a plant grows compared with its own size.

```math
\text{RGR} = \frac{\text{increase in size in the period}}{\text{size at the start of the period}}
```

### Kinds of growth: arithmetic, geometric, sigmoid

- **Arithmetic growth:** after mitosis, one daughter cell keeps dividing and the other differentiates and stops. The size rises by the same amount each time: `$L_t = L_0 + rt$`, a straight line. A root tip lengthening at a steady rate is an example.
- **Geometric growth:** both daughter cells keep dividing, so the size multiplies: `$W_t = W_0 e^{rt}$` (r is the relative growth rate, W₀ the starting size). It happens in the early period of growth in fruits, tissues and populations.
- **Sigmoid growth:** over the whole life, the growth curve is S-shaped: a slow **lag phase**, a fast **log (exponential) phase** and a **stationary phase** when nutrients or space become limiting.

### Conditions for growth

Plants need **water** (cells stretch by turgor), **oxygen** (for respiration), **nutrients** (macro and micro), the right **temperature** (the best range is often about 28 to 30 °C for many crop plants), and **light**. Growth is also governed by the **genes** and by plant hormones.

## 2. Differentiation, dedifferentiation and redifferentiation

Cells formed by the meristems **differentiate**: they change in structure to take on a job. A cell that becomes a xylem vessel loses its cell contents and develops thick walls. Mature living cells (for example, parenchyma) may lose their specialisation and divide again: **dedifferentiation**. The vascular cambium between bundles and the cork cambium form this way. The cells so formed can then specialise again: **redifferentiation**. Cells that have lost the power to divide for good cannot go back (the xylem vessel).

**Development** is all the changes an organism goes through from seed germination to death: growth, differentiation, flowering, fruiting, senescence. **Plasticity** is the ability of a plant to form different structures in different conditions: the heterophylly of cotton, coriander and larkspur (juvenile and adult leaves differ), or buttercup leaves that are broad in air and narrow underwater. The different structures are the results of the environment acting on the same genes.

## 3. Plant growth regulators

**Plant growth regulators (PGRs)**, or plant hormones, are small chemicals made in tiny amounts that control growth. Some **promote** growth (auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins) and some **inhibit** it (abscisic acid, ethylene, which also has other jobs). The effect depends on the **dose** and on which tissue it meets.

### Auxins

Named from the Greek *auxein*, to grow. The natural auxin is **indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)**, made at the shoot tips. Experiments by Charles and Francis Darwin showed that coleoptiles bend toward light because "something" moves down from the tip. Went (1928) collected it in agar blocks and found it was a chemical.

**Roles:** elongation of cells in stems; **apical dominance** (the tip stops the side buds); rooting of cuttings; prevents fruit and leaf fall at first; **parthenocarpy** (fruit without fertilisation, in tomato); controls xylem differentiation; the synthetic auxin **2,4-D** kills broad-leaved weeds in a wheat field. A gardener removes the tip, to stop the auxin and make a hedge bushy.

### Gibberellins

Discovered in Japan from a fungus (*Gibberella fujikuroi*) that made rice seedlings grow too tall (the "foolish seedling" disease). There are more than 100; **GA₃** is the best known. **Roles:** longer stems (sugarcane stalks, with more sugar), **bolting** of rosette plants such as cabbage before flowering, bigger fruits and longer stalks in grapes, breaking seed dormancy, and in **malting** barley, where GA makes the seed make amylase.

### Cytokinins

First found as a growth factor from coconut milk and herring sperm DNA (**kinetin**). Natural cytokinin is **zeatin** from maize. They are made at root tips and spread upwards. **Roles:** cell division (cytokinesis); new leaves, chloroplasts and buds; **break apical dominance** (the opposite of auxin, so side shoots grow); **delay senescence** (leaves stay green, as in cut flowers in cytokinin); help in tissue culture when balanced with auxin.

### Ethylene

A **gas**, made in ripening fruit and in tissues under stress. **Roles:** **ripens fruit** (a raw banana or mango in a paper bag with a ripe one turns ripe faster), makes leaves and fruits fall (abscission), promotes **flowering in pineapple** and fruit set in mango (ethephon, which releases ethylene, is used on farms), makes the stem of deepwater rice grow, and in a seedling the hook and the "triple response". Ethephon, which releases ethylene, is sprayed on tomatoes to ripen them together.

### Abscisic acid (ABA)

The **stress hormone**. **Roles:** closes stomata in drought; keeps seeds and buds dormant; slows growth; promotes leaf fall (abscission) and helps the plant resist stress. It stops the effects of gibberellins, so the ratio of GA to ABA decides whether a seed sleeps or germinates.

|---|---|---|---|
| Auxin | shoot tip | elongation, apical dominance, rooting | cytokinin |

| ABA | stressed leaves | stomatal closure, dormancy | gibberellin |

<div class="myth">

**“Each hormone has one job.”** Most hormones act in different tissues differently, and several act together. Plants respond to the **balance**: auxin with cytokinin decides root or shoot in tissue culture.

</div>

## 4. Photoperiodism

Many plants flower only when the day (or night) length is right: the response to the relative length of light and dark in a day is **photoperiodism**, first studied by Garner and Allard (1920) in tobacco.

- **Short-day plants (SDP)** flower when the day is shorter than a critical length, that is, when the **night is longer** than a critical length: rice, soybean, chrysanthemum.
- **Long-day plants (LDP)** flower when the night is shorter than critical: wheat, spinach, radish.
- **Day-neutral plants (DNP)** flower whatever the length: tomato, cucumber, maize.

It is the length of the **uninterrupted dark period** that counts. A short flash of light in the middle of a long night stops flowering in an SDP, and makes an LDP flower. The sensor is the pigment **phytochrome** in the leaves, and the signal travels to the shoot tips (a "florigen" in the phloem).

## 5. Vernalisation

Some plants, such as winter wheat, biennials like cabbage and carrot, flower only after a **cold treatment**. In **vernalisation** the exposure to low temperature (about 0 to 5 °C) for some weeks in the young plant or seed makes the later flowering possible. Winter varieties sown in spring will not flower in the first year; a farmer can chill the soaked seeds first so that they can be sown in spring.

## 6. Seed dormancy

A seed or bud may fail to grow even when conditions look good: this is **dormancy**. Causes: a hard, waterproof **seed coat**, an immature embryo, **inhibitors** like ABA in the seed or fruit juice, and the need for light or cold. This helps the plant to survive bad seasons and germinate at the right time (after the rains). Ways to break it: **scarification** (scratching or acid on the seed coat), **stratification** (chilling the wet seeds), treating with **gibberellin or nitrate**, and washing off the inhibitors.

<div class="history">

**Beyond the syllabus.** Wounded plants make jasmonic acid and plants under attack also use brassinosteroids and salicylic acid as hormone-like signals; in a laboratory the same hormones are used in tissue culture to grow whole plants from single cells.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Growth is a permanent increase in size; plants keep growing through meristems (open growth). Phases: meristematic, elongation, maturation.
- Arithmetic growth: `$L_t = L_0 + rt$`. Geometric: `$W_t = W_0 e^{rt}$`. Sigmoid: lag, log, stationary. RGR = increase ÷ starting size.
- Differentiation, dedifferentiation, redifferentiation; plasticity gives different forms in different conditions (heterophylly).
- Auxin (IAA): elongation, apical dominance, rooting, parthenocarpy, 2,4-D weedkiller. Gibberellin: stem elongation, bolting, malting. Cytokinin: cell division, side buds, delays senescence. Ethylene: ripening, abscission, a gas. ABA: stress hormone, stomatal closure, dormancy.
- Photoperiodism: SDP, LDP, DNP; the uninterrupted night counts. Vernalisation: cold makes flowering possible. Dormancy is broken by scarification, stratification, GA.

</div>
