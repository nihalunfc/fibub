**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 19 · about 75 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name the endocrine glands and the main hormone of each, explain what each hormone does, describe how hormones act on cells, explain negative feedback in the thyroid and blood-sugar systems, and name the disorders of hormone excess and deficiency.

<div class="bigidea">The nervous system is like a phone call: fast, aimed at one place, over quickly. The endocrine system is like a letter posted to a whole city: each gland releases a hormone into the blood, which reaches every cell, but only cells with the right receptor "open the letter". Hormones are held within narrow limits by negative feedback, like a thermostat.</div>

## 1. Endocrine glands and hormones

The **endocrine glands** have **no ducts**; they pour their secretions, the **hormones**, directly into the blood (unlike the sweat or salivary glands, which have ducts). **Hormones** are chemical messengers made in very small quantity, carried in blood to **target cells**, where they act and are then broken down. A hormone does not supply energy or building material: it changes the work of the target cells. The older definition ("a hormone is a chemical made by an endocrine gland and carried to a distant target") now includes also hormones made by cells of the heart, kidney and gut.

Hormones fall into groups by chemistry: **peptide, polypeptide and protein hormones** (insulin, glucagon, pituitary hormones); **steroids** (cortisol, testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, aldosterone); **iodothyronines** (thyroid hormones); and **amino acid derivatives** (adrenaline, noradrenaline).

## 2. The human endocrine system

### Hypothalamus

The hypothalamus, the base of the forebrain, is the link between the nervous and the endocrine systems. Its neurons make hormones in two groups: **releasing hormones**, which stimulate the pituitary to release its hormones (for example **gonadotropin-releasing hormone, GnRH**, which stimulates the release of LH and FSH), and **inhibiting hormones**, which stop the pituitary releasing a hormone (for example **somatostatin** inhibits growth hormone release). The releasing hormones reach the **anterior pituitary** through a small **portal blood system**. The posterior pituitary is controlled by **nerve fibres** from the hypothalamus.

### Pituitary gland

The pituitary lies in a bony cavity at the base of the brain and is attached to the hypothalamus by a stalk. It has two main lobes.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-chem-pituitary"><figcaption>Switch between the lobes. The front lobe makes its own hormones under orders carried by blood; the back lobe only stores and releases hormones made in the hypothalamus.</figcaption></figure>

**Anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis)** makes six hormones.

- **Growth hormone (GH):** growth of body tissues and bones; also raises blood sugar. Too little in childhood gives **pituitary dwarfism**; too much in childhood gives **gigantism**; too much in an adult gives **acromegaly** (enlarged jaw, hands and feet).
- **Prolactin (PRL):** growth of the mammary glands and milk formation.
- **Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH):** the thyroid gland makes and releases thyroid hormones.
- **Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH):** the adrenal cortex releases its steroids.
- **Luteinising hormone (LH)** and **follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH):** the gonads. In males LH makes the Leydig cells release androgens, and FSH with androgens stimulates sperm production; in females FSH starts the growth of follicles in the ovary and LH causes ovulation and keeps the corpus luteum.
- The **intermediate lobe** makes melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH), which acts on skin pigment cells; it is small in humans.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-chem-growth"><figcaption>Choose a growth hormone disorder to compare it with normal growth. In a child the bones can still lengthen; in an adult they can only thicken.</figcaption></figure>

**Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)** stores and releases two hormones made in the hypothalamus.

- **Oxytocin:** makes the smooth muscles of the uterus contract strongly during childbirth, and milk ejection from the mammary glands when the baby sucks.
- **Vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone, ADH):** makes the kidney tubules take back water, so less urine is passed (see the excretion chapter). Lack of ADH causes **diabetes insipidus**.

### Pineal gland

The pineal gland, on the roof of the forebrain, secretes **melatonin**. It helps to regulate the 24-hour (diurnal) cycle: sleep and waking, body temperature, and (in some animals) the seasons, and it influences skin pigmentation and the menstrual cycle. Melatonin rises in the dark, which is why bright screens late at night can delay sleep.

### Thyroid gland

The thyroid is on the windpipe in the neck. It makes **thyroxine (T4)** and **triiodothyronine (T3)**, which need **iodine**, and **thyrocalcitonin (calcitonin)**.

- T3 and T4 control the **basal metabolic rate**, support the growth of the brain and body, help red blood cell formation, and control the metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins and fats.
- **Calcitonin** lowers blood calcium by slowing the breakdown of bone.

**Disorders.** A lack of iodine causes **simple (endemic) goitre**: the gland enlarges because high TSH keeps stimulating it. If the mother's iodine is short in pregnancy, the child may have **cretinism** (stunted growth, low intelligence). In adults, low thyroid hormone is **hypothyroidism** (tiredness, weight gain, cold feeling); in women it may disturb the menstrual cycle. **Exophthalmic goitre (Graves' disease)** is an autoimmune form of **hyperthyroidism**: raised metabolism, a fast heartbeat, sweating, weight loss and bulging eyes. In India, iodised salt has helped to prevent goitre.

### Parathyroid gland

Four small glands sit on the back of the thyroid. They make **parathyroid hormone (PTH)**, which **raises blood calcium**: it takes calcium out of bone, makes the kidney keep it and helps the gut take it up (through vitamin D). PTH and calcitonin work in opposite ways, so blood calcium stays steady.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-chem-calcium"><figcaption>Slide the blood calcium and see which hormone responds. Calcium is needed for nerve signals, muscle contraction and blood clotting, so it must stay in the normal band.</figcaption></figure>

### Thymus

The thymus lies between the lungs, behind the breastbone. It makes **thymosins**, which help to make **T-lymphocytes** and so provide cell-mediated immunity; it also helps antibody production (humoral immunity). It shrinks with age, so immunity weakens in the old.

### Adrenal gland

The two adrenal glands lie on top of the kidneys. Each has an outer **cortex** and an inner **medulla**.

**Adrenal medulla** makes **adrenaline (epinephrine)** and **noradrenaline**, the **emergency hormones** (fight or flight), released in stress, fear, anger or exercise: raised heart rate and strength of contraction, faster breathing, wider pupils, more glucose in the blood from glycogen breakdown, more blood to the muscles, and less to the gut.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-chem-adrenal"><figcaption>Raise the stress slider to see the body's emergency response, as controlled by adrenaline within seconds.</figcaption></figure>

**Adrenal cortex** has three layers and makes **corticoids**:

- **Glucocorticoids** (mainly **cortisol**): raise blood glucose by gluconeogenesis, break down proteins and fats for energy, support the cardiovascular system, lower inflammation and the immune response, and help in stress. They also raise blood pressure and red blood cell production.
- **Mineralocorticoids** (mainly **aldosterone**): act on the distal tubule to keep Na⁺ and water and remove K⁺ (see excretion).
- Small amounts of **sex steroids** (androgens).

Too little cortex hormone causes **Addison's disease** (weakness, low blood pressure, low sugar). Too much cortisol causes **Cushing's syndrome**.

### Pancreas

The pancreas is both an exocrine gland (digestive juice) and an endocrine gland. The endocrine part is the **islets of Langerhans** (1 to 2 million), with **alpha cells** (about 25 %) making **glucagon** and **beta cells** (about 60 %) making **insulin**.

- **Glucagon** acts on the liver to break down glycogen (**glycogenolysis**) and to make glucose from other substances (**gluconeogenesis**): it **raises** blood glucose, and it lowers the use of glucose by cells.
- **Insulin** helps glucose enter cells (muscle and fat) and makes the liver store glucose as glycogen (**glycogenesis**): it **lowers** blood glucose.

Together they keep blood glucose near 90 mg/dL (fasting). Lack of insulin, or cells that do not respond to it, causes **diabetes mellitus**: high blood sugar, glucose in the urine (**glycosuria**), much urine, thirst, and, if untreated, damage to the eyes, kidneys and nerves. Insulin injections treat the type that needs them.

<div class="myth">

**“Diabetes insipidus and diabetes mellitus are the same.”** Both give lots of urine, but they have different causes. Diabetes mellitus is a shortage or failure of insulin, with sugar in the urine. Diabetes insipidus is a shortage of ADH: huge amounts of dilute urine with no sugar.

### Testis

The testes in the scrotum make **androgens**, mainly **testosterone**, from the **Leydig (interstitial) cells**. It controls the development of the male sex organs, the formation of sperm, and the male secondary sexual characters (deep voice, facial hair, muscle), and it affects behaviour.

### Ovary

The ovaries make **oestrogen** (from the growing follicles) and **progesterone** (from the corpus luteum). Oestrogen controls the development of the female reproductive organs, female secondary characters, the menstrual cycle and the growth of mammary glands. Progesterone prepares the uterus for the embryo, keeps the pregnancy and prepares the mammary glands for milk (see the reproduction chapters).

### Hormones of the heart, kidney and gut

- **Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF)** from the heart's atria widens blood vessels and lowers blood pressure.
- **Erythropoietin** from the kidney stimulates the formation of red blood cells.
- The **gastrointestinal tract** makes **gastrin** (HCl and pepsinogen from the stomach), **secretin** (pancreatic juice and bicarbonate), **cholecystokinin (CCK)** (pancreatic enzymes and bile release) and **gastric inhibitory peptide (GIP)** (slows gastric secretion and helps insulin).
- Many other tissues, such as growth factors, also make hormones that affect growth and repair.

## 3. Mechanism of hormone action

A hormone acts on a target cell only if the cell has a specific **receptor**: a protein that fits the hormone like a lock fits a key. There are two kinds.

- **Membrane-bound receptors** on the cell surface bind **peptide and protein hormones**, which cannot cross the membrane. The hormone-receptor complex makes a **second messenger** inside, such as **cyclic AMP (cAMP)**, Ca²⁺ or IP₃, which starts a chain of changes in enzymes and so the cell's response.
- **Intracellular receptors** (in the cytoplasm or nucleus) bind **steroid hormones and iodothyronines**, which pass through the membrane. The hormone-receptor complex goes to the nucleus and **regulates gene expression** (the DNA copied into mRNA), so new proteins are made and the cell's activity changes.

<div class="history">

**Beyond the syllabus.** The first hormone ever named was **secretin**, found in 1902 by William Bayliss and Ernest Starling in London. They showed that the gut sends a chemical in the blood that makes the pancreas release juice, even with all the nerves cut. In 1921 Frederick Banting and Charles Best in Toronto extracted insulin from the pancreas, and the first patient was treated in 1922.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Endocrine glands are ductless; hormones are carried by blood to target cells with specific receptors.
- Hypothalamus: releasing and inhibiting hormones; posterior pituitary stores oxytocin and ADH; anterior pituitary: GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH.
- Pineal: melatonin (biological clock). Thyroid: T3, T4 (metabolism; need iodine) and calcitonin. Parathyroid: PTH (raises calcium). Thymus: thymosins (T-cells).
- Adrenal medulla: adrenaline and noradrenaline (emergency). Cortex: cortisol (glucose, stress), aldosterone (Na⁺ and water).
- Pancreas: insulin lowers and glucagon raises blood glucose. Testis: testosterone. Ovary: oestrogen, progesterone.
- Peptide hormones act through surface receptors and second messengers (cAMP); steroid and thyroid hormones act through inside receptors on genes.
- Negative feedback keeps hormone levels steady. Disorders: dwarfism, gigantism, acromegaly, diabetes insipidus, goitre, cretinism, Graves' disease, Addison's disease, Cushing's syndrome, diabetes mellitus.

</div>
