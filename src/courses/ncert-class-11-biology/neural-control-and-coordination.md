**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 18 · about 80 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the neuron and the kinds of nervous system, explain how a nerve impulse starts and travels, describe a synapse, name the parts of the brain and their jobs, trace a reflex arc, and explain how the eye and the ear work.

<div class="bigidea">The nervous system is a network of cables and switches. A neuron is a living wire: it keeps its inside electrically negative, and when stimulated it lets a wave of charge race along its length in a fraction of a millisecond. At the end of the wire a chemical messenger carries the signal across a tiny gap to the next cell. Billions of these units, joined in the brain and spinal cord, make us sense, think and act.</div>

## 1. Neural system

Coordination means working together of organs so that the body acts as one. In animals two systems do this: the **neural system** (fast, short-lived, by electrical impulses and chemical messengers) and the **endocrine system** (slower, longer lasting, by hormones; the next chapter).

In simple animals the nervous system is simple. *Hydra* has a **nerve net** with no centre. Insects have a **brain** and a **ventral nerve cord** with ganglia (clusters of nerve cells). Vertebrates have a **central** and a **peripheral** system.

## 2. Human neural system

- The **central nervous system (CNS)** is the **brain and spinal cord**: the site of information processing and control.
- The **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** is all the nerves outside the CNS. Afferent (**sensory**) nerve fibres carry impulses from tissues and organs **to** the CNS; efferent (**motor**) fibres carry impulses **from** the CNS to the muscles and glands. The PNS has two parts: the **somatic** system (to skeletal muscles, voluntary) and the **autonomic** system (to smooth muscles, heart and glands, involuntary). The autonomic system has a **sympathetic** division (fight or flight: faster heartbeat) and a **parasympathetic** division (rest and digest: slows the heart).

### The neuron: structural and functional unit

A **neuron** has three parts: a **cell body (cyton)** with the nucleus and **Nissl granules**; **dendrites**, short branched fibres that bring impulses **to** the cell body; and an **axon**, a long fibre that carries impulses **away**, ending in **axon terminals** with **synaptic knobs** that hold vesicles of **neurotransmitter**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-neural-neuron"><figcaption>Choose each part to highlight it. Impulses flow in one direction: dendrites, cell body, axon, terminals.</figcaption></figure>

By number of processes, neurons are **multipolar** (one axon and two or more dendrites, in the cerebral cortex), **bipolar** (one axon and one dendrite, in the retina) and **unipolar** (the cell body has one fibre, as in the embryo and in the dorsal root ganglia). Axons may be **myelinated**: wrapped in a fatty **myelin sheath** made by **Schwann cells**, with gaps called **nodes of Ranvier**. Myelinated nerve fibres are in the spinal and cranial nerves; unmyelinated fibres lack the sheath. The cell bodies of a nerve are collected in the **grey matter**; the myelinated axons make up the **white matter**.

## 3. Generation and conduction of the nerve impulse

A nerve impulse is a **wave of change in the electric charge across the membrane** of the axon.

### Resting potential

Inside a resting neuron is about **−70 mV** compared with the outside: the membrane is **polarised**. The reasons: the membrane is permeable to K⁺ (which leaks out) and almost impermeable to Na⁺; large negative proteins stay inside; and the **Na⁺/K⁺ pump** uses ATP to move **3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in**, so there is more Na⁺ outside and more K⁺ inside.

### Action potential

If a stimulus lifts the membrane potential to the **threshold (about −55 mV)**, the membrane suddenly becomes permeable to Na⁺ and a full impulse starts. This is the **action potential**.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-neural-ap">
<div class="step">At rest the voltage is about −70 mV. K⁺ leaks out; voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels are closed.</div>
<div class="step">Depolarisation: Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ rushes in. The inside goes to about +30 mV; the polarity reverses.</div>
<div class="step">Repolarisation: Na⁺ channels close and K⁺ channels open; K⁺ leaves and the inside becomes negative again.</div>
<div class="step">Undershoot: K⁺ channels close slowly, so the voltage dips below −70 mV for a moment.</div>
<div class="step">Recovery: the Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores the resting ion balance. The neuron is ready for the next impulse.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“A stronger stimulus makes a bigger impulse.”** The size of an action potential is always the same. A stronger stimulus makes the neuron fire more often (more impulses per second), and recruits more neurons, but never a taller spike. After firing, a neuron has a short **refractory period** when it cannot fire again, which keeps the impulse moving only forward.

</div>

### Conduction along the axon

The depolarised patch of membrane makes the next patch reach the threshold, so the action potential moves down the axon like a wave of falling dominoes. In a **myelinated fibre** the myelin stops ions crossing, so the impulse **jumps** from one node of Ranvier to the next (**saltatory conduction**), which is much faster and saves energy. Speeds range from about 1 m/s in thin unmyelinated fibres to over 100 m/s in thick myelinated ones.

### Transmission of the impulse across a synapse

A **synapse** is the junction between the axon terminal of one neuron (**pre-synaptic**) and the dendrite or cell body of the next (**post-synaptic**), or between a neuron and a muscle. A narrow gap, the **synaptic cleft**, lies between them.

- At an **electrical synapse** the two membranes are very close and the current flows directly from one to the next, as in the heart. Transmission is faster, but this kind is rare in the human nervous system.
- At a **chemical synapse** (the usual kind) the membranes are separated by the cleft and the signal is carried by a **neurotransmitter**. It works in one direction only.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-synapse-steps"><figcaption>Step through the chemical synapse: calcium enters the knob, vesicles fuse, the transmitter crosses the cleft and fits its receptors, then it is cleared away.</figcaption></figure>

When an impulse reaches the axon terminal it opens Ca²⁺ channels; the Ca²⁺ makes synaptic **vesicles** fuse with the membrane and release neurotransmitter (for example acetylcholine) into the cleft. It binds **receptors** on the post-synaptic membrane, opens ion channels and makes a new potential, which may be an **excitatory** (EPSP) or an **inhibitory** (IPSP) change. The transmitter is then removed by enzymes (such as acetylcholinesterase) or taken back, so the signal ends.

## 4. Central neural system

### The human brain

The brain is the central information processing organ. It is protected by the skull and by three membranes, the **meninges**, and is bathed in **cerebrospinal fluid**. It has three main parts: **forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-neural-brain"><figcaption>Choose a region to light it up and read what it does. Notice the four lobes of the cerebrum and the position of the cerebellum and brainstem.</figcaption></figure>

**Forebrain**

- **Cerebrum.** The largest part, divided by a deep groove into the **left and right cerebral hemispheres**, joined by a bridge of fibres, the **corpus callosum**. The outer layer is the **cerebral cortex**, with many folds that increase its area; it is grey matter (the cell bodies). It has **motor areas, sensory areas and association areas** and four lobes: **frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital**. Inside is white matter. The cerebrum controls thinking, intelligence, memory, speech and voluntary movement.
- **Thalamus.** The main relay and coordination centre for sensory and motor signals.
- **Hypothalamus.** Controls body temperature, hunger and thirst, and emotions; makes hormones that control the pituitary. It contains the "biological clock" for sleep.
- The **limbic system** (the cerebral cortex edges with the amygdala and hippocampus) controls emotion, memory and motivation.

**Midbrain.** Between the forebrain and the hindbrain: reflexes for sight and sound (**corpora quadrigemina**) and for movement of the eye.

**Hindbrain**

- **Pons.** Fibre tracts that link different regions; helps to control breathing.
- **Cerebellum.** Balance, posture and smooth, coordinated movement.
- **Medulla oblongata.** Continues into the spinal cord; centres for breathing, heartbeat, and for swallowing, coughing and vomiting.

The midbrain, pons and medulla together are the **brainstem**.

### Spinal cord

The **spinal cord** runs inside the vertebral column from the medulla. Seen in cross-section it has an inner **grey matter** (H-shaped, with cell bodies) and an outer **white matter**. Spinal nerves enter at the back (**dorsal root**, sensory) and leave from the front (**ventral root**, motor).

## 5. Reflex action and reflex arc

A **reflex** is a quick, involuntary response to a stimulus, controlled by the spinal cord or the brainstem: pulling the hand from a hot pan, a blink, the knee jerk. The path is the **reflex arc**: receptor → sensory (afferent) neuron → spinal cord (a relay neuron) → motor (efferent) neuron → effector (muscle or gland). The signal does not wait for the brain, so the reaction is faster; the brain learns about it afterwards.

## 6. Sensory reception and processing

### The eye

The eye is a hollow ball in the **orbit** of the skull. Its wall has three layers.

- The outer **fibrous coat (sclera)**, white, with the transparent **cornea** at the front.
- The middle **vascular coat (choroid)**, with blood vessels and dark pigment; it continues at the front as the **ciliary body** and the **iris**. The iris, with its **pupil**, controls the amount of light.
- The inner **retina**, which has the photoreceptors: **rods** (dim light, black and white, with the pigment rhodopsin made from vitamin A) and **cones** (bright light and colour; three kinds, red, green and blue). The **fovea** (yellow spot) has only cones and is the place of sharpest vision. Where the optic nerve leaves there are no receptors: the **blind spot**.

The **lens** is held by **suspensory ligaments** to the ciliary body. It divides the eye into the **aqueous chamber** (watery fluid, in front) and the **vitreous chamber** (jelly-like fluid, behind).

**How we see.** Light passes through the cornea, aqueous humor, pupil, lens and vitreous humor to the retina. In the rods and cones the light changes the pigment (for rhodopsin the **opsin** and **retinal** separate), which starts a change of potential and generates impulses in the bipolar and ganglion cells; the **optic nerve** carries them to the **visual cortex** in the occipital lobe.

### The ear

The ear has two jobs: **hearing** and **balance**. It has three parts.

- **External ear:** the **pinna** and the **external auditory canal**, ending at the **tympanic membrane** (eardrum). Wax glands in the canal trap dust.
- **Middle ear:** a cavity with three tiny bones, the **malleus, incus and stapes**, which transmit and amplify the vibrations. The **Eustachian tube** joins it to the throat and equalises pressure on the two sides of the eardrum.
- **Inner ear (labyrinth):** the **cochlea** (a coiled tube for hearing) and the **vestibular apparatus** (balance). The cochlea is filled with fluid; the **organ of Corti** on the **basilar membrane** has **hair cells**, which are the hearing receptors.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-neural-ear">
<div class="step">The pinna collects sound waves.</div>
<div class="step">The sound travels down the canal and makes the eardrum vibrate.</div>
<div class="step">The vibration passes along the malleus, incus and stapes, which amplify it.</div>
<div class="step">The stapes pushes on the oval window, which sets the fluid in the cochlea moving.</div>
<div class="step">The basilar membrane moves and bends the hair cells of the organ of Corti, which generate impulses.</div>
<div class="step">The auditory nerve carries them to the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe.</div>
</div>

**Balance.** The **vestibular apparatus** has three **semicircular canals** (sensing rotation of the head) and the **otolith organ** (sensing the position of the head and linear movement). Hair cells in them bend when the fluid or the crystals move, and send the information to the cerebellum.

<div class="history">

**Beyond the syllabus.** The squid giant axon, about 1 mm wide, let Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley put a wire inside a nerve in 1952 and record the action potential. They described it with a few equations that still work, and received a Nobel Prize in 1963.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Neural system: CNS (brain, spinal cord) and PNS (somatic and autonomic: sympathetic and parasympathetic). Neuron = cell body, dendrites, axon.
- Resting potential about −70 mV (Na⁺/K⁺ pump 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in; K⁺ leak). Action potential: Na⁺ in (to +30 mV), then K⁺ out; threshold about −55 mV; all-or-none.
- Myelin gives saltatory conduction (nodes of Ranvier): much faster.
- Chemical synapse: Ca²⁺ → vesicles → neurotransmitter → receptors; one-way.
- Brain: forebrain (cerebrum with four lobes, thalamus, hypothalamus), midbrain, hindbrain (pons, cerebellum, medulla).
- Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → motor neuron → effector.
- Eye: cornea and lens focus light on the retina (rods, cones); the lens thickens for near objects. Ear: pinna, eardrum, three ossicles, cochlea (organ of Corti) for hearing; semicircular canals for balance.

</div>
