**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 16 · about 75 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name the nitrogenous wastes and the animals that make them, describe the human excretory system, explain how urine is formed step by step, use the numbers for filtration, say how the kidney makes concentrated urine and how hormones fine-tune it, and describe disorders of the kidney.

<div class="bigidea">Cells burn food and make waste, and the nitrogen in proteins turns into poisonous ammonia. The body must get rid of this waste without losing water, salts or sugar. The kidney does it by a two-step trick: first filter almost everything out of the blood (180 litres a day), then take back what the body wants (all but about 1.5 litres). What is left is urine.</div>

## 1. Nitrogenous wastes

When proteins and nucleic acids are broken down, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed and ends up as a **nitrogenous waste**. Animals make three kinds, depending on how much water they can spare.

- **Ammonia** is the most toxic but needs the least energy to make. It dissolves easily, so it needs a lot of water to wash it out. **Ammonotelic** animals live in water: bony fishes, aquatic amphibians and aquatic insects. Ammonia leaves by diffusion across the gills or body surface; the kidney has almost no role.
- **Urea** is less toxic. The liver makes it from ammonia and carbon dioxide, and it leaves in urine with a moderate amount of water. **Ureotelic** animals: mammals including humans, adult frogs, marine fishes. Some urea is kept in the body to match the salt level of the surroundings.
- **Uric acid** is the least toxic, hardly dissolves, and leaves as a paste with almost no water. **Uricotelic** animals: birds, reptiles, land snails and insects. Cost: it takes the most energy to make.

A hen's egg is sealed in a shell, so a chick has to put its waste somewhere safe: uric acid is the answer.</figcaption></figure>

**Everyday link.** A pigeon's droppings are mostly white. That white paste is the uric acid. A chicken saves water in the same way.

## 2. Human excretory system

The system has a pair of **kidneys**, a pair of **ureters**, one **urinary bladder** and one **urethra**. Each kidney is bean-shaped, about 10 to 12 cm long, and lies on either side of the backbone near the last thoracic and third lumbar vertebrae, protected by the ribs. The inner curved edge is the **hilum**, where the renal artery enters, and the renal vein and the ureter leave.

Cut a kidney lengthwise and you see two zones.

- The **cortex**, the outer part, holds the glomeruli, the convoluted tubules and the blood vessels.
- The **medulla**, the inner part, is divided into cone-shaped **medullary pyramids** that project into the **calyces** (cup-like spaces). The cortex dips in between the pyramids as **renal columns**.
- The calyces join to form the **renal pelvis**, which leads into the ureter.

Each kidney has about **one million nephrons**, the **functional units** of the kidney.

### The nephron

A nephron has two parts, the **renal corpuscle** and the **renal tubule**.

1. The **renal corpuscle** is the **glomerulus** (a knot of capillaries) inside the **Bowman's capsule**. Blood comes in through the **afferent arteriole** and leaves through a **narrower efferent arteriole**. Because the exit is narrower than the entrance, pressure builds up in the glomerulus, like water in a hose when you put your thumb over the end.
2. The **renal tubule** begins as the **proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)**, which has a brush border to widen its surface. It leads to the **loop of Henle** (a descending limb and an ascending limb), then the **distal convoluted tubule (DCT)**, which empties into a **collecting duct**. Many nephrons share one collecting duct, and the ducts open into the renal pelvis.
3. Around the tubule is a network of capillaries, the **peritubular capillaries**. A parallel U-shaped vessel along the loop of Henle is the **vasa recta**. In most nephrons the loop is short (**cortical nephrons**); in about 15 per cent it dips deep into the medulla (**juxtamedullary nephrons**) and is the one that makes concentrated urine.

<div class="step">The proximal tubule takes back 65 to 70 per cent of the water and sodium, nearly all glucose and amino acids; it adds H⁺ and ammonia to the filtrate.</div>

## 3. Urine formation

Urine is made in **three steps**: glomerular filtration, reabsorption and secretion.

### Glomerular filtration

Blood pressure forces water and small molecules out of the glomerular capillaries through three thin layers: the capillary wall, the basement membrane and the special cells (**podocytes**) of the Bowman's capsule, which leave tiny **filtration slits**. Blood cells and big proteins are too large to pass, so the filtrate is plasma without proteins. This is called **ultrafiltration**.

The kidneys filter about **1100 to 1200 mL of blood each minute**, about a fifth of what the heart pumps. The **glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** is about **125 mL per minute**, or about **180 litres a day**. That is more than four times the water in your whole body, so the same water is filtered and taken back many times a day.

Filtration is a balance of pressures. The blood pressure in the glomerulus pushes fluid out. Two pressures push back: the osmotic pull of the proteins in the blood and the back-pressure of the fluid already in the capsule.

```math
\text{Net filtration pressure} = P_{\text{glomerulus}} - \pi_{\text{blood}} - P_{\text{capsule}}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>P glomerulus</dt><dd>blood pressure in the glomerular capillaries, about 55 mm Hg</dd>
<dt>π blood</dt><dd>the osmotic pull of plasma proteins that keep water in the blood, about 30 mm Hg</dd>
<dt>P capsule</dt><dd>pressure of the fluid in the Bowman's capsule, about 15 mm Hg</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>It is a tug of war. Blood pressure pulls one way (out), protein pull and capsule pressure pull the other. The net 10 mm Hg wins slightly, so about 125 mL a minute goes out. If the glomerular pressure falls to 45 mm Hg, the net pressure is zero and filtration stops.</dd>
</dl>

The **juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)** is a special region where the distal tubule touches the afferent arteriole. Its **JG cells** sense blood pressure and flow and help control the GFR (section 6).

### Reabsorption and secretion

Of 180 litres filtered, only about 1.5 litres leave as urine, so **99 per cent** of the filtrate is reabsorbed. Reabsorption happens by **active transport** (glucose, amino acids, Na⁺) and by **passive transport** (water, urea, some Cl⁻).

- **PCT.** Reabsorbs nearly all of the glucose and amino acids, and 65 to 70 per cent of water and sodium. Secretes hydrogen ions, ammonia and potassium into the filtrate, which helps keep the blood pH and ion balance right. The filtrate stays **isotonic** with blood (300 mOsm/L).
- **Descending limb of the loop of Henle.** Permeable to water but almost not to salts. Water leaves, so the filtrate gets more concentrated as it goes down into the medulla.
- **Ascending limb.** Permeable to salts but **impermeable to water**. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ leave (some by active transport), so the filtrate becomes more dilute again.
- **DCT.** Conditional reabsorption of Na⁺ and water. Takes back bicarbonate, and secretes H⁺, K⁺ and ammonia. This keeps the pH and sodium-potassium balance of the blood.
- **Collecting duct.** Takes back large amounts of water to make concentrated urine. Passes a little urea into the medulla and secretes H⁺ and K⁺ to keep the pH and ion balance.

<div class="myth">

**“The kidney filters out only the waste.”** It filters almost everything small, including glucose, amino acids, salts and water, and then takes back what is useful. The skill of the kidney is in the taking back. That is why glucose appears in urine in diabetes: the blood level is so high that the tubule cannot take back all of it.

</div>

## 4. Function of the tubules: how urine becomes concentrated

Humans can make urine up to **four times more concentrated than blood** (1200 mOsm/L against 300). This is done by the loop of Henle and the vasa recta, working as a **counter-current mechanism**: two limbs of the loop carry fluid in opposite directions side by side.

- The fluid in the medulla around the loop gets saltier and saltier as you go down: **300 mOsm/L at the cortex to about 1200 at the tip** of the loop, the **medullary gradient**. It is made mostly of **NaCl and urea**.
- The descending limb loses water to this salty medulla; the ascending limb loses salt into it. A small difference at each level, repeated all along the loop, adds up to a large gradient.
- The **vasa recta**, which run along the loop with blood flowing in the opposite direction, carry away water and salt without washing away the gradient: they are a counter-current exchanger.
- **NaCl** is moved from the ascending limb to the descending limb by the vasa recta and the interstitium; **urea** goes the other way round in a small cycle.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-excr-gradient">
<div class="step">Start: everything is 300 mOsm/L, the same as blood.</div>
<div class="step">The ascending limb pumps salt out into the medulla. Water cannot follow, so the tube gets 200 less than the medulla beside it.</div>
<div class="step">The descending limb, which passes water, lets water out until it matches the saltier medulla.</div>
<div class="step">New fluid keeps arriving and the 200 mOsm/L difference is added again and again: the gradient grows deeper.</div>
<div class="step">Steady state: 300 at the top of the medulla to 1200 at the tip. The collecting duct can now pull water into this salty region.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Water is pumped out of the tube.”** Water is never pumped. It follows the salt by osmosis, from the less salty to the saltier fluid. All the active work is the pumping of salt.

</div>

## 5. Regulation of kidney function

Three hormone systems keep the volume and the salt level of the blood steady.

**Hypothalamus and ADH.** **Osmoreceptors** in the hypothalamus sense the concentration of the blood. If the body loses water (sweating, little drinking) or the volume falls, they signal the posterior pituitary to release **antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin)**. ADH makes the collecting duct more permeable to water, so more water is taken back and urine becomes small in volume and concentrated. If you drink a lot, ADH falls, water stays in the tubule and urine is plentiful and dilute. ADH also tightens blood vessels. Lack of ADH gives **diabetes insipidus**: huge volumes of dilute urine.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c11b-excr-urinevol"></div>

**JGA and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS).** A fall in blood pressure, blood volume or GFR makes the JG cells release **renin**. Renin acts on **angiotensinogen** (a plasma protein from the liver) to make **angiotensin I**, which is changed to **angiotensin II**. Angiotensin II:

- narrows blood vessels, raising blood pressure and GFR;
- makes the **adrenal cortex** release **aldosterone**, which makes the distal tubule take back Na⁺ and water, increasing blood volume and pressure.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-excr-raas">
<div class="step">Blood pressure or GFR falls (for example after losing blood).</div>
<div class="step">The JG cells of the kidney release renin.</div>
<div class="step">Renin starts the change from angiotensinogen to angiotensin I and then angiotensin II.</div>
<div class="step">Angiotensin II narrows the vessels and the adrenal cortex releases aldosterone.</div>
<div class="step">The distal tubule keeps Na⁺ and water; urine becomes less.</div>
<div class="step">Blood volume and pressure rise.</div>
<div class="step">The signal for renin release switches off: negative feedback.</div>
</div>

**Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF).** If blood volume or pressure is too high, the atrial wall of the heart is stretched and releases ANF. It **widens the blood vessels** and lowers blood pressure; it works as a check on RAAS.

## 6. Micturition

Urine passes down the ureters by waves of muscle contraction (peristalsis) and is stored in the **urinary bladder**. When it holds about 300 to 400 mL, **stretch receptors** in the wall send signals to the central nervous system. A command comes back that contracts the bladder muscle and relaxes the **sphincter** at the urethra, and the urine is released through the urethra. This process is **micturition**, and the nervous reflex that causes it is the **micturition reflex**. Adults can override it voluntarily. An adult passes about 1 to 1.5 litres a day, which is slightly acidic (pH about 6), pale yellow from the pigment **urochrome**, and has a smell from the urea. About 25 to 30 grams of **urea** are passed per day.

**Normal urine tests** are useful clues. **Glycosuria** (glucose in urine) points to diabetes mellitus. **Ketonuria** (ketone bodies) also suggests diabetes. Protein in urine can mean kidney damage.

## 7. Role of other organs in excretion

- **Lungs** remove about 200 mL of CO₂ per minute and some water.
- **Liver** secretes bile with bilirubin, biliverdin, cholesterol, and degraded steroid hormones, vitamins and drugs; most leave in the faeces.
- **Skin.** **Sweat glands** make sweat, which has water, NaCl, a little urea and lactic acid, mainly to cool the body. **Sebaceous glands** secrete **sebum**, with sterols, hydrocarbons and wax.

## 8. Disorders of the excretory system

- **Uremia.** High urea in the blood, because the kidney is failing. It can damage the heart and brain.
- **Renal failure.** The kidney stops working. The treatment is **haemodialysis**: the patient's blood is taken out, passed through a machine with a **semi-permeable membrane**, and the nitrogenous wastes diffuse into a **dialysis fluid** with the same composition as plasma but with no wastes; the cleaned blood returns, treated with an anticoagulant (heparin). A **kidney transplant** is the final remedy; the new kidney from a close relative reduces the chance of rejection.
- **Renal calculi.** Hard crystals of **calcium oxalate** and other salts (kidney stones) form in the renal pelvis and can block the ureter.
- **Glomerulonephritis.** Inflammation of the glomeruli, often after an infection; proteins and blood leak into the urine.

**Beyond the syllabus.** Doctors measure kidney health by estimating the GFR from the blood level of **creatinine**, a waste from muscle. A GFR below 60 mL per minute for three months suggests chronic kidney disease (KDIGO). The best protection is controlling blood pressure and blood sugar.

<div class="keypoints">

- Ammonotelic (fishes), ureotelic (mammals, adult frogs) and uricotelic (birds, reptiles, insects) animals.
- Kidney: cortex and medulla; about a million nephrons; nephron = renal corpuscle (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule) + tubule (PCT, loop of Henle, DCT) → collecting duct. Juxtamedullary nephrons make concentrated urine.
- Urine formation: ultrafiltration (GFR 125 mL/min, 180 L/day), selective reabsorption (65-70 % of water in the PCT) and secretion (H⁺, K⁺, NH₃).
- Counter-current mechanism: loop of Henle and vasa recta create a gradient of 300 to 1200 mOsm/L, letting humans make urine four times as concentrated as blood.
- Control: ADH (water), RAAS (renin, angiotensin II, aldosterone raise Na⁺ and pressure), ANF (lowers pressure).
- Micturition is a reflex. Other organs: lungs (CO₂), liver (bile pigments), skin (sweat, sebum).
- Disorders: uremia, renal failure (haemodialysis, transplant), renal calculi, glomerulonephritis.

</div>
