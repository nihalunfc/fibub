**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 1 · about 45 minutes.** By the end you will be able to say what makes something alive, explain why we need a naming system, write scientific names correctly, place an organism in the seven ranks of classification, and use herbaria, museums and keys.

<div class="bigidea">There are millions of kinds of living things, and a mango is called "aam" in Hindi, "manga" in Malayalam and "mango" in English. Biology fixes this with two ideas: a single scientific name for each kind, and a ladder of groups (kingdom down to species) so that every organism has a place. What counts as "alive" is the first question; the rest of the course is built on the answer.</div>

## 1. What is living?

Ask a child "what is alive?" and you hear: it moves, it grows, it eats. Biologists look for properties that are true of **all** living things.

- **Growth.** A living organism grows from **inside**: cells divide and the body gets bigger. A snowball or a crystal also "grows", but only by material sticking on from outside, so growth alone is not proof of life. (Some living things do not grow after maturity, and in single-celled organisms growth and reproduction are the same event.)
- **Reproduction.** Organisms make new individuals like themselves. A mule cannot reproduce, yet it is alive, so reproduction is not a defining property of each individual.
- **Metabolism.** All the chemical reactions in a body (making food, breaking it down for energy, building parts). **Metabolism is found in every living thing and nothing non-living.** Even a dry seed or a sleeping bear keeps a slow metabolism.
- **Cellular organisation.** All living things are made of one or more cells, the unit of life.
- **Consciousness (response to the environment).** Every organism senses its surroundings (light, water, touch, temperature) and responds. Plants turn to light and shut leaves at night; humans are also aware of themselves.

<div class="myth">

**Myth: "If it moves, it is alive; if it does not move, it is not."** A car and a river move, and are not alive. A tree, a sleeping seed and a spore do not visibly move, and are alive. Movement is not the test; metabolism and cells are.

<div class="meaning">

**What does "living" mean for a virus?** A virus has no cells and no metabolism of its own, and it can be crystallised like a salt. Inside a host cell it uses the cell's machinery to make copies. So many biologists call it non-living outside a host. You meet viruses in Chapter 2.

</div>

## 2. Diversity in the living world

Scientists have named about **1.7 to 1.8 million** species of plants, animals and other organisms. Careful estimates say the true number is several times more, since many insects, fungi, bacteria and deep-sea animals are still unknown. India alone has crowds of them: tigers in Sundarbans, lion-tailed macaques in the Western Ghats, snow leopards in Ladakh, thousands of flowering plants in the Northeast.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-living-diversity"><figcaption>If the named species were only 100 dots, about 50 would be insects. Pick a group to light up its dots.</figcaption></figure>

Learning about all these, one by one, is impossible. We need three things: (1) a **name** that everybody uses, (2) a **description** of each kind, and (3) a **group** system. Naming and grouping are the job of **taxonomy**, and finding out the identity of an organism is **identification**.

## 3. Naming: binomial nomenclature

Common names change with the language and the place. In the north "kachha aam" and in Kerala "pacha manga" can both mean green mango; "jackal" or "wolf" can mean different animals in different villages. A biologist in Japan and one in Brazil need one name that works everywhere.

**Carolus Linnaeus** (Sweden, 1700s) gave the world a simple rule: every organism gets a name of **two words**, the **genus** and the **species epithet**. This is **binomial nomenclature**. The *mango* is *Mangifera indica*; a human is *Homo sapiens*; the tiger is *Panthera tigris* and the lion *Panthera leo*: same genus, so they are close relatives.

The rules are worked out by international codes: the **ICBN** (now the code for algae, fungi and plants) for plants and the **ICZN** for animals.

1. The name is in **Latin form**, whatever its origin.
2. The first word is the **genus** and starts with a **capital** letter. The second is the **species epithet** and starts with a **small** letter.
3. In print the name is in **italics**. Handwritten, **underline** the two words separately.
4. The name of the person who first described the species may follow, in plain type, often shortened: *Mangifera indica* Linn. (L. or Linn. = Linnaeus).

## 4. Taxonomic categories (the ranks)

**Classification** is putting organisms into groups on the basis of shared features. Each group is a **taxon** and its level is a **category or rank**. There are seven main ranks, from the largest to the smallest:

**Kingdom → Phylum (Division in plants) → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species**

- **Species**: a group of individuals that are very alike and can breed with each other to make fertile offspring.
- **Genus**: a group of related species with common features. *Panthera* has the lion, tiger, leopard and jaguar. Potato and brinjal are in *Solanum*; this shows they share flower features.
- **Family**: related genera. Cat, lion and tiger genera are in the family Felidae (the cat family). Potato and brinjal's family is Solanaceae.
- **Order**: related families. Cats, dogs and bears are in **Carnivora** (flesh-eaters).
- **Class**: related orders; Primates and Carnivora both fall in the class **Mammalia** (they feed milk to young).
- **Phylum / Division**: related classes. Mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish have a backbone, and belong to **Chordata**. Plants use the word **Division**.
- **Kingdom**: all animals in **Animalia**; all plants in **Plantae**.

## 5. Taxonomical aids

To identify an unknown organism, we compare it with something already known. Some aids keep samples and information safe.

- **Herbarium.** A store of dried, pressed and labelled plant specimens on sheets, filed by family, so that botanists anywhere can compare. It also keeps **records** of where and when a plant was found; the labels carry name, family, place, date and collector. Follow the steps below to make one.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-living-herbarium">
<div class="step">Collect a twig with leaves and a flower or fruit, and note the place and date.</div>
<div class="step">Press the plant flat between newspaper sheets under a weight.</div>
<div class="step">Change the damp paper until the plant is dry; a treatment keeps pests away.</div>
<div class="step">Mount the dry plant on a stiff herbarium sheet.</div>
<div class="step">Fix the label and file the sheet in its family cupboard.</div>
</div>

- **Botanical garden.** Collections of living plants, each with a label of its common and scientific name. Famous examples: the Indian Botanic Garden in Howrah, the Lalbagh in Bengaluru and Kew Gardens in London. They help in study and conservation.
- **Museum.** Collections of preserved plants and animals for study and teaching. Specimens are dried, or kept in jars with preservative. Insects are pinned in boxes, and larger animals can be **stuffed** (taxidermy).
- **Zoological park (zoo).** Places where wild animals are kept in conditions like their natural homes, with food habits and behaviour recorded for study.
- **Key.** A printed or digital set of contrasting **couplets** (two choices) used to identify an organism. The first couplet divides all the candidates in two; each next one splits the remainder, until one remains. A separate key is made for each taxonomic category (family, genus and species); the keys are based on the contrasting features of the group.

<div class="history">

**A short timeline of naming.**

- Early 1500s to 1600s: botanists in Europe, and Indian works much earlier, list plants by use and look, with long descriptive names.
- 1735 to 1758: **Linnaeus** publishes *Systema Naturae* and *Species Plantarum*, fixing two-word names.
- 1800s: international codes appear for plants (ICBN) and animals (ICZN).
- Today: DNA sequences help check whether two organisms are really the same species.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Living things have **cellular organisation and metabolism**; growth is from inside (cells divide); reproduction and movement are not defining on their own.
- A **mule** is alive though sterile, and a **crystal** grows from outside, so neither growth nor reproduction alone decides.
- About 1.7 to 1.8 million species are named; many more are unnamed.
- **Binomial nomenclature** (Linnaeus): genus (capital) + species epithet (small); italics in print, underline by hand; author name follows.
- Ranks: **Kingdom, Phylum/Division, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species**. Higher rank: bigger group, fewer shared features.
- A **taxon** is a group at any rank; **taxonomy** names and classifies; **systematics** studies relationships.
- Aids: herbarium, botanical garden, museum, zoological park, keys (couplets), flora, manuals, monographs.

</div>
