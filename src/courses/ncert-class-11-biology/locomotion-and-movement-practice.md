**Practice · Class 11 Biology · Chapter 17.** Read the chapter lesson, [Locomotion and Movement](/learn/courses/ncert-class-11-biology/locomotion-and-movement/), first. Try each question on paper before opening the answer.

<div class="bigidea">For muscle questions, always tell the story in order: nerve signal, acetylcholine, Ca²⁺, troponin, tropomyosin moves, cross-bridge, power stroke, ATP releases. For bone questions, learn the numbers as a sum: axial 80 = 29 + 26 + 1 + 24 and appendicular 126 = 4 × 30 + 4 + 2, and the total is 206.</div>

## How to set out answers

1. For the sliding filament theory, draw a sarcomere before and after contraction and mark the A band, I band, H zone and Z lines.
2. For comparisons (red and white fibres; the three kinds of muscle), use a table.
3. For joints, give the name, the movement it allows and an example.
4. For numbers, write the relation (for example H zone = sarcomere length − 2 × thin filament length) and then substitute.

## 1-mark questions

1. Name the contractile proteins of a muscle.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Actin (thin filaments) and myosin (thick filaments).</details>

2. What is a sarcomere?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The part of a myofibril between two successive Z lines; the functional unit of contraction.</details>

3. Name the joint at the elbow.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A hinge joint.</details>

4. How many pairs of floating ribs does a human have?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Two pairs (the 11th and 12th).</details>

5. Which ion binds to troponin to begin contraction?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Calcium ion (Ca²⁺).</details>

## 2-mark questions

6. Differentiate between skeletal and smooth muscle with respect to striations and control.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Skeletal muscle is striated (striped) and voluntary; smooth muscle is non-striated and involuntary.</details>

7. Why does the body become stiff after death?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>After death, no ATP is made. Without ATP the myosin heads cannot detach from actin, so the cross-bridges stay locked: rigor mortis.</details>

8. What are the red muscle fibres? Give one feature.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Red (slow-twitch) fibres have a lot of myoglobin and many mitochondria; they respire aerobically, contract slowly and tire only after a long time.</details>

9. Name the bones of the pectoral girdle.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The clavicle (collar bone) and the scapula (shoulder blade) on each side: four bones in all.</details>

10. What causes tetany?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A low level of Ca²⁺ in the body fluids, which causes rapid, uncontrolled spasms of the muscles.</details>

## 3-mark questions

11. Describe the structure of a thin filament.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A thin filament is made of two strands of the protein actin twisted around each other, like two strings of beads. Along the grooves lies a double strand of tropomyosin, and at regular intervals there is a troponin complex. At rest, tropomyosin covers the sites on actin where the myosin heads attach.</details>

12. List the three kinds of joint by structure, with an example of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Fibrous (no movement): sutures of the skull. Cartilaginous (slight movement): joints between the vertebrae. Synovial (free movement, with a fluid-filled cavity): knee, shoulder, elbow.</details>

13. A sarcomere is 3.0 µm long at rest and 2.2 µm when contracted. What percentage did it shorten, and what is the width of the H zone at rest? (A band 1.6 µm, thin filaments 1.0 µm.)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Shortening: `$(3.0 - 2.2) \div 3.0 \times 100 = 26.7$` %. H zone at rest = 3.0 − 2 × 1.0 = 1.0 µm.

</details>

14. Why is it that the A band does not change in length when a muscle contracts?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The A band is the length of the thick myosin filaments. In contraction the thick filaments do not shorten; the thin filaments slide inward over them. Only the I band and H zone narrow and the Z lines come closer.</details>

## 5-mark questions

15. Explain the sliding filament theory of muscle contraction, step by step.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, which sets up an action potential in the sarcolemma. This makes the sarcoplasmic reticulum release Ca²⁺. Ca²⁺ binds troponin, and tropomyosin moves away from the myosin-binding sites on the actin. Myosin heads, which hold ADP and Pi, bind to actin and form cross-bridges. The head tilts (the power stroke), pulling the actin filament toward the centre of the sarcomere and releasing ADP and Pi. A new ATP binds the head so that it lets go; the ATP is split and the head returns to its cocked position and repeats. Repeated cycles pull the thin filaments inward: the Z lines come closer, the I band and H zone shorten, the A band stays the same. When the nerve signal ends, Ca²⁺ goes back to the reticulum, tropomyosin covers the sites and the muscle relaxes.</details>

16. Describe the structure of the human axial skeleton, with the number of bones in each part.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The axial skeleton has 80 bones: the skull (29: cranium 8, face 14, hyoid 1, ear ossicles 6), the vertebral column (26: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 1 sacral, 1 coccygeal), the sternum (1) and 12 pairs of ribs (24). The first 7 pairs of ribs are true ribs; the next 3 are false; the last 2 are floating. Ribs, sternum and thoracic vertebrae together form the rib cage.</details>

17. Name the five types of synovial joints, say what movement each allows and give an example.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ball and socket (all directions: shoulder, hip); hinge (one plane: knee, elbow); pivot (rotation: atlas and axis); gliding (sliding: between carpals); saddle (two planes: base of the thumb).</details>

18. Describe four disorders of the muscular and skeletal system.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Myasthenia gravis: autoimmune attack on acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, so muscles are weak and tire quickly. Muscular dystrophy: genetic progressive destruction of skeletal muscle. Osteoporosis: loss of bone mass, fragile bones, common after menopause because of the fall in oestrogen. Gout: uric acid crystals in a joint cause painful inflammation. Tetany and arthritis are other examples.</details>

## Case-based question

*Savitri, aged 64, slipped at home and broke her wrist. A scan showed that her bones were thin and porous. The doctor told her that bone mass in women falls faster after menopause, and advised calcium, vitamin D, sunshine and daily walking.*

19. (a) Name the disorder. (b) Why does it become common after menopause? (c) How does exercise help? (d) Name two food sources of calcium.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) Osteoporosis. (b) The hormone oestrogen falls after menopause, and it normally helps to keep bone mass, so bone is lost faster. (c) Weight-bearing exercise stimulates bone formation and slows loss. (d) Milk and curd, ragi, green leafy vegetables, sesame seeds (any two).</details>

## Assertion-reason

Choose: (a) both true and R explains A; (b) both true but R does not explain A; (c) A true, R false; (d) A false, R true.

20. **A:** Muscles contract only in response to a nerve signal. **R:** A motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a). The acetylcholine starts the action potential in the sarcolemma.</details>

21. **A:** In the sliding filament theory the thick filaments shorten. **R:** The thin filaments slide inward.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(d). A is false (the filaments do not shorten); R is true.</details>

22. **A:** The vertebral column of an adult human has 26 bones. **R:** The five sacral vertebrae fuse into one and the four coccygeal vertebrae fuse into one.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a). 7 + 12 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 26, and the fusion explains the lower count.</details>
