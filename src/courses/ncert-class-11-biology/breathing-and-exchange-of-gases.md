**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 14 · about 75 minutes.** By the end you will be able to follow air through the human respiratory system, explain how we breathe in and out, use lung volumes and capacities, explain how O₂ and CO₂ cross the lungs and travel in the blood, say how breathing is controlled and name the main lung disorders.

<div class="bigidea">Every cell needs O₂ for respiration and must get rid of CO₂. The respiratory system moves air in and out (breathing), gases cross a very thin wall between air and blood (diffusion down a pressure difference), and blood carries them to and from the cells. Nothing is pumped across the thin wall: gases simply move from where their pressure is higher to where it is lower.</div>

## 1. Respiratory organs

Small animals can breathe through the skin or body surface (earthworm), insects use tracheal tubes, fishes use gills, and land vertebrates use lungs. In humans the **respiratory tract** has a **conducting part** and a **respiratory part**.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-breath-tract">
<div class="step">Nostrils and the nasal chamber.</div>
<div class="step">Pharynx and larynx.</div>
<div class="step">Trachea.</div>
<div class="step">Bronchi.</div>
<div class="step">Bronchioles.</div>
<div class="step">Alveoli.</div>
</div>

The two **lungs** sit in the thoracic (chest) cavity, an airtight chamber bounded by the ribs and sternum in front, the backbone behind, and the **diaphragm** below. Each lung is covered by a double membrane, the **pleura**, with a little fluid between the layers to reduce friction. Because the chest cavity is airtight, anything that changes its volume changes the volume of the lungs.

The wall of an alveolus is only one cell thick, next to the thin wall of a capillary. These two layers, with the tissue between them, form the **diffusion membrane**, only about 0.5 µm thick in all.

## 2. Mechanism of breathing

Breathing has two stages: **inspiration** (air in) and **expiration** (air out). Air moves because of a **pressure gradient**: it flows from higher to lower pressure, and the lung pressure is made lower or higher than the atmosphere by changing the chest volume (Boyle's law: if volume goes up, pressure goes down).

- **Inspiration:** the **diaphragm contracts** and flattens, and the **external intercostal muscles** contract and lift the ribs and sternum. The chest cavity grows in both directions, the lungs expand, and the pressure inside falls below atmospheric. Air rushes in.
- **Expiration:** the diaphragm and the external intercostals relax. The chest returns to its smaller size, the lung pressure rises above atmospheric and air flows out. (Extra abdominal and internal intercostal muscles can help in forced breathing.)

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-breathing-mechanics"><figcaption>Watch the diaphragm and ribs. The volume rises and the pressure falls as the lungs fill, and the reverse on the way out. Try forced breathing.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“The lungs pull the air in by themselves.”** Lungs have no muscle. They are passively stretched by the muscles of the chest and the diaphragm; air is pushed in by the higher outside pressure.

</div>

### Lung volumes and capacities

A **spirometer** records the air breathed in and out. A healthy adult breathes about 12 to 16 times a minute.

| Name | Meaning | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal volume (TV) | air in or out in a normal breath | about 500 mL |
| Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) | extra air that can be breathed in after a normal inspiration | 2500 to 3000 mL |
| Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) | extra air that can be breathed out after a normal expiration | 1000 to 1100 mL |
| Residual volume (RV) | air left after the strongest expiration | 1100 to 1200 mL |

Capacities are sums: **inspiratory capacity** IC = TV + IRV; **expiratory capacity** EC = TV + ERV; **functional residual capacity** FRC = ERV + RV; **vital capacity** VC = ERV + TV + IRV (the biggest breath out after the biggest breath in); **total lung capacity** TLC = VC + RV (about 5800 to 6000 mL).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-breath-spiro"><figcaption>The trace rises on breathing in and falls on breathing out. Change the tidal volume and the residual volume and read the capacities in the text. "Deep" shows one maximal breath.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c11b-breath-vc"></div>

**Ventilation.** The air breathed per minute is the **pulmonary ventilation** (rate × tidal volume, about 12 × 500 = 6000 mL, or 6 L, per minute). About 150 mL of every breath stays in the airways (**dead space**), where no gas exchange happens, so only the rest reaches the alveoli: **alveolar ventilation** = rate × (TV − 150).

## 3. Exchange of gases

Gas exchange takes place in the **alveoli** (between air and blood) and in the **tissues** (between blood and cells). It is by **simple diffusion**, driven by the difference in **partial pressure**: the pressure that one gas of a mixture would have by itself (pO₂, pCO₂). The rate depends on:

- the pressure gradient of each gas,
- the **solubility** of the gas (CO₂ dissolves about 20 to 25 times more easily than O₂, so the CO₂ crosses faster even though its gradient is smaller),
- the **thickness** of the membrane (thin is good) and its **surface area** (big is good: about 70 m²).

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-breath-gradient">
<div class="step">The atmosphere: high pO₂, almost no CO₂.</div>
<div class="step">The alveoli: pO₂ 104, pCO₂ 40.</div>
<div class="step">Blood in the lung picks up O₂, gives out CO₂.</div>
<div class="step">Tissues take O₂ from the blood and give it CO₂.</div>
<div class="step">Blood goes back to the lung.</div>
</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-alveolar-exchange"><figcaption>A red cell moving along a lung capillary takes up O₂. Try exercise, a thicker membrane or the thin air at 5000 m, and see how far the blood gets to being full.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“Blood leaving the lungs is full of O₂ and blood leaving the tissues has none.”** Oxygenated blood is about 97 % saturated and returning blood is still about 75 % saturated: the tissues take only about a quarter at rest. There is a big reserve for exercise.

</div>

## 4. Transport of gases

**Oxygen.** About 97 % travels bound to **haemoglobin** in red blood cells (as oxyhaemoglobin), and about 3 % dissolved in plasma. Each haemoglobin has four haem groups, so each binds up to four O₂ molecules. A normal 100 mL of blood carries about 20 mL O₂. At the tissues about 5 mL per 100 mL is released.

The **oxygen dissociation curve** plots the % saturation against pO₂. It is S-shaped because binding is **co-operative**: after the first O₂ binds, the next ones bind more easily. The steep part lies between pO₂ 20 and 50, right where tissues work, so a small fall in pO₂ releases a lot of O₂. Higher pCO₂, **lower pH (acid)** and **higher temperature** shift the curve to the right, so haemoglobin gives up more O₂ in active tissue (the **Bohr effect**); in the lungs, the opposite. Foetal haemoglobin has a curve to the left of the adult's so it takes O₂ from the mother's blood.

**Carbon dioxide.** CO₂ is carried in three ways: about **70 % as bicarbonate** (HCO₃⁻) in the plasma, **20 to 25 % as carbamino-haemoglobin** and about **7 % dissolved**. In the red cells the enzyme **carbonic anhydrase** (rich in red cells, also found in small amounts in plasma) speeds up CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. In the tissues the reaction goes to the right; at the alveoli, to the left.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-breath-co2">
<div class="step">Tissue CO₂ enters the blood.</div>
<div class="step">7 % dissolves in the plasma.</div>
<div class="step">20 to 25 % binds to haemoglobin.</div>
<div class="step">70 % becomes bicarbonate.</div>
<div class="step">All reversed in the lungs.</div>
</div>

<dl>
<dt>Partial pressure</dt><dd>the share of the total pressure due to one gas. Air at sea level is 760 mm Hg, and 21 % is O₂, so pO₂ is about 159 mm Hg.</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Gases flow "downhill" in pressure, like water in a pipe, and each gas follows its own slope whatever the others do. O₂ goes in and CO₂ goes out at the same moment.</dd>
</dl>

## 5. Regulation of respiration

Breathing is controlled by the brain. The **respiratory rhythm centre** in the **medulla** sets the basic rhythm. The **pneumotaxic centre** in the pons can switch off inspiration early, giving a faster rate. Close to the rhythm centre is a **chemosensitive area**, highly sensitive to **CO₂ and H⁺**; when they rise, it signals the rhythm centre to make breathing deeper and faster to get rid of CO₂. Receptors in the **aortic arch and carotid artery** also sense CO₂ and H⁺ and send messages. The O₂ has a much smaller role in control.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-breath-control">
<div class="step">CO₂ and H⁺ rise.</div>
<div class="step">Chemosensitive area and arterial receptors detect the change.</div>
<div class="step">The rhythm centre speeds up breathing.</div>
<div class="step">Muscles move more air; the pneumotaxic centre fine-tunes.</div>
</div>

## 6. Disorders of the respiratory system

- **Asthma:** inflammation and narrowing of the bronchi and bronchioles gives wheezing and difficult breathing (often an allergy to dust, pollen, smoke).
- **Emphysema:** the walls between alveoli are damaged, so there is less surface for gas exchange; the main cause is **cigarette smoking**, and it is a form of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
- **Occupational respiratory disorders:** dust in some industries (grinding or stone-breaking: **silicosis**; asbestos: **asbestosis**) causes inflammation, fibrosis and then lung cancer. Workers in these industries should use protective masks.

<div class="keypoints">

- Path of air: nostrils → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli (the exchange surface).
- Inspiration: diaphragm and external intercostals contract, thoracic volume rises, lung pressure falls below atmospheric. Expiration is the reverse.
- TV 500, IRV 2500-3000, ERV 1000-1100, RV 1100-1200 mL; VC = TV + IRV + ERV; TLC = VC + RV; alveolar ventilation = rate × (TV − 150).
- Gases cross by diffusion according to partial pressure: pO₂ 104 (alveoli) → 95 (blood) → 40 (tissue); pCO₂ 45 (tissue) → 40 (alveoli).
- O₂: 97 % bound to haemoglobin (4 per Hb, S-shaped curve; right shift with CO₂, H⁺, temperature). CO₂: 70 % bicarbonate, 20-25 % carbamino-Hb, 7 % dissolved.
- Control: rhythm centre (medulla), pneumotaxic centre (pons), chemosensitive area sensing CO₂ and H⁺.
- Disorders: asthma, emphysema, silicosis and asbestosis.

</div>
