**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 15 · about 80 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the parts of blood, match blood groups for transfusion, explain clotting and lymph, follow blood through the heart and the double circulation, read a cardiac cycle and an ECG, and name the common heart disorders.

<div class="bigidea">Cells are far from the air and the gut, so a transport system brings them O₂ and food and carries wastes away. In humans a pump (the heart) pushes blood, a fluid tissue, through a closed set of tubes, and blood goes round twice on each lap: once through the lungs, once through the body. The heart keeps its own time with a built-in pacemaker.</div>

## 1. Blood

Blood is a fluid **connective tissue**: cells (formed elements) in a liquid matrix, **plasma**. A healthy adult has about 5 litres of blood.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-circ-blood"><figcaption>Blood after spinning in a centrifuge: plasma on top, a thin layer of white cells and platelets, and red cells at the bottom. Move the slider to see anaemia and thick blood.</figcaption></figure>

**Plasma** is about 55 % of the blood, straw-coloured, and about 90 to 92 % water. It carries **proteins** (**fibrinogen** for clotting, **globulins** for defence, **albumins** for osmotic balance), nutrients, hormones, salts and wastes. **Serum** is plasma without the clotting factors (the clot has used up the fibrinogen).

**Formed elements** are about 45 % of the blood:

- **Red blood cells (erythrocytes, RBCs):** 5 to 5.5 million per mm³; biconcave discs, **no nucleus** in mammals, packed with **haemoglobin** (an iron-containing pigment, 12 to 16 g per 100 mL of blood). They live about 120 days; the **spleen** (the "graveyard") removes old ones. Made in the red bone marrow. Low haemoglobin is **anaemia**.
- **White blood cells (leucocytes, WBCs):** 6000 to 8000 per mm³, colourless with a nucleus, for defence. **Granulocytes** are **neutrophils** (most numerous, about 60 to 65 %, phagocytes), **eosinophils** (about 2 to 3 %, allergic reactions, parasites), and **basophils** (the least, 0.5 to 1 %, release histamine, serotonin and heparin). **Agranulocytes** are **lymphocytes** (20 to 25 %, B and T cells make the immune response) and **monocytes** (6 to 8 %, large phagocytes).
- **Platelets (thrombocytes):** 1.5 to 3.5 lakh per mm³, pieces of megakaryocyte cells; they help in **clotting**. A low count (as in dengue) can cause bleeding.

<div class="myth">

**“Blood is red because all of it is red cells.”** More than half of blood is pale yellow plasma. The red colour comes from haemoglobin inside the red cells only.

</div>

## 2. Blood groups

Landsteiner found in 1900 that mixing blood of some people causes **agglutination** (clumping), which led to the ABO groups.

**ABO grouping** depends on two **antigens** (surface proteins) on the red cell, A and B. The plasma has the **natural antibodies** against the antigen the person does not have.

| Group | Antigen on red cells | Antibodies in plasma | Can give to | Can receive from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | A | anti-B | A, AB | A, O |
| B | B | anti-A | B, AB | B, O |
| AB | A and B | none | AB | all (universal recipient) |
| O | none | anti-A and anti-B | all (universal donor) | O |

A donor's red cells must not carry an antigen that the recipient's plasma has an antibody against, or the donor's cells will clump. The alleles Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i control the antigens: it is an example of multiple alleles and co-dominance (see the Class 12 chapter on inheritance).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-circ-abo"><figcaption>Choose a recipient, then see which donor groups are safe. Add the Rh factor to see why Rh⁻ people must be careful.</figcaption></figure>

**Rh grouping.** About 80 % of humans have another antigen, first found in the rhesus monkey, called **Rh factor**; they are **Rh positive**. Others, **Rh negative**, make anti-Rh antibodies only after they are exposed to Rh⁺ blood.

**Rh incompatibility in pregnancy:** if an Rh⁻ mother carries an Rh⁺ foetus, a little foetal blood mixes with hers at delivery, and she makes anti-Rh antibodies. In a **second** pregnancy with an Rh⁺ baby, these antibodies cross the placenta and attack the baby's red cells (**erythroblastosis foetalis**), which can cause severe anaemia or death. The mother is given **anti-Rh antibodies right after the first delivery** to remove the foetal cells before she makes her own.

## 3. Coagulation of blood

When a vessel is cut, blood clots to stop bleeding. The steps are shown below.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c11b-circ-coag">
<div class="step">Injury and platelets.</div>
<div class="step">Thromboplastin released.</div>
<div class="step">Prothrombin to thrombin (with Ca²⁺).</div>
<div class="step">Fibrinogen to fibrin.</div>
<div class="step">The clot.</div>
</div>

The clot is a network of **fibrin** threads in which dead and damaged red and white cells are trapped (a scab). Calcium ions are needed at several steps; vitamin K is needed to make some of the clotting factors in the liver. A blood clot that forms **inside** a vessel, such as in a coronary artery, is a **thrombus** and can cause a heart attack.

## 4. Lymph (tissue fluid)

As blood flows through the capillaries, some water and small solutes (not the big proteins or red cells) leak out into the spaces between cells. This is the **tissue fluid** (interstitial fluid), and it is what the cells really bathe in: O₂ and nutrients diffuse from it into the cells, and CO₂ and wastes diffuse out. Most returns to the blood, and the rest goes into tiny **lymph capillaries**, which join to larger **lymph vessels** and finally empty into the large veins near the heart. The fluid in these is **lymph**.

Lymph is like plasma but has **less protein**, and it carries many **lymphocytes** and carries **fats** absorbed in the intestine (in the lacteals). It passes through **lymph nodes**, which filter it and make lymphocytes, so it is a part of the body's defence.

## 5. Circulatory pathways

Animals have either an **open** circulatory system (cockroach and other arthropods, molluscs: the blood leaves the vessels and flows in open spaces) or a **closed** one (annelids and chordates: the blood stays in vessels, and so it can be at a high pressure and be controlled). In vertebrates the heart has 2 chambers in fishes (single circulation), 3 in amphibians and most reptiles (incomplete double circulation, with some mixing), and 4 in crocodiles, birds and mammals (complete double circulation).

### The human heart

The heart is a hollow, muscular organ, the size of one's fist, in the thoracic cavity between the lungs, tilted slightly left, in a double-layered sac, the **pericardium**. It has four chambers: two thin-walled **atria** above, which receive blood, and two thick-walled **ventricles** below, which pump it out. The **septum** separates the right and left sides, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix. **Valves** keep the blood moving one way: the **tricuspid** valve (three flaps) between the right atrium and ventricle, the **bicuspid (mitral)** valve (two flaps) on the left, and **semilunar valves** at the base of the pulmonary artery and the aorta. The left ventricle has the thickest wall, as it pumps blood to the whole body.

### The nodal tissue: the heart's own pacemaker

The heart is **myogenic**: the beat starts in the heart muscle and does not need nerve impulses to begin. Special **nodal tissue** has this power. The **sinoatrial node (SAN)** in the upper right atrium starts the beat and sets the rate (about 70 to 75 a minute), so it is the **pacemaker**. The impulse spreads over the atria, reaches the **atrioventricular node (AVN)**, which delays it a little, and then goes down the **bundle of His**, its right and left branches and the **Purkinje fibres** to the ventricles.

### The cardiac cycle

The events of one heartbeat make the **cardiac cycle**. At 72 to 75 beats a minute it lasts about **0.8 s**:

1. **Atrial systole (0.1 s):** the atria contract and fill the ventricles (the ventricles are already about 70 % full).
2. **Ventricular systole (0.3 s):** the ventricles contract; the pressure shuts the AV valves ("**lub**") and then opens the semilunar valves, so that blood is pumped into the pulmonary artery and the aorta.
3. **Joint diastole (0.4 s):** all chambers relax; the semilunar valves close ("**dub**"); the AV valves open and blood flows into the atria and ventricles again.

### Electrocardiogram (ECG)

An **ECG** records the electrical activity of the heart through electrodes on the skin (the standard leads are on both wrists and the left ankle). Each beat gives:

- the **P wave**: depolarisation of the atria (just before atrial systole);
- the **QRS complex**: depolarisation of the ventricles (just before ventricular systole);
- the **T wave**: repolarisation (return to rest) of the ventricles.

The atria repolarise during the QRS complex and are hidden. The ECG counts the heart rate (the number of QRS complexes a minute) and reveals abnormal rhythms and damage.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-ecg-trace"><figcaption>P, QRS and T line up with the chambers lit in the small heart. A dropped beat shows a P wave with no QRS after it (heart block).</figcaption></figure>

## 6. Double circulation

Blood passes through the heart **twice** in each complete lap. The **pulmonary circuit** carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle through the pulmonary artery to the lungs and back as oxygenated blood to the left atrium through the pulmonary veins. The **systemic circuit** carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle through the aorta to the body and returns deoxygenated blood via the vena cavae to the right atrium.

<div class="steps" data-fig="@bio-double-circulation">
<div class="step"><b>Right atrium.</b> Deoxygenated blood arrives from the vena cavae.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Right ventricle.</b> It pumps the blood out at low pressure.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pulmonary artery.</b> The blood goes to the lungs. This is the only artery that carries deoxygenated blood.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Lungs.</b> The blood picks up O₂ and loses CO₂.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pulmonary veins.</b> Oxygenated blood returns to the left atrium.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Left ventricle.</b> The thick wall pushes blood out at high pressure.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Aorta.</b> Branches carry the blood to the organs.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Tissues and back.</b> The blood gives up O₂ and returns via the vena cavae to the right atrium.</div>
</div>

<!--fig:tool heart-circulation-->

Two special loops: the **hepatic portal system** (blood from the intestine goes through the liver by the hepatic portal vein before returning to the heart, so that the liver can take up the nutrients) and the **coronary circulation** (the heart muscle itself is fed by the coronary arteries and drained by the coronary veins).

## 7. Regulation of cardiac activity

The cardiac cycle is **myogenic**, but it is regulated by the **cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata** of the brain through the autonomic nervous system. **Sympathetic** nerves increase the heart rate, the strength of the contraction and so the cardiac output. **Parasympathetic** nerves (the vagus) slow the rate and reduce the force of contraction. **Adrenaline** (and noradrenaline) from the adrenal medulla also increases the cardiac output, which is why the heart pounds in fear or exams.

## 8. Disorders of the circulatory system

- **High blood pressure (hypertension):** blood pressure is measured as systolic over diastolic, normal about **120/80 mm Hg** (120 is the pressure when the ventricles contract, 80 when they relax). If it is repeatedly **140/90 or above**, the person has hypertension. It damages the heart, brain and kidneys.
- **Coronary artery disease (CAD), atherosclerosis:** deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol and fibrous tissue narrow the coronary arteries and cut the blood supply of the heart muscle.
- **Angina (angina pectoris):** chest pain when the heart muscle gets too little O₂, often with the effort; a sign of poor blood supply; it can come when the arteries are narrowed.
- **Heart failure:** the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs (it is not the same as a heart attack, though the heart attack may cause it), with fluid congestion. **Cardiac arrest** is a sudden stop of the heart; a **heart attack** is damage to the muscle due to a blocked coronary artery.

<div class="myth">

**“Heart failure means the heart has stopped.”** It means the heart is still beating but is too weak to meet the needs of the body. When the heart has stopped, it is cardiac arrest.

</div>
