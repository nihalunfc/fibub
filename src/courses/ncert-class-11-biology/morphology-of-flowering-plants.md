**Biology · Class 11 · Chapter 5 · about 75 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the root, stem, leaf, inflorescence, flower, fruit and seed of a flowering plant, name their modifications with examples, and write the floral formula of a flower.

<div class="bigidea">Morphology is the study of external form. A flowering plant is a shoot system (stem, leaves, flowers) over a root system, and every part can be changed to do something else: store food, climb, protect, breathe or catch insects. Learn the basic plan first, then each change is just that part with a new job. The flower is the plant's reproductive shoot, and its parts can be written as a short code, the floral formula.</div>

## 1. The root

The root system is usually underground. It **fixes** the plant, **absorbs** water and minerals and, in some plants, **stores** food or helps in other ways.

**Types of root systems.**

- **Tap root system:** the **radicle** (first root of the seedling) grows into the primary root, with secondary and tertiary branches. Found in dicots (mustard, gram, mango).
- **Fibrous root system:** the radicle dies early and many thin roots of the same kind come from the base of the stem. Found in monocots (wheat, rice, grass).
- **Adventitious roots:** roots from parts other than the radicle, such as the stem or leaves (grass, banyan prop roots, the leaf of *Bryophyllum*, money plant).

**Modifications of roots.**

| Modification | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Storage roots (tap root: **conical**, **fusiform** or **napiform**) | store food | carrot, radish (fusiform), turnip (napiform), beet |
| **Prop roots** (from branches) | support the branches | banyan |
| **Stilt roots** (from lower nodes, slanting) | support the stem | maize, sugarcane |
| **Pneumatophores** (grow upward) | take in oxygen in swampy soil | *Rhizophora* (mangrove) |

## 2. The stem

The stem is the part of the shoot system that bears **nodes** (where leaves arise) and **internodes** (spaces between). It carries buds, leaves, flowers and fruits, and transports water, minerals and food. A **terminal bud** at the tip makes the stem grow longer; **axillary buds** in the angle between a leaf and the stem can grow into branches or flowers.

**Modifications of stems.** Slide through the gallery of root, stem and leaf modifications. It covers every example in this chapter.

| Modification | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Tuber** (underground, swollen tip) | stores food | potato |
| **Rhizome** (thick horizontal underground stem) | stores food, survives dry seasons | ginger, turmeric |
| **Corm** | stores food | *Colocasia*, banana |
| **Bulb** (fleshy leaves on a small stem) | stores food | onion |
| **Stem tendril** (thin coiled branch) | climbing | cucumber, pumpkin, grape vine |
| **Thorn** (hard pointed branch) | protection | *Citrus*, *Bougainvillea* |
| **Phylloclade** (flat, green, fleshy) | photosynthesis | *Opuntia* |
| **Runner** (creeping on the ground) | vegetative spread | grass, *Oxalis* |
| **Stolon / sucker** (lateral branch that bends down and roots) | spread | mint, jasmine |

<div class="myth">

**Myth: "A potato is a root because it grows underground."** The potato is a stem tuber: it has "eyes" (buds in the axils of scale leaves), nodes and no root cap. Carrot is the real root: it has no eyes. Where it grows does not decide; what it is made from does.

</div>

## 3. The leaf

A **leaf** is a flattened lateral outgrowth of the stem, which arises at a node and has a bud in its axil. It is the chief organ of **photosynthesis**. A typical leaf has three parts: the **leaf base** (which may have two small **stipules** at its sides in dicots, or form a sheath round the stem in monocots), the **petiole** (stalk) and the **lamina** (blade) with a **midrib** and veins.

**Venation** is the pattern of veins in the lamina. **Reticulate** (a net of veins): dicots such as peepal, mango. **Parallel** (veins side by side): monocots such as wheat, grass, banana.

**Types of leaves.** A **simple** leaf has one undivided blade (mango). A **compound** leaf has the blade cut into **leaflets**; the buds are in the axils of the leaves, but not in the axils of leaflets. In a **pinnately compound** leaf the leaflets lie on a common axis, the **rachis** (neem). In a **palmately compound** leaf the leaflets join at one point at the tip of the petiole (silk cotton).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-morph-leaf"><figcaption>Switch venation and leaf type. The orange bud in the axil marks a true leaf, which is how you tell a compound leaf from a branch with many simple leaves.</figcaption></figure>

**Phyllotaxy** is the arrangement of leaves on the stem: **alternate** (one leaf at each node: China rose, mustard, sunflower), **opposite** (two leaves at each node, facing each other: *Calotropis*, guava) or **whorled** (more than two leaves at a node: *Alstonia*, the devil tree).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-phyllotaxy"><figcaption>Top view of alternate, opposite and whorled leaf arrangement with a slider for the number of nodes. Notice how each pattern reduces shading of the leaf below.</figcaption></figure>

**Modifications of leaves:** **tendrils** (pea) for climbing, **spines** (cactus) for protection and less water loss, **fleshy leaves** that store food (onion, garlic), and **pitchers** (*Nepenthes*) and traps (*Utricularia*, bladderwort) in insectivorous plants. In some plants, such as *Australian acacia*, the petioles become flat and green like leaves (**phyllodes**) and do photosynthesis.

## 4. The inflorescence

The **inflorescence** is the arrangement of flowers on the floral axis. The axis may keep growing or end in a flower, which gives two main types.

- **Racemose:** the main axis has unlimited growth. Flowers are borne **laterally** in an **acropetal** order: the oldest at the base, the youngest near the tip (mustard, radish, *Crotalaria*).
- **Cymose:** the main axis ends in a flower, so its growth is limited. The flowers open in **basipetal** order: the oldest at the top or the centre, the youngest below or outside (*Bougainvillea*, cotton, jasmine).

## 5. The flower

The flower is the **reproductive unit** of an angiosperm. A flower has a **pedicel** (stalk), a **thalamus** (receptacle) and four whorls: **calyx** (sepals, usually green), **corolla** (petals, often coloured to attract pollinators), **androecium** (the male organs, stamens: **anther** with **filament**) and **gynoecium** (female organs, carpels: **stigma, style, ovary**).

**Symmetry.** **Actinomorphic** (radial; can be cut into equal halves by any vertical plane through the centre: mustard, datura, chilli) or **zygomorphic** (bilateral; only one plane: pea, gulmohur, bean, *Cassia*). Some are **asymmetric** (canna).

**Sexuality.** **Bisexual** (both stamens and carpels) or **unisexual** (only one kind: cucumber, papaya, maize).

**Parts and position.** The calyx and corolla together are the **perianth** when not different (lily: **tepals**). **Bracteate** flowers have a leaf-like bract at the base of the stalk; **ebracteate** flowers do not. A **trimerous**, **tetramerous** or **pentamerous** flower has its parts in threes, fours or fives.

**Position of the ovary.** Slide the whorls and switch the ovary position.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-morph-flower"><figcaption>Add the whorls one at a time, then change the ovary position: superior (the other whorls below), half-inferior (round it) or inferior (above it).</figcaption></figure>

**Aestivation.** The arrangement of sepals or petals in the bud: **valvate** (edges touch, *Calotropis*), **twisted** (each overlaps the next, China rose, cotton), **imbricate** (irregular overlap, cassia, gulmohur) and **vexillary** (the standard petal covers the wings, which cover the keel: pea and bean).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c11b-morph-aestivation"><figcaption>The four types of aestivation, drawn as a top view of the petals in a bud.</figcaption></figure>

**Androecium.** **Stamens** may be free, or joined: by the filaments (**monadelphous**, in one bundle: China rose; **diadelphous**, two bundles: pea; **polyadelphous**, many: *Citrus*), or by the anthers only (**syngenesious**, sunflower). Stamens joined to the petals are **epipetalous** (brinjal).

**Gynoecium.** **Apocarpous** (carpels free, e.g. lotus, rose) or **syncarpous** (carpels fused, e.g. mustard, tomato). The ovary has **ovules** attached to a flap of tissue, the **placenta**, and the arrangement is **placentation**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-placentation"><figcaption>Ovary cross-sections showing marginal, axile, parietal, free-central and basal placentation.</figcaption></figure>

| Placentation | Where ovules are attached | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal | along the ventral suture of a one-chambered ovary | pea |
| Axile | on a central axis in a multichambered ovary | China rose, tomato, lemon |
| Parietal | on the inner wall of a one-chambered ovary | mustard, *Argemone* |
| Free central | on a central column with no partitions | *Dianthus*, primrose |
| Basal | at the base of a one-chambered ovary | sunflower, marigold |

### The floral formula

The floral formula is a short code that gives symmetry, sex, the number and fusion of each whorl and the ovary position. The symbols: **Br** bracteate; **⊕** actinomorphic; **%** zygomorphic; **⚥** bisexual; **K** calyx, **C** corolla, **P** perianth, **A** androecium, **G** gynoecium; a number in **brackets** means fused; a **line under G** means a superior ovary, a **line over G** an inferior ovary; **+** separates whorls of one kind.

For example: **mustard** ⊕ ⚥ K2+2 C4 A2+4 G̲(2) and **tomato** ⊕ ⚥ K(5) C(5) A5 G̲(2). Build one yourself and see the floral diagram appear.

## 6. The fruit

The fruit is the **mature ovary**, which develops after fertilisation. If a fruit forms with no fertilisation, it is **parthenocarpic** (banana). The wall of the fruit is the **pericarp**, in three layers in fleshy fruits: **epicarp** (outer), **mesocarp** (middle) and **endocarp** (inner). In the **mango** and the coconut (**drupes**, from a single-seeded ovary) the mesocarp is fleshy or fibrous and the endocarp is stony. Follow the changes from a flower to a fruit.

## 7. The seed

The seed is the fertilised, mature **ovule**. It has a **seed coat** (outer **testa**, inner **tegmen**) and an **embryo** with an **embryonal axis** (the **plumule** is the future shoot, the **radicle** the future root) and **cotyledons**.

- **Dicot seed** (bean, gram): two cotyledons store food, **no endosperm** in the mature seed (non-endospermic); a **hilum** (scar where the seed was attached) and a tiny pore, the **micropyle**.
- **Monocot grain** (maize): the seed coat is fused with the fruit wall; a large **endosperm** (food), with an outer **aleurone layer**; one cotyledon, the **scutellum**; the plumule has a sheath, the **coleoptile**, and the radicle a sheath, the **coleorhiza**.

## 8. Describing a plant in "semi-technical" language

Botanists describe a plant in a fixed order: **habit** (tree, shrub, herb), **vegetative** characters (root, stem, leaf), then **floral** characters (inflorescence, flower with its calyx, corolla, androecium, gynoecium), followed by the **floral formula** and **floral diagram**. Use that order when you describe any plant in an exam.

<div class="keypoints">

- Roots: tap (dicot), fibrous (monocot), adventitious. Tip regions: root cap, meristematic, elongation, maturation (root hairs). Modifications: storage, prop, stilt, pneumatophore.
- Stems: tuber, rhizome, corm, bulb; tendril, thorn, phylloclade, runner. Leaves: reticulate or parallel venation; simple, pinnate, palmate; phyllotaxy alternate, opposite or whorled; modified to tendrils, spines, pitchers.
- Inflorescence: racemose (acropetal, unlimited axis), cymose (basipetal, limited axis).
- Flower: calyx, corolla, androecium, gynoecium; symmetry, sexuality, ovary position (hypogynous, perigynous, epigynous); aestivation (valvate, twisted, imbricate, vexillary); placentation (marginal, axile, parietal, free central, basal).
- Floral formula: ⊕ or %, ⚥, K, C, A, G; brackets for fusion; line under G for superior ovary.
- Fruit = ripened ovary (pericarp with epi-, meso-, endocarp). Seed = ovule; dicot (2 cotyledons, no endosperm), monocot (1 cotyledon, endosperm).

</div>
