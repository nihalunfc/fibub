**Module 8 · about 12 minutes.** The essay comes first on test day, so it sets your mood for the rest of the test. A calm, practised plan makes it the easiest part.

<div class="bigidea">The Issue essay rewards a clear position, backed by reasons and specific examples, that answers the exact instructions. Fancy words do not rescue a weak argument.</div>

## 1. The task in one picture

You see a short claim, such as *"Governments should spend more on science research than on the arts"*, and under it one set of instructions. You have **30 minutes** to write an essay in a plain text box (no spell checker). There is no right side: readers score how well you argue, not what you believe.

The instructions come in a few fixed shapes. Each asks you to do something slightly different, and answering the wrong one is the commonest way to lose marks.

| The instructions say | What you must also do |
|---|---|
| Discuss how far you agree, and consider ways the claim might or might not hold | Show when the claim is true and when it is not |
| Discuss how far you agree, and address the most compelling reasons someone might disagree | Answer the other side's best reason |
| Discuss how far you agree, and consider specific circumstances in which following the recommendation would or would not be wise | Name situations where the advice helps or harms |
| Discuss which view more closely matches your own (two views given) | Weigh both views, then choose |

<div class="principle">

**Your one rule:** underline the verb and the extra demand in the instructions before you plan. Every body paragraph should do what they ask.

</div>

## 2. What the readers reward

Each essay is read by a trained reader and by a scoring program. If the two disagree by much, a second person reads it. The final score runs from **0 to 6 in half points**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="issue-scale"><figcaption>Tap what an essay does. Watch how the likely band moves, and how ignoring the task caps it.</figcaption></figure>

In plain words, the score bands mean:

- **6 and 5:** a clear, thoughtful position; reasons that build; specific, well-chosen examples; logical paragraphs; clear sentences with few errors.
- **4:** a clear position with relevant reasons, but examples that are general or thin, and some loose structure.
- **3 and below:** vague or missing position, reasons that repeat, no real examples, or errors that make sentences hard to follow.

<div class="myth">

**"Longer is always better."** Length helps only because strong essays need room for reasons and examples. Padding with repeated points lowers the score.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I must use famous quotations and rare words."** Readers want reasoning. One plain, specific example from history, science, work or everyday life beats a quotation you cannot connect to your point.

</div>

## 3. Where examples come from

You need two or three examples that you can describe in a few sentences. Build a small store before test day so you never stare at a blank box:

<div class="steps" data-title="Build an example store in one evening">
<div class="step">Pick one example from science (a vaccine, the internet, a space mission).</div>
<div class="step">Pick one from history or public life (a law, a reform, a famous decision).</div>
<div class="step">Pick one from work or study (a project, a team, a mistake you learned from).</div>
<div class="step">For each, write three lines: what happened, why it mattered, and which ideas it can support (progress, risk, leadership, education, government).</div>
</div>

Personal examples are allowed. A specific story from your own work, told in four sentences, is better than a vague "many people believe".

<div class="keypoints">

- One 30-minute Issue essay, scored 0 to 6 in half points.
- Answer the exact instructions: they change what the body must do.
- Position, reasons, specific examples, structure, then clear language.
- Keep a small store of examples you can adapt to any prompt.

</div>

## Practise: sharpen the reasoning

Readers reward essays that see what a claim takes for granted. Train that eye with five argument questions: spot the unstated assumption in each.
