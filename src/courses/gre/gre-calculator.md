**Module 2 · about 9 minutes.** By the end you will reach for the calculator only when it saves time.

<div class="bigidea">The calculator is for ugly arithmetic, not for thinking. Most GRE questions are built so that a smart step makes the numbers easy.</div>

## 1. What it does

A basic on-screen calculator: the four operations, square root, a memory and brackets, plus **Transfer Display**, which copies the result into a Numeric Entry box. On the [ETS structure page](https://www.ets.org/gre/test-takers/general-test/prepare/test-structure.html) it is listed as part of Quantitative Reasoning only.

## 2. When to use it, when not

| Use it | Skip it |
|---|---|
| percent of a messy number (17.3% of 4,860) | comparisons that simplify (49 × 51 against 50²) |
| square roots that are not perfect squares | fractions that cancel |
| long data-interpretation sums | "which is greater" with unknowns |

<div class="steps" data-title="The brackets trap">
<div class="step">You want the cost of 3 + 4 items at 5 dollars each: 35 dollars.</div>
<div class="step">You type 3 + 4 × 5. The calculator follows the order of operations and multiplies first: it shows 23.</div>
<div class="step">Fix: type (3 + 4) × 5, or add first and then multiply. Always estimate first, so a wrong display jumps out.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**"With a calculator I don't need mental maths."** Typing takes time and invites slips. The [GMAT mental maths lesson](/learn/courses/gmat/mental-maths/) trains the quick estimates that make the calculator a backup, not a crutch.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Calculator: four operations, square root, memory, brackets, Transfer Display.
- Estimate first so you notice a wrong result.
- The calculator follows the order of operations: use brackets to say what you mean.
- Simplify before you calculate: many questions need no calculator at all.

</div>

## Practise: geometry with and without the calculator

Circle areas to the nearest tenth need the calculator; triangles and angles do not.
