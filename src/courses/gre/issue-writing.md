**Module 8 · about 14 minutes, plus one timed essay.** With a plan in hand, writing is mostly turning each line into a paragraph.

<div class="bigidea">Reason, example, tie-back. Repeat three times, answer the other side, conclude, then check.</div>

## 1. Split the 30 minutes

<figure class="fig" data-fig="essay-timer"><figcaption>Move the sliders to split the time. About 5 minutes planning and 3 checking leaves 22 for writing.</figcaption></figure>

## 2. Build a body paragraph

<div class="steps" data-title="Reason, example, tie-back">
<div class="step"><b>Topic sentence:</b> state the reason in one sentence. "Public research pays back in health and jobs."</div>
<div class="step"><b>Example:</b> two or three sentences of specific detail. "Many vaccines began in publicly funded labs. Their development took years of work no private buyer would have paid for at the start."</div>
<div class="step"><b>Tie-back:</b> one or two sentences on why it proves your point. "This shows that money spent on research can return far more than it costs, which is why it deserves the larger share."</div>
</div>

A full sample paragraph, written for the prompt *"Students learn more from their mistakes than from their successes"*:

<div class="principle">

The first reason mistakes teach more is that a mistake points to exactly what needs fixing. When a student keeps an error log while preparing for an exam, each wrong answer names a topic: percents, perhaps, or misreading a question. A correct answer says only that something went right, often without saying why. The error, by contrast, gives the student a precise next step. In this sense, a mistake that is studied is worth more than a success that is simply enjoyed.

</div>

## 3. Link ideas and vary sentences

Readers follow your argument through linking words. Use them at the start of paragraphs and between ideas:

| To | Use |
|---|---|
| Add a reason | Moreover, In addition, A second reason |
| Give an example | For instance, Consider, A clear case is |
| Contrast | However, Yet, By contrast, Admittedly |
| Show a result | Therefore, As a result, This shows that |
| Conclude | In the end, Overall |

Mix short and longer sentences. Avoid "I think" in every line: your position is clear from the essay. Use words you are sure of; a rare word used wrongly costs more than it gains.

<div class="myth">

**"A conclusion should add a new idea."** It should not. Restate the position and its limit in two sentences, and stop.

</div>

## 4. Check in the last 3 minutes

<div class="steps" data-title="The 3-minute check">
<div class="step">Read the introduction: is the position clear in the first two sentences?</div>
<div class="step">Skim each topic sentence: does each give a different reason?</div>
<div class="step">Fix missing words, doubled words and typos (there is no spell checker).</div>
</div>

## 5. Write one timed essay

Pick one prompt you planned in lesson 8.2, set a 30-minute timer, and type it in a plain text editor with spelling checks turned off. Then score it with this list, one point each, and aim for 6:

1. Clear position in the introduction, with a limit.
2. Three distinct reasons, one per paragraph.
3. A specific example in each body paragraph.
4. A tie-back sentence in each body paragraph.
5. The extra demand in the instructions answered.
6. Few errors, and linking words between paragraphs.

Write one essay a week in the last month before the test. The [study plan](/learn/courses/gre/gre-study-plan/) leaves room for it.

<div class="keypoints">

- About 5 minutes planning, 22 writing, 3 checking.
- Every body paragraph: reason, specific example, tie-back.
- Link ideas clearly and use words you are sure of.
- Practise under time with no spell checker.

</div>

## Practise: sharpen the reasoning

Strong essays avoid the reasoning flaws readers notice: a small sample, cause from a coincidence, a false choice. Practise spotting them.
