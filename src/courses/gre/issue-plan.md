**Module 8 · about 14 minutes.** Strong essays are planned. Five minutes of planning turns the writing into filling in boxes.

<div class="bigidea">Decide your position and your three reasons before you write one sentence. A plan you can see is an essay you can finish.</div>

## 1. The five-minute plan

<div class="steps" data-title="Plan on scrap paper">
<div class="step"><b>Read and underline (30 seconds).</b> The claim, and the extra demand in the instructions.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Brainstorm both sides (1 minute).</b> Two columns: reasons for, reasons against. Write words, not sentences.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pick your side (30 seconds).</b> The column with the better reasons and examples. "Mostly agree, with a limit" is fine and often strongest.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Choose three reasons and an example for each (2 minutes).</b> One line each.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Write the limit or the other side (1 minute).</b> The best point against you, and your answer to it.</div>
</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="issue-builder"><figcaption>Choose a prompt and a side to see a full five-minute plan. Notice that the "other side" box answers the instructions.</figcaption></figure>

## 2. The shape of the essay

| Paragraph | What it does | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Your position, with its limit, and a hint of your reasons | 3 to 4 sentences |
| Body 1, 2, 3 | One reason each: state it, give the example, explain why the example proves it | 5 to 7 sentences |
| Other side | The best opposing point, why it has some truth, and why your position still holds | 4 to 5 sentences |
| Conclusion | Restate the position and the limit | 2 sentences |

The phrase *"explain why the example proves it"* is where most essays lose a point. Do not just tell the story: tie it back with a sentence such as *"This shows that ..."*.

<div class="principle">

**Worked example.** Prompt: *"Students learn more from their mistakes than from their successes."* Instructions: address the most compelling reasons someone might disagree.

1. Position: mostly agree, but only when a mistake is followed by feedback.
2. Reasons: a mistake shows exactly what to fix (error logs in exam practice); mistakes are remembered (a lab unit error); success can hide luck (a guessed right answer).
3. Other side: success builds the confidence to keep going. Answer: true for beginners, yet confidence without correction repeats errors.
4. Conclusion: mistakes teach most, provided someone explains them.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Taking both sides fully is the safe choice."** An essay that never decides reads as having no position. Choose a side and admit its limit.

</div>

## 3. Practise the plan

Plan each of these original prompts in five minutes with a timer. Only plan: writing comes in lesson 8.3.

1. *"The best way to understand a society is to study its everyday life, not its leaders."* Consider ways the claim could be true and false.
2. *"Every student should be required to learn a second language."* Consider circumstances in which the requirement would or would not be wise.
3. *"Technology has made people less patient."* Address the most compelling reasons someone might disagree.
4. *"Leaders should be judged mainly by their results, not their methods."* Discuss how far you agree.
5. *"Cities should ban private cars from their centres."* Consider circumstances in which the policy would or would not be wise.

Check each plan against this list: a clear position; three different reasons (not one reason said three ways); a specific example for each; the extra demand answered.

<div class="keypoints">

- Read, brainstorm both sides, pick, then three reasons with examples, then the other side.
- One reason per body paragraph, always tied back to the position.
- A qualified position is still a clear position.

</div>
