**Module 5 · about 18 minutes · read first: [2.4](/learn/courses/lsat/conditional-logic/), [2.5](/learn/courses/lsat/quantifiers/).** By the end you will pick the one answer that the passage proves, and see through the four that only sound right.

<div class="bigidea">The passage is the evidence, the answer is the conclusion. A Must Be True answer can be proven word for word from the passage, usually by combining two statements. If you can imagine the passage true and the answer false, the answer is wrong.</div>

## 1. The question stem

*"If the statements above are true, which one of the following must also be true?"* or *"...can be properly inferred from the statements above?"* Often the passage is just a set of facts, with no conclusion of its own.

## 2. Combine, then prove

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Passage:</b> "Every apartment in the Elm building has a balcony. None of the apartments with balconies is rented to tenants with large dogs. Some apartments in the Elm building are on the top floor."</div>
<div class="step">Chain the first two: Elm → balcony → no large-dog tenant.</div>
<div class="step">Add the third: some top-floor apartments are in Elm, so those have balconies and no large-dog tenants.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Provable:</b> "Some top-floor apartments are not rented to tenants with large dogs." Not provable: "No top-floor apartment has a large dog" (other buildings' top floors are not covered).</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Must be true</dt><dd>true in every situation where all the passage's statements are true</dd>
<dt>The "could it be false?" test</dt><dd>imagine the passage true and the answer false; if you can, eliminate it</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>You are a careful accountant: only what is written in the books counts, however reasonable a guess about the rest might be.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

## 3. Wrong-answer patterns

- **New information.** Mentions something the passage never discusses.
- **Too strong.** "All", "always", "never" when the passage says "some" or "most".
- **Reversed conditional.** The converse or inverse of a rule in the passage.
- **Probably true, not proven.** Sensible in real life, but the passage does not force it.

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: a full question</summary>

"Only students who complete the safety course may use the woodshop. Everyone who uses the woodshop must also wear goggles. Lin has not completed the safety course."

If the statements above are true, which one of the following must also be true?

(A) Lin does not use the woodshop.
(B) Lin does not wear goggles.
(C) Everyone who wears goggles uses the woodshop.
(D) Lin will take the safety course soon.
(E) Most students complete the safety course.

**Answer: (A).** Woodshop → safety course. Lin has not completed it, so (contrapositive) Lin does not use the woodshop. (B) is the inverse of the goggles rule applied to Lin; Lin might wear goggles elsewhere. (C) reverses the goggles rule. (D) and (E) are new information.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"The answer that goes furthest is the best inference."** The safest answer is usually the best. Modest answers ("some", "at least one", "not all") are easier to prove.

</div>
