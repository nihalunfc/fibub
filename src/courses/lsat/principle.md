**Module 3 · about 18 minutes · read first: [2.4](/learn/courses/lsat/conditional-logic/), [3.2](/learn/courses/lsat/sufficient-assumption/).** By the end you will match principles to cases and pick the principle that justifies an argument.

<div class="bigidea">A principle is a general rule, usually a conditional. Questions either give you the rule and ask which case fits it, or give you a case and ask which rule would make its conclusion follow.</div>

## 1. Two kinds of principle question

| Stem | What to do |
|---|---|
| "Which principle, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning?" | Find a general rule that bridges premise to conclusion (like a sufficient assumption). |
| "Which judgment conforms most closely to the principle stated above?" | Translate the principle into "if ..., then ...", and find the case where the "if" is met and the "then" is done. |

## 2. Matching a case to a rule

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Principle:</b> "A journalist should name a source only if the source agreed to be named and naming them puts no one at risk."</div>
<div class="step">Translate: <b>name the source → (source agreed AND no one at risk)</b>. Both are necessary.</div>
<div class="step">A case "conforms" if a journalist named a source who agreed and when no one was put at risk, <b>or</b> if a journalist <i>declined</i> to name a source who had not agreed.</div>
<div class="step">A case "violates" it if a journalist named a source who had not agreed, or named one and put someone at risk. Note: the rule never says journalists <i>must</i> name sources who agree. Declining is also allowed.</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Conforms</dt><dd>the case is consistent with the rule (and usually shows it being applied)</dd>
<dt>Justifies</dt><dd>the rule, plus the facts in the argument, makes its conclusion follow</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>A principle is a law of conduct. Matching a case to it is like a judge checking whether each condition of a law is met before applying it.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: a justify question</summary>

"Marta borrowed her neighbour's ladder without asking because she needed to rescue a cat stuck on her roof during a storm, and her neighbour was away. Marta acted acceptably."

Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify this judgment?

(A) Borrowing property without permission is never acceptable.
(B) It is acceptable to use someone's property without asking if there is an urgent need and the owner cannot be asked in time.
(C) Neighbours should always lend each other tools.
(D) Rescuing animals is more important than any other duty.
(E) People should own the tools they need for emergencies.

**Answer: (B).** Its two conditions (urgent need, owner unavailable) match the facts, and it gives the judgment (acceptable). (A) opposes the judgment. (C) is about lending, not using without asking. (D) is too sweeping and not about property. (E) is advice, not a judgment about Marta.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"The principle that sounds most moral is right."** The test is a logical match, not which rule sounds kindest. A principle can sound fine and still fail to cover the facts.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Translate principles into conditionals and identify every condition.
- "Conforms" cases meet the conditions and follow the result, or avoid the action whose necessary condition fails.
- "Justify" questions are sufficient assumption questions in general terms.
- Check every condition; one unmatched condition makes the answer wrong.
