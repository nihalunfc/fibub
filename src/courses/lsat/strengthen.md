**Module 3 · about 20 minutes · read first: [3.1](/learn/courses/lsat/necessary-assumption/).** By the end you will pick the fact that most helps an argument, in the two ways the LSAT tests most.

<div class="bigidea">Every argument has a gap between its evidence and its claim. A strengthener narrows that gap: it supports the assumption the argument relies on, or knocks out a rival explanation.</div>

## 1. The question stem

*"Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?"* or *"...provides the most support for the conclusion?"* The words <b>if true</b> mean: accept every answer as a fact, then ask which one helps most.

## 2. Two ways to strengthen

<div class="steps" data-fig="arg-map" data-p1="Since the city added late-night buses, fewer drunk-driving arrests have been made on weekends." data-c="The late-night buses reduced drunk driving." data-a="Nothing else (such as fewer police patrols) explains the drop in arrests." data-x="The number of police patrols on weekend nights was the same before and after the buses were added." data-xs="+">
<div class="step" data-show="">"Since the city added late-night buses, fewer drunk-driving arrests have been made on weekends. So the buses reduced drunk driving."</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 c">Evidence: arrests fell after the buses started. Claim: the buses caused less drunk driving.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 c a">The gap: arrests could fall for other reasons. Maybe there were fewer patrols to make arrests.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 c a x">A strengthener closes that door: patrols were the same, so the drop in arrests is more likely a real drop in drunk driving. <b>Ruling out an alternative</b> is the most common strengthener.</div>
</div>

The other common way is to **confirm the assumption** directly ("many people who used to drive home from bars now take the late bus") or to **add more of the same evidence** ("arrests also fell in another city after it added late buses").

## 3. Method

1. Find the conclusion and the evidence.
2. Name the gap in your own words (the assumption, or the rival explanation).
3. Before reading the answers, predict: "the right answer will show ..."
4. Pick the answer that narrows the gap. Ignore answers that are only about the topic, or that support a premise the LSAT already treats as true.

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: a full question</summary>

"Patients at Riverside Clinic who joined a weekly walking group had lower blood pressure a year later than patients who did not join. The walking group therefore helps lower blood pressure."

Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?

(A) Riverside Clinic has more patients than most clinics in the region.
(B) Before joining, the walkers' blood pressure was about the same as that of patients who did not join.
(C) Some patients who joined the group stopped attending after a few weeks.
(D) Walking is more popular than running among older adults.
(E) Patients who did not join the group were offered a free cooking class.

**Answer: (B).** A rival explanation is that healthier patients chose to join. (B) rules that out: both groups started level, so the later difference points to the walking. (C) slightly weakens. (A) and (D) are off topic. (E) raises a new possible cause for the non-joiners, which muddies rather than helps.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"The answer that proves the conclusion is the only right one."** Often no answer proves it. Pick the one that helps most. An answer that helps a little is better than one that is merely related to the topic.

</div>

- Accept every answer as true; pick the one that most increases support for the conclusion.
- Strengthen by confirming the assumption, ruling out a rival explanation, or adding similar evidence.
- Predict the answer from the gap before you read the choices.
- Ignore answers that are merely on topic or only repeat a premise.
