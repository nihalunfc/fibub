**Module 2 · about 18 minutes · read first: [1.1](/learn/courses/lsat/how-the-lsat-works/).** By the end you will split any short argument into its conclusion, its premises and its background, which is the first step of nearly every Logical Reasoning question.

<div class="bigidea">An argument is a claim plus the reasons given for it. Find the claim (the conclusion) and the reasons (the premises), and you can see exactly what the argument depends on.</div>

## 1. Claims and reasons in everyday life

"Take an umbrella. The forecast says heavy rain this afternoon." The speaker wants you to accept one thing (take an umbrella) and gives a reason (the forecast). That is an argument:

- **Conclusion**: the claim the author wants you to accept.
- **Premise**: a statement offered as a reason to accept it. On the LSAT, premises are treated as true: you attack the <i>link</i>, not the facts.
- **Background**: context that sets the scene but is not a reason (who is talking, what the debate is about).

Not every paragraph is an argument. A list of facts with no claim drawn from them ("The museum opened in 1921. It has three floors.") is not one. Questions about such passages ask what you can <i>infer</i> (Module 5), not what is assumed.

## 2. Watch an argument come apart

<div class="step" data-show="">Read it: "Last year the town painted bike lanes on Main Street, and since then bicycle accidents there have fallen by half. So the town should paint bike lanes on all of its busy streets."</div>
<div class="step" data-show="c">Find the claim. "So" points to it: <b>the town should paint bike lanes on all busy streets.</b> That is what the author wants us to accept.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 c">The first reason: bike lanes were painted on Main Street.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 p2 c">The second reason: accidents there fell by half. The two premises together are the evidence.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 p2 c a">Now look at the gap. The evidence is about <b>one</b> street; the claim is about <b>all</b> busy streets. The argument quietly assumes Main Street is typical. Finding gaps like this is what Module 3 is about.</div>

## 3. Indicator words

| Introduce a conclusion | Introduce a premise |
|---|---|
| therefore, thus, so, hence, consequently | because, since, for, given that |
| it follows that, clearly, this shows that | as shown by, after all, in view of the fact that |
| we can conclude that, accordingly | the reason is that, as |

Words like *should*, *must*, *probably* and *it is likely that* often mark a conclusion too, because they make a claim that needs support.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Conclusion</dt><dd>what the author is trying to prove</dd>
<dt>Premise</dt><dd>evidence given for it, accepted as true on the LSAT</dd>
<dt>Background</dt><dd>context that helps you understand but supports nothing</dd>
<dt>Gap</dt><dd>the distance between what the premises show and what the conclusion claims</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Premises are the floor the argument stands on; the conclusion is how high it tries to reach. The bigger the jump from one to the other, the more the argument is assuming.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

## 4. The "therefore test"

When there are no indicator words, try each statement as the conclusion: "[The others], <b>therefore</b> [this one]." The version that makes sense is the right one.

"Wool keeps you warm even when it is wet. Hikers in rainy regions should wear wool socks." Try it: "Wool keeps you warm when wet, therefore hikers should wear wool socks" makes sense. The reverse ("hikers should wear wool socks, therefore wool keeps you warm when wet") does not.

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: find the conclusion</summary>

"Many people assume that coffee dehydrates you. Yet studies of regular coffee drinkers find that their bodies hold on to almost as much water from coffee as from plain water. So for most people, a daily cup of coffee counts towards their fluid intake."

**Answer:** the conclusion is the last sentence: a daily cup of coffee counts towards fluid intake for most people. The studies are the premise. "Many people assume that coffee dehydrates you" is background: the opposing view the author is arguing against, not a reason for the conclusion.

</details>

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: conclusion in the middle</summary>

"The new parking rules will not reduce traffic downtown. After all, most drivers downtown are commuters who already pay for monthly parking passes, and the rules only change the price of hourly parking."

**Answer:** the conclusion comes first: the new rules will not reduce traffic downtown. "After all" introduces the two premises. Conclusions can sit at the start, the middle or the end.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"The conclusion is always the last sentence."** On the LSAT it is often the first sentence, or tucked in the middle, sometimes followed by "after all" or "since". Use indicators and the therefore test, never position alone.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- An argument = conclusion (the claim) + premises (the reasons); background is neither.
- "Therefore, so, thus" point to conclusions; "because, since, for, after all" point to premises.
- With no indicators, use the therefore test.
- Premises are accepted as true; your job is to judge whether they really support the conclusion.
- The gap between evidence and claim is where assumptions, strengthen, weaken and flaw questions live.

</div>
