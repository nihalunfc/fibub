**Module 2 · about 18 minutes · read first: [2.1](/learn/courses/lsat/argument-anatomy/).** By the end you will find the main conclusion of any argument, even when it is hidden among intermediate conclusions and opposing views.

<div class="bigidea">The main conclusion is the one claim that everything else in the argument is there to support. It is supported, but it does not go on to support anything else.</div>

## 1. The question stem

Main Point questions look like: *"Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion drawn in the argument?"* or *"The main point of the argument is that..."*. They are a few per test, but the skill behind them is used in every other question type.

## 2. Intermediate conclusions

Some arguments are chains: a premise supports a claim, and that claim supports another. The claim in the middle is an **intermediate** (or sub-) conclusion.

<div class="steps" data-fig="arg-map" data-p1="The new highway will cut travel time between the two cities by an hour." data-p2="Businesses that save an hour of travel a day can serve more customers." data-c="The highway will boost the region's economy." data-a="Saved time will actually be used for more business, not lost elsewhere.">
<div class="step" data-show="">"The new highway will cut travel time between the two cities by an hour. Since businesses that save an hour of travel a day can serve more customers, more trade will follow. So the highway will boost the region's economy."</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1">Premise: travel time falls by an hour.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 p2">Premise: saving travel time lets businesses serve more customers.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 p2">"More trade will follow" is supported by both premises, <b>and</b> it supports something else. It is an intermediate conclusion.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 p2 c">The last claim, the economy will get a boost, is supported but supports nothing further: the <b>main conclusion</b>.</div>
</div>

**The test:** for each claim, ask "does this support another claim in the passage?" If yes, it is not the main conclusion.

## 3. Opposing views and the author's reply

LSAT authors often start with someone else's view:

"Some nutritionists claim that eating late at night causes weight gain. But in controlled studies, people who ate the same total calories gained the same weight whether they ate late or early. What matters for weight, then, is how much you eat, not when."

The first sentence is an opposing view. The author's main conclusion is the reply: <b>how much you eat matters for weight, not when.</b> Words like *but*, *however*, *yet* and *in fact* often mark the turn from the view being rejected to the author's own.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Main conclusion</dt><dd>supported by the rest, supports nothing else</dd>
<dt>Intermediate conclusion</dt><dd>supported by a premise, and itself supports the main conclusion</dd>
<dt>Opposing view</dt><dd>a claim the author introduces in order to reject it</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Think of the argument as a tower: premises at the bottom, intermediate conclusions in the middle, and the main conclusion on top with nothing resting on it.</dd>

## 4. Wrong answers to expect

1. **A premise**, restated. True, in the passage, and not the point.
2. **The intermediate conclusion.** Supported, but it supports something else.
3. **The opposing view.** The author rejects it.
4. **Too strong or too broad.** The passage says "most patients"; the answer says "all patients".
5. **A new claim** the passage never makes, even if it sounds sensible.

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: a full question</summary>

"City planners argue that the old train station should be torn down to make room for offices. But the station is the only building in the city by its celebrated architect, and visitors come from abroad to see it. Tearing it down would cost the city more in lost tourism than the offices would bring in. The station should therefore be kept."

Which one of the following most accurately expresses the overall conclusion of the argument?

(A) City planners want offices in place of the train station.
(B) The station is the only building in the city by its architect.
(C) Demolishing the station would cost more in lost tourism than the offices would bring in.
(D) The train station should not be demolished.
(E) Buildings by famous architects should never be demolished.

**Answer: (D).** "Therefore" marks it, and everything else supports it. (A) is the opposing view. (B) is a premise. (C) is an intermediate conclusion: it is supported by (B) and supports (D). (E) is far too broad: the passage is about one station.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"The correct answer must use the passage's exact words."** Correct answers usually paraphrase ("should be kept" becomes "should not be demolished"). Wrong answers often copy exact phrases from premises to look familiar.

<div class="keypoints">

- The main conclusion is supported by everything else and supports nothing else.
- An intermediate conclusion is supported and then supports another claim; check every claim with "does this support something?"
- "Some say... but..." usually means the author's own conclusion comes after the turn.
- Reject premises, intermediate conclusions, opposing views and over-broad answers.
