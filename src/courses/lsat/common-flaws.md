**Module 2 · about 28 minutes · read first: [2.1](/learn/courses/lsat/argument-anatomy/), [2.4](/learn/courses/lsat/conditional-logic/).** By the end you will recognise the reasoning errors that appear on almost every LSAT, and the abstract wording answer choices use for each.

<div class="bigidea">The LSAT reuses a short list of reasoning mistakes. Learn each one well enough to name it in a second, and Flaw, Weaken, Assumption and Parallel Flaw questions all get much easier.</div>

## 1. Why learn names

Flaw answer choices are abstract: *"The argument presumes, without providing justification, that a correlation between two things implies that one causes the other."* If you already know "correlation is not causation", you recognise that sentence instantly. Each flaw below has the plain idea, an example, and the answer-choice wording.

## 2. Cause and effect

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Correlation → causation.</b> "Students who eat breakfast get better grades, so breakfast improves grades." Maybe families that make breakfast also help with homework. <i>Wording:</i> "infers a causal relationship from a correlation".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Reversed cause.</b> "People who own exercise bikes are fitter, so the bikes make them fit." Perhaps fit people are the ones who buy bikes. <i>Wording:</i> "fails to consider that the supposed effect may be the cause".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Before, so because.</b> "The team started winning after the new mascot arrived, so the mascot brought luck." Timing alone proves nothing. <i>Wording:</i> "assumes that because one event followed another, the first caused the second".</div>
</div>

## 3. Evidence that is too thin

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Unrepresentative sample.</b> "We asked visitors to the gym's website, and 90% want longer gym hours, so most townspeople do." Website visitors are gym fans. <i>Wording:</i> "draws a conclusion about a group from a sample that may not be representative".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Overgeneralising.</b> "My two neighbours' electric cars broke down, so electric cars are unreliable." <i>Wording:</i> "generalises from too few cases".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Absence of evidence.</b> "No one has proved the old mine is unsafe, so it is safe." <i>Wording:</i> "treats a lack of evidence for a claim as proof that the claim is false".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Possible → certain.</b> "The suspect could have reached the shop in time, so he did." <i>Wording:</i> "takes the mere possibility of something as proof that it occurred".</div>
</div>

## 4. Parts, wholes and numbers

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Part → whole (composition).</b> "Every player is excellent, so the team will be excellent." Excellent players can play badly together.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Whole → part (division).</b> "The company is profitable, so each of its branches is profitable."</div>
<div class="step"><b>Percent versus number.</b> "Crime in Lakeside rose 50% but in Hillview only 10%, so Lakeside has more crime." 50% of 4 cases is 2; 10% of 300 is 30. <i>Wording:</i> "confuses a rate of increase with an absolute amount".</div>
</div>

## 5. Logic errors

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Necessary versus sufficient.</b> "A permit is required to build a deck. Lee has a permit, so Lee will build a deck." The permit is needed, not enough. (The converse error from Lesson 2.4.) <i>Wording:</i> "mistakes a condition required for an outcome for one that guarantees it".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Circular reasoning.</b> "The book is trustworthy because it says it tells the truth." The conclusion is assumed in the premise. <i>Wording:</i> "presupposes the truth of the conclusion it is trying to establish".</div>
<div class="step"><b>False choice.</b> "Either we close the library or we raise taxes." There may be other options. <i>Wording:</i> "presents two options as the only ones when others exist".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Equivocation.</b> "A feather is light. What is light cannot be dark. So a feather cannot be dark." "Light" changes meaning. <i>Wording:</i> "uses a key term in two different senses".</div>
</div>

## 6. Attacking the wrong target

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Attacking the person (ad hominem).</b> "Ms Chen's plan for the river should be rejected: she lost the last election." Her record says nothing about the plan. <i>Wording:</i> "attacks the person making the argument rather than the argument itself".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Straw man.</b> "My opponent wants to reduce the military budget; clearly he wants our country undefended." <i>Wording:</i> "distorts the opponent's position".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Irrelevant authority.</b> "A famous actor says this vitamin cures colds." Fame is not medical expertise. <i>Wording:</i> "relies on the opinion of someone whose expertise is not in the relevant field".</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Flaw</dt><dd>a reason the premises, even if true, do not prove the conclusion</dd>
<dt>"Presumes, without justification"</dt><dd>the classic way answer choices say "it assumes"</dd>
<dt>"Fails to consider"</dt><dd>the argument ignores a possibility that would undercut it</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>A flaw is not a false premise. On the LSAT the premises are accepted as true; a flaw is a broken bridge between true premises and the conclusion.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: name the flaw</summary>

"A survey of the city's tennis club found that 80% of members support building more public tennis courts. Clearly, most of the city's residents want more tennis courts."

**Answer:** unrepresentative sample. Tennis club members are much more likely than the average resident to want courts. Answer-choice wording: "It generalises from a sample that is unlikely to be representative of the population."

</details>

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: name the flaw</summary>

"In towns where more people own smoke alarms, fewer people die in house fires. So installing smoke alarms saves lives. Therefore the council should pay for alarms in every home."

**Answer:** this one is more careful than it looks: it moves from a correlation to a cause. Towns with more alarms may also have newer houses or better fire services. Wording: "It takes a correlation as evidence of a causal link without ruling out other factors."

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"A flaw answer is right if it describes something the argument does."** The answer must describe something the argument does <i>wrong</i>. Arguments often appeal to experts or use examples legitimately; only pick the answer when that move actually weakens the reasoning.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Cause: correlation, reversed cause and "after, so because" do not prove causation.
- Evidence: unrepresentative samples, tiny samples, lack of evidence and mere possibility prove little.
- Parts and numbers: composition, division, and percent versus amount.
- Logic: necessary versus sufficient, circular reasoning, false choice, equivocation.
- Wrong target: attacking the person, straw man, irrelevant authority.
