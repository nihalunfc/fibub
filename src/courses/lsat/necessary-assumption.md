**Module 3 · about 22 minutes · read first: [2.1](/learn/courses/lsat/argument-anatomy/), [2.6](/learn/courses/lsat/common-flaws/).** By the end you will find what an argument quietly depends on and prove it with the negation test.

<div class="bigidea">An assumption is an unstated premise. A necessary assumption is one the argument cannot survive without: if it were false, the conclusion would no longer follow.</div>

## 1. The question stem

*"Which one of the following is an assumption on which the argument depends?"* or *"The argument requires assuming which one of the following?"* The words <b>depends</b>, <b>requires</b> and <b>relies on</b> mark a necessary assumption. (Words like "if assumed, allows the conclusion to be properly drawn" mark the different, sufficient type in Lesson 3.2.)

## 2. Find the gap

<div class="steps" data-fig="arg-map" data-p1="The new reading program raised average test scores at Pine School by 15 points." data-c="Adopting the program at Oak School will raise its scores too." data-a="Oak School is not different from Pine School in ways that would stop the program working.">
<div class="step" data-show="">"The new reading program raised average test scores at Pine School by 15 points. So adopting it at Oak School will raise Oak's scores too."</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1">Premise: it worked at Pine.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 c">Conclusion: it will work at Oak.</div>
<div class="step" data-show="p1 c a">The gap: evidence about Pine, claim about Oak. The argument <b>needs</b> Oak to be similar enough to Pine. That is the necessary assumption.</div>
</div>

## 3. The negation test

To check an answer, negate it (make it say the opposite, as mildly as possible) and ask: does the argument now collapse?

- Answer: "Oak's students are not so different from Pine's that the program could not help them."
- Negated: "Oak's students <i>are</i> so different that the program could not help them." → The argument collapses. ✓ Necessary.
- Answer: "The program will raise Oak's scores by at least 15 points."
- Negated: "It will raise them by less than 15." → The argument still stands (it only claimed scores would rise). ✗ Not necessary.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Necessary assumption</dt><dd>must be true for the conclusion to follow from the premises</dd>
<dt>Negation</dt><dd>the mildest opposite: "all" → "not all"; "some" → "none"; "will" → "will not necessarily"</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>A necessary assumption is a support beam. Remove it (negate it) and the building falls. Decorations can be removed and nothing happens; those are the wrong answers.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

## 4. Two classic patterns

1. **New term in the conclusion.** If the conclusion mentions something the premises never do, the assumption links them. "Mia studied physics, so she will be a good engineer" assumes physics study helps engineering.
2. **Ruling out an alternative.** Arguments from evidence to cause assume nothing else explains the evidence. "Sales rose after the ad campaign, so the ads worked" assumes, for example, that prices were not cut at the same time.

<details class="try">
<summary>Try it: a full question</summary>

"Residents of Brookfield complain about noise from the new airport. But the airport's flights all take off over the lake, away from the town. Therefore, the noise residents hear must come from some other source."

The argument depends on assuming which one of the following?

(A) Noise from aircraft taking off over the lake cannot be heard in Brookfield.
(B) Brookfield residents complain about noise more than residents of other towns.
(C) The airport will soon add flights over the town.
(D) Before the airport opened, residents never complained about noise.
(E) Every source of noise in Brookfield can be identified.

**Answer: (A).** Negate it: "Noise from aircraft taking off over the lake <i>can</i> be heard in Brookfield." Then the airport could be the source after all, and the argument collapses. (B), (C) and (D) are irrelevant to whether this noise comes from the airport. (E) is far more than the argument needs; negating it ("not every source can be identified") does not hurt the argument.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"The right answer must be a strong, decisive statement."** Necessary assumptions are usually the opposite: modest and narrow. A sweeping answer often goes beyond what the argument needs, and negating it leaves the argument standing.

<div class="keypoints">

- A necessary assumption is an unstated premise the argument cannot do without.
- Find the gap: new terms in the conclusion, or alternative explanations the argument ignores.
- Negation test: negate mildly; if the argument collapses, the answer is necessary.
- Expect modest answers; reject sweeping ones and ones about side issues.
