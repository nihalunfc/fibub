**Practice · Class 12 Physics · Chapter 12.** Read the chapter lesson, [Atoms](/learn/courses/ncert-class-12-physics/atoms/), first. Try each question on paper before opening the answer. Board answers are marked in steps, so write the law, the formula in symbols, the numbers with units, then the answer.

<div class="bigidea">Four results carry the chapter: the distance of closest approach r₀ = 2.88Z/K fm, the Rydberg formula 1/λ = R(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²), the Bohr results r ∝ n², v ∝ 1/n, E = −13.6/n² eV, and hν = E_i − E_f. Draw the energy levels and mark the jump before you calculate.</div>

## How to set out a numerical

1. Draw the levels involved and the jump (upper to lower for emission).
2. Write the formula in symbols: 1/λ = R(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²), E_n = −13.6/n² eV, r_n = 0.0529 n² nm.
3. Put in the numbers; keep eV for energies, change to joules or metres only if needed.
4. Name the series from the final level n_f: 1 Lyman, 2 Balmer, 3 Paschen.

## 1-mark questions

1. What did the fact that most alpha particles passed straight through a gold foil show?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>That an atom is mostly empty space.</details>

2. Which series of hydrogen lies in the visible region?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The Balmer series (n_f = 2).</details>

3. What is the ground-state energy of hydrogen?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>−13.6 eV.</details>

4. State Bohr’s quantisation condition for the angular momentum of an electron in a hydrogen atom.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>`mvr = nh/2π`, with n = 1, 2, 3 …</details>

5. What is the ionisation energy of hydrogen?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>13.6 eV.</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

6. (2) Why was Thomson’s plum-pudding model rejected?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In it the positive charge is spread through the atom, so an alpha particle would feel only weak forces and be deflected by a tiny angle. But Geiger and Marsden found that about 1 in 8000 alpha particles was scattered by more than 90°, which needs a strong force from a very small, concentrated positive charge.</details>

7. (2) State two drawbacks of Rutherford’s model.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(i) It cannot explain the stability of the atom: an orbiting electron would radiate and spiral into the nucleus. (ii) It cannot explain the line spectra: the radiation would be continuous.</details>

8. (2) Find the energy of the photon emitted when an electron in hydrogen jumps from n = 3 to n = 2.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$E = 13.6\left(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{9}\right) = 13.6\times\tfrac{5}{36} = 1.89$` eV (this is the red H-alpha line, 656 nm).

</details>

9. (3) Find the longest and shortest wavelengths of the Balmer series. (R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Longest (n_i = 3): `$1/\lambda = R(1/4 - 1/9) = 1.097\times10^7\times5/36$`, so λ = 656 nm.

Shortest (n_i = ∞): `$1/\lambda = R/4$`, so `$\lambda = 4/(1.097\times10^7) = 365$` nm.

</details>

10. (3) An alpha particle of kinetic energy 4.5 MeV is fired straight at a gold nucleus (Z = 79). Find the distance of closest approach.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$r_0 = \dfrac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0K} = \dfrac{2\times79\times1.44\ \text{MeV fm}}{4.5\ \text{MeV}} = 50.6$` fm = 5.1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m.

</details>

11. (3) Find the speed and the radius of the electron in the n = 3 orbit of hydrogen.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$v_3 = 2.19\times10^6/3 = 7.3\times10^5$` m/s and `$r_3 = 0.0529\times 9 = 0.476$` nm.

</details>

## 5-mark questions

12. (5) State Bohr’s postulates for the hydrogen atom. Using them, derive an expression for the radius of the n-th orbit.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Postulates: (1) the electron moves in certain stable (stationary) circular orbits without radiating; (2) the angular momentum in these orbits is `$mvr = nh/2\pi$`; (3) radiation is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between orbits, with `$h\nu = E_i - E_f$`.

Derivation: the electric force gives the centripetal force: `$\dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{ke^2}{r^2}$`, with `$k = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$`, so `$mv^2 = ke^2/r$`. From the second postulate `$v = nh/2\pi mr$`. Substituting: `$m\dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2m^2r^2} = \dfrac{ke^2}{r}$`, which gives

`$r_n = \dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2mke^2} = 0.0529\,n^2$` nm.

The radius grows as n².

</details>

13. (5) Show that the total energy of an electron in the n-th Bohr orbit is −13.6/n² eV. Draw the energy-level diagram of hydrogen and mark the Balmer series.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Kinetic energy: `$K = \tfrac12mv^2 = ke^2/2r$` (from `$mv^2 = ke^2/r$`). Potential energy: `$U = -ke^2/r$`. Total: `$E = K + U = -ke^2/2r$`. Putting `$r_n = 0.0529n^2$` nm gives `$E_n = -\dfrac{ke^2}{2\times0.0529\times10^{-9}\,n^2} = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}$` eV.

Levels: n = 1 at −13.6 eV, n = 2 at −3.40 eV, n = 3 at −1.51 eV, n = 4 at −0.85 eV, n = ∞ at 0. The Balmer series is the set of jumps from n = 3, 4, 5 … down to n = 2.

</details>

## Case-based question

**A neon sign and the Sun.** A student looks at a street lamp through a diffraction grating and sees a few separate bright lines. She then looks at sunlight through the same grating and sees a continuous rainbow with dark lines across it. Her teacher explains that the dark lines include those of hydrogen, and shows her the formula 1/λ = R(1/2² − 1/n²) for the visible ones.

(a) Why does the street lamp give separate lines?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The gas in the lamp is excited, and its electrons fall between certain fixed energy levels, emitting photons of only certain energies, so only certain wavelengths appear.</details>

(b) Why are there dark lines in the Sun’s spectrum?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The cooler gases of the Sun’s outer layers absorb light from the hot interior at exactly the wavelengths they can emit, leaving dark lines.</details>

(c) Find the wavelength of the line for n = 4.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$1/\lambda = 1.097\times10^7(1/4 - 1/16) = 1.097\times10^7\times3/16 = 2.057\times10^6$` m⁻¹, so λ = 486 nm (blue-green, H-beta).

</details>

(d) To which series do these lines belong, and in what region of the spectrum?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The Balmer series (n_f = 2), in the visible region.</details>

## Assertion and reason

Choose: (A) both A and R are true and R explains A; (B) both true, R does not explain A; (C) A true, R false; (D) A false, R true.

14. **Assertion:** Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus. **Reason:** The nucleus contains protons and neutrons, each about 1836 times heavier than an electron.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A). The electrons are very light, so the nucleus has nearly all the mass.</details>

15. **Assertion:** The energy of the electron in a hydrogen atom is negative. **Reason:** The electron is bound to the nucleus: energy has to be supplied to free it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A). E = 0 is “just free”; a bound electron has less energy than that, so the value is negative.</details>

16. **Assertion:** In the hydrogen spectrum the Balmer series is the only one in the visible region. **Reason:** Its lines are made by jumps to the level n = 2.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) (taking the first four lines). The energy gaps of jumps to n = 2 are 1.9 to 3.0 eV, which match visible photons; jumps to n = 1 are ultraviolet and to n = 3 infrared.</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Rutherford’s nucleus: tiny (about 10⁻¹⁵ m), heavy and positive; the atom is mostly empty. r₀ = 2.88Z/K fm.
- Bohr: stationary orbits, mvr = nh/2π, hν = E_i − E_f.
- r_n = 0.0529 n² nm; v_n = 2.19 × 10⁶/n m/s; E_n = −13.6/n² eV (hydrogen-like: r ∝ n²/Z, E ∝ Z²/n²).
- 1/λ = R(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²), R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹. Lyman n_f = 1 (UV); Balmer 2 (visible); Paschen 3 (IR).
- Series limit λ = n_f²/R. Number of lines from level n: n(n − 1)/2.

</div>
