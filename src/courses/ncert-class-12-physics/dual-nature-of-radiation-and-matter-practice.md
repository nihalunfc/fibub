**Practice · Class 12 Physics · Chapter 11.** Read the chapter lesson, [Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter](/learn/courses/ncert-class-12-physics/dual-nature-of-radiation-and-matter/), first. Try each question on paper before opening the answer. Board answers are marked in steps, so write the law, the formula in symbols, the numbers with units, then the answer.

<div class="bigidea">Three equations cover almost every numerical: photon energy E = hν = 1240/λ(nm) eV, Einstein’s hν = φ₀ + eV₀, and de Broglie’s λ = h/p. Convert everything to eV for photoelectric questions and to SI for de Broglie questions.</div>

## How to set out a numerical

1. Convert the photon to an energy in eV: E = 1240 / λ (nm).
2. Write Einstein’s equation: K_max = hν − φ₀, and eV₀ = K_max.
3. If K_max comes out negative, there is <b>no emission</b>; say so.
4. For de Broglie: get the momentum p first (p = √(2meV) for an accelerated electron), then λ = h/p.

## 1-mark questions

1. Define the work function of a metal.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of the metal.</details>

2. What is the SI unit of Planck’s constant?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Joule second (J s), the same as kg m² s⁻¹.</details>

3. What happens to the stopping potential when the intensity of the light is doubled, with the frequency unchanged?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It does not change. V₀ depends only on the frequency (and the metal).</details>

4. What is the rest mass of a photon?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Zero.</details>

5. Name the experiment that first showed the wave nature of electrons.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The Davisson–Germer experiment (electron diffraction from a nickel crystal, 1927).</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

6. (2) State two features of the photoelectric effect that the wave theory of light cannot explain.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(i) The existence of a threshold frequency: below it there is no emission however intense the light. (ii) The maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons depends on the frequency and not on the intensity. (Also: there is no time lag, though the wave theory predicts one for dim light.)</details>

7. (2) Write the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through a potential difference V.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$\lambda = h/\sqrt{2meV} = 1.227/\sqrt{V}$` nm, with V in volts.

</details>

8. (2) The threshold wavelength of a metal is 500 nm. Find its work function in eV.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$\varphi_0 = hc/\lambda_0 = 1240/500 = 2.48$` eV.

</details>

9. (3) Light of wavelength 300 nm falls on a metal of work function 2.3 eV. Find (a) the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons and (b) the stopping potential.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Photon energy = 1240/300 = 4.13 eV.

(a) `$K_{max} = 4.13 - 2.3 = 1.83$` eV.

(b) `$eV_0 = K_{max}$`, so `$V_0 = 1.83$` V.

</details>

10. (3) Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at 2 × 10⁶ m/s. (m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$\lambda = h/mv = 6.63\times10^{-34}/(9.1\times10^{-31}\times2\times10^{6}) = 3.64\times10^{-10}$` m = 0.364 nm.

</details>

11. (3) A 100 W sodium lamp sends out light of wavelength 589 nm evenly in all directions, assuming that all the power is light. How many photons are emitted every second?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Energy of one photon: `$E = hc/\lambda = 6.63\times10^{-34}\times3\times10^{8}/(589\times10^{-9}) = 3.38\times10^{-19}$` J.

`$N = P/E = 100/(3.38\times10^{-19}) = 2.96\times10^{20}$` photons per second.

</details>

## 5-mark questions

12. (5) Explain the experimental observations of the photoelectric effect and show how Einstein’s equation accounts for each of them.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Observations: (1) for a given frequency above the threshold, the saturation photocurrent is proportional to the intensity; (2) the stopping potential, and so the maximum kinetic energy, does not depend on the intensity; (3) the stopping potential rises linearly with frequency, and below a threshold frequency ν₀ there is no emission; (4) emission is instantaneous.

Einstein: light travels in photons of energy hν. One photon is absorbed by one electron, which uses φ₀ to escape. So `$K_{max} = h\nu - \varphi_0$`, or `$eV_0 = h\nu - \varphi_0$`.

(1) More intense light means more photons per second, so more electrons per second: the saturation current is proportional to intensity. (2) Each photon has energy hν whatever the intensity, so K_max is independent of intensity. (3) K_max = h(ν − ν₀) is linear in ν; if hν is less than φ₀ no electron can be freed: threshold `$\nu_0 = \varphi_0/h$`. (4) The energy is given at once by one photon, so there is no delay.

</details>

13. (5) Describe the Davisson–Germer experiment briefly. What does it prove? Show that the result is consistent with the de Broglie hypothesis.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A beam of electrons, accelerated through a potential difference V, was fired at a nickel crystal. A detector, which could be moved around the crystal, counted the electrons scattered at different angles φ. The number of scattered electrons had a strong peak at a particular angle, and for 54 V the peak was at about 50°. This diffraction pattern is a wave phenomenon: it proves that electrons have wave properties.

Check: for 54 V, the de Broglie wavelength is `$\lambda = 1.227/\sqrt{54} = 0.167$` nm. For nickel the spacing of the atomic rows is d = 0.215 nm, and the condition `$d\sin\varphi = \lambda$` gives `$\sin\varphi = 0.167/0.215 = 0.78$`, so φ ≈ 51°. It agrees with the observed peak, so the de Broglie wavelength is correct.

</details>

## Case-based question

**A light meter in a camera.** A camera light meter uses a cell with a surface of caesium (work function 2.1 eV, threshold wavelength about 590 nm). When light falls on it, a current flows through a meter in proportion to the brightness.

(a) What is the threshold frequency of the caesium surface?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$\nu_0 = \varphi_0/h = 2.1\times1.6\times10^{-19}/6.63\times10^{-34} = 5.1\times10^{14}$` Hz.

</details>

(b) The cell is used in a room lit by a strong red lamp (wavelength 700 nm). Will there be a current? Explain.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. 700 nm is longer than the threshold wavelength 590 nm: a red photon has energy 1240/700 = 1.77 eV, which is less than φ₀ = 2.1 eV. No electron is emitted, however bright the lamp.</details>

(c) Blue light of wavelength 450 nm now falls on the cell. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Photon energy = 1240/450 = 2.76 eV. `$K_{max} = 2.76 - 2.1 = 0.66$` eV.

</details>

(d) The blue light’s brightness is doubled. What happens to K_max and to the photocurrent?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>K_max stays at 0.66 eV (it depends on the frequency, not the intensity), and the photocurrent doubles because twice as many photons arrive each second.</details>

## Assertion and reason

Choose: (A) both A and R are true and R explains A; (B) both true, R does not explain A; (C) A true, R false; (D) A false, R true.

14. **Assertion:** The photocurrent in a photocell is proportional to the intensity of the incident light (above the threshold frequency). **Reason:** The number of photoelectrons emitted per second is proportional to the number of photons that arrive per second.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A). Each photon frees at most one electron, so more photons per second means more electrons per second.</details>

15. **Assertion:** A heavier particle moving with the same speed has a shorter de Broglie wavelength. **Reason:** The wavelength is inversely proportional to the momentum of the particle.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A). λ = h/mv: larger m at the same v gives a larger p and a smaller λ.</details>

16. **Assertion:** The photoelectric effect cannot be explained by the wave theory of light. **Reason:** According to the wave theory the energy of the light is spread over the whole wavefront, so an electron needs time to collect enough energy.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(B). Both are true, but R explains only the time-lag failure; the failures about the threshold and about K_max versus intensity are separate points.</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Photon energy E = hν = hc/λ = 1240/λ(nm) eV; momentum p = h/λ.
- Einstein: hν = φ₀ + K_max; K_max = eV₀; threshold ν₀ = φ₀/h; below it there is no emission.
- Saturation current is proportional to intensity; V₀ does not depend on intensity; V₀ against ν has slope h/e.
- de Broglie: λ = h/p = h/mv = h/√(2mK); an electron through V volts has λ = 1.227/√V nm.
- Davisson–Germer: electron diffraction from nickel, d sin φ = λ: matter waves are real.

</div>
