<div class="bigidea">A log turns a night of looking into a record you can learn from and share: the time in UT, your site, instrument, conditions and what you actually saw.</div>

## 1. Why this matters

Every scientific observation starts as a note in a log. Your log shows your skills growing ("last year I couldn't see M51's arms"), helps you plan ("M33 needs a darker night"), and, when it holds careful times and conditions, can be useful data: a nova you noticed, a meteor fireball, a change on Jupiter.

## 2. What goes in an entry

- **Date and time in UT** (and the JD if you'll analyse it later; Lesson 2.4).
- **Site:** name or coordinates and altitude.
- **Instrument:** telescope, eyepiece, magnification, filters.
- **Conditions:** seeing (for example 1 to 5), transparency or naked-eye limiting magnitude (Lesson 3.2), Moon phase, wind, temperature.
- **Object** and what you **actually saw**: shape, brightness, detail, colour, with averted vision or not (Lesson 3.1).
- Optional: a sketch. Sketching trains the eye to notice detail better than any other habit.

## 3. From log to science

- **Variable stars:** estimates go to the AAVSO with time, comparison stars and chart (Lesson 5.4).
- **Meteors:** counts go to the IMO with effective time, limiting magnitude and radiant altitude (Lesson 5.5).
- **Fireballs and transient events:** an accurate time and direction let networks triangulate them.

In each case the extras you wrote down (UT, conditions, method) are what make an observation usable by someone else.

Write one log entry:

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>When.</b> 2027-03-12, 03:15 UT (not local time), so anyone can compare.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Where.</b> Site name, latitude and longitude, Bortle class or SQM reading.</div>
<div class="step"><b>With what.</b> 200 mm Dobsonian, 10 mm eyepiece (120×), no filter.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Conditions.</b> Seeing III on the Antoniadi scale, transparency good, naked-eye limit 6.0.</div>
<div class="step"><b>What you saw, honestly.</b> "M51: two glows; with averted vision a hint of the spiral arm towards NGC 5195. Not certain." Uncertainty is part of the data.</div>
</div>

<details class="maths" open>
<summary>Maths and principle</summary>

**Principle:** a measurement without its conditions and uncertainty can't be compared. Logging is the everyday version of reporting a value with its error bar ([Astronomy 5.7](/learn/courses/astronomy/fitting-errors/)).

**Example of a seeing scale** (Antoniadi, adapted, 1 best to 5 worst in the original; many observers reverse it, so always say which):

- I: perfect, no quivering;
- II: slight undulations, moments of calm;
- III: moderate, large tremors;
- IV: poor, constant troublesome undulations;
- V: very bad, hardly allows a rough sketch.

**Time to JD for a log:** 2026-07-14 02:35 UT is `$JD = 2461235.6076$` (Lesson 2.4).
</details>


<div class="meaning">

<dl>

<dt>Seeing scale</dt><dd>Antoniadi I to V (say which way round you use it).</dd>
<dt>NELM / SQM</dt><dd>How dark the sky was, by eye or by meter.</dd>

<div class="myth">

**"Only "proper" scientific observers need a log."** Logs make you a better observer: you notice more when you write it down, and you can see your skills and sky improving over months. Many famous amateur discoveries started with a careful log.

## 4. Worked example

*One complete entry.*

```
2026-07-14 02:35 UT (JD 2461235.608)
Site: back garden, 43.1° N 79.1° W, 180 m
Instrument: 200 mm f/6 Dobsonian, 25 mm eyepiece (48×), then 9 mm (133×)
Conditions: seeing 3/5, NELM 5.8, Moon set, light breeze, 17 °C
Object: M13 globular cluster, Hercules
Notes: Bright round glow, about 8′ across at 48×. At 133× edges resolve
into many faint stars with averted vision; core stays a grainy glow.
```

Anyone reading it knows when, where, with what, in which conditions, and what was seen.

## 5. Practice

<div class="problem" data-problem="to-ut"></div>

**More practice** (answers below):

1. Convert 22:15 local time in New Delhi (UT + 5:30) into UT.
2. Name three conditions worth recording on every night.
3. Why record the magnification as well as the telescope?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. 16:45 UT the same day.
2. Any three of: seeing, transparency or NELM, Moon phase and altitude, wind, temperature, humidity.
3. What you can see depends on it: detail at high power, faint extended glow at low power.
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

**Key points**

- Always log time in UT, place, instrument and magnification.
- Record seeing, transparency and sky darkness.
- Describe what you actually saw, including uncertainty.
- Logs turn casual observing into data you can share.

</div>
