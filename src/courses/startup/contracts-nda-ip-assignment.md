**Module 11 · about 13 minutes.** You'll read the startup contracts you meet most often, spot the missing clause, and make sure the company owns what it pays for.

<div class="bigidea">A contract is a written promise you can enforce: for a startup the three that matter most are the NDA, the IP assignment and the customer terms, and the missing clause costs more than the lawyer.</div>

## 1. What makes a contract valid

In all three countries a contract needs an **offer**, an **acceptance**, something of value exchanged (**consideration**), people able to contract, and a lawful purpose. Most need not be on paper, but write them down: memory fails, and some (IP assignments, share transfers, property) must be in writing. **E-signatures** are valid for almost all business contracts (India's IT Act, 2000; Canadian federal and provincial electronic commerce acts; the US ESIGN Act and state UETA laws). You can sign a PDF on your own device with the [[sign-pdf]] tool.

## 2. The contracts you will sign

### Watch it happen

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>Day 1:</b> a startup hires a freelance designer for its app screens over chat: "₹40,000, done in 3 weeks." No document.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Week 3:</b> the screens arrive and are paid for. The designer still owns the copyright: nothing assigned it.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Month 9:</b> an investor's lawyer asks for IP assignments from everyone who built the product. One is missing.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Fix:</b> the founders ask the designer to sign a one-page assignment, and pay a little extra for it. From then on every freelancer signs a short agreement with an IP clause before starting.</p></div>
</div>

**NDA (non-disclosure agreement).** Use it before sharing code, data, detailed financials or a supplier's pricing. Make it mutual when both sides share. Do not expect investors to sign one for a pitch.

**Contractor agreement with IP assignment.** Scope, fees, deadlines, confidentiality, and the clause that matters most: all work product is **assigned to the company** on payment.

**Customer terms of service.** What you provide, price and renewal, refunds, limit of liability, data ownership, governing law (Lesson 11.5 covers the website pages around them).

**Offer letters and employment contracts.** Pay, role, notice, confidentiality, invention assignment, options (Lesson 9.6).

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

The **Indian Contract Act, 1872** governs contracts; section 27 makes most post-employment **non-competes void**, so rely on confidentiality and non-solicitation instead. Copyright assignments must be **in writing**, state the rights, territory and duration (otherwise 5 years and India only, under section 19), so write "worldwide, for the full term of copyright". Pay **stamp duty** on agreements as your state requires (often a few hundred rupees; e-stamping is available), or the document cannot be used as evidence until the duty and a penalty are paid.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Contract law is provincial (Quebec uses the Civil Code). Contractors keep copyright and **moral rights** unless they assign the copyright in writing and waive moral rights. **Ontario** banned non-competes for employees in 2021 (except senior executives and business sales); courts elsewhere enforce them only when narrow. Quebec requires French versions of standard-form consumer contracts unless the customer asks for English.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

Contract law is state law; pick a governing state in each contract. A contractor's work is a **work made for hire** only in a few categories with a signed agreement, so add an **assignment** clause. Non-competes are void in **California** and limited in many states; the FTC's 2024 national ban was struck down in court and not pursued. California, Washington and some other states also limit invention assignments for work done entirely on an employee's own time and equipment.

</div>

## 3. Read these clauses first

1. **Parties and scope:** the right legal entity (your company, not you personally), and exactly what is delivered.
2. **Payment and term:** amounts, due dates, renewal and how to end it.
3. **IP ownership:** who owns what is made, and any licence back.
4. **Liability cap and indemnity:** how much each side owes if things go wrong.
5. **Confidentiality.**
6. **Governing law and disputes:** which law and which court or arbitration.

Keep a **contract register**: a simple sheet with the other party, date, value, renewal date, notice period and where the signed copy is stored.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Consideration</dt><dd>what each side gives in exchange: money, work, a promise</dd>
<dt>IP assignment</dt><dd>a written transfer of ownership of work to the company</dd>
<dt>Indemnity</dt><dd>a promise to cover the other side’s losses from certain events</dd>
<dt>Limitation of liability</dt><dd>a cap on how much one side can owe</dd>
<dt>Non-solicitation</dt><dd>a promise not to poach staff or customers for a time</dd>
<dt>What it means for you</dt><dd>Use short standard templates for NDAs, contractors and customers, always include IP assignment, and log every signed contract in one register.</dd>
</dl>

## 4. Worked example

*Made-up founders.* A Vancouver SaaS startup signs its first enterprise customer. The customer's draft says the startup's liability is unlimited and the customer owns "all deliverables", including the core software. The founders reply with three changes: liability capped at 12 months' fees (excluding data breaches caused by gross negligence), the customer owns only its own data and custom reports, and the startup keeps the platform with a licence to the customer. The customer accepts two and asks for a higher cap for data breaches; they settle at 2 times annual fees for that one case.

<div class="myth">

**"A contract downloaded from the internet is good enough."** A template is a fine start, but it was written for someone else's business, law and country. Fit the scope, IP, liability and governing law to yours, and get a lawyer to read the ones with real money at stake.

</div>

## 5. Practice

1. Which clause makes sure your company owns a freelancer's code?
2. Why sign contracts in the company's name, not your own?
3. What goes in a contract register?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. An IP assignment clause (plus a moral-rights waiver in Canada and India).
2. So the company, not you personally, has the rights and the liabilities.
3. The other party, date, value, renewal date, notice period and where the signed copy is.
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- A valid contract needs offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity and a lawful purpose; e-signatures work for most.
- The startup basics: NDA, contractor agreement with IP assignment, customer terms, offer letters.
- Without a written assignment a freelancer keeps the copyright, even after payment.
- Most non-competes are void in India, Ontario and California; use confidentiality and non-solicitation.
- Read parties, scope, payment, IP, liability, confidentiality and governing law first; keep a contract register.
