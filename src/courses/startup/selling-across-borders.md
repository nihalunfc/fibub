**Module 7 · about 13 minutes.** You'll know which taxes apply on your side of the border and on your customer's side, for goods, digital products and services.

<div class="bigidea">Every cross-border sale has two tax sides: your export is usually zero-rated at home, while the buyer’s country taxes the import or makes you register for its tax.</div>

## 1. Two sides of every cross-border sale

Every sale abroad has two tax questions:

1. **Your side:** is the export taxed at home? Usually not: exports are **zero-rated**, so you charge 0 % but still claim credits on your costs.
2. **The buyer's side:** does the buyer's country tax the import? For goods, usually at the border. For digital sales to consumers, more and more countries make the foreign *seller* register and charge their tax.

<!--fig:border-tax-->

### Watch it happen

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>The order:</b> a Toronto maker sells $1,000 of goods to a UK shop on DDP terms (she pays the import costs).</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Her side:</b> an export, so 0 % GST/HST on the invoice; she still claims credits on her materials. She keeps the shipping proof.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>At the UK border:</b> duty at 4 % of the value = $40.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Import VAT:</b> 20 % of value plus duty = 20 % × $1,040 = $208.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Landed cost:</b> $1,248 for the maker. If she is VAT-registered in the UK she reclaims the $208; if not, she should quote DAP and let the buyer pay it.</p></div>
</div>


## 2. Physical goods

- **Paperwork leaves with the parcel:** a commercial invoice, the correct HS code (the same 6-digit base in every country) and the country of origin. Wrong codes mean wrong duty and delays.
- **Incoterms** on your quote say who pays shipping, duty and tax. **DDP** (delivered duty paid) means you pay the buyer's import costs; the customer gets no surprise bill but you take the risk. **DAP** leaves duty to the buyer.
- **Low-value parcels:** many countries waive duty below a small value, but the US ended its $800 de minimis exemption for every country on 29 August 2025, so all parcels into the US now face duty and an entry. Price and ship with that in mind (Lesson 5.5 has a landed cost calculator).

## 3. Digital products to consumers

Apps, courses, e-books, streaming and SaaS sold to individuals are taxed where the **customer** lives in most of the world:

| Customer in | Rule for a foreign seller |
|---|---|
| India | Register under OIDAR (simplified), charge 18 % GST from the first rupee |
| Canada | Register for simplified GST/HST once sales to Canadian consumers pass $30,000 in 12 months (plus QST for Quebec) |
| USA | State sales tax where you pass the economic nexus threshold and the state taxes digital goods |
| EU | VAT at the customer's country rate from the first euro, through the non-Union OSS |
| UK | VAT from the first pound, registered with HMRC |

App stores (Apple, Google) and merchants of record (such as Paddle or Lemon Squeezy) act as the seller and handle these taxes for you, at a higher fee. For a small startup selling worldwide, that trade is often worth it.

## 4. Services to businesses

Business-to-business services are usually taxed by **reverse charge**: the client accounts for the tax in its own country. Your invoice shows 0 % and a reverse charge note (with the client's VAT or GST number where they have one). Keep proof the client is a business abroad: contract, registration number, address.

## 5. Getting paid from abroad

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

Receive through a bank or an RBI-authorised cross-border payment service, quote the purpose code for your service, and keep the FIRC or e-FIRA for every receipt: it proves the export for GST and income tax. Export earnings must reach India within the period set under FEMA rules; your bank will tell you the current limit.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Multi-currency accounts avoid converting each payment. US clients may ask for a **W-8BEN-E** (a corporation) or **W-8BEN** (an individual) form so they do not withhold 30 % US tax; the Canada–US tax treaty usually means no US tax on services performed in Canada.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

Foreign clients pay by wire, card or a payment platform. Watch currency conversion fees, and the buyer's country's withholding tax on services; a tax treaty can lower it if you send the client a residency certificate (IRS Form 6166).

</div>

## 6. Worked example

*Made-up founder.* Sana in Hyderabad sells an online course for $49 to consumers worldwide and does consulting for a German company. Course sales go through a merchant of record, which charges VAT, GST or sales tax for each buyer's country and pays Sana net. The German contract is an export of services: no IGST under her LUT, and the client reverse-charges German VAT. She keeps each e-FIRA for her GST and income tax records.

<div class="myth">

**"Exports are tax-free, so I can ignore the buyer’s country."** Your side is usually 0 %, but the buyer’s country may make you register: digital sales to EU and UK consumers are taxed from the first sale, India from the first rupee, Canada above $30,000.

</div>

## 7. Practice

1. Which Incoterm means you pay the buyer's import duty?
2. Why does a merchant of record cost more than a payment processor?
3. What does a US client want from a Canadian contractor to avoid withholding tax?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. DDP.
2. It becomes the legal seller and takes on the tax registrations, filings and refunds in every country.
3. A W-8BEN (individual) or W-8BEN-E (business).
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Exports of goods and services are usually zero-rated at home (India needs an LUT; keep proof of export).
- Goods: duty and import tax at the buyer’s border; the US ended its low-value de minimis exemption on 29 August 2025.
- Digital sales to consumers are taxed where the consumer lives: OIDAR in India, simplified GST/HST in Canada, OSS in the EU.
- B2B services: reverse charge, so the client accounts for the tax.
- Incoterms on your quote decide who pays shipping, duty and import tax.

</div>
