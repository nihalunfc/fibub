**Module 8 · about 15 minutes · Canada.** You'll know what a sole proprietor and a small corporation pay, and when incorporating starts to make sense.

<div class="bigidea">A sole proprietor pays personal tax and both halves of CPP on all profit; a small corporation pays about 9 % to 12 % on its first $500,000 and defers the rest of the tax until you take the money out.</div>

## 1. Sole proprietor (and partners)

Report the business on form **T2125** with your T1 return. Your profit is added to your other income and taxed at federal rates (14 % to 33 % in 2026) plus provincial rates. On top you pay **both halves of CPP**: 11.9 % of earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, plus 8 % CPP2 up to $85,000 (QPP in Quebec). You deduct the employer half. EI is optional for the self-employed (it covers special benefits such as parental leave).

Losses from the business reduce your other income for the year, such as a part-time salary.

## 2. Corporation (CCPC)

A Canadian-controlled private corporation files its own **T2** return. On its first **$500,000** of active business income it gets the **small business deduction**:

| Province | Small business rate (federal 9 % + provincial) | General rate (above $500,000) |
|---|---|---|
| Ontario | 12.2 % | 26.5 % |
| British Columbia | 11 % | 27 % |
| Alberta | 11 % | 23 % |
| Manitoba | 9 % | 27 % |
| Quebec | 12.2 % (with enough staff hours; otherwise higher) | 26.5 % |

The limit shrinks for corporations with large passive investment income or taxable capital over $10 million, and is shared between associated corporations.

<div class="step"><p><b>Profit $160,000</b> in Ontario; the founder needs $70,000 to live.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Sole proprietor:</b> income tax and both halves of CPP on all $160,000: about $53,000.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Corporation:</b> pays a $70,000 salary (deductible), plus its share of CPP.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Corporate tax:</b> 12.2 % on the remaining ~$86,000 ≈ $10,500. With the founder’s tax on the salary, about $30,000 in all this year.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>The catch:</b> about $75,000 stays in the company, and the founder pays personal tax on it later when it comes out.</p></div>
</div>


> **Key idea:** A corporation does not make tax disappear; it **defers** it. Profit left in the company is taxed lightly now and taxed again when you take it out as salary or dividends. The system is designed so the total ends up close to what you would pay personally (integration). The win comes from leaving money in the company to grow the business, and from spreading income over years.

## 3. Instalments and deadlines

- **Sole proprietor:** quarterly instalments (March, June, September and December 15) once your tax owing was over $3,000 this year and in either of the two previous years. File by **June 15**, pay by **April 30**.
- **Corporation:** monthly instalments (quarterly for many small CCPCs) once tax was over $3,000; balance due 2 months after year end (3 months for a small CCPC); T2 due **6 months** after year end. Provincial corporate tax is collected with the T2, except in Quebec.

## 4. Worked example

*Made-up founder.* Diego in Toronto has $160,000 of profit and needs $70,000 a year to live on. As a sole proprietor he pays income tax and CPP on all $160,000, roughly $53,000. With a corporation paying him a $70,000 salary, total tax this year (his income tax and CPP, the company’s CPP share and corporate tax) is about $30,000, and about $75,000 stays in the company for equipment and hiring. When he later takes that money out as dividends he will pay the deferred personal tax. He also budgets about $2,000 a year for a corporate accountant.

<div class="myth">

**"Incorporating cuts my tax in half."** It cuts tax on profit you leave in the company. Money you take out is taxed again as salary or dividends, and the total ends up close to what a sole proprietor pays, minus the cost of a corporate accountant.

</div>

1. Why does a self-employed person's CPP cost about twice an employee's?
2. Name two costs of running a corporation that a sole proprietor avoids.
3. Does a small business owner with a corporation have to pay EI on their own salary?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. They pay both the employee and the employer share.
2. Incorporation fees, annual returns, a separate T2 return with financial statements, a separate bank account and payroll.
3. Not if they own more than 40 % of the voting shares (they are not insurable under EI).
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Sole proprietors report on T2125 and pay federal + provincial tax plus both halves of CPP (11.9 % to $74,600, plus 8 % CPP2 to $85,000 in 2026).
- A CCPC pays the small business rate on its first $500,000 of active income: 12.2 % in Ontario, 11 % in BC and Alberta, 9 % in Manitoba.
- Corporate tax is a deferral: profit is taxed again when paid to you.
- Sole proprietors file by June 15 but pay by April 30; corporations pay 2 or 3 months after year end and file the T2 within 6 months.
- Instalments start once tax owing passes $3,000.
