**Module 8 · about 13 minutes.** You'll choose how to take money out of your business without paying more tax than you must, or breaking a rule.

<div class="bigidea">Proprietors simply draw money and are taxed on profit; company owners choose between salary (deductible, taxed on you) and dividends (from taxed profit), and the best mix depends on the country.</div>

## 1. If you are not a company: draws

A sole proprietor, partner or default US LLC owner takes **draws**: money moved from the business account to a personal one. A draw is not a cost and not taxed by itself; you pay tax on the whole profit anyway, whether you draw it or not. Keep it simple: a fixed monthly draw, and leave enough in the business for tax and lean months.

## 2. If you own a company: salary or dividends

| | Salary | Dividend |
|---|---|---|
| For the company | A deductible cost: lowers company tax | Paid from profit after company tax |
| For you | Taxed at your personal rates; payroll deductions | Taxed at personal or special dividend rates, with credits in Canada |
| Paperwork | Payroll, withholding, year-end slips | Board resolution, dividend slips |
| Builds pension and benefits | Yes (CPP, Social Security, EPF if registered) | No |

### Watch it happen

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>The company earns ₹30 lakh</b> of profit before paying its founder.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Route 1, salary:</b> ₹30 lakh paid as salary is a cost, so company tax is nil. The founder pays slab tax on ₹30 lakh (less ₹75,000 standard deduction): about ₹4.8 lakh. Keeps about ₹25.2 lakh.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Route 2, dividend:</b> the company first pays 25.17 % tax: ₹7.55 lakh. It pays ₹22.45 lakh as dividend, taxed again at slab rates: about ₹2.7 lakh.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Compare:</b> the founder keeps about ₹19.7 lakh with dividends against ₹25.2 lakh with salary. In India, company tax is not credited back, so salary usually wins for a working founder.</p></div>
</div>


<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

Directors' salary is deductible for the company; the company deducts **TDS** under section 192 and issues Form 16. Dividends are taxed at your slab rate (no standard deduction), and the company deducts **10 % TDS** on dividends over ₹10,000 a year. Because company tax (25.17 %) is not credited back, salary up to the higher slabs is usually cheaper overall; dividends make sense for profit you take out above that, or for shareholders who do not work in the company.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Salary builds **RRSP room** (18 % of last year's earned income) and CPP, and gives you a T4 that lenders like. Dividends from small business income are **non-eligible dividends**: grossed up 15 % and given a dividend tax credit, so overall tax is close to salary (integration), without CPP. Many owners pay enough salary for RRSP room and CPP, take the rest as dividends, and leave what they do not need in the corporation.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

In an **S corp**, pay a **reasonable salary** for your role through payroll, then take distributions. The IRS looks at what you would pay someone else for the work; setting salary at zero is a common audit trigger. In a **C corp**, salary is deductible and dividends are taxed twice (21 % in the company, then 0 %, 15 % or 20 %), so founders usually take salary and leave the rest in the business.

</div>

## 3. Loans from your company: careful

Taking money from your company as a "loan" to avoid tax does not work:

- **India:** a loan to a shareholder with 10 % or more of the voting power can be taxed as a **deemed dividend** up to the company's accumulated profits; the Companies Act also restricts loans to directors.
- **Canada:** a shareholder loan not repaid within **one year after the corporation's year end** is generally added to your income.
- **USA:** the IRS can reclassify an informal loan as salary or a dividend; a real loan needs a written note, interest at least at the applicable federal rate, and repayments.

> **Key idea:** Decide how you will pay yourself before the year starts, run it the same way every month, and write it down (board minutes, payroll records). Consistency is what makes the arrangement stand up.

## 4. Worked example

*Made-up founder.* Rohit's Indian company makes ₹30 lakh profit before paying him. All as salary: he keeps about **₹25.2 lakh** after tax. All as dividend: the company pays ₹7.55 lakh tax first, and he keeps about **₹19.7 lakh**. A mix (₹12.75 lakh salary, the rest as dividend) leaves about ₹22.3 lakh. Salary wins here, because company tax is not credited back to him. He pays himself mainly by salary, with TDS, and keeps some profit in the company for growth.

<div class="myth">

**"Dividends are always tax-free or cheaper than salary."** Dividends come out of profit that already paid company tax. In India that tax is not credited back, so salary is usually cheaper for a working founder; in Canada the two end up close; in a US C corp dividends are taxed twice.

</div>

## 5. Practice

1. Why is "zero salary, all distributions" risky in a US S corp?
2. Name one reason a Canadian owner might choose some salary even though dividends save CPP.
3. A proprietor draws nothing all year. Is their tax lower?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The IRS requires a reasonable salary for an owner who works in the business and can reclassify distributions as wages, with back payroll taxes and penalties.
2. RRSP room, CPP pension, a T4 for mortgages, or deductible childcare.
3. No: proprietors are taxed on profit, not draws.
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Proprietors and default LLC owners take draws; tax is on profit, not on draws.
- Salary is deductible for the company and builds pension rights; dividends come from taxed profit.
- India: salary usually beats dividends for working founders; TDS applies to both.
- Canada: salary gives RRSP room and CPP; non-eligible dividends get a gross-up and credit; many owners mix.
- Never use informal "loans" from your company to avoid tax: India deemed dividend, Canada one-year rule, US reclassification.

</div>
