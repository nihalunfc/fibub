**Module 1 · about 12 minutes.** You'll be able to decide whether to start alone or with others, and know what each choice means in law.

## 1. Solo: simple and all yours

A solo founder decides fast, keeps 100 % of the ownership and has the simplest paperwork. The costs: every job lands on you, there is nobody to cover when you are ill, and some investors prefer teams.

Solo founders have more legal options than ever:

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

Sole proprietorship (no incorporation) or a **One Person Company (OPC)**, a company with one member and limited liability. Since 2021 OPCs have no paid-up capital or turnover limits, and a non-resident Indian can form one.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Sole proprietorship, or a corporation with you as the only shareholder and director (allowed federally and in every province).

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

Sole proprietorship, or a **single-member LLC** (taxed like a sole proprietor by default), or a corporation with one shareholder.

</div>

## 2. A team: more hands, more paperwork

A team covers more skills and shares the risk and the work. It needs clear answers to: who owns how much, who decides what, what happens if someone leaves. Write these down **before** you start (Lesson 10.1).

> **Key idea:** If two or more people run a business together for profit and sign nothing, the law usually says they have formed a **general partnership**, where each partner can be personally liable for all its debts. Choosing a structure on purpose avoids this.

<!--fig:team-gaps-->

## 3. Three ways to fill a gap

| Option | Costs | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Learn it | Your time | Things you will do every week, like selling |
| Pay for it | Cash (freelancer, accountant, lawyer) | Specialist or one-off work: bookkeeping, a logo, a contract |
| Co-founder | Equity, often 10 to 50 % | The core skill the business cannot exist without |

A co-founder is the most expensive option, because shares are forever. Pick co-founders for trust and commitment as much as for skills.

## 4. Worked example

*Made-up founders.* Dev (software) and Lena (sales) want to build a booking app.

- Dev alone would need to pay for sales help in cash he doesn't have.
- With Lena, both jobs are covered. They form a company (India: private limited; Canada: corporation; USA: Delaware C corp because they want investors), split shares 50/50 with four-year vesting (Lesson 10.3), and hire a freelance designer and an accountant.

## 5. Practice

1. Name one reason to stay solo and one reason to find a co-founder.
2. In the USA, how is a single-member LLC taxed by default?
3. Two Canadian friends sell T-shirts together without registering anything. What are they in law?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Solo: full ownership and fast decisions. Co-founder: covers a missing core skill and shares the load.
2. As a "disregarded entity": profit goes on the owner's Schedule C, like a sole proprietorship.
3. Most likely a general partnership under their province's Partnerships Act, with personal liability for debts.
</details>
