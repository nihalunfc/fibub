**Module 3 · about 13 minutes.** You'll choose where to register your company or LLC and know the extra registrations that follow.

<div class="bigidea">You register where you form the business and also wherever you actually operate. Pick the place your investors, customers and staff need, not the one with the best advertising.</div>

## 1. Why the place matters

Where you register decides **which law governs** the company's inside workings (shareholder rights, director duties), **which registry** you file with every year, and **which fees and taxes** you pay. Where you *operate* adds more registrations on top.

<!--fig:where-register-->

## 2. By country

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

There is one company law for the whole country (the Companies Act, 2013, run by the MCA), so there is no "Delaware of India". The choice is your **registered office**: its state decides which **Registrar of Companies** looks after you and the **stamp duty** on your incorporation documents (fees differ by state). One registration lets you do business all over India, but GST is registered **per state** where you have a place of business (Lesson 7.2), and each state's shop and labour rules apply to staff there. Moving the registered office to another state later needs a special resolution and approval from the Regional Director, so choose carefully.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

- **Federal** (Corporations Canada): the name is protected across Canada; well known to investors and banks abroad; $200 to incorporate online plus a NUANS name search report; $12 annual return. At least **25 % of directors must be resident Canadians** (if there are fewer than four directors, at least one). You must also register **extra-provincially** in each province where you carry on business, including your home province, which adds a fee.
- **Provincial:** one province's law and registry, often cheaper overall if you'll stay in one province. Ontario charges $300 online; Ontario, BC, Alberta and Quebec have no resident director rule.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

- **Delaware** is the default for startups raising venture capital: its corporate law is flexible and well tested, its Court of Chancery specialises in business disputes, and investors' standard documents assume it. Annual cost: a $50 annual report plus franchise tax (from $175 a year under the authorised shares method, or often $400 under the assumed par value method; calculate both and pay the lower). LLCs pay a flat $300 a year.
- **Your home state** is usually better without VC: you'd have to register as a "foreign" entity in your home state anyway, paying two sets of fees and filing two annual reports.
- **Wyoming and Nevada** are sometimes sold for privacy or no state income tax, but if you live and work elsewhere, your home state taxes and regulates you anyway.

</div>

> **Key idea:** You register where you form, and *also* where you physically operate. "Selling to customers in a place" usually doesn't count as operating there for company law, but it can for sales tax (Module 7).

## 3. Worked example

*Made-up founders.* A team in Seattle raising a seed round forms a **Delaware C corp** and qualifies it in **Washington** as a foreign corporation. A café owner in Vancouver incorporates **provincially in BC**. A SaaS team in Pune puts its registered office at their **Pune** flat with the owner's no-objection letter, under the **ROC Pune**, and registers GST in Maharashtra.

<div class="myth">

**"Forming in Delaware or Wyoming means I avoid my home state’s taxes."** Wherever you form, the state where you live and work still registers, taxes and regulates you. Delaware is about investors and corporate law, not lower tax for a small business.

</div>

## 4. Practice

1. Name two costs of a Delaware corporation for a founder who lives in New York.
2. When does federal incorporation make sense in Canada?
3. Can an Indian company registered in Karnataka sell to customers in Delhi?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Delaware franchise tax and annual report, plus New York foreign qualification fees and New York taxes (and often a registered agent fee in Delaware).
2. When you'll operate in several provinces, want the name protected nationally, or expect investors who prefer it.
3. Yes. Company registration covers all of India; GST rules decide whether it needs another state registration (only for a place of business there).
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Register where you form and where you operate.
- India: one company covers all of India; GST registration is per state of business.
- Canada: federal (name protected nationally, 25 % resident directors) or provincial (cheaper for one province).
- USA: Delaware for venture capital; otherwise usually your home state.
- Selling to customers somewhere rarely needs company registration there, but may need sales tax registration.

</div>
