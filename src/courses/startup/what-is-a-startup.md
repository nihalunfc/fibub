**Module 1 · about 12 minutes · no background needed.** You'll be able to say whether your idea is a startup or a small business, and why that one answer shapes almost every legal and money choice later in this course.

<div class="bigidea">A startup is a business built to grow fast by serving many more customers without costs growing at the same pace; a small business grows with its effort. The plan, not the product, decides which you are.</div>

## 1. Two kinds of new business

Every new business is a bet that people will pay for something. What differs is how it plans to grow.

- A **small business** grows roughly in step with its effort: more shops, more vans, more billable hours. Think of a bakery, a plumbing firm or a freelance designer. Most businesses in every country are like this, and many are very profitable.
- A **startup** is built to <mark>repeat one thing for many more customers without costs growing at the same pace</mark>. Software, marketplaces, new products sold nationally or worldwide. It usually spends money before it earns it, often from investors, to grab a large market quickly.

<!--fig:startup-or-small-->

Neither is better. A startup aims for a big outcome and most fail; a small business aims for a steady living and many survive. This course covers both: every step from registering to tax applies to either.

> **Key idea:** "Startup" describes the growth plan, not the product. An online shop can be a small business; a factory making a new kind of battery can be a startup.

## 2. Why the difference matters legally

Your growth plan decides three things early:

1. **Structure.** Investors buy shares. Only a company or corporation issues shares, so if you will raise money, you need one from the start (Module 3).
2. **Equity.** If co-founders and early staff are paid partly in shares, you need vesting and an option plan (Module 10).
3. **Spending.** A startup often loses money for a year or more, so runway (Lesson 2.5) and losses carried forward for tax (Module 8) matter more.

## 3. What the governments call a startup

Each country has a different official meaning, and it matters only for specific benefits.

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

Has a formal label. The Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (**DPIIT**) "recognises" a startup through the Startup India portal if it is a private limited company, a registered partnership firm or an LLP, is **under 10 years old**, has had turnover **below ₹100 crore** in every year, and works towards innovation, improvement of products or a scalable model. It must not be formed by splitting up an existing business. Recognition is free and opens tax and compliance benefits (Lesson 4.3).

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Has no single legal "startup" status. "Small business" usually means a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) for tax (with a lower small business tax rate on its first $500,000 of active business income) or fewer than 100 employees in government statistics. Programmes such as the Start-up Visa and some grants have their own definitions.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

**The USA** has no legal "startup" status either. The SBA defines "small business" by industry size standards (employees or revenue), which matter for SBA loans and government contracts. Tax law has its own tests, such as qualified small business stock (QSBS) for C corporations.

</div>

## 4. Worked example

*Made-up founders.* Priya in Pune and Marcus in Toronto both want to sell clinic booking software.

- Priya wants to grow across India and raise from angel investors. She is building a **startup**: she should form a private limited company and apply for DPIIT recognition.
- Marcus wants a calm, profitable business serving 40 clinics in Ontario, funded from his savings. That is a **small business**, even though it is software. He can start as a sole proprietor and incorporate when profit grows.

Same product, different plan, different first steps.

<div class="myth">

**"Having an app makes us a startup."** The growth plan decides it. An app sold to 40 local clients is a small business; a new battery factory aiming for a national market is a startup.

</div>

## 5. Practice

1. A YouTuber who edits videos for other creators at an hourly rate: startup or small business?
2. Your friend says "we're a startup because we have an app". What is the better test?
3. In India, can a 12-year-old company get DPIIT recognition?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Small business: income grows with hours worked.
2. Whether the plan is to grow fast by serving many more customers without costs rising at the same pace.
3. No: DPIIT recognition is for entities up to 10 years from incorporation or registration.
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Startup: built to scale fast, usually losing money first; small business: grows with effort and aims for steady profit.
- Neither is better; this course covers both.
- Raising investment means a company or corporation, because only they issue shares.
- India has a formal startup label (DPIIT: under 10 years, turnover under ₹100 crore); Canada and the USA do not.
- Scalable means the cost per customer falls as customers grow.

</div>
