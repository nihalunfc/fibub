**Module 7 · about 11 minutes.** You'll understand why the tax you collect is not your money, how input credits work, and why US sales tax behaves differently.

<div class="bigidea">A business is the government’s tax collector: it charges tax on what it sells, takes back the tax on what it buys, and passes on only the difference, so the final customer pays it all.</div>

## 1. The tax is not your money

When you register for GST (India), GST/HST (Canada) or sales tax (USA), you become a **tax collector**. The tax you add to a price belongs to the government from the moment the customer pays. You hold it for a few weeks and then send it in with a return.

> **Key idea:** Keep the tax you collect apart from your own money, for example in a separate savings account. Founders who spend it get a large bill at return time and pay interest and penalties.

## 2. Value added tax: the chain

India's GST and Canada's GST/HST are **value added taxes**. Every registered business in a chain charges tax on its sales (**output tax**) and claims back the tax it paid on its business purchases (**input tax credit**, ITC). It sends the government only the difference: tax on the value it added.

```math
\text{tax you pay} = \text{output tax} - \text{input tax credits}
```

### Watch it happen

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>Farm sells cotton</b> for 100 plus 5 % = 5 tax. It sends 5 to the government.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Mill sells cloth</b> for 250 plus 12.50 tax. It already paid 5 on the cotton, so it sends 12.50 − 5 = 7.50.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Tailor sells the shirt</b> for 600 plus 30 tax, minus the 12.50 it paid: sends 17.50.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Shop sells to you</b> for 1,000 plus 50 tax, minus 30 paid: sends 20.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Add it up:</b> 5 + 7.50 + 17.50 + 20 = 50, exactly 5 % of the final price. Every business got its tax back; you paid all 50.</p></div>
</div>


Three things follow from the chain:

1. **The end customer pays it all.** A consumer cannot claim credits, so the full tax on the final price stays with them.
2. **Registered businesses are not out of pocket.** They get back the tax on what they buy (as long as they hold valid invoices).
3. **Unregistered businesses are.** A small business that is not registered cannot claim credits, so the tax on its costs becomes a real cost. That is why business buyers prefer registered suppliers.

## 3. Retail sales tax: the US way

The USA has no national GST or VAT. Instead, 45 states and Washington DC (and thousands of cities and counties) charge **sales tax** on retail sales to the end user. Businesses that buy to resell give the seller a **resale certificate** and pay no tax; the shop charges the full rate at the till. Equipment a business uses itself is usually taxable to the business, with no credit to claim.

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

One GST, split in two for sales inside a state: CGST (central) and SGST (state), half each. Sales between states carry IGST at the full rate. Since 22 September 2025 the main rates are **5 %** and **18 %**, with **40 %** for a short list of luxury and sin goods, plus special rates for gold and stones; many basics are exempt (0 %). Lesson 7.2 covers registration and returns.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Federal **GST 5 %**; five provinces combine it with their own tax as one **HST** (13 % Ontario, 14 % Nova Scotia, 15 % New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, PEI). BC, Saskatchewan and Manitoba add a separate retail **PST** (closer to US sales tax: no credits), and Quebec adds the **QST**, a value added tax like GST. Lesson 7.3 has the details.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

Each state sets its own rules: what is taxable (many services and groceries are not), the rate (state plus local), and when a seller must register (nexus). Five states have no statewide sales tax: Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon. Lesson 7.4 covers nexus.

</div>

## 4. Zero-rated, exempt and outside the system

| Type | Tax charged | Can the seller claim input credits? | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taxable at a rate | 5 %, 13 %, 18 %… | Yes | Most goods and services |
| Zero-rated | 0 % | Yes | Exports; basic groceries in Canada |
| Exempt | None | No | Many health, education and financial services |

The difference matters: a zero-rated exporter gets its input tax back, an exempt clinic does not.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Output tax</dt><dd>the GST/HST you add to your sales</dd>
<dt>Input tax credit (ITC)</dt><dd>the GST/HST you paid on business purchases, which you subtract</dd>
<dt>Net tax</dt><dd>output tax − input tax credits: what you send in</dd>
<dt>Zero-rated</dt><dd>taxed at 0 %, with credits still allowed (exports)</dd>
<dt>Exempt</dt><dd>no tax charged and no credits allowed</dd>
<dt>What it means for you</dt><dd>Put the tax you collect aside the day you are paid; it was never your money. Keep every purchase invoice: each one is cash back.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

## 5. Worked example

*Made-up founder.* Meera runs a registered design studio in Pune. In a month she bills ₹4,00,000 + 18 % GST = ₹72,000 output tax. She paid GST of ₹9,000 on a laptop, ₹2,700 on software and ₹5,400 on rent: ₹17,100 input credit. She pays **₹54,900** with her return. The ₹72,000 was never hers; the ₹17,100 she got back lowered her real costs.

<div class="myth">

**"GST is a cost my business pays."** For a registered business it is not: you pass it on in your prices and claim back the tax on your costs. It becomes a cost only if you are not registered (no credits) or sell exempt services.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Sales tax collected belongs to the government; you only hold it until the return is due.
- Value added tax (India GST, Canada GST/HST, Quebec QST): tax you pay = output tax − input tax credits.
- The final consumer carries the whole tax; registered businesses are reimbursed through credits.
- US sales tax is charged once, at the retail sale; resellers buy tax-free with a resale certificate.
- Zero-rated sales (exports) keep credits; exempt sales do not.

</div>
