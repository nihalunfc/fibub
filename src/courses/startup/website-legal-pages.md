**Module 11 · about 11 minutes.** You'll work out which legal pages your site needs, what each must say, and how to keep them true.

<div class="bigidea">Your legal pages are promises to customers and regulators: publish the ones your site actually needs, write them for what you really do, and update them when that changes.</div>

## 1. Which pages you need

<!--fig:legal-pages-->

The pages a site needs depend on what it does. A simple brochure site needs contact details and, if it has a contact form or analytics, a privacy policy. A shop adds terms, refunds and shipping. A subscription app adds renewal terms; anything with user posts adds content rules and a takedown process.

### Watch it happen

<div class="step"><p><b>Launch week:</b> a Delhi startup's shop goes live with a footer that says only "© 2026".</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Week 2:</b> the payment gateway refuses to activate: it needs a privacy policy, terms, refund and shipping policies, and contact details on the site.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Week 3:</b> the founders publish the five pages, add the company name, CIN and registered office to the footer, and name a grievance officer with an email and a 48-hour acknowledgement promise.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Month 3:</b> they add a subscription box and update the terms and refund pages the same day, with a "last updated" date on each.</p></div>

## 2. What each page must say

1. **About and contact:** your legal entity name, address and how to reach you.
2. **Privacy policy:** what you collect, why, who you share it with, how long you keep it, people's rights, and a privacy contact (Lesson 11.4).
3. **Terms of service:** what you provide, acceptable use, payment, liability limits, governing law (Lesson 11.3).
4. **Refunds, returns and cancellation:** in plain words, before the customer pays.
5. **Shipping:** times, costs, countries.
6. **Cookie choices:** where the law asks for consent before tracking.
7. **Accessibility statement:** your standard and how to ask for help.

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

A company must show its **name, CIN, registered office, phone and email** on its website (Companies Act, section 12). The **Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020** require sellers to show legal name and address, return, refund, exchange, warranty, delivery and shipping terms, the total price with taxes, country of origin, and a **grievance officer** who acknowledges complaints within 48 hours and resolves them within a month. Platforms with user content follow the **IT Rules, 2021** (published rules, grievance officer, takedown timelines). Payment gateways ask for these pages before activating your account.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Provincial consumer laws require online sellers to disclose their identity, address, full price, delivery and cancellation terms before the sale, and to send a copy of the agreement afterwards (within 15 days in Ontario). **Quebec** requires French versions of consumer-facing pages and contracts, a published privacy officer, and tracking off by default. Ontario businesses with 50 or more staff must meet **WCAG 2.0 AA** under the **AODA** and file accessibility reports.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

The FTC treats a privacy policy or terms that do not match your practices as **deceptive**. California's **CalOPPA** requires a privacy policy on any site that collects personal data from California residents (which in practice means almost every US site), and state privacy laws above their thresholds add "Do Not Sell or Share" links. Auto-renewals need clear terms and **easy online cancellation** (ROSCA and state laws). Courts apply the **ADA** to many websites; aim for WCAG 2.1 AA. Sites with user uploads register a **DMCA agent** with the US Copyright Office for safe harbour.

</div>

## 3. Keeping them true

- Write from what you actually do: list your tools (analytics, payments, email, hosting) and what each receives.
- Put a "last updated" date on each page; tell users before material changes.
- Link the pages in the footer of every page, and at checkout and sign-up.
- Review them when you add a feature, a country or a new tool that handles data.

## 4. Worked example

*Made-up founders.* A two-person Boston startup sells a $9 a month planning app to the USA and Canada, with a free trial. Its checklist: about and contact; privacy policy naming its analytics, payment and email tools, with access and deletion requests by email; terms of service under Massachusetts law; a clear renewal notice at sign-up ("$9 a month after your 14-day trial, cancel any time in Settings") with one-click cancellation; a refund policy; an accessibility statement aiming for WCAG 2.1 AA; and, for Quebec users, French versions of the terms and privacy policy. It does not need a shipping page or a DMCA agent, because nothing ships and users cannot post content.

<div class="myth">

**"A privacy policy protects me, so the longer the better."** A policy is a promise, and regulators enforce what it says. A long, vague or copied policy that does not match your real practices is itself a breach. Short, true and specific is safer.

</div>

## 5. Practice

1. Your site has only a contact form and analytics. Which two pages do you need at minimum?
2. Why put legal page links at checkout as well as in the footer?
3. You add a new email tool that receives customer emails. What should you update?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. About and contact details, and a privacy policy (plus cookie choices in Quebec, the EU or wherever consent is required).
2. Many laws require terms, prices and refund rules to be shown before the customer agrees to pay.
3. The privacy policy (who you share data with) and your data-processing agreements.
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Every site needs contact details; a privacy policy as soon as it collects any personal data or uses analytics.
- Shops add terms, refunds and shipping; subscriptions add renewal terms and easy cancellation.
- India: company name, CIN and registered office on the site, and a grievance officer for e-commerce.
- Canada: provincial disclosure rules, French in Quebec, AODA in Ontario; USA: FTC truth rules, CalOPPA, ADA, DMCA agent.
- Write the pages from what you actually do, date them, and update them when the product changes.

</div>
