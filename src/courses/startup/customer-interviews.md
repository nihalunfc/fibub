**Module 2 · about 13 minutes.** You'll be able to run a customer interview that tells you the truth, and know when you have learned enough to build.

## 1. Why interviews go wrong

People are kind. If you describe your idea and ask whether they like it, most will say yes, and then never buy. The fix is to <mark>talk about their life, not your idea</mark>: what happened, what they did, what it cost.

<!--fig:interview-sort-->

> **Key idea:** Past behaviour and money spent are facts. Opinions about the future are guesses.

## 2. Who and how many

- Talk to people who **have the problem now** and, ideally, **make the buying decision** (the shop owner, not the cashier).
- Aim for **10 to 20 conversations** before building. Stop when you hear the same stories again.
- Find them where they already gather: industry associations, local business groups, online communities, trade fairs, LinkedIn, or simply walking into shops at a quiet hour.

## 3. A simple script

<div class="su-steps">

Interview script (20 minutes)

1. Thank them; say you are researching a problem, not selling anything.
2. "Tell me about your role and a normal week."
3. "When did [the problem] last happen? Walk me through it."
4. "What did you do about it? What did that cost in time or money?"
5. "What else have you tried? Why did you stop?"
6. "If you could wave a magic wand, what would change?"
7. "Who else should I talk to?" (gets your next interview)
8. Only at the end, if they ask, describe your idea and ask for a small commitment: a pilot, a pre-order or a meeting with their boss.

</div>

## 4. Respect their data

Even research conversations involve personal information. Ask before recording, store notes safely, and delete what you no longer need.

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 needs clear consent for digital personal data, used only for the stated purpose.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

The Criminal Code lets you record a call you take part in, but PIPEDA expects meaningful consent when a business collects personal information, so tell people you are recording and why.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

About a dozen states (including California) require **all parties** to agree before a call is recorded. Ask everyone, every time.

</div>

## 5. Worked example

*Made-up founder.* Ravi (from Lesson 2.1) interviews 12 pharmacy owners. Notes after each call:

- 9 of 12 throw away stock monthly; median ₹10,000.
- 7 use a paper register; 3 use billing software with no expiry alerts.
- 4 said "I'd pay if it saves half the waste"; 2 agreed to a free 1-month pilot.

That last line, a real commitment, is worth more than everything else.

## 6. Practice

1. Fix this question: "Wouldn't an app that tracks expiry dates be useful?"
2. Why ask "Who else should I talk to?"
3. What is a "commitment" in an interview? Give two examples.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. "How do you keep track of expiry dates today? When did something last expire unnoticed?"
2. It gives you introductions to more people with the same problem.
3. Something that costs them time, reputation or money: a pilot, a pre-order, an introduction to the decision-maker.
</details>
