**Module 2 · about 12 minutes.** You'll be able to judge whether a problem is worth building a business around, before spending money on it.

## 1. Start from the problem, not the product

Most new businesses fail because they build something nobody needs badly enough to pay for. The cure is to <mark>fall in love with a problem</mark>, not with your solution. A good problem is:

- **Painful:** it costs people money, time, health or stress.
- **Frequent:** it happens weekly, not once in a lifetime.
- **Already paid for:** people spend on a workaround (an agent, a spreadsheet, a weekly trip, an expensive tool).
- **Common:** enough people have it for a business to live on.
- **Reachable:** you know where those people are and can talk to them.

<!--fig:problem-score-->

> **Key idea:** The best evidence is behaviour, not opinion. "I pay ₹3,000 a month to an agent for this" beats "Yes, I'd use that!"

## 2. Where good problems come from

1. **Your job.** What does your team complain about? Which task eats hours in spreadsheets?
2. **Your community.** Students, immigrants, parents, farmers, small shop owners: what do they struggle with that you understand?
3. **Rules and changes.** New laws (e-invoicing, privacy rules, new tax forms) create problems overnight for thousands of businesses.
4. **Workarounds you see.** Whenever people glue three tools together, there's a product.

## 3. Country notes

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

Big opportunities come from formalisation (GST, digital payments through UPI, e-invoicing), small-city demand and services in regional languages. Price sensitivity is high, so "already paying" is a strong filter.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

A smaller, spread-out market means many founders sell to the USA early. Bilingual (English and French) needs, cold-weather logistics and regulated sectors (health, finance) are common niches.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

The largest single market, but crowded. Narrow niches (one industry, one job title) are easier to win, and state-by-state rules create local problems worth solving.

</div>

## 4. Worked example

*Made-up founder.* Ravi notices his uncle's pharmacy throws away expired medicine every month.

| Signal | Evidence Ravi found | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | ₹15,000 a month of stock written off | High |
| Frequency | Every month | High |
| Already paying | Uses a paper register and a part-time helper | Medium |
| Common | Tens of thousands of independent pharmacies | High |
| Reach | Knows 3 owners; no route to more yet | Low |

Next step: test whether he can get 10 pharmacy owners to talk to him (Lesson 2.2).

## 5. Practice

1. Rewrite as a problem: "an app for gym bookings".
2. Which signal counts double in the scorer, and why?
3. Name one problem from your own work or community and score it.

2. "Already paying for a fix", because money spent is the clearest proof of pain.
3. Your own answer: note the weakest signal and how you will test it.
