<div class="bigidea">Income tax is charged on profit, not sales; who pays it, you or your company, depends on the structure you chose.</div>

## 1. Tax is on profit

Income tax is charged on **profit**, not on sales:

```math
\text{taxable profit} = \text{revenue} - \text{deductible expenses} - \text{depreciation}
```

Sales tax you collected is not revenue (it belongs to the government, Module 7), and some costs are not deductible (Lesson 8.6). Equipment is claimed over several years as **depreciation** (capital cost allowance in Canada).

## 2. Who pays: the owner or the business

| Structure | Who pays income tax | Return |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietor | You, on your personal return, at your personal rates | India ITR-3/ITR-4; Canada T1 with T2125; USA Form 1040 with Schedule C |
| Partnership (and LLP in India) | Canada and USA: each partner on their share; India: the firm or LLP itself at 30 % | Partnership return plus partners' returns |
| US LLC (default) | Owners, like a sole proprietor or partnership (pass-through) | Schedule C or Form 1065 |
| Company or corporation | The company, at the corporate rate; you pay again on salary or dividends | India ITR-6; Canada T2; USA Form 1120 (1120-S for an S corp, which passes through) |

> **Key idea:** A company is a separate taxpayer. Its profit is not your income until it pays you, as salary (a cost for the company, taxed on you) or as dividends (paid from profit after company tax, taxed again on you). Lesson 8.5 compares them.

## 3. Rates at a glance

<div class="cc" data-cc="in">

Individuals pay slab rates (new regime: nil to ₹4 lakh, rising to 30 % over ₹24 lakh, no tax up to ₹12 lakh income thanks to the rebate, plus 4 % cess). LLPs and firms pay 30 %. Most companies choose section 115BAA: 22 % plus surcharge and cess, 25.17 % in all. Small businesses can use **presumptive** tax (44AD, 44ADA). From 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act: section numbers changed but these rates and schemes carried over; this course uses the familiar old numbers.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="ca">

Individuals pay federal plus provincial rates (federal from 14 % to 33 % in 2026) and both halves of CPP on self-employment income. A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) pays about 9 % federal plus 0 to 3.2 % provincial on its first $500,000 of active business income (the **small business deduction**), and about 23 % to 31 % above that.

</div>

<div class="cc" data-cc="us">

Pass-through owners pay federal rates from 10 % to 37 % plus **self-employment tax** (15.3 % up to the Social Security wage base, $184,500 in 2026) and state income tax, less a 20 % qualified business income deduction. C corporations pay a flat 21 % federal plus state tax; dividends are taxed again at 0, 15 or 20 %.

</div>

## 4. Tax years, losses and paying ahead

- **Tax year:** India uses the financial year April to March for everyone. In Canada and the USA individuals use the calendar year; corporations can choose their year end.
- **Losses:** a loss in the early years is not wasted. A business loss usually reduces your other income (for a proprietor) or is **carried forward** to reduce future profit: 8 years in India (if you file on time), 20 years in Canada (non-capital losses), indefinitely in the USA (limited to 80 % of taxable income each year).
- **Pay as you go:** nobody withholds tax from a founder's profit, so each country expects payments during the year: advance tax in India, instalments in Canada, estimated tax in the USA (Lesson 8.7). Set aside a share of each payment you receive.

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Revenue</dt><dd>what you invoiced, without the sales tax you collected</dd>
<dt>Deductible expense</dt><dd>a cost of earning business income</dd>
<dt>Depreciation / CCA</dt><dd>the share of equipment you may deduct each year</dd>
<dt>Pass-through</dt><dd>the business’s profit is taxed on the owner’s personal return</dd>
<dt>Double tax</dt><dd>company tax on profit, then personal tax on the dividend</dd>
<dt>What it means for you</dt><dd>From day one, set aside a share of every payment you receive (20 % to 30 % is a common start) in a separate account for income tax.</dd>
</dl>

## 5. Worked example

*Made-up founder.* Diego, a Toronto consultant, invoices $150,000 plus $19,500 HST, and spends $50,000 on software, a co-working desk and a contractor. The HST is not income. Profit: $100,000. As a sole proprietor he pays about $20,000 of income tax plus about $9,300 of CPP. If a corporation earned it and kept it, corporate tax would be about $12,200; he would pay personal tax later when he takes the money out.

<div class="myth">

**"If I leave the money in the business account, it isn’t taxed."** In a sole proprietorship, partnership or default US LLC, all the profit is taxed on you this year whether you take it out or not. Only a company or corporation keeps profit taxed at its own rate until it pays you.

</div>

## 6. Practice

1. Which of these is not revenue for income tax: fees billed, GST collected, interest on business savings?
2. Your company made a profit but paid you nothing. Do you owe personal tax on that profit?
3. Why should a new founder save part of every client payment?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The GST collected.
2. Not in a company or corporation (the company pays corporate tax); yes in a sole proprietorship, partnership or default US LLC.
3. No tax is withheld; advance tax, instalments or estimates are due during the year.
</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Taxable profit = revenue − deductible expenses − depreciation; sales tax collected is not revenue.
- Proprietors and partners (and default US LLC owners) pay personal tax on the business profit.
- Companies pay corporate tax; owners pay again on salary (deductible for the company) or dividends (paid from taxed profit).
- Losses can usually be carried forward: 8 years India, 20 years Canada, indefinitely in the USA (80 % limit).
- No tax is withheld from profit, so advance tax, instalments or estimated payments are due during the year.

</div>
