**Module 6 · about 12 minutes · read first: 5.2.** You'll be able to prepare, give and follow up a short data presentation that ends with a decision.

## 1. Why this matters

For many analysts, presenting is where the work becomes visible to the people who decide promotions. A clear 10-minute presentation can carry more weight than weeks of analysis nobody sees. The good news: presenting data well is mostly preparation and a few habits, not charisma.

## 2. The idea in one picture

**Before:** big idea, three to six slides, rehearse. **During:** answer first, one point per slide, walk through charts, stop for questions. **After:** follow-up email with decisions and next steps.

## 3. Before

1. **Know the slot:** 10 minutes on the agenda often means 5 in practice. Plan the 2-minute version.
2. **Pre-wire:** share the main finding with the key decision-maker before the meeting if it's surprising or bad news. No one likes being surprised in front of their boss.
3. **Rehearse out loud,** once, with a timer. Cut whatever doesn't fit.
4. **Prepare backup:** appendix slides for the three questions you expect.
5. **Check every number** on the slides against the source. One wrong number undermines all the others.

## 4. During

1. **Open with the answer and the ask:** "I'm going to recommend we report like-for-like growth monthly. Here's why."
2. **Walk through each chart:** "This shows monthly sales; blue is 2025, grey is 2024. Look at February: the only month below last year."
3. **One slide, one point;** pause after each. Let them read.
4. **Speak in plain words:** "sales from the same four stores", not "LFL YoY delta".
5. **Invite questions early,** and keep track of time.
6. **End with the ask:** "Can we agree to switch the monthly report from January?"

## 5. After

Send a short email the same day: the decision (or the open question), next steps with owners and dates, and a link to the deck and data.

<details class="maths" open><summary>Principle</summary>

**Principle:** orient, then direct. A new chart is a new map: first say what the axes and colours are, then point to the one place that matters. Skipping the first step leaves people lost; skipping the second leaves them wandering.

**Analogy:** a tour guide on a bus: "On your left is the old harbour (orient). Look at the red building: that's where the city began (direct)."
</details>

## 6. Worked example

*Opening for the Northbay review (30 seconds):*
"Thanks for the time. In short: 2025 beat target by 6.6 %, but the older stores grew 14 %, half the headline, because the new store is in the numbers. I'm going to ask you to approve a monthly like-for-like report. Three slides, then questions."

*Follow-up email:* "Thanks for today. Decided: like-for-like growth goes on the monthly dashboard from January (owner: me, by 15 Dec). Open: what happened in February; Priya will check stock records by Friday. Deck and data: link."

## 7. Practice

1. You have 10 minutes on the agenda and the meeting is running late. What do you do?
2. Write the sentence you'd use to walk someone through the weekday sales chart.
3. Why share bad news with the decision-maker before the meeting?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Give the 2-minute version: the answer, the one chart that proves it, the ask. Offer the rest by email.
2. For example: "Each column is one weekday's sales over two years. Look at the two blue ones on the right: Saturday and Sunday together are over a third of all sales."
3. So they have time to think and aren't surprised in front of others; they're more likely to support your recommendation.
</details>
