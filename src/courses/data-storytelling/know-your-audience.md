**Module 1 · about 10 minutes · read first: 1.1.** You'll be able to describe your audience in three questions and fit the same finding to different people.

## 1. Why this matters

Most charts fail not because they are wrong but because they were made for nobody in particular. A store manager needs to know what to change in their store this week. A finance director needs to know if the year's plan is safe. The same table can't serve both. Knowing who decides, and what they need, tells you what to show and, just as important, what to cut.

## 2. The idea in one picture

The same number, February 2025 sales of $13.4k, written for three people:

| Audience | What they care about | What to show them |
|---|---|---|
| CEO | Is the year on track? | One line: "February missed target by 8 %, but 2025 finished 7 % over target. No action needed beyond a check." |
| Regional manager | Which stores, and why? | Feb sales by store vs last year, with likely causes. |
| Store manager | What do I do on Monday? | Their own store's daily sales in February, stock-outs and staffing. |

## 3. Three questions about your audience

1. **Who decides?** Name the person, not "management". If several people, who has the final say?
2. **What do they already know and believe?** If they think the new store is a success, and your data shows it's hiding a slowdown elsewhere, you're telling them something unwelcome: plan for that.
3. **What do you want them to do?** Approve, change, stop, look into, or just know. If the answer is "nothing", ask whether the analysis needs presenting at all.

Also ask: **how much time do they have, and how will they see it?** In a meeting with you there to explain, or alone on a phone between meetings? The second needs every chart to make sense with no voice-over.

<details class="maths" open><summary>Principle</summary>

**Principle:** the right level of detail is the level at which the audience can act. Detail below that is noise for them; detail above it is too vague to use.

**Analogy:** directions. A tourist needs "turn left at the church"; a taxi driver needs only the street name; a pilot needs coordinates. All correct; each one useful to only one person.
</details>

## 4. Worked example

*The Northbay regional manager (West) asks: "Is Forks Market working?"*

- **Who decides:** the regional manager, who will recommend to the CEO whether to open another store like it.
- **What they believe:** they championed the new store and expect good news.
- **What we want:** an honest yes or no, and agreement to judge new stores on a fair test.

So the story: "Forks Market sold $30.2k from March to December, 99.6 % of its target, about 11 % of chain sales in its first year. It is on plan. To decide on the next store, we suggest comparing it with older stores' first years once we have that history." It respects what they know, answers their question first, and asks for something specific.

## 5. Practice

1. Your finding: Electronics has the highest sales but the lowest margin outside Grocery. Write one sentence for a finance director and one for a category buyer.
2. A colleague says "just send the dashboard to everyone". What's the risk?
3. You will present for 5 minutes to someone who has 2 minutes of interest. What do you do?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Finance: "Electronics brings 36 % of sales but only 49 cents of margin per dollar, so growth there adds less profit than the same growth in Home." Buyer: "Electronics margin is 49 % against 54 % in Home; can we review the earbuds' cost price?"
2. Nobody is told what matters; each person digs for their own point, or gives up.
3. Put the answer and the ask in the first 2 minutes; keep the rest as backup for questions.
</details>
