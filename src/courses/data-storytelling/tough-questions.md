**Module 6 · about 10 minutes · read first: 6.1.** You'll have a calm way to handle the three hardest moments in a data presentation.

## 1. Why this matters

Questions are good news: they mean people are engaged. But a tough question handled badly can undo the whole presentation, and one handled well can win the room. Interviewers also ask "tell me about a time someone challenged your numbers", so a method helps there too.

## 2. The idea in one picture

**Listen fully → repeat the question → answer briefly → check it answered them.**

## 3. The three hard moments

**"Your number is wrong."**
1. Stay calm; don't defend yet. "Thanks. Which number, and what were you expecting?"
2. Compare definitions: gross or net? Which dates? Which stores? Refunds included?
3. If it's a definition difference, say both: "Mine is net sales by order date; finance reports by invoice date, which moves about $2k into January." If you made a mistake, say so plainly and when you'll send the corrected figure.

**"I don't know" questions.**
"Good question. I don't know yet. I'll check the stock records and send it by Thursday." Then do it. Never guess a number in a meeting.

**Disagreement with your recommendation.**
1. Understand: "What's your concern?" They may know something you don't (a planned promotion, a lease issue).
2. Restate your strongest evidence once, briefly. Don't repeat the whole deck.
3. Offer a way forward: a trial, more data, a smaller first step. "Could we try it in one store for a month and compare?"

<details class="maths" open><summary>Principle</summary>

**Principle:** separate the person from the problem. A challenge to your number is a question about the data, not about you. Treating it as a shared puzzle ("let's find where our numbers differ") keeps everyone on the same side.

**Analogy:** a pilot and air traffic control disagreeing about altitude: they don't argue, they compare readings and find which instrument is set differently.
</details>

## 4. Worked example

*CFO: "Your 29 % growth doesn't match the 26 % in my report."*

You: "Thanks. Is yours net of refunds? Mine is before refunds and includes Forks Market from March." CFO: "Mine is net, and it's by invoice date." You: "That likely explains it. I'll reconcile the two after the meeting and send one agreed figure and definition by tomorrow. Either way, the point holds: like-for-like growth is about half the headline."

(The numbers in this conversation are an example of how to handle the moment; Northbay's own data has no refunds.)

## 5. Practice

1. Write a reply to: "Why should we trust this data?"
2. Someone asks the same question twice. What does that tell you?
3. A manager asks for a breakdown you haven't prepared. What do you do?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. For example: "It's from the sales system, refreshed daily. I reconciled the 2025 total with the finance monthly report and they match to within 1 %. The known gap is refunds, which I've excluded."
2. Your first answer didn't land: ask what part is unclear, and answer more simply.
3. If you can show it quickly from the dashboard, do; otherwise note it and promise it by a specific time.
