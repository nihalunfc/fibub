**Module 5 · about 12 minutes · read first: 1.3.** You'll be able to shape any analysis into a short story with a beginning, a tension and an answer.

## 1. Why this matters

A list of findings is hard to remember; a story is easy. Stories have a shape our brains expect: things were like this, then something happened, so here's what we do. Using that shape for data makes your presentations easier to follow, and much more likely to lead to action.

## 3. The SCR pattern

1. **Situation:** the context the audience already knows and agrees with. One or two sentences. "2025 sales are up 29 %."
2. **Complication:** what changed, what's surprising, what's at risk. This is why they should listen. "But most of that is the new store; the older stores grew 14 %, and February fell below last year."
3. **Resolution:** what you recommend, and what you need from them. "Report like-for-like growth monthly and review February before setting 2026 targets."

Each part gets one or two charts at most. The complication usually needs the strongest chart.

**Order:** for a curious or junior audience, S → C → R builds understanding. For a senior, busy audience, **R first**: state the recommendation, then the situation and complication as the reasons (Barbara Minto's pyramid principle).

<details class="maths" open><summary>Principle</summary>

**Principle:** tension holds attention. A story with no complication is a status report; nobody needs to act. Find what changed, what's off-plan or what's surprising, and build around it.

**Analogy:** every film has the same skeleton: normal life, then a problem, then how it's solved. Your audience follows a data story the same way.
</details>

## 4. Worked example

*The Northbay 2025 review, in three slides:*

1. **Situation:** "2025 sales reached $262k, 29 % more than 2024 and 6.6 % over target." Chart: monthly sales 2025 vs 2024.
2. **Complication:** "Forks Market accounts for over half of that growth. Like-for-like, the four older stores grew 14 %, and February was below last year." Chart: headline vs like-for-like bars, February highlighted on the trend.
3. **Resolution:** "Report like-for-like monthly from January; investigate February's dip before setting 2026 targets. Decision needed: approve the new monthly report." Chart: a mock-up of the new KPI card.

## 5. Practice

1. Write a situation and complication for: weekends bring over a third of sales, but staffing is the same every day.
2. What's missing from: "Sales grew 29 %. Margins were steady. Forks Market opened in March."?
3. Your manager says "skip to the point". Rearrange the Northbay story.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Situation: "We staff every store evenly across the week." Complication: "But Saturdays and Sundays bring over a third of sales in two days, so weekend queues are likely costing sales."
2. A complication and a resolution: it's a list of facts with no tension or ask.
3. "I recommend we report like-for-like growth monthly. Here's why: headline growth of 29 % is mostly the new store; the older stores grew 14 %, and February was below last year."
</details>
