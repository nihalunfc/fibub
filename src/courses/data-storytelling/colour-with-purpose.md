**Module 3 · about 11 minutes · read first: 3.1.** You'll be able to use colour to point at your message, and avoid colour that confuses.

## 1. Why this matters

Colour is noticed before anything else on a page: before position, size or words. That makes it powerful and dangerous. Used well, it says "look here" in a fraction of a second. Used everywhere, it says nothing, and readers start hunting for meaning that isn't there.

## 3. The rules

1. **Start grey.** Make the whole chart grey (or one muted colour).
2. **Add one strong colour** for the data you're talking about. Two at most.
3. **Match the title:** if the title says "Weekends", the weekend bars are the coloured ones. You can colour words in the title to act as the legend.
4. **Meaning colours mean something.** Red/orange for bad or "needs attention", green/blue for good, used the same way everywhere, and always with a second cue (▲▼, +/−, a label).
5. **Be consistent.** A category keeps its colour on every page.
6. **Sequential data, one hue:** for low to high values (like a heat map), use light to dark of one colour, not a rainbow.

<details class="maths" open><summary>Principle</summary>

**Principle:** pre-attentive attributes. Some differences (colour, size, position, orientation) are noticed in under a quarter of a second, before conscious reading. One coloured item among grey ones "pops out"; ten colours compete and nothing pops out.

**Physical meaning:** your visual system scans for what is different. A red car in a grey car park is found instantly; a red car in a car park of every colour takes a search.
</details>

## 4. Worked example

*A slide about Electronics' Christmas peak* shows four category lines in four bright colours. The reader has to match colours to a legend, then work out which line matters. Fix: Electronics in blue, the other three in light grey, title "Electronics jumps every November and December", with "Electronics" in the same blue. Now the point arrives before the reader has read the axis.

## 5. Practice

1. Your company's brand has five colours. Should every chart use all five?
2. A KPI card shows margin in green when it's above target. What should accompany the green?
3. What's wrong with a heat map coloured from red through yellow, green and blue to purple?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Use one brand colour as the highlight and grey for the rest; keep the others for when categories truly need different colours.
2. A second cue such as ▲, a "+" sign or the words "above target", so it works without colour.
3. A rainbow has no natural order (is green more than yellow?) and changes brightness unevenly, so it creates false boundaries. Use light-to-dark of one hue.
</details>
