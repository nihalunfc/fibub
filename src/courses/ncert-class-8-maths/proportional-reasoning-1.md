**Maths · Class 8 · Chapter 7 · about 45 minutes.** By the end you will be able to write and simplify a ratio, find a missing term of a proportion, share an amount in a ratio, use the unitary method for direct proportion, and read a map scale.

<div class="bigidea">A ratio compares two amounts of the same kind by saying how many of one go with how many of the other. Two ratios are equal when one is just the other scaled up or down. Once you see that scaling, recipes, maps, prices and speed are all the same idea.</div>

## 1. What is a ratio?

A chai stall mixes 2 cups of milk with 1 cup of water. The **ratio** of milk to water is 2 : 1, read "2 to 1". Order matters: the ratio of water to milk is 1 : 2. A ratio has no unit because both amounts are in the same unit and the unit cancels.

**Same units first.** The ratio of 2 m to 50 cm is not 2 : 50. Change both to centimetres: 200 : 50, which is 4 : 1.

**Simplest form.** Divide both terms by their highest common factor (HCF). 24 : 36 has HCF 12, so it is 2 : 3. A ratio is in its simplest form when the two terms have no common factor.

<div class="myth">

**“The ratio of 1 m to 1 cm is 1 : 1.”** No. Both must be in the same unit: 100 cm to 1 cm is 100 : 1. Always convert before you write a ratio.

</div>

## 2. Equal ratios: mixing paint

A painter mixes 2 cups of red with 3 cups of white. To make more of the same shade, she doubles both: 4 red and 6 white. The ratios 2 : 3 and 4 : 6 are **equivalent** because each is the other with both terms multiplied by the same number. Multiply or divide both terms by the same number and the shade does not change.

## 3. Proportion

Four numbers a, b, c, d are **in proportion** when a : b = c : d. Writing them as fractions, a/b = c/d. Multiply both sides by b and d and you get a rule that is very handy:

```math
a \times d = b \times c
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>a and d</dt><dd>the **extremes** (first and last terms)</dd>
<dt>b and c</dt><dd>the **means** (the two middle terms)</dd>
<dt>Rule</dt><dd>In a proportion the product of the extremes equals the product of the means.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

Test 4, 6, 10, 15: 4 × 15 = 60 and 6 × 10 = 60. Equal, so 4 : 6 = 10 : 15. Test 2, 5, 6, 16: 2 × 16 = 32 and 5 × 6 = 30. Not equal, so not in proportion. The area picture is a rectangle a by d and another b by c with the same area.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-prop1-cross"><figcaption>Slide a, b, c and d. If the two rectangles a × d and b × c have equal areas, the numbers are in proportion. The readout shows both fractions so you can see they match.</figcaption></figure>

**Finding a missing term.** If 3 : 4 = x : 20 then 3 × 20 = 4 × x, so x = 60 ÷ 4 = 15.

## 4. Sharing an amount in a ratio

Asha, Bilal and Charu share ₹1,200 in the ratio 2 : 3 : 1. Total parts = 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. One part = ₹1,200 ÷ 6 = ₹200. So Asha gets 2 × 200 = ₹400, Bilal ₹600 and Charu ₹200.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-prop1-share"><figcaption>Change the total and the parts. The bar shows equal parts and the lines show what each person gets. The shares always add back to the total.</figcaption></figure>

The steps are always the same: add the parts, divide the total by the number of parts to find one part, multiply by each person's number of parts, and check the shares add up to the total.

## 5. The unitary method

The **unitary method** finds the value of one unit first, then multiplies. If 6 pens cost ₹90, then one pen costs ₹90 ÷ 6 = ₹15, and 9 pens cost 9 × ₹15 = ₹135.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-prop1-unitary">
<div class="step">6 pens cost ₹90. We want the cost of 9 pens.</div>
<div class="step">Draw 6 equal boxes that together are worth ₹90.</div>
<div class="step">Divide ₹90 by 6. Each box (one pen) is ₹15.</div>
<div class="step">Every box is ₹15.</div>
<div class="step">Draw 9 boxes, one for each pen we want.</div>
<div class="step">9 × ₹15 = ₹135. Nine pens cost ₹135.</div>
</div>

## 6. Direct proportion

Two quantities are in **direct proportion** if when one doubles the other doubles, when one halves the other halves. The cost of potatoes, the distance covered at steady speed, the amount of flour for a number of rotis are all in direct proportion. The ratio cost ÷ quantity stays the same: it is the **unit rate** (₹ per kg, km per hour).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-prop1-direct"><figcaption>Choose the price per kg and the number of kg. The points lie on a straight line that starts at (0, 0). Every point has the same cost ÷ kg, which is the price of one kg.</figcaption></figure>

A graph of direct proportion is a **straight line through the origin**. If the line does not pass through (0, 0), the quantities are not in direct proportion: a taxi with a ₹50 starting charge is not.

## 7. Reading maps: scale

A map shows a big area on a small page, so every length is shrunk by the same number. That number is the **scale**. A scale of 1 : 500 000 means 1 cm on the map is 500 000 cm on the ground. Since 100 000 cm = 1 km, 1 cm stands for 5 km.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-prop1-map"><figcaption>Slide the length of the road on the ruler and pick a scale. The readout shows map cm × scale, then the change from cm to km (divide by 100 000).</figcaption></figure>

Scales are also given in words: "1 cm = 2.5 km" is the same as 1 : 250 000, because 2.5 km = 250 000 cm.

<div class="keypoints">

- A ratio compares two amounts in the same unit. Write it in simplest form by dividing by the HCF.
- Multiplying or dividing both terms by the same number gives an equal ratio.
- a : b = c : d is a proportion when a × d = b × c (extremes times extremes = means times means).
- To share in a ratio: add parts, find one part, multiply.
- Unitary method: find the value of one, then multiply.
- Direct proportion: cost ÷ quantity is constant, and the graph is a line through the origin.
- Scale 1 : N means 1 cm on the map is N cm on the ground; divide by 100 000 to get km.

</div>
