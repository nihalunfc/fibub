**Maths · Class 8 · Chapter 4 · about 55 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name every kind of four-sided shape, say which rules each one follows, find missing angles and lengths, and explain why a parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus and square are all members of one family.

<div class="bigidea">A quadrilateral family is built by adding rules one at a time. Start with any four sides, add one pair of parallel sides, then two, then right angles or equal sides, and each new rule gives a more special shape.</div>

## 1. Four sides and four angles

A **quadrilateral** is a closed shape with four straight sides, four corners (vertices), four angles and two **diagonals** (lines joining opposite corners). A cricket field plan, a window, a kite in the sky and a plot of land can all be quadrilaterals. We name the corners in order round the shape: ABCD has sides AB, BC, CD, DA and diagonals AC and BD.

**The four angles of any quadrilateral add up to 360°.** A quick way to see it is to tear off the corners of a paper quadrilateral and fit them together around one point: they make a full turn. Step through it.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-quad-sum">
<div class="step">Any quadrilateral ABCD (a lopsided one).</div>
<div class="step">Mark its four angles. They are all different.</div>
<div class="step">Tear off the four corners (shown pulled apart).</div>
<div class="step">Fit the corners together with their points touching. They fill the whole way round.</div>
<div class="step">A full turn is 360°, so the four angles add up to 360°. A diagonal gives the same answer: it makes two triangles, 2 × 180°.</div>

## 2. Four sides are floppy: why we name special shapes

A triangle is **rigid**: if you know its three sides you know its exact shape. But a four-sided frame can lean over, like a bookshelf pushed sideways or a collapsible gate. Lean the frame below. The side lengths stay the same, the shape does not. To fix a quadrilateral you need **five** measurements, for example four sides and one diagonal.

## 3. The family of quadrilaterals

Start with any quadrilateral and add rules.

- **Trapezium**: at least one pair of parallel sides.
- **Kite**: two pairs of equal sides that sit next to each other.
- **Parallelogram**: both pairs of opposite sides parallel.
- **Rectangle**: a parallelogram with four right angles.
- **Rhombus**: a parallelogram with four equal sides.
- **Square**: four equal sides and four right angles. It is both a rectangle and a rhombus.

<div class="myth">

**“A square is not a rectangle.”** A rectangle needs four right angles, and a square has them. Every square is a rectangle, a rhombus and a parallelogram. But most rectangles are not squares. In the same way, every parallelogram is a trapezium if “trapezium” means at least one pair of parallel sides.

</div>

## 4. The parallelogram

Push a rectangular tile sideways and you get a parallelogram. Opposite sides stay parallel, but the angles are no longer 90°. A railway gate, a pantograph and a folding clothes-stand all use this shape. A parallelogram has four properties:

1. **Opposite sides are equal** (and parallel).
2. **Opposite angles are equal.**
3. **Neighbouring angles add up to 180°.**
4. **The diagonals bisect each other** (cut each other in half).

The first three are easy to see from the angles below. Slide angle A and read the others: B and D are always 180° − A, and C equals A.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-quad-angles"><figcaption>Slide angle A. Opposite angles stay equal, neighbouring ones always add up to 180°, and the four angles together are always 360°.</figcaption></figure>

Why are neighbours 180°? Both of them lie between the same pair of parallel sides (a ladder between two rails): angles on the same side add up to 180°.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8m-quad-ratio"></div>

There is a single picture that explains properties 1, 2 and 4 at once: **a parallelogram looks the same after a half-turn about the point where its diagonals meet.** Slide the turn to 180° and watch the copy land on the original.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-quad-halfturn"><figcaption>Turn a copy of ABCD about O. At 180° A goes to C and B goes to D, and the copy lies exactly on the original. So AB = CD, AD = BC, the opposite angles match and O is the middle of both diagonals.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8m-quad-diagonals"></div>

<div class="myth">

**“The diagonals of a parallelogram are equal.”** Only in a rectangle. In a leaning parallelogram one diagonal is clearly longer. What is always true is that they cut each other in half.

</div>

## 5. Rectangle, rhombus and square

These three are parallelograms with something extra, so they keep all four properties above.

- **Rectangle**: four right angles. Extra property: **the diagonals are equal.** (A door frame is checked for squareness by measuring both diagonals: if they match, the frame is a rectangle.)
- **Rhombus**: four equal sides. Extra property: **the diagonals cross at right angles** and bisect the angles at the corners. A rhombus is a leaning square; its area is half the product of the diagonals.
- **Square**: both, so its diagonals are equal, perpendicular and bisect each other.

## 6. The kite and the trapezium

A **kite** has two pairs of equal sides next to each other. It is symmetric about one diagonal. Fold along that diagonal and the two halves match exactly. Slide the fold.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-quad-kitefold"><figcaption>A kite ABCD with AB = AD and CB = CD, folded along the line of symmetry AC. The halves match, so the angles at B and D are equal and the diagonals cross at 90°. AC cuts the other diagonal in half, but not the other way round.</figcaption></figure>

So in a kite: the angles at the two ends of the cross-diagonal (B and D) are **equal**, the diagonals are **perpendicular**, and only one diagonal is bisected. The angles at A and C are usually different.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8m-quad-kite"></div>

A **trapezium** has one pair of parallel sides, the bases. The other two sides are the legs. If the legs are equal it is an **isosceles trapezium** and then its diagonals are equal, as are the angles at each base. In any trapezium, the angles on the same leg add up to 180°, because they sit between parallel lines. The shape of a typical Indian hand fan, a bucket seen from the side and a tea-stall counter are trapeziums.

<div class="history">

**Shapes of the field.** The Sulba Sutras, ancient Indian texts on building fire altars (about 800 to 500 BCE), give rules for drawing rectangles, squares, trapeziums and other shapes accurately with cords and pegs, and for turning one into another of the same area. Quadrilaterals have been in daily life for a very long time.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- The four angles of any quadrilateral add up to 360°. Four sides alone do not fix the shape; a triangle’s three sides do.
- Trapezium: one pair of parallel sides. Kite: two pairs of equal neighbouring sides. Parallelogram: two pairs of parallel sides.
- Parallelogram: opposite sides and opposite angles equal, neighbouring angles add to 180°, diagonals bisect each other.
- Rectangle: equal diagonals. Rhombus: perpendicular diagonals. Square: both. Kite: perpendicular diagonals, and one is bisected.
- Every square is a rectangle and a rhombus; every rectangle and rhombus is a parallelogram.

</div>
