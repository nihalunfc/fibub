**Maths · Class 8 · Chapter 8 · about 55 minutes.** By the end you will be able to move between fractions, decimals and percents, work out profit, loss, discount and tax, find a percent change, and compare simple and compound interest.

<div class="bigidea">A percent is a fraction whose bottom number is 100. "30%" means 30 out of every 100. Fractions, decimals and percents are three disguises of the same number, so you can pick whichever is easiest. The big question in money problems is always: percent of what? Find the base, and everything else follows.</div>

## 1. Fractions, decimals and percents

The word **per cent** comes from Latin for "out of a hundred". 1/4 = 25/100 = 0.25 = 25%. To change a fraction to a percent, divide top by bottom and multiply by 100: 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%. To change a percent to a fraction, put it over 100 and simplify: 60% = 60/100 = 3/5.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-pct-grid"><figcaption>Choose a denominator and slide the numerator. The 10 × 10 grid has 100 squares and the shaded ones are the percent. The readout shows the same amount as a fraction, a decimal and a percent.</figcaption></figure>

| Fraction | Decimal | Percent |
|---|---|---|
| 1/2 | 0.5 | 50% |
| 1/4 | 0.25 | 25% |
| 3/4 | 0.75 | 75% |
| 1/5 | 0.2 | 20% |
| 1/8 | 0.125 | 12.5% |
| 1/10 | 0.1 | 10% |

Percents can be more than 100 (a price that doubles has risen to 200% of the old price) and less than 1 (0.5% means half of one in a hundred).

## 2. A percent of a quantity, and "what percent?"

**Finding a percent of an amount.** 15% of 240 means 15/100 × 240 = 36. A mental trick: 10% of 240 is 24 and 5% is 12, and 24 + 12 = 36.

**Finding what percent one number is of another.** What percent of 80 is 20? 20/80 = 1/4 = 25%.

**Finding the whole when a part is known.** If 20% of a number is 30, then 1% is 1.5 and 100% is 150.

## 3. Profit and loss

The price a shopkeeper pays is the **cost price (CP)**. The price at which it is sold is the **selling price (SP)**.

```math
\text{Profit} = SP - CP \quad (SP > CP), \qquad \text{Loss} = CP - SP \quad (CP > SP)
```

```math
\text{Profit \%} = \frac{\text{Profit}}{CP} \times 100, \qquad \text{Loss \%} = \frac{\text{Loss}}{CP} \times 100
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>CP</dt><dd>what the seller paid (the base for the percent)</dd>
<dt>SP</dt><dd>what the buyer pays the seller</dd>
<dt>Profit % and loss %</dt><dd>always taken on the cost price, not on the selling price</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-pct-profit"><figcaption>Set a cost price and a selling price. The two bars show them and the strip between the ends is the profit (green) or loss (red). The readout divides by the cost price to give the percent.</figcaption></figure>

**Example.** A lamp bought at ₹400 is sold at ₹460. Profit = ₹60. Profit % = 60 ÷ 400 × 100 = 15%.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8m-pct-profit"></div>

<div class="myth">

**“Profit percent is on the selling price.”** It is not. A shopkeeper who buys for ₹100 and sells for ₹150 earns ₹50, which is 50% of the cost price (not 33% of the selling price).

</div>

## 4. Discount, marked price and tax

Shops print a **marked price** (MRP) on an item and then offer a **discount**, a percent off the marked price. A shirt marked ₹800 with a 25% discount costs 800 − 25% of 800 = 800 − 200 = ₹600.

**Tax.** In India GST is a tax added to the price after the discount. If GST on the item is 12%, ₹600 becomes 600 + 12% of 600 = ₹672. (GST rates differ by item: check the official source for the current rate.)

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8m-pct-disc"></div>

**Finding the marked price backwards.** If a 20% discount leaves ₹640, then ₹640 is 80% of the marked price. 1% is ₹8 and 100% is ₹800.

## 5. Percent change

The **percent change** compares the change with the **old** value:

```math
\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100
```

A bus fare goes from ₹40 to ₹50: change = 10, percent = 10 ÷ 40 × 100 = 25% increase. If it then goes back from 50 to 40 the fall is 10 ÷ 50 × 100 = 20%. Rise and fall are different percents because the base is different.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-pct-compare"><figcaption>Slide the old and new values. The first percent is on the old value and the second percent is on the new value, so they are not the same.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8m-pct-change"></div>

**One change after another.** A phone costs ₹100. The price rises 20%, and later falls 20%. Do you get back to ₹100?

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-pct-change">
<div class="step">The price is ₹100.</div>
<div class="step">A 20% rise adds 20% of 100, which is ₹20.</div>
<div class="step">The new price is ₹120.</div>
<div class="step">A 20% fall takes off 20% of 120, which is ₹24, not ₹20.</div>
<div class="step">The final price is ₹96, which is 4% below the start.</div>
<div class="step">The two 20% were taken on different amounts. In short: 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“A 20% rise and a 20% fall cancel out.”** They do not. Each percent is taken of the amount at that moment. Two successive 10% discounts are also not one 20% discount: ₹1,000 → ₹900 → ₹810.

</div>

## 6. Simple interest

When you put money in a bank or borrow it, the extra paid is **interest**. The money is the **principal (P)**, the percent paid each year is the **rate (R)** and the number of years is the **time (T)**. For **simple interest** the interest each year is the same, a percent of the original principal:

```math
SI = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}, \qquad \text{Amount} = P + SI
```

₹8,000 at 5% a year for 4 years: SI = 8000 × 5 × 4 ÷ 100 = ₹1,600, so the amount is ₹9,600.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8m-pct-si"></div>

## 7. Compound interest

In **compound interest** the interest is added to the money every year, and the next year's interest is on the bigger sum: "interest on interest". After one year the amount is P(1 + R/100). After n years:

```math
A = P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100}\right)^n
```

₹10,000 at 10% for 2 years: year 1: 10,000 × 1.1 = 11,000. Year 2: 11,000 × 1.1 = 12,100. Compound interest = 12,100 − 10,000 = ₹2,100. Simple interest would be only ₹2,000.

In year 1 both are equal; compound pulls ahead after that.</figcaption></figure>

Compound growth also works downward for **depreciation**: a ₹80,000 scooter losing 10% a year is worth 80,000 × 0.9 = ₹72,000 after one year and ₹64,800 after two. <div class="keypoints">

- A percent is a fraction out of 100: 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.
- Percent of an amount = percent ÷ 100 × amount. Always ask: percent of what?
- Profit and loss percent are on the cost price: (SP − CP) ÷ CP × 100.
- Discount is on the marked price. Tax is added after the discount.
- Percent change = change ÷ old value × 100. A 20% rise followed by a 20% fall leaves a 4% loss.
- Simple interest SI = P × R × T ÷ 100; compound amount A = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ.
- Compound interest beats simple interest after the first year because it earns interest on interest.

</div>
