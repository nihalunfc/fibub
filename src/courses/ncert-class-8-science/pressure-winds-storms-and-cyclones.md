**Science · Class 8 · Chapter 6 · about 45 minutes.** By the end you can use `$p = F/A$`, explain why liquids and air press on things, say why winds blow, and describe how a cyclone forms and how to stay safe.

<div class="bigidea">Pressure is how hard a force presses on each bit of area. Differences in pressure push liquids through pipes and air across the sky. That moving air is wind, and a huge spinning wind is a cyclone.</div>

## 1. Pressure: force on an area

Stand on soft sand in sandals, then in high heels. The heels sink in much deeper, though your weight is the same. The difference is the **area**. **Pressure** is the force on each unit of area:

```math
p = \frac{F}{A}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>p</dt><dd>pressure in pascal (Pa); 1 Pa = 1 newton on every square metre</dd>
<dt>F</dt><dd>force pressing at right angles to the surface, in newtons</dd>
<dt>A</dt><dd>area it presses on, in square metres</dd>
</dl>
</div>

The same force on a **small area** gives a **large pressure** (a nail, a knife, a needle). Spread over a **large area** it gives a small pressure (a camel's wide feet, a bus's wide tyres, a school bag's wide straps).

**“Pressure is the same as force.”** No. A needle and a thumb can push with the same force, yet only the needle pricks you. Pressure includes the area.

## 2. Pressure in liquids

A liquid has weight, so it presses on the bottom and the walls of its container, and also sideways and upward on anything inside it. The deeper you go, the more liquid is above you, so the pressure grows with depth. You feel it in your ears when you dive in a swimming pool.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-press-jets"><figcaption>Water squirts out of three holes in a tank. The lowest hole has the deepest water above it, so its jet leaves fastest. Lower the water level and every jet weakens.</figcaption></figure>

This is why:

- dam walls are built **thicker at the bottom**;
- water tanks are placed on top of buildings: the deep water in the tank makes pressure that pushes water through the taps below;
- deep-sea divers and submarines need strong suits and hulls.

You can explore the depth idea in the [Liquid pressure](/learn/liquid-pressure/) tool, and the way a liquid passes pressure on in the [Hydraulic lift](/learn/hydraulic-lift/) tool.

## 3. Air pressure

The air around Earth is a thick layer of gas that reaches up for hundreds of kilometres. It has weight too, so it presses on everything: this is **atmospheric pressure**. At sea level it is about **100 000 Pa (1013 hPa)**, which is about 10 tonnes of weight on every square metre. We do not feel crushed because the air and fluids inside our bodies push outward with the same pressure.

Air pressure explains many everyday things:

- A **drinking straw**: sucking lowers the pressure inside; the air outside pushes the juice up.
- A **rubber suction hook** sticks because the air is pushed out, and the outside air presses it on the wall.
- A **sealed can** can be crushed by the outside air if the air inside is removed. Watch how:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-press-can">
<div class="step">A little water boils in an open can. Steam pushes out most of the air.</div>
<div class="step">Flip the can quickly into cold water so nothing can get in.</div>
<div class="step">The steam condenses, leaving very little inside, so the pressure falls.</div>
<div class="step">The much greater outside pressure crushes the can.</div>
</div>

### Air pressure and height

As you climb a mountain there is less air above you, so the pressure **falls**. At the top of Mount Everest it is only about one third of the pressure at sea level, so climbers carry oxygen.

## 4. Winds: air moving from high to low pressure

Air flows from a place of **high pressure** to a place of **low pressure**, just as water flows downhill. That moving air is **wind**. The bigger the pressure difference, the stronger the wind.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-press-wind"><figcaption>Change the pressure on each side. The dots drift from the high side to the low side, and equal pressures give no wind at all.</figcaption></figure>

<!--fig:tool wind-from-pressure-->

Why do pressures differ? Because the Sun heats the ground unevenly. Warm air **expands and rises**, leaving a region of lower pressure near the ground. Cooler air nearby has higher pressure and rushes in to fill the gap.

### Sea breeze and land breeze

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-press-seabreeze">
<div class="step">By day the land warms faster than the sea.</div>
<div class="step">Air over the hot land rises, leaving low pressure near the ground.</div>
<div class="step">Cooler air from the sea flows in: a <b>sea breeze</b>.</div>
<div class="step">At night the land cools faster, so the flow reverses: a <b>land breeze</b>.</div>
</div>

The same idea on a huge scale gives the **monsoon**: in summer the land of India heats up, a low pressure forms, and moist air from the sea blows in with the rains.

<div class="myth">

**“Wind blows because the air is cold.”** Wind blows because of a *pressure difference*. Temperature differences are one way to make that difference.

</div>

## 5. Thunderstorms and cyclones

A **thunderstorm** forms when warm, moist air rises fast, cools and makes tall dark clouds with heavy rain, lightning and thunder. Lightning is a huge electric spark between clouds, or between cloud and ground. We see it first because light travels much faster than sound.

A **cyclone** is a much bigger, spinning storm that forms over warm oceans. Warm moist air rises over the sea, leaving low pressure below. Surrounding air rushes in, but because Earth spins it curls into a **spiral** (anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere). The rising air makes huge clouds and heavy rain.

Cyclones bring three dangers: **very strong winds**, **heavy rain and floods**, and a **storm surge**, a wall of sea water pushed onto the coast. India's coasts on the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea are often hit, which is why the **India Meteorological Department (IMD)** tracks cyclones with satellites and radar and issues warnings.

### Staying safe

- Listen to official alerts on the radio, phone or TV; do not trust rumours.
- Move from low coastal areas to a cyclone shelter when told to.
- Stay indoors, away from windows. Do not shelter under trees or poles.
- Keep a torch, drinking water, dry food, a first-aid kit and your documents ready.
- After the storm, keep away from fallen wires and flooded areas.

<div class="keypoints">

- Pressure = force ÷ area (Pa). Small area, big pressure; big area, small pressure.
- Liquid pressure grows with depth and acts in all directions.
- Air presses too: about 100 000 Pa at sea level, falling with height.
- Wind is air moving from high to low pressure; uneven heating makes the pressure difference (sea breeze, land breeze, monsoon).
- A cyclone is a spinning storm over warm seas with a calm eye, a strong eye wall and low central pressure; early warnings and shelters keep people safe.

</div>
