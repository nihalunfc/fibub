**Biology · Class 8 · Chapter 2 · about 45 minutes.** By the end you can say how a microscope helps us see what the eye cannot, work out its magnification, name the main groups of microorganisms, and explain how microbes both help and harm us.

<div class="bigidea">Around you, on you and inside you live billions of living things too small to see. A few cause disease, but most are harmless and many are friends: they make curd, clean up dead leaves, enrich the soil and give us medicines.</div>

## 1. Seeing the tiny: lenses and the microscope

The naked eye cannot see anything smaller than about 0.1 mm, about the width of a fine hair. A **hand lens** (magnifying glass) makes things look 5 to 10 times bigger. To see smaller things you need a **microscope**.

In 1665 Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork under a simple microscope and saw tiny box-like rooms. He called them **cells**. A few years later, in the 1670s, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek ground tiny lenses so good that he saw moving "animalcules" in pond water and in the scum from his own teeth. They were the first microorganisms ever seen by a human.

A **compound microscope** uses two lenses. The **objective lens** (near the slide) makes a magnified image, and the **eyepiece** magnifies it once more.

```math
\text{total magnification} = \text{eyepiece power} \times \text{objective power}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>eyepiece power</dt><dd>usually 10×</dd>
<dt>objective power</dt><dd>4×, 10×, 40× or 100× (you pick one by turning the nose-piece)</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>the image is that many times wider than the real object. A bacterium 2 µm long looks 2 × 400 = 800 µm = 0.8 mm long at 400×.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

## 2. Meet the microorganisms

A **microorganism** (microbe) is a living thing that can be seen only with a microscope. They are found everywhere: in soil, water, air, on your skin, inside your gut, even in hot springs and on ice. There are five main groups.

| Group | What it is | Examples | Where / what it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | single cell, no nucleus, can be rod, round or spiral shaped | *Lactobacillus*, *Rhizobium*, *Vibrio cholerae* | in curd, soil, your gut; some cause disease |
| **Fungi** | yeast (single cell) and moulds (threads) | yeast, bread mould | rising dough, decay, antibiotics like penicillin |
| **Protozoa** | single-celled, animal-like, can move | *Amoeba*, *Plasmodium*, *Paramecium* | ponds; malaria is caused by a protozoan |
| **Algae** | simple plant-like, contain chlorophyll | *Spirogyra*, *Chlamydomonas* | make food and oxygen in ponds and the sea |
| **Viruses** | not cells at all; a little genetic material in a protein coat | influenza, polio, measles, coronavirus | multiply only inside a living cell |

Viruses are very odd. They have no cell, they do not eat, grow or make energy by themselves, but once inside a host cell they multiply wildly. Scientists often call them "on the border" between living and non-living.

<div class="myth">

**"All microbes are germs that make us ill."** No. Only a small fraction cause disease. Most are harmless, and many are essential: without microbes dead plants would never rot, cows could not digest grass and there would be no curd.

## 3. Friends: microbes that help us

- **Curd and yoghurt.** *Lactobacillus* bacteria turn milk sugar into lactic acid, so the milk sets and turns sour.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-micro-curd">
<div class="step">Warm the milk to about body temperature. Too hot kills the bacteria; too cold slows them.</div>
<div class="step">Add a spoonful of old curd. It carries the helpful bacteria.</div>
<div class="step">Keep it warm and covered. The bacteria feed on milk sugar and divide again and again.</div>
<div class="step">They make lactic acid, which makes the milk proteins clump together.</div>
<div class="step">Curd is ready. The sour taste is the lactic acid. Cooling it slows the bacteria down.</div>
</div>

- **Bread, idli and dosa.** Yeast eats sugar and releases carbon dioxide gas. The bubbles puff up the dough or the idli batter, which is why we leave it overnight in a warm place.
- **Medicines.** In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed that the mould *Penicillium* killed bacteria around it. This led to **penicillin**, the first **antibiotic**. Antibiotics kill bacteria, but not viruses.
- **Vaccines** use dead, weakened or harmless pieces of microbes to train our body (Chapter 3).
- **Cleaning nature.** Bacteria and fungi are **decomposers**: they break down dead plants, animals and waste into simple substances that go back to the soil. Sewage plants use bacteria to clean dirty water.
- **Fertile soil.** *Rhizobium* bacteria live in the root nodules of gram, peas and other pulses and turn nitrogen from the air into a form the plant can use. That is why farmers grow pulses between crops.
- **Gut helpers.** Bacteria in your intestine help to digest food and make some vitamins.

### How fast do microbes multiply?

A bacterium splits into two, and each of those splits again, and so on. If one divides every 20 minutes, after 1 hour there are 2³ = 8, after 3 hours 2⁹ = 512, and after 8 hours more than 16 million.

```math
N = 2^{\,n} \qquad n = \dfrac{\text{time}}{\text{doubling time}}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>N</dt><dd>number of bacteria after starting from one</dd>
<dt>n</dt><dd>how many times the group has doubled</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>growth by doubling is explosively fast, which is why a drop of milk left in the sun turns sour so quickly. Cold, dryness and salt slow the doubling.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

## 4. Foes: microbes that harm us

- **Human diseases.** Cholera and typhoid (water, bacteria), tuberculosis (air, bacteria), malaria (mosquito, protozoan), dengue and measles (viruses). More in Chapter 3.
- **Plant diseases.** Rust and blight fungi, and viruses such as the one that causes yellow-vein mosaic in okra, ruin crops.
- **Animal diseases.** Foot-and-mouth disease in cattle (virus) and anthrax (bacterium).
- **Food spoilage.** Microbes in cooked rice, cut fruit or milk make the food smell, change colour or grow fuzzy. Eating spoilt food can cause **food poisoning**.

## 5. Keeping food safe

Microbes need **food, water, warmth and time**. Every method of preserving food takes one of these away, or kills the microbes.

| Method | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Cooling | cold slows microbes | refrigerator, ice |
| Drying | removes water | dried fish, papad, raisins, sun-dried chillies |
| Salting and sugar | draws water out of microbes | pickles, jam, salted fish |
| Oil | keeps air and water away | mango pickle |
| Heating and sealing | kills microbes, the seal keeps new ones out | canned and packaged food |
| Chemical preservatives | stop growth | sodium benzoate in jams and sauces (small amounts) |

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-micro-spoil"><figcaption>Choose a method and slide the time. Without treatment the microbe level rises within hours. Salting and drying slow it for weeks; a boiled, sealed jar stays safe.</figcaption></figure>

Good habits at home: wash hands before cooking and eating, keep food covered, drink boiled or filtered water, wash fruit and vegetables, and keep cooked food in the fridge.

<div class="myth">

**"Boiling food once makes it safe for ever."** Boiling kills the microbes that are there at the time. Left uncovered, new microbes from the air and from hands settle in and multiply again. Sealing or cooling is needed to keep it safe.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- The unaided eye cannot see objects below about 0.1 mm. A microscope magnifies; total magnification = eyepiece × objective.
- Hooke named the cell (1665); Leeuwenhoek saw the first microbes (1670s).
- Main groups: bacteria, fungi, protozoa, algae and viruses. Viruses are not cells and multiply only inside a host.
- Useful microbes: curd (Lactobacillus), bread and idli (yeast), antibiotics (Penicillium), decomposers, Rhizobium in pulse roots, gut bacteria.
- Harmful microbes cause human, plant and animal diseases and spoil food.
- Bacteria multiply by doubling, N = 2ⁿ; cold, dryness, salt and heat stop or slow the growth.
- Preserve food by cooling, drying, salting, oiling, heating and sealing, or using preservatives.

</div>
