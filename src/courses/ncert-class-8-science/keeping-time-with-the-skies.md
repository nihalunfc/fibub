<div class="bigidea">People have kept time with the sky for thousands of years because the sky repeats: the Sun rises daily, the Moon's phases repeat monthly, and the seasons return yearly. All of these patterns come from three motions: the Earth spinning, the Moon going round the Earth, and the Earth going round the Sun.</div>

## 1. Day and night

The Earth spins on its axis once in about **24 hours**, turning from west to east. At any moment half of the Earth faces the Sun and has **day**; the other half faces away and has **night**. As a place turns into the sunlight we see **sunrise**; as it turns out of it, **sunset**.

The Sun looks as if it moves across the sky from east to west, but that is only because we are moving. It is like sitting in a train: the trees seem to rush backwards.

The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so it turns **15° every hour**, or **1° in 4 minutes**. This is why places to the east see the Sun earlier. When the Sun is at its highest in Kolkata, it is still about 45 minutes before noon by the Sun in Delhi. India has one standard time (IST) for the whole country, based on the line of longitude 82.5° E near Prayagraj. ## 2. Telling time by a shadow

Plant a straight stick upright in the sun. In the morning the Sun is low in the east, so the shadow is **long and points west**. As the Sun climbs, the shadow shrinks. At **noon** the Sun is highest and the shadow is **shortest**. In the afternoon the Sun descends in the west and the shadow lengthens again, pointing east.

This is exactly how a **sundial** tells time, and the Jantar Mantar observatories built by Maharaja Jai Singh in Delhi and Jaipur are giant sundials. A stick used like this is called a **gnomon**.

## 3. The Moon and its phases

The Moon does not make its own light. It reflects sunlight, so **one half of it is always lit**. The Moon goes round the Earth once in about **29.5 days**, and as it moves we see different amounts of its lit half. These are the **phases**.

- **New Moon:** the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun, and the lit half faces away from us. We cannot see it.
- **Waxing crescent:** a thin sliver on the right (in India) appears after sunset in the west.
- **First quarter:** half of the face is lit.
- **Waxing gibbous:** more than half, less than full.
- **Full Moon:** the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon and we see the whole lit face. It rises at sunset.
- **Waning gibbous, last quarter, waning crescent:** the lit part shrinks again until the next new Moon.

"Waxing" means growing and "waning" means shrinking. The shape you see is the same lit half seen from different angles, not the Earth's shadow.

## 4. Eclipses

An **eclipse** happens when the Sun, the Moon and the Earth line up so that one's shadow falls on another.

- In a **solar eclipse** the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth, so the Moon's shadow falls on part of the Earth. It can only happen on a **new Moon**. People in the dark central shadow see a total eclipse and the Sun's corona; people in the lighter shadow see a partial one.
- In a **lunar eclipse** the Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, so the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. It can only happen on a **full Moon**. The Moon often turns a dim red.

Then why is there not an eclipse every month? Because the Moon's orbit is **tilted about 5°** to the Earth's path around the Sun. At most new and full Moons the Moon passes a little above or below the line, and the shadow misses. Slide the Moon to see how exact the line-up must be.

## 5. Calendars and leap years

A **day** is one turn of the Earth, a **month** was first one cycle of the Moon (29.5 days) and a **year** is one trip of the Earth around the Sun, about **365.25 days** (more exactly 365.2422). The trouble is that these do not divide neatly.

- A **lunar calendar** with 12 months has 12 × 29.5 = 354 days, about 11 days short of a year. Without a fix, festivals would slide through the seasons. Many Indian calendars (the Hindu panchang) add an extra "adhika" month every two or three years; the Islamic calendar does not, so Ramadan moves through the seasons.
- A **solar calendar**, such as the one we use daily, has 365 days. To fix the missing quarter day, every fourth year gets an extra day (29 February): a **leap year** has 366 days.

Even that is slightly too much, since the real year is 365.2422 days, not 365.25. So century years are leap years only if divisible by 400: 1900 was not, 2000 was, and 2100 will not be.

```math
\text{drift} = \text{years} \times (365.2422 - \text{days in calendar year})
```

## 6. The night sky: pole star and constellations

On a clear night the stars seem to turn once a day around a fixed point in the northern sky. A long-exposure photo shows each star leaving a circle, or an arc, around that point. This is because the Earth is spinning, not because the stars move. The star almost exactly at the centre is the **pole star** (*Dhruva Tara*, Polaris), which lies almost directly above the Earth's north pole. It barely moves, so for travellers and sailors it always shows **north**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-sky-polestar"><figcaption>Slide the hours of exposure or press Play. Each star turns 15° every hour on an arc around the pole star; stars near it make small circles, far ones big circles.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8s-sky-trail"></div>

A **constellation** is a pattern of stars that people have joined up with imaginary lines and given a name, a bit like joining dots. The stars in one constellation are usually nowhere near each other in space; they only look close together from Earth. Two to know:

- **Saptarishi** (Big Dipper, or Ursa Major): seven bright stars shaped like a ladle. The two stars at the end of the "bowl" point towards the pole star. In Indian tradition they are the seven sages.
- **Orion** (*Mriga*, the hunter): visible in the winter evening sky, with three stars in a row as its belt. Extend the belt downwards and it points to Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky.

<div class="keypoints">

- The Earth spins once in 24 hours (west to east), giving day and night; it turns 15° an hour. The Sun's apparent motion across the sky is due to this spin.
- A stick's shadow is longest at sunrise and sunset and shortest at noon, pointing west in the morning and east in the evening. This is how a sundial works.
- The Moon reflects sunlight; half of it is always lit. Its phases come from seeing that lit half at different angles as the Moon goes round the Earth in 29.5 days.
- A solar eclipse happens at new Moon (the Moon between the Sun and Earth); a lunar eclipse at full Moon (the Earth between Sun and Moon). The Moon's tilted orbit means eclipses are not monthly.
- The year is about 365.25 days, so every fourth year is a leap year, with century years leaping only if divisible by 400.
- Lunar calendars (12 × 29.5 = 354 days) need extra months to stay in step with the seasons.
- The stars seem to circle the pole star because the Earth spins. The pole star shows north. Saptarishi points to it, and Orion's belt points to Sirius.

</div>
