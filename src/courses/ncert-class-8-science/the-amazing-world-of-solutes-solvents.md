**Science · Class 8 · Chapter 9 · about 45 minutes.** By the end you can say what happens when something dissolves, tell a solute from a solvent, explain saturation and solubility, work out the strength of a solution, and tell a true solution from a suspension and a colloid.

<div class="bigidea">When something dissolves, its particles are pulled apart and spread evenly among the particles of the solvent. Nothing is lost: it is the same stuff, only mixed so finely that you cannot see it.</div>

## 1. What happens when something dissolves?

Drop a spoon of sugar into a glass of water and stir. The sugar seems to vanish, but sip it and it is still there: the water is sweet. The glass is heavier by exactly the mass of the sugar you added. So the sugar did not disappear. It broke into particles too small to see and spread out between the water particles.

- The substance that dissolves (sugar) is the **solute**.
- The substance it dissolves in (water) is the **solvent**.
- Together they make a **solution**, which looks the same all the way through (a *homogeneous* mixture).

Solutions are not only solid in liquid. Soda water is a gas (carbon dioxide) in water. Vinegar is a liquid in a liquid. Brass is a solid (zinc) in a solid (copper). If there is more of one part, that part is usually called the solvent.

Watch it happen, one picture at a time. The water particles keep moving and bumping into the crystal, and they pull particles off its surface:

<div class="step">A crystal of solute sits in water. The water particles are always moving.</div>
<div class="step">Water particles bump into the crystal and tug at the particles on its surface.</div>
<div class="step">Solute particles break away and spread out among the water particles.</div>
<div class="step">The particles are mixed evenly: a solution. The mass is the same as before.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Sugar disappears when it dissolves.”** It does not. The mass of the solution equals the mass of the sugar plus the mass of the water. You could get the sugar back by letting the water evaporate.

</div>

### Making it dissolve faster

Three things speed things up, and each one has a reason you can see in the picture above.

1. **Stir.** Stirring carries dissolved particles away and brings fresh water to the crystal.
2. **Use a powder, not a lump.** Powder has much more surface for the water to attack.
3. **Warm the water.** Hot water particles move faster and bump harder. (This is why sugar dissolves quicker in hot tea than in cold lassi.)

## 2. Saturation and solubility

Keep adding sugar to a glass of water. At first each spoon vanishes. Then a spoon refuses to dissolve and just sits at the bottom, however long you stir. The water is now **saturated**: it cannot take any more at that temperature.

- **Unsaturated solution:** can still dissolve more solute.
- **Saturated solution:** has dissolved all it can at that temperature.

The most solute that dissolves in 100 g of water at a given temperature is called its **solubility**. It is different for each substance. At room temperature, 100 g of water dissolves about 36 g of common salt but about 200 g of sugar.

### Solubility and temperature

For most solids, hotter water dissolves more. A hot saturated solution that cools down must give back the extra solute, which appears as **crystals**. That is how sugar candy and salt crystals are made at home and in salt pans.

Gases behave in the opposite way. **A gas dissolves less in warm water.** Open a warm cold-drink bottle and it fizzes out far quicker than a cold one. Fish in a warm pond can struggle to breathe, because warm water holds less dissolved oxygen.

## 3. Concentration: how strong is the solution?

Two cups of tea can both be sweet, but one with three spoons of sugar is *stronger* than one with one spoon. The **concentration** of a solution tells you how much solute is mixed into a given amount of solution.

- A **dilute** solution has little solute for its amount of solvent.
- A **concentrated** solution has a lot.

A common way to write it is **mass per cent**:

```math
\text{mass per cent} = \frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>mass of solute</dt><dd>grams of the substance that dissolved (sugar, salt...)</dd>
<dt>mass of solution</dt><dd>solute plus solvent together, in grams</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Out of every 100 g of solution, this many grams are solute. A 10% salt solution has 10 g of salt in every 100 g of solution.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

**Worked example.** 20 g of sugar is dissolved in 180 g of water. The solution has mass 20 + 180 = 200 g. So the mass per cent is 20 ÷ 200 × 100 = **10%**.

Think of it as particles per cup. More spoons in the same water means more particles in every cup; more water with the same spoons spreads them thinner.

### Diluting a solution

To **dilute** a solution you add more solvent. The amount of solute does not change, only the volume does. So concentration × volume stays the same before and after. If you add water to double the volume, the concentration halves. Doctors, chemists and cooks all use this: squash concentrate, disinfectants and fertiliser mixtures are all made by dilution.

## 4. Solution, suspension or colloid?

Not every mixture of a solid in a liquid is a true solution. It depends on how big the particles are.

| Mixture | Particle size | Settles on standing? | Scatters a light beam? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Solution** | smaller than 1 nm | no | no | salt in water |
| **Colloid** | 1 nm to 1000 nm | no | yes | milk, fog, ink |
| **Suspension** | bigger than 1000 nm | yes | yes | chalk or mud in water |

(1 nm is a millionth of a millimetre.) A shaft of sunlight through a dusty room, or a torch beam in fog, shows up because the specks scatter light. This is the **Tyndall effect**. A true solution has particles too small to scatter much light, so the beam passes without being seen.

You can separate each kind in a different way: a suspension can be filtered; a colloid passes through filter paper (it needs special methods, such as spinning in a centrifuge); the solute of a true solution can be got back by evaporating the solvent or by crystallisation.

## 5. What dissolves in what?

Water dissolves salt, sugar, washing soda and many gases, so it is often called the **universal solvent**. But it does not dissolve everything. Oil floats on water. Candle wax and grease do not dissolve in water either, yet both dissolve readily in kerosene, petrol or turpentine. The rule of thumb is **like dissolves like**: water-loving substances dissolve in water, and oily substances dissolve in oily solvents.

That is why you need soap and hot water, not plain cold water, to wash an oily plate, and why a painter uses turpentine to clean oil paint from a brush.

<div class="keypoints">

- A solution is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent. Dissolving separates particles; the total mass is unchanged.
- Stirring, powdering the solute and warming the solvent make a solid dissolve faster.
- A saturated solution cannot dissolve more solute at that temperature. Solubility is the most that dissolves in 100 g of water.
- The solubility of most solids rises with temperature; the solubility of gases falls.
- Concentration tells how much solute is in a given amount of solution. Mass per cent = mass of solute ÷ mass of solution × 100.
- Diluting means adding solvent: the solute stays the same, the concentration falls.
- Solution (under 1 nm, no scattering, no settling), colloid (1 to 1000 nm, scatters light, no settling), suspension (over 1000 nm, scatters, settles).
- Like dissolves like: water for salt and sugar; kerosene for oils and wax.

</div>
