**Science · Class 8 · Chapter 12 · about 45 minutes.** By the end you can name the parts of an ecosystem, build a food chain and a food web, explain why energy shrinks along a chain, say what decomposers do, and predict how removing one species affects the others.

<div class="bigidea">An ecosystem is a team. Plants capture the Sun's energy, animals pass it along by eating, and decomposers return the materials to the soil. Pull one player out, and everyone else feels it.</div>

## 1. What is an ecosystem?

A pond, a forest, a paddy field or even a pot of soil is an **ecosystem**: all the living things in a place together with the non-living things around them, all affecting each other.

- **Biotic** (living) parts: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria.
- **Abiotic** (non-living) parts: sunlight, water, air, soil, temperature.

Living things have three roles.

- **Producers** make their own food from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide by photosynthesis: green plants, algae.
- **Consumers** cannot make food, so they eat other organisms. **Herbivores** eat plants, **carnivores** eat other animals and **omnivores** eat both.
- **Decomposers** break down dead plants, animals and waste: fungi, bacteria, earthworms and some insects.

## 2. Food chains

A **food chain** shows who eats whom, with arrows pointing from the **food** to the **eater**, because the arrow shows the direction in which energy goes. For example, in a paddy field:

rice plant → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle

Each step is a **trophic level**. The producer is level 1, a **primary consumer** (a herbivore) is level 2, a **secondary consumer** eats the herbivore (level 3), and so on up to the **top carnivore**.

<div class="myth">

**“The arrow shows who eats whom, pointing at the one that is eaten.”** No. The arrow goes from the food to the eater. In grass → deer, the arrow points to the deer, because the energy goes into the deer.

</div>

## 3. Energy flows along the chain

Energy enters the ecosystem as sunlight. Plants capture a little. When a grasshopper eats a leaf, only about **one tenth** of the plant's energy reaches the grasshopper. Most of it is used by the plant to live and grow, or lost as heat. The same happens at every step. This is the **10 per cent rule**: about 10% of the energy of one trophic level passes to the next.

```math
\text{energy at level } n = \text{energy at level 1} \times 0.1^{\,n-1}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>Level n</dt><dd>1 for producers, 2 for herbivores, 3 for the next consumers, and so on</dd>
<dt>0.1</dt><dd>the one tenth that passes up at each step</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>If plants capture 10 000 units, herbivores get 1000, the next level 100, the next 10. By the fifth step almost nothing is left, so food chains are short and top carnivores are rare.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

## 4. Food webs

In real life, a frog eats more than one kind of insect, and a snake may eat frogs, mice and birds. So food chains cross and join into a **food web**. A web is stronger than a chain: if one food runs short, animals can switch to another.

## 5. Decomposers: nature's recyclers

When a leaf falls or an animal dies, its body still contains nutrients. **Decomposers** such as fungi, bacteria and earthworms break it down into simple substances which go back into the soil, where plants take them up. Without decomposers dead matter would pile up for ever and the soil would run out of nutrients.

Decomposers work faster in warm, damp conditions and slower when it is cold or dry. That is why leaf litter in a monsoon forest vanishes in weeks, but in a cold dry place it stays for years.

### Matter goes round, energy goes through

Nutrients such as nitrogen and carbon are used again and again: soil → plant → herbivore → carnivore → dead matter → decomposers → soil. But energy goes in one direction only: from the Sun, through the chain, to heat that escapes to space. This is why the Sun must keep shining for life to go on, while the same atoms are recycled.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-eco-cycle">
<div class="step">Plants take up nutrients from the soil (and carbon dioxide from the air) to build their bodies.</div>
<div class="step">Herbivores eat the plants, taking in their nutrients.</div>
<div class="step">Carnivores eat herbivores, and the nutrients pass up the chain.</div>
<div class="step">Living things die and leave waste, so the nutrients end up in dead matter.</div>
<div class="step">Decomposers break the dead matter down into simple nutrients.</div>
<div class="step">The nutrients return to the soil, ready for plants to take up again.</div>
</div>

## 6. Balance in nature

In a healthy ecosystem, populations stay roughly in balance. If there are many rabbits, foxes find plenty of food and increase. More foxes eat more rabbits, so the rabbits fall; then the foxes starve and fall, and the rabbits recover. The numbers rise and fall in step around an average.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-eco-balance"><figcaption>Rabbits and foxes over time in a simple teaching model. Change the starting foxes. Then hunt all the foxes on a chosen day and watch the rabbits shoot up towards the limit set by the grass.</figcaption></figure>

If the foxes are all hunted, the rabbits will multiply until they eat the grass bare. Then the rabbits starve too. A **top predator** that looks like a pest is often what keeps the whole ecosystem in balance. This is why tigers matter to a forest and why Project Tiger protects whole habitats, not just tigers.

## 7. People and ecosystems

People change ecosystems more than any other species. Some actions break the balance.

- **Cutting forests** removes food and homes for many species.
- **Spraying pesticides** kills pests but also the frogs and birds that eat them, and poison builds up in the animals at the top of the chain.
- **Overfishing and hunting** remove species faster than they can breed.
- **Pollution and plastic** harm plants, fish and soil life.

<div class="keypoints">

- An ecosystem is all the living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) things in an area interacting.
- Producers make food by photosynthesis; consumers eat others (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores); decomposers break down dead matter.
- A food chain starts with a producer; the arrow goes from food to eater. Many chains join to make a food web, which is more stable.
- Only about 10% of the energy passes from one trophic level to the next, so chains are short and top carnivores are few.
- Decomposers (fungi, bacteria, earthworms) return nutrients to the soil; they work faster in warm, damp conditions.
- Matter is recycled; energy flows one way from the Sun to heat.
- Predator and prey numbers balance each other. Removing a species disturbs the whole web.
- Deforestation, pesticides and overhunting harm ecosystems; planting trees and protecting habitats help.

</div>
