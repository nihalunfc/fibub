**Science · Class 8 · Chapter 7 · about 40 minutes.** By the end you can explain the three states of matter with the picture of moving particles, say why a smell or ink spreads, and describe what happens when matter is heated or cooled.

<div class="bigidea">Everything is made of tiny particles that are always moving and have spaces between them. How close they are, and how fast they move, decide whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas.</div>

## 1. Matter is made of particles

Look at a heap of sand: it is made of grains. Zoom in on a glass of water and, though it looks smooth, it too is made of particles. They are far too small to see even with a normal microscope. We know they exist from simple clues:

- A few crystals of potassium permanganate colour a whole bucket of water. Each crystal must hold a huge number of tiny pieces.
- A small bottle of perfume fills a whole room with its smell.

How small? A water particle is about 0.3 nanometre across. A line one millimetre long could hold about three million of them side by side.

## 2. Spaces and forces between particles

**There are spaces between particles.** Fill a jar with marbles, then pour sand in. The sand slips into the gaps and the level rises very little. In the same way sugar dissolves in water without raising the level much: sugar particles go into the gaps between water particles.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-part-gaps"><figcaption>The jar holds 50 mL of marbles. Pour in the sand: the first 18 mL disappears into the gaps and the level stays put. Only after that does the level rise.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c8s-part-gap"></div>

**Particles attract each other.** The pull is strong when particles are close and weak when they are far apart. That pull is why a stone does not fall into powder and why water forms drops.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-part-forces"><figcaption>Slide the two particles apart. The pull shrinks quickly with distance. The colour bar below tells you which state matches that spacing.</figcaption></figure>

**Particles are always moving.** The more heat energy they have, the faster they move.

<div class="myth">

**“Particles in a solid do not move.”** They do not wander, but they never stop. In a solid they vibrate about fixed positions, and they vibrate harder when the solid is heated.

</div>

## 3. Solids, liquids and gases

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle spacing | very close | close | far apart |
| Pull between particles | strong | medium | very weak |
| Movement | vibrate in place | slide past each other | fly freely |
| Shape | fixed | takes shape of container | fills the container |
| Volume | fixed | fixed | no fixed volume |
| Compressible? | no | hardly | easily |

## 4. Diffusion: particles mixing by themselves

Open a bottle of perfume at the back of the class and soon students at the front smell it. Drop ink into water and, without stirring, the colour slowly spreads. This spreading is called **diffusion**. It happens because particles keep moving at random and bump into each other, and over time they spread from where there are many to where there are few.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-part-diffuse"><figcaption>Release an ink drop. Particles take small random steps and the cloud spreads. Switch to warm water and start again: the particles move faster, so the spreading is quicker.</figcaption></figure>

Diffusion is **fastest in gases**, slower in liquids and **extremely slow in solids**. It is faster at **higher temperature**.

## 5. Changes of state with temperature

Heat ice and watch the particles. At first they vibrate more. At 0 °C, they break free from their fixed places and the ice **melts**. The water warms. At 100 °C bubbles of steam form and the water **boils**. Cool the steam and it **condenses**; cool the water and it **freezes**.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-part-change">
<div class="step">Ice at -5 °C: particles vibrate in a regular pattern.</div>
<div class="step">At 0 °C the pattern breaks up: ice is melting. The temperature stays flat.</div>
<div class="step">Water at 60 °C: particles slide past each other faster.</div>
<div class="step">At 100 °C particles escape as steam bubbles: boiling. The temperature stays flat.</div>
<div class="step">Steam at 110 °C: particles are far apart and move freely.</div>
</div>

Why does the temperature stay flat during melting and boiling? Because the heat is used to pull the particles apart, not to speed them up. This heat is hidden in the change of state and is given back when the steam condenses, which is why steam burns are worse than boiling-water burns.

Some solids, such as **camphor** and **naphthalene**, turn straight into vapour on heating: this is **sublimation**. Evaporation is a similar change that takes place from the surface of a liquid at any temperature, and is why wet clothes dry and sweat cools us.

<div class="keypoints">

- Matter is made of very tiny particles with spaces between them, and they attract each other.
- The particles are always moving; heating makes them move faster.
- Solid: particles close and vibrating; liquid: close and sliding; gas: far apart and flying.
- Diffusion is the mixing of particles by themselves; it is faster in gases and when warm.
- During melting and boiling the temperature stays constant while the heat loosens the particles.

</div>
