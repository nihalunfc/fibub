**Science · Class 8 · Chapter 1 · about 40 minutes.** By the end you can turn a curious question into a fair test, say which thing you change, which you measure and which you keep the same, record and graph your readings, and draw a careful conclusion.

<div class="bigidea">Science is not a pile of facts to memorise. It is a way of finding out: ask a question, make a guess you can test, run a fair test, measure carefully, and let the evidence decide, even when you wanted a different answer.</div>

## 1. Curiosity: where science begins

Why does the milk boil over just when you look away? Why do some pebbles float in a pond and others sink? Every discovery began with someone asking "why?" or "what happens if?". Curiosity is the engine of science.

But a question like "why is the sky pretty?" is hard to test. A good scientific question is one where you can **do something and measure the result**. Compare:

- "Is cold water nicer?" (opinion, no measurement)
- "Does salt dissolve faster in hot water than in cold water?" (you can time it)

Science also grows slowly and in many places. Indian thinkers such as Aryabhata (calculating the Earth's rotation and eclipses), Sushruta (surgery) and, much later, C. V. Raman (how light scatters in liquids, Nobel Prize 1930) all asked careful questions and checked their ideas against what they saw and measured.

<div class="history">

- **About 500 CE:** Aryabhata explains day and night by the Earth turning and gives a reason for eclipses.
- **1928:** C. V. Raman and K. S. Krishnan find that light changes colour a little when it scatters in a liquid (the Raman effect).
- **Today:** Every shared measurement, in any language, can be checked by anyone who repeats the test.

</div>

## 2. The steps of an investigation

Scientists do not always follow a fixed ladder, but good investigations usually pass through these stages. Step through one example with salt and water.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-inv-method">
<div class="step">Ask: a question you can test. "Does salt dissolve faster in hot water than in cold?"</div>
<div class="step">Guess: a hypothesis. "Salt dissolves faster in hot water."</div>
<div class="step">Test: plan a fair test and carry it out. Same salt, same water, same stirring. Change only the temperature.</div>
<div class="step">Record: write down every reading in a table, and repeat the test.</div>
<div class="step">Conclude: what do the data say? Share the result, and say how sure you are.</div>
</div>

A **hypothesis** is a guess that can be tested and shown wrong. "Salt dissolves faster in hot water" is a good hypothesis. "Salt is magical" is not, because no test could ever show it wrong.

It also helps to separate three kinds of statement.

| Kind | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | what you see, hear, feel or measure | The puddle is gone by afternoon. |
| **Inference** | a reason you work out from observations | The Sun heated and dried the water. |
| **Hypothesis** | a testable guess about what will happen | Clothes dry faster on a windy day. |

<div class="myth">

**"A hypothesis is just a fact I already know."** No. A hypothesis is a guess before you have tested it. If the test supports it, it becomes a conclusion you can trust; if not, you change the guess. A wrong hypothesis is not a failure: you still learned something.

</div>

## 3. Variables and the fair test

Any experiment has things that can change. These are called **variables**.

- The **variable you change** on purpose is the *independent variable* (for example, the temperature of the water).
- The **variable you measure** to see the effect is the *dependent variable* (for example, the time the salt takes to dissolve).
- The things you **keep the same** are the *controlled variables* (amount of salt, amount of water, stirring, the glass).

A **fair test** changes only one variable at a time. If you change two things at once, you cannot tell which one caused the result. Imagine you plant one bean in the sun with lots of water and another in a cupboard with little water. The sunny bean grows more: was it the light or the water? You will never know.

Often you also keep a **control**: a set-up where nothing is changed, to compare against.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-inv-fairtest"><figcaption>Pot A is the control. Tap the buttons to make light, water, soil or pot size different in pot B. With exactly one difference the test is fair; with more, you cannot say what caused B's different height.</figcaption></figure>

Fair testing even works on the pendulum. A heavier bob feels as if it should swing slower. Does it? Keep the length and angle the same and change only the mass, then do the reverse.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-inv-pendulum"><figcaption>Change one slider at a time. The mass does nothing to the swing time. The length does: a longer pendulum swings more slowly. The starting angle (for small swings) hardly matters.</figcaption></figure>

For a small swing the time of one swing is `$T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$`, with `$g = 9.8$` m/s². You do not need this formula to do the experiment; the experiment tells you the same story: the time depends on the length and not on the mass.

## 4. Measuring well

A claim in science is only as good as the measurement behind it.

- **Use the right unit.** Length in metres (m) or centimetres (cm), mass in grams (g) or kilograms (kg), time in seconds (s), temperature in °C. 1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm; 1 kg = 1000 g.
- **Know the smallest division** of your instrument (its *least count*). A ruler marked in millimetres cannot give you tenths of a millimetre.
- **Read the scale straight on.** If you look from the side, the pointer seems to sit against a different mark. This is called **parallax error**. Put your eye directly in front of the mark.
- **Start from zero.** Check that the pointer or the ruler's end starts exactly at 0 before you measure.

Practise the straight-on reading:

<!--fig:tool parallax-reading-->

Small errors are normal. A stopwatch, for example, is started and stopped by a human hand, so each reading can be a little early or late. Two ways to deal with that:

1. **Repeat the measurement** several times.
2. **Take the mean** (the average): add the readings and divide by how many there are.

```math
\text{mean} = \frac{\text{sum of all readings}}{\text{number of readings}}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>mean</dt><dd>the typical value of a set of readings</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>the random "too high" and "too low" errors partly cancel when you add the readings, so the mean is usually closer to the true value than a single reading.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

To time a short event, time many events and divide. For a pendulum, time 10 swings and divide by 10; the same hand-error is now shared among ten swings, so each swing's time is far more exact.

## 5. Recording data and drawing graphs

Write readings in a **table** with clear headings and units, one row per test.

| Load (g) | Spring extension (cm) |
|---|---|
| 100 | 1.4 |
| 200 | 3.1 |
| 300 | 4.4 |
| 400 | 6.2 |
| 500 | 7.6 |

A table hides the pattern; a **graph** shows it. Put the variable you changed on the horizontal axis (x) and the variable you measured on the vertical axis (y), label both axes with units, choose even scales and plot a point for each reading. If the points lie close to a straight line, draw the **line of best fit**: a smooth line that passes as near as possible to all the points, with about as many points above as below.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-inv-graph"><figcaption>Spring extension against load. Draw the best-fit line, then add one careless reading: it stands far away from the pattern. Use it to ask "was that reading wrong, or is something new going on?" before you throw it away.</figcaption></figure>

Reading a graph: the **slope** of the line tells you how fast y changes with x. Here the line rises about 1.5 cm for every 100 g, so the spring stretches 1.5 cm per 100 g.

## 6. Conclusions, honesty and sharing

After the test, ask three questions.

1. **What do the data show?** Describe the pattern in plain words ("the hotter the water, the faster the salt dissolved").
2. **Does it support the hypothesis?** If yes, say so; if not, say that too and suggest a better guess.
3. **How sure am I?** Were there few readings? Were there errors? Could another variable have changed? Suggest how to improve the test.

Good scientists:

- report **all** results, even the ones they did not expect;
- never change data to fit the guess;
- **share** their method so others can repeat it (if nobody can repeat a result, it is not trusted);
- give credit to others and change their mind when the evidence changes.

<div class="myth">

**"If an experiment does not work, the scientist failed."** Not at all. A result that disproves a hypothesis is also a discovery: it rules something out. Many great ideas, from penicillin to the shape of the Earth's orbit, grew out of surprising results that someone took seriously.

</div>

Science and technology go together: new instruments (a thermometer, a microscope, a satellite) let scientists measure more, and new measurements lead to new instruments. <div class="keypoints">

- Science starts with curious questions that can be tested by measuring or observing.
- Steps: ask, make a hypothesis, test fairly, record and repeat, conclude and share.
- Observation = what you see or measure; inference = a reason you work out; hypothesis = a testable guess.
- Fair test: change only one variable, measure the effect, and keep every other variable the same. A control is the unchanged set-up.
- Measure with the right unit, read the scale straight on (avoid parallax), repeat readings and take the mean.
- Record readings in a table and plot a graph: changed variable on x, measured variable on y, best-fit line through the points.
- Conclusions must follow the data. Report all results honestly and share your method so others can repeat it.

</div>
