**Science · Class 8 · Chapter 5 · about 40 minutes.** By the end you can name the types of force, say when forces are balanced, explain friction and use `$W = m \times g$` to tell mass from weight.

<div class="bigidea">A force is a push or a pull. Forces change how things move or what shape they have. When forces cancel, nothing changes; when they do not, the object speeds up, slows down or turns.</div>

## 1. What is a force?

Kick a football, pull a door, squeeze a sponge. Each time you apply a **force**: a push or a pull. A force has a **size** (how strong) and a **direction** (which way), and we measure it in **newtons (N)**. One newton is about the pull of Earth on a small apple.

A force can do four things to an object:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c8-force-effects">
<div class="step">Make a still object <b>move</b>, or an object already moving <b>speed up</b>.</div>
<div class="step">Make a moving object <b>slow down</b> or stop.</div>
<div class="step">Change the <b>direction</b> of motion.</div>
<div class="step">Change the <b>shape</b> of the object.</div>
</div>

A **spring balance** measures force. The more you pull, the more the spring stretches, and the stretch grows in the same proportion as the pull. That is why its scale has equal gaps.

## 2. Contact and non-contact forces

**Contact forces** need the two objects to touch.

- **Muscular force**: made by our muscles when we lift, push or pull. Bullocks and camels use it too.
- **Friction**: the rubbing force between surfaces (section 4).

**Non-contact forces** act across a gap.

- **Magnetic force**: a magnet pulls iron from a distance. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel.
- **Electrostatic force**: a rubbed comb or balloon becomes charged and pulls light paper bits.
- **Gravity**: Earth pulls every object toward its centre, which is why a dropped ball falls.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-force-noncontact"><figcaption>Drag the right magnet (or use the slider). The pull or push gets weaker as the gap grows, but nothing ever touches.</figcaption></figure>

Rub a plastic comb on dry hair and bring it near small paper bits. The comb has picked up charge; the force it makes lifts the bits when it beats their weight.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-force-static"><figcaption>Rub the comb more to give it more charge. Raise it too high and the pull becomes too weak to lift the bits.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“A force always needs touching.”** No. Magnets, charges and gravity all act across empty space. Only muscular force and friction need contact.

</div>

## 3. Balanced and unbalanced forces

Several forces can act on one object. Add them up, taking direction into account, and you get the **net force**. When two forces act in a straight line:

- **Opposite directions:** net force = bigger − smaller, towards the bigger.
- **Same direction:** net force = sum.

If the net force is zero the forces are **balanced**: the object stays at rest or keeps moving as before. If not, the forces are **unbalanced** and the motion changes. In a tug of war the rope moves toward the stronger team.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-force-balance"><figcaption>Set the pulls of the two teams. Equal pulls give zero net force and the flag stays put. Make one team stronger and the flag runs toward it.</figcaption></figure>

## 4. Friction

**Friction** is the force that opposes the sliding of one surface over another. It always acts **against** the motion (or against the push that tries to cause motion). It comes from the tiny bumps on every surface, even smooth ones, catching on each other.

What decides how big it is?

- **Roughness:** rougher surfaces give more friction (sandpaper over ice).
- **Pressing harder:** a heavier box is harder to drag.

Friction has three kinds. **Static friction** holds a resting object and grows to match your push, up to a limit. **Sliding friction** acts once it moves and is a bit smaller. **Rolling friction** is the smallest, which is why wheels are so useful.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c8-force-friction"><figcaption>Pick a surface and raise the push. While the push is below the limit the block stays still because friction exactly cancels it. Beyond the limit it slides. Notice how ice has a very small limit.</figcaption></figure>

<!--fig:tool friction-ramp-->

**Friction is useful** for walking, holding a pen, braking a cycle and lighting a matchstick. **It is a nuisance** when it wears out machine parts and wastes energy as heat. We **increase** it with treads on tyres and chalk on hands. We **reduce** it with oil, grease, polish, powder, smooth shapes and **ball bearings** that turn sliding into rolling.

## 5. Mass and weight, and gravity

**Mass** is the amount of matter in an object, in kilograms, and it is the same everywhere. **Weight** is the pull of gravity on that mass. It is a force, in newtons, and it depends on where you are:

```math
W = m \times g
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>m</dt><dd>mass in kg: how much matter</dd>
<dt>g</dt><dd>strength of gravity in newtons per kg (m/s²): 9.8 on Earth, 1.6 on the Moon, 3.7 on Mars, 24.8 on Jupiter</dd>
<dt>W</dt><dd>weight in N: the pull of the planet on you</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Weight and mass are the same.”** In daily life we say “my weight is 40 kg”, but a kilogram is a unit of mass. Weight is a force measured in newtons. A balance in a shop compares masses; a spring balance measures weight.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons. It can change speed, direction or shape.
- Contact forces: muscular, friction. Non-contact forces: gravity, magnetic, electrostatic.
- Balanced forces give zero net force and no change in motion; unbalanced forces change the motion.
- Friction opposes sliding and is larger on rough surfaces; use oil, wheels and ball bearings to reduce it.
- Mass (kg) is the same everywhere; weight W = m × g (N) changes with g.

</div>
