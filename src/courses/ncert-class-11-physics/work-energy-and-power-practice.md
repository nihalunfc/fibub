**Practice · Class 11 Physics · Chapter 5.** Read the chapter lesson, [Work, Energy and Power](/learn/courses/ncert-class-11-physics/work-energy-and-power/), first. Try each question on paper before opening the answer.

<div class="bigidea">Most problems here are solved by one of two routes: the work-energy theorem (net work = change in kinetic energy) or conservation of energy (K + U constant). Pick the route, write the energy at the start and end, and solve.</div>

## How to set out a numerical

1. Write what is given with units (convert cm to m, km/h to m/s, litres to kg).
2. State the law: W = F d cos θ, W = ΔK, K + U = constant, P = W/t, or momentum conservation.
3. Substitute with units and solve; give the answer with its unit and a sensible number of digits.

## 1-mark questions

1. State the SI unit of work and of power.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Joule (J) for work; watt (W = J/s) for power.</details>

2. Can the work done by a force be negative? Give one example.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. When the force is opposite to the displacement (angle above 90°), for example friction on a sliding block.</details>

3. What is the work done by the tension in the string of a pendulum bob?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Zero, because the tension is always at 90° to the displacement of the bob.</details>

4. Write the dimensions of energy.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>[M L² T⁻²].</details>

5. In which type of collision is kinetic energy conserved?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In an elastic collision.</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

6. (2) A body is moved along a circle by a force always directed towards the centre. Why is the work zero?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The force is along the radius and the displacement is along the tangent, so they are at 90° and W = F d cos 90° = 0. The speed stays constant.</details>

7. (2) Explain why a stiff spring stores more energy than a soft one for the same stretch.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>U = ½ k x². For the same x, the energy is proportional to k, and a stiff spring has a larger k.</details>

8. (3) A car of mass 1000 kg moving at 20 m/s brakes to rest over 25 m. Find the average braking force.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Work-energy theorem: F d = ½ m v², so F = m v² / (2d) = 1000 × 400 / 50 = 8000 N (8 kN), directed opposite to the motion.

</details>

9. (3) State the work-energy theorem and use it to show that the stopping distance of a vehicle is proportional to the square of its speed.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The net work done on a body equals the change in its kinetic energy. For a braking force F, the work −F d removes the kinetic energy: F d = ½ m v², so d = m v² / (2F). For the same m and F, d is proportional to v².

</details>

10. (3) A 0.5 kg ball dropped from 20 m hits the ground and rebounds to 12.8 m. How much mechanical energy is lost? Where does it go? (g = 9.8 m/s²)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Lost energy = m g (20 − 12.8) = 0.5 × 9.8 × 7.2 = 35.3 J. It becomes heat and sound in the ball and the ground (an inelastic impact).

</details>

11. (2) Two bodies of equal mass collide head-on elastically. Body A moves at 6 m/s and B is at rest. What are the velocities afterwards?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Equal masses swap velocities in an elastic head-on collision: A stops (0 m/s) and B moves at 6 m/s.</details>

12. (3) Explain the difference between kilowatt and kilowatt-hour. A 2 kW geyser runs 30 minutes. How many units (kWh) does it use?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A kilowatt is a unit of power, a kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy (1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J). Energy = 2 kW × 0.5 h = 1 kWh, i.e. 1 unit on the electricity bill.

</details>

## 5-mark questions

13. (5) Derive the work-energy theorem for a constant force acting along a straight line.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Let a body of mass m, with initial speed u, be acted on by a constant net force F along its motion over a distance d. Newton’s second law gives a = F/m. The equation of motion gives

v² − u² = 2 a d.

Multiply both sides by m/2:

½ m v² − ½ m u² = m a d = F d.

The right side is the work W done by the net force and the left side is the change in kinetic energy, so W = K_f − K_i.

</details>

14. (5) Show that the potential energy stored in a spring stretched by x is ½ k x².

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

To stretch a spring slowly we apply an external force equal to the spring force, F = k x, which increases from 0 to k x. The work for a tiny step dx is F dx = k x dx. Adding over the stretch from 0 to x:

W = ∫₀ˣ k x dx = ½ k x².

This work is stored as potential energy: U = ½ k x². It is also the area of the triangle under the F–x graph, ½ × base × height = ½ × x × k x.

</details>

15. (5) Derive the velocities after a head-on elastic collision of masses m₁ (speed u₁) and m₂ (at rest). Discuss the cases m₁ = m₂, m₁ ≫ m₂ and m₁ ≪ m₂.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Momentum: m₁u₁ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂. Energy: ½m₁u₁² = ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂².

From the first, m₁(u₁ − v₁) = m₂ v₂. From the second, m₁(u₁² − v₁²) = m₂ v₂². Dividing: u₁ + v₁ = v₂. Substituting v₂ in the momentum equation:

v₁ = (m₁ − m₂) u₁ / (m₁ + m₂), v₂ = 2 m₁ u₁ / (m₁ + m₂).

- m₁ = m₂: v₁ = 0, v₂ = u₁ (velocities exchanged).
- m₁ ≫ m₂: v₁ ≈ u₁, v₂ ≈ 2u₁ (the heavy body barely slows; the light one flies off at about double speed).
- m₁ ≪ m₂: v₁ ≈ −u₁, v₂ ≈ 0 (the light body bounces back; the heavy one stays almost still).

</details>

## Case-based question

**A water slide.** A water slide at a park starts 5 m above the pool. A 40 kg girl starts from rest at the top. Take g = 9.8 m/s².

(a) What is her gravitational potential energy at the top relative to the pool surface?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

U = m g h = 40 × 9.8 × 5 = 1960 J.

</details>

(b) Ignoring friction, find her speed at the bottom.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

v = √(2 g h) = √(2 × 9.8 × 5) = 9.9 m/s.

</details>

(c) She actually reaches the bottom at 8 m/s. How much energy is lost, and as what?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Kinetic energy at the bottom = ½ × 40 × 64 = 1280 J. Lost = 1960 − 1280 = 680 J, converted to heat by friction between her and the slide.

</details>

(d) Would a heavier child reach the bottom faster in the frictionless case? Why?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. The mass cancels in ½ m v² = m g h, so the speed depends only on the height drop.</details>

## Assertion and reason

For each, choose: (a) both true and R explains A; (b) both true but R does not explain A; (c) A true, R false; (d) A false, R true.

16. **A:** A person carrying a bucket of water along a level road does no work on the bucket against gravity. **R:** The force of gravity and the displacement of the bucket are perpendicular.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a). The upward force the person applies is at 90° to the forward displacement, so W = 0, which is explained by the perpendicular directions.</details>

17. **A:** In an inelastic collision the total momentum of the colliding bodies is not conserved. **R:** Some kinetic energy is lost as heat.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(d). A is false: momentum is conserved in every collision without an external force. R is true.</details>

18. **A:** The kinetic energy of a body is never negative. **R:** Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a). K = ½ m v² and v² ≥ 0, so K ≥ 0.</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Use W = F d cos θ for constant forces, the F–x area for varying ones.
- Net work = change in kinetic energy; K + U is constant when only gravity and springs do work.
- Stopping distance ∝ v². Spring energy ∝ x².
- Power = work ÷ time = F v; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
- Collisions: momentum always conserved; elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy.

</div>
