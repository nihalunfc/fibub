**Biology · Class 11 · about 34 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name each class of biomolecule with its building block and bond, describe the four levels of protein structure, explain how enzymes speed up reactions, read a velocity curve and solve the small numbers NEET sets on peptides, DNA and enzyme kinetics.

<div class="bigidea">Life is built from a few small building blocks joined into long chains: amino acids into proteins, sugars into polysaccharides, nucleotides into nucleic acids. Each join releases a water molecule. Enzymes, mostly proteins themselves, make every reaction in the cell fast enough to matter.</div>

## 1. What a cell is made of

Grind living tissue in trichloroacetic acid and filter it. The **acid-soluble** filtrate holds small molecules (mass under about 1000 Da): amino acids, sugars, nucleotides. The **acid-insoluble** pellet holds the big molecules (proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides) plus **lipids**, which are small but sit in membranes and so come down with the pellet. Water is the most abundant chemical in a cell (70 to 90% of its mass).

**Primary metabolites** have known jobs in normal life (amino acids, sugars). **Secondary metabolites** are not needed for basic growth but are useful to people: pigments (carotenoids, anthocyanins), alkaloids (morphine, codeine), terpenoids (monoterpenes, diterpenes), essential oils (lemon grass oil), toxins (abrin, ricin), lectins (concanavalin A), drugs (vinblastine, curcumin), polymeric substances (rubber, gums, cellulose).

## 2. The building blocks and their bonds

| Class | Building block | Bond | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proteins | amino acids (21 kinds) | **peptide** (–CO–NH–), between –COOH of one and –NH₂ of the next | heteropolymers |
| Polysaccharides | monosaccharides | **glycosidic** | cellulose, starch, glycogen (glucose); inulin (fructose) |
| Nucleic acids | nucleotides (sugar + base + phosphate) | **phosphodiester** (phosphate links the sugars) | DNA, RNA |
| Lipids (fats) | fatty acids + glycerol | **ester** | not polymers |

**Amino acids** are substituted methanes: a carbon carrying a hydrogen, an amino group, a carboxyl group and an **R group**. R decides the amino acid: H in glycine, CH₃ in alanine, CH₂OH in serine. They can be acidic (glutamic acid), basic (lysine), neutral (valine) or aromatic (tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan). In solution an amino acid carries both charges at once (a **zwitterion**). **Essential** amino acids must come from food; non-essential ones the body makes.

**Lipids.** A fatty acid is a carboxyl group on an R chain: palmitic acid has 16 carbons (including the carboxyl carbon), arachidonic acid 20. Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds; unsaturated ones have one or more. Glycerol with one, two or three fatty acids gives mono-, di- and triglycerides; oils have a lower melting point than fats. Phospholipids (for example lecithin) carry a phosphate group and build membranes.

**Nucleotides.** Bases are **purines** (adenine, guanine: two rings) or **pyrimidines** (cytosine, uracil, thymine: one ring). Base + sugar = **nucleoside** (adenosine); add phosphate = **nucleotide** (adenylic acid).

**Polysaccharides.** In a chain, the right end is the reducing end and the left end the non-reducing end. **Starch** coils into helices that hold iodine (blue-black colour); **cellulose** cannot hold iodine. Plant walls are cellulose (cotton is nearly pure cellulose, paper is made from it). **Glycogen** is the animal store. **Chitin**, in arthropod exoskeletons and fungal walls, is a complex polysaccharide built from amino sugars.

<div class="myth">

**"Lipids are macromolecules because they are in the acid-insoluble pellet."** Lipids are small (under 800 Da). They end up in the pellet only because, when the tissue is ground, membranes break into water-insoluble vesicles.

</div>

## 3. Proteins and nucleic acids

**Protein jobs:** collagen (intercellular ground substance; the most abundant protein in animals), **RuBisCO** (CO₂ fixation; the most abundant in the biosphere), trypsin (enzyme), insulin (hormone), antibodies (fight infection), receptors (sense smell, taste, hormones), GLUT-4 (lets glucose into cells).

<div class="step"><b>Primary structure.</b> The order (sequence) of amino acids. The first is the <b>N-terminal</b> amino acid (free –NH₂), written on the left; the last is the <b>C-terminal</b> (free –COOH).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Secondary structure.</b> Parts of the chain fold into regular shapes held by hydrogen bonds; NCERT shows the right-handed <b>α-helix</b>. Other parts form other shapes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Tertiary structure.</b> The whole chain folds on itself into a 3-D shape, like a ball of wool. This shape is needed for the protein to work.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Quaternary structure.</b> Two or more chains fit together. Adult haemoglobin has four: two α and two β chains.</div>
</div>

**DNA** (Watson and Crick, B-form): two antiparallel strands in a right-handed double helix, with the sugar-phosphate backbone outside and bases inside. **A pairs with T by two hydrogen bonds; G pairs with C by three.** One turn holds about 10 base pairs, with 3.4 Å (0.34 nm) between pairs, so a turn is 34 Å. RNA is usually single-stranded with ribose and uracil.

## 4. Enzymes

Almost all enzymes are proteins; some nucleic acids act as enzymes too (**ribozymes**). An enzyme's folded shape makes a pocket, the **active site**, where the substrate fits. Inorganic catalysts often need high temperature and pressure; most enzymes are damaged (**denatured**) above about 40 °C, but enzymes from thermophilic organisms in hot springs stay stable up to 80 to 90 °C.

**How they work.** A reaction must climb an energy hill, the **activation energy**, through an unstable **transition state**. The enzyme binds substrate to form an **enzyme–substrate complex** (ES), which lowers the hill; the products leave and the enzyme is free again. Carbonic anhydrase speeds up CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ about ten million times (around 600,000 molecules a second).

**What changes the rate.**
- **Temperature and pH:** each enzyme has an **optimum**; activity falls on either side. Low temperature only slows the enzyme (it recovers); high temperature denatures it.
- **Substrate concentration:** rate rises, then levels off at **V<sub>max</sub>** when every active site is busy. **K<sub>m</sub>** (Michaelis constant) is the substrate concentration at half V<sub>max</sub>; a low K<sub>m</sub> means high affinity. v = V<sub>max</sub>[S] / (K<sub>m</sub> + [S]).
- **Inhibitors:** a **competitive inhibitor** looks like the substrate and blocks the active site (malonate on succinate dehydrogenase; used in drugs against bacteria). A **non-competitive** one binds elsewhere.

**Six classes:** (1) oxidoreductases (dehydrogenases): oxidation-reduction between two substrates; (2) transferases: move a group other than hydrogen; (3) hydrolases: break bonds by adding water (ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic); (4) lyases: remove groups without hydrolysis, often leaving a double bond; (5) isomerases: rearrange within a molecule; (6) ligases: join two compounds (C–O, C–S, C–N, P–O bonds).

**Cofactors.** The protein part alone is the **apoenzyme**; with its cofactor it works. **Prosthetic groups** bind tightly (haem in peroxidase and catalase). **Coenzymes** bind only during the reaction; many contain vitamins (NAD and NADP contain niacin). **Metal ions** form bonds with the active site and substrate (zinc in carboxypeptidase). Remove the cofactor and activity is lost.

<div class="keypoints">

- Acid-soluble pool: small molecules; acid-insoluble: proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, plus lipids (small, but in membrane vesicles).
- Bonds: peptide (proteins), glycosidic (sugars), phosphodiester (nucleic acids), ester (fats).
- Purines A and G; pyrimidines C, U, T; nucleoside = base + sugar; nucleotide adds phosphate.
- Starch holds iodine, cellulose does not; chitin has amino sugars; inulin is a fructose polymer.
- Collagen most abundant in animals; RuBisCO in the biosphere; haemoglobin has 2 α + 2 β chains.
- A–T two hydrogen bonds, G–C three; n amino acids in a chain make n − 1 peptide bonds.
- Enzymes lower activation energy; competitive inhibitor raises Km, non-competitive lowers Vmax.
- Six classes: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases; cofactors: prosthetic groups, coenzymes, metal ions.

</div>
