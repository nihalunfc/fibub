**Biology · Class 11 · about 28 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name every phase and stage, say what changes in each, track the DNA content and chromosome number through both kinds of division, and explain why each kind matters.

<div class="bigidea">A cell grows, copies its DNA and then splits. Mitosis makes two identical cells (growth and repair); meiosis makes four cells with half the chromosomes, each different (sexual reproduction).</div>

## 1. The phases of the cell cycle

A typical human cell in culture divides about once every 24 hours. Of that, the division itself (M phase) takes about an hour; the rest is **interphase**, the busy preparation time (more than 95% of the cycle).

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| G1 (gap 1) | The cell grows and works normally; it gets ready to copy its DNA |
| S (synthesis) | DNA replicates (2C → 4C); in animal cells the centriole duplicates in the cytoplasm |
| G2 (gap 2) | Proteins are made for division; the cell keeps growing |
| M (mitosis) | The nucleus divides (karyokinesis), then the cytoplasm (cytokinesis) |
| G0 (quiescent) | Cells that stop dividing leave G1 and stay active without dividing (for example, many heart and nerve cells) |

<div class="myth">

**"Interphase is the resting phase."** Interphase is when the cell grows and copies all its DNA. It only looks quiet under the microscope because the chromosomes are not condensed. The true non-dividing state is G0.

</div>

## 2. DNA content and chromosome number

The trick in most numerical NEET questions is to separate **DNA content** (C) from **chromosome number** (counted by centromeres). S doubles the DNA but not the chromosome number; anaphase of mitosis (and anaphase II) doubles the chromosome number for a moment by splitting centromeres.

## 3. Mitosis, stage by stage

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Prophase.</b> Chromatin condenses into chromosomes, each with two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The centrosomes move to opposite poles and the spindle begins to form. By the end, the nucleolus, Golgi and ER are no longer seen and the nuclear envelope breaks down.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Metaphase.</b> Chromosomes are most condensed: the best stage to count them and study their shape. Spindle fibres attach to the kinetochores, and the chromosomes line up at the metaphase plate.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Anaphase.</b> Centromeres split; sister chromatids (now daughter chromosomes) move to opposite poles, centromere first, arms trailing.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Telophase.</b> Chromosomes reach the poles and decondense. The nuclear envelope, nucleolus, Golgi and ER form again: two nuclei.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Cytokinesis.</b> Animal cells: a furrow in the membrane deepens until the cell pinches in two. Plant cells (rigid wall): a cell plate forms from the centre outwards and becomes the middle lamella. If cytokinesis does not follow karyokinesis, a multinucleate cell (syncytium) forms, as in the liquid endosperm of coconut.</div>
</div>

**Why mitosis matters:** growth of multicellular organisms, repair (for example, the lining of the gut and the outer skin), and keeping the chromosome number constant; in plants it continues throughout life in the apical and lateral meristems.

## 4. Meiosis: two divisions, one DNA copy

Meiosis has **one** round of DNA replication followed by **two** divisions, so a diploid cell gives four haploid cells.

<div class="steps" data-ms="7000">
<div class="step"><b>Leptotene.</b> Chromosomes become visible as long thin threads.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Zygotene.</b> Homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis), held together by the synaptonemal complex. Each pair is a bivalent (a tetrad of four chromatids).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Pachytene.</b> Recombination nodules appear; crossing over swaps segments between non-sister chromatids of homologues, using the enzyme recombinase.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Diplotene.</b> The synaptonemal complex dissolves and homologues begin to separate, except at the X-shaped chiasmata (where crossing over happened). In the oocytes of some vertebrates this stage can last months or years.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Diakinesis.</b> Chiasmata move to the ends (terminalisation), chromosomes fully condense, the spindle forms, the nucleolus disappears and the nuclear envelope breaks down.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Metaphase I to telophase I.</b> Bivalents line up at the plate; in anaphase I homologous chromosomes separate (sister chromatids stay joined). The two daughter cells (a dyad) are haploid in chromosome number. A short interkinesis follows, with no DNA replication.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Meiosis II.</b> Like mitosis: centromeres split in anaphase II and sister chromatids separate. Result: four haploid cells (a tetrad of cells).</div>
</div>

**Why meiosis matters:** it halves the chromosome number so that fertilisation restores it, keeping the number constant from one generation to the next, and crossing over plus the independent arrangement of chromosomes create variation, the raw material of evolution.

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>2n / n</dt><dd>diploid and haploid chromosome number (counted by centromeres)</dd>
<dt>C</dt><dd>DNA content: 2C in G1, 4C after S, 1C in a gamete</dd>
<dt>Bivalent</dt><dd>a pair of homologous chromosomes in meiosis I = 4 chromatids</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Mitosis copies a cell; meiosis shuffles and halves it. The DNA doubling in S is shared by both divisions of meiosis, which is why four cells end up with half the DNA each.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Cycle: G1 → S (DNA 2C → 4C, centriole duplicates) → G2 → M; G0 is the non-dividing exit from G1.
- Mitosis: prophase (condense, envelope breaks), metaphase (plate, best to count), anaphase (centromeres split), telophase (nuclei re-form), then cytokinesis (furrow in animals, cell plate in plants).
- Prophase I: leptotene, zygotene (synapsis, synaptonemal complex), pachytene (crossing over, recombination nodules), diplotene (chiasmata), diakinesis (terminalisation).
- Anaphase I separates homologues (reduction); anaphase II separates sister chromatids.
- One DNA replication, two divisions: one 2n cell → four n cells.
- Mitosis: growth and repair, same chromosome number. Meiosis: halves the number and creates variation.

</div>
