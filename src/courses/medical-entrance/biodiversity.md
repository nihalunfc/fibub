**Biology · Class 12 · about 30 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the three levels of biodiversity, quote the key numbers for the world and India, explain why the tropics are richer, use the species-area relationship, name the four main causes of loss with examples, and sort conservation methods into in situ and ex situ.

<div class="bigidea">Biodiversity is the variety of life at every level: genes, species and ecosystems. It is richest near the equator and in large areas, it keeps ecosystems stable and productive, and humans are now destroying it far faster than nature ever did. Protecting habitats is the best way to keep it.</div>

## 1. Levels of biodiversity

The term was popularised by the sociobiologist **Edward Wilson**.

- **Genetic diversity**: variation within one species. The medicinal plant ***Rauwolfia vomitoria*** of the Himalayas varies in the strength and amount of the drug **reserpine** it makes. India has more than **50,000 genetically different strains of rice** and about **1000 varieties of mango**.
- **Species diversity**: the number of species in an area. The **Western Ghats** have more amphibian species than the Eastern Ghats.
- **Ecological diversity**: variety of ecosystems. India, with deserts, rainforests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows, has more ecosystem diversity than a Scandinavian country such as Norway.

## 2. How many species?

According to the IUCN (2004), a little more than **1.5 million** species have been described. Robert May's estimate of the global total is about **7 million**, since many organisms, especially in the tropics and among insects and microbes, are still undiscovered. More than **70%** of recorded species are animals; plants (algae, fungi, bryophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms) are no more than **22%**. Among animals, **insects** are the richest group, about **70%** of all animals. Prokaryotes are not counted well, because conventional methods do not identify microbial species reliably and many cannot be cultured.

India has only **2.4%** of the world's land area but about **8.1%** of its species, making it one of the **12 mega diversity countries**. About 45,000 plant and twice as many animal species are recorded. Using May's ratio, India may still have more than 1,00,000 plant and 3,00,000 animal species to discover.

## 3. Patterns of biodiversity

**Latitudinal gradient**: diversity falls from the equator towards the poles. Colombia, near the equator, has nearly **1400** bird species, New York (41° N) **105**, Greenland (71° N) **56**; India, mostly in the tropics, has more than **1200**. The **Amazon rainforest** is the richest place on Earth: more than 40,000 plant species, 3000 fishes, 1300 birds, 427 mammals, 427 amphibians, 378 reptiles and over 1,25,000 invertebrates.

Why the tropics are richer: (1) they have stayed relatively undisturbed for millions of years, giving a long time for speciation, while temperate regions went through repeated glaciations; (2) the environment is less seasonal and more predictable, which favours niche specialisation; (3) more solar energy means higher productivity, which can support more species.

**Species-area relationship**: **Alexander von Humboldt** observed that within a region, species richness rises with the area explored, but only up to a limit. On ordinary axes the curve is a rectangular hyperbola; on log-log axes it is a straight line:

**log S = log C + Z log A**

where S is species richness, A is area, Z is the slope (regression coefficient) and C is the y-intercept. Z lies between **0.1 and 0.2** for most regions, regardless of the taxon. For very large areas, such as whole continents, the line is much steeper, with Z between **0.6 and 1.2**; for frugivorous birds and mammals in tropical forests of different continents, Z = 1.15.

## 4. Why biodiversity matters

Communities with more species tend to be more stable: less variation in productivity from year to year, more resistance to disturbances such as alien species, and more resilience. **David Tilman**'s long-term outdoor plot experiments showed that plots with more species had **less year-to-year variation in total biomass**, and more diversity also gave **higher productivity**.

**Paul Ehrlich's rivet popper hypothesis** compares an ecosystem to an aeroplane held together by thousands of rivets (species). Passengers pulling out a rivet each (species going extinct) may not harm the plane at first, but as more are pulled out the plane becomes dangerously weak. And losing a rivet on the wings (a key species that drives a major function) is far more serious than losing one from the seats.

## 5. Loss of biodiversity

The IUCN Red List (2004) documents **784 extinctions** in the last 500 years: 338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates and 87 plants. Recent examples: the **dodo** (Mauritius), the **quagga** (Africa), the **thylacine** (Australia), **Steller's sea cow** (Russia), and three subspecies of tiger (Bali, Javan, Caspian). Twenty-seven species vanished in the last twenty years alone. More than **15,500** species face extinction: 12% of birds, 23% of mammals, **32% of amphibians** and 31% of gymnosperms. The current rate is estimated at **100 to 1000 times** faster than before humans, and we may be in a **sixth mass extinction**, unlike the earlier five because humans cause it.

Effects of loss: lower plant production, lower resistance to drought and other disturbances, and more variable ecosystem processes such as plant productivity, water use and pest and disease cycles.

**The evil quartet** (four main causes):

1. **Habitat loss and fragmentation**, the most important. Tropical rainforests once covered about **14%** of the land surface and now cover no more than **6%**. The Amazon, called the "lungs of the planet", is being cut for soya beans and for grassland for beef cattle. Fragmenting a habitat hurts animals that need large territories, and migratory animals.
2. **Over-exploitation**: Steller's sea cow and the passenger pigeon were hunted to extinction; many marine fish are over-harvested.
3. **Alien species invasions**: **Nile perch** in Lake Victoria wiped out over **200 cichlid species**; *Parthenium* (carrot grass), *Lantana* and water hyacinth (*Eichhornia*) damage native species; the African catfish *Clarias gariepinus*, brought in for aquaculture, threatens India's native catfishes.
4. **Co-extinctions**: when a species dies out, the plants and animals tied to it in an obligatory way also vanish. If a host fish dies out, its unique parasites go too; a plant and its only pollinator depend on each other.

<div class="myth">

**"Extinction is mostly caused by hunting."** Hunting matters, but habitat loss and fragmentation is the biggest single cause today.

</div>

## 6. Conservation

Three reasons: **narrowly utilitarian** (direct goods: food, fibre, firewood, timber, medicines; more than 25% of drugs sold worldwide come from plants, and 25,000 plant species are used in traditional medicine; "bioprospecting" looks for useful molecules), **broadly utilitarian** (ecosystem services: the Amazon produces an estimated 20% of Earth's oxygen through photosynthesis; pollination, flood and erosion control, aesthetic pleasure), and **ethical** (every species has a right to exist; we hold the planet in trust for the future).

**In situ conservation** (in the natural home), the best way to protect a whole ecosystem:

- **Biodiversity hotspots** (Norman Myers): regions with very high species richness and high endemism that are also under threat. There are now **34**; three cover India: **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka**, **Indo-Burma**, and the **Himalaya**. Together the hotspots cover less than 2% of the Earth's land but hold a very large share of its species, and strict protection could reduce ongoing extinctions by up to 30%.
- **Protected areas**: India has **14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks and 448 wildlife sanctuaries** (NCERT figures).
- **Sacred groves**: forest patches protected by communities for religious reasons, for example in the **Khasi and Jaintia Hills** (Meghalaya), the **Aravalli Hills** (Rajasthan), the **Western Ghats** (Karnataka and Maharashtra) and the Sarguja, Chanda and Bastar areas (Madhya Pradesh). In Meghalaya they are the last refuges of many rare and threatened plants.

**Ex situ conservation** (outside the natural home): **zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks**, and modern methods: **cryopreservation** of gametes (kept alive and fertile for long periods), in vitro fertilisation, plant propagation by **tissue culture**, and **seed banks** for commercially important plants.

**International efforts**: the **Earth Summit** in Rio de Janeiro (**1992**) called on all nations to conserve biodiversity and use it sustainably (Convention on Biological Diversity). The **World Summit on Sustainable Development** in Johannesburg (**2002**): 190 countries pledged to significantly reduce the rate of biodiversity loss by 2010.
