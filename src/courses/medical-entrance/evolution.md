**Biology · Class 12 · about 32 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how life may have begun, sort evidence into homology and analogy, apply natural selection to real examples, solve every Hardy-Weinberg question, name what changes allele frequencies, and order the steps of human evolution.

<div class="bigidea">Evolution is change in the allele frequencies of a population over generations. Variation arises by mutation and recombination; natural selection keeps what helps organisms leave more offspring; chance, migration and time do the rest. The Hardy-Weinberg principle is the "no evolution" baseline that lets us measure it.</div>

## 1. Origin of life

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old (from Planck satellite measurements; older textbooks give about 20 billion, so an exam may still use that figure); the Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. Its early atmosphere had water vapour, methane, carbon dioxide and ammonia but no free oxygen. Life appeared about 4 billion years ago.

**Oparin and Haldane** proposed **chemical evolution**: simple inorganic molecules, powered by lightning, UV and heat, formed organic molecules, which later organised into the first cells. **S. L. Miller (1953)** tested it: a closed flask with **CH₄, H₂, NH₃ and water vapour** at 800 °C and electric discharges produced **amino acids**. Others later made sugars, nitrogen bases, pigments and fats. Meteorite analysis found similar compounds, so the idea is plausible. The first non-cellular forms may have appeared about 3 billion years ago, as giant molecules (RNA, proteins); the first cells probably in water.

The older idea of **spontaneous generation** (life from rotting matter) was disproved by **Louis Pasteur**: pre-sterilised flasks stayed lifeless, but flasks open to air grew microbes.

## 2. Evidence for evolution

- **Fossils**: rocks form in layers, and each layer holds the life of its time. Deeper layers have simpler, extinct forms.
- **Homologous organs** (same origin, different job: **divergent evolution**): the forelimbs of whale, bat, cheetah and human share the same bones (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges). Also vertebrate hearts and brains, and in plants the thorn of *Bougainvillea* and the tendril of *Cucurbita*.
- **Analogous organs** (different origin, same job: **convergent evolution**): wings of butterfly and bird, eyes of octopus and mammal, flippers of penguin and dolphin, sweet potato (root) and potato (stem).
- **Biochemical similarity**: proteins and genes of related organisms are alike.
- **Natural selection seen in action**: in England, collections of the moth *Biston betularia* from before 1850 had more white-winged moths; after industrialisation, soot darkened tree trunks and lichens died, and dark (melanised) moths became common. Birds picked off whichever form showed up against the bark. Herbicide- and pesticide-resistant pests and antibiotic-resistant microbes show the same thing within years.

<div class="myth">

**"The moths turned dark because of the soot."** No moth changed. Dark moths were already present; they survived better on dark trunks and left more offspring, so their share of the population rose. Selection acts on existing variation.

</div>

## 3. Adaptive radiation and the theories

**Adaptive radiation**: one ancestor spreads into many habitats and gives rise to many forms. **Darwin's finches** of the Galápagos evolved from seed-eaters into insect-eaters and others with different beaks. **Australian marsupials** radiated from one ancestor. When two separate radiations produce look-alikes in similar habitats (placental wolf and Tasmanian wolf, flying squirrel and flying phalanger), it is **convergent evolution**.

**Darwin** (voyage of HMS Beagle; with Alfred Wallace, who reached the same idea in the Malay Archipelago) proposed: organisms vary; populations make more offspring than resources support; the better fitted leave more offspring (**fitness = reproductive fitness**); over generations new types arise. The two key ideas are **branching descent** and **natural selection**. **Lamarck** had earlier suggested use and disuse of organs (the giraffe stretching its neck), which is not accepted. **Hugo de Vries**, working on evening primrose (*Oenothera lamarckiana*), proposed that large sudden changes, **mutations**, cause evolution in jumps (**saltation**), whereas Darwin's variations are small and directional.

## 4. The Hardy-Weinberg principle

In a large population with random mating and no evolutionary forces, allele and genotype frequencies stay constant from generation to generation. For one gene with alleles A (frequency p) and a (frequency q):

**p + q = 1** and **p² + 2pq + q² = 1** (AA, Aa, aa).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="hardy-weinberg"><figcaption>Genotype frequencies at equilibrium for every allele frequency. Slide q down: affected people (q²) shrink much faster than carriers (2pq).</figcaption></figure>

Five factors disturb it, and each is a way evolution happens: **gene migration (gene flow)**, **genetic drift** (chance changes, strong in small populations; when a few individuals found a new population, the **founder effect**), **mutation**, **genetic recombination** and **natural selection**.

Natural selection takes three forms, picturing a trait such as body size: **stabilising** (the average is favoured, the peak gets taller and narrower), **directional** (one extreme is favoured, the peak shifts) and **disruptive** (both extremes are favoured, two peaks form).

## 5. A brief history of life

About 2000 million years ago (mya) the first cellular life; about 500 mya invertebrates; about 350 mya jawless fish. Lobe-finned fish led to the first amphibians that lived on land and in water; a lobefin, the **coelacanth**, once thought extinct, was caught off South Africa in 1938. Amphibians gave rise to reptiles with thick-shelled eggs that do not dry out. Reptiles dominated for a long time; dinosaurs vanished about 65 mya. In plants, sea weeds and simple plants came first, then land plants; giant ferns (pteridophytes) fell into coal deposits; gymnosperms, then angiosperms followed. From early reptiles came the mammals, which spread after the dinosaurs went. Pouched mammals of Australia survived because there was little competition there.

## 6. Human evolution

| Ancestor | When | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Dryopithecus* and *Ramapithecus* | about 15 mya | hairy, walked like gorillas and chimpanzees; *Ramapithecus* more human-like |
| Australopithecines | about 2 mya, East African grasslands | hunted with stone weapons, ate fruit |
| *Homo habilis* | | first human-like being; brain 650 to 800 cc; probably did not eat meat |
| *Homo erectus* | about 1.5 mya (fossils from Java, 1891) | brain about 900 cc; probably ate meat |
| Neanderthal man | 1,00,000 to 40,000 years ago, Near East and Central Asia | brain about 1400 cc; used hides, buried their dead |
| *Homo sapiens* | arose in Africa, spread across continents | prehistoric cave art about 18,000 years ago; agriculture about 10,000 years ago |
