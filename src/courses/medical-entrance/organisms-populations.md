**Biology · Class 12 · about 32 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how organisms respond to their surroundings, calculate population rates and growth, read age pyramids, use the exponential and logistic equations, and classify every interaction between species with its standard example.

<div class="bigidea">A population is all the individuals of one species in one area. It has properties no single organism has: a birth rate, a death rate, an age structure and a growth curve. Growth is unlimited only until resources run short; then the population levels off at what the place can support. Other species help or hinder it all along.</div>

## 1. Organisms and their environment

Ecology studies the interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical surroundings, at four levels: **organism, population, community, biome**. The key **abiotic factors** are **temperature**, **water**, **light** and **soil**. Organisms that tolerate a wide range of temperature are **eurythermal** (most); a narrow range, **stenothermal**. For salt: **euryhaline** and **stenohaline**.

Faced with stress, organisms can:

- **Regulate**: keep the internal state constant (homeostasis), as birds and mammals keep body temperature constant. That costs energy, which is why very small mammals (like shrews) are rare in polar regions: a small body has a large surface area for its volume and loses heat fast.
- **Conform**: let the internal state follow the outside. About 99% of animals and nearly all plants are conformers.
- **Migrate**: move away for a while. Every winter, birds from Siberia and other cold northern regions migrate to **Keoladeo National Park (Bharatpur)** in Rajasthan.
- **Suspend**: bacteria, fungi and lower plants form thick-walled spores; seeds become dormant; bears **hibernate** in winter; some snails and fish **aestivate** in summer; many zooplankton enter **diapause**.

**Adaptations** are features that help an organism survive and reproduce. The **kangaroo rat** of North American deserts meets its water needs from the oxidation of fat (metabolic water) and makes very concentrated urine. Many desert plants have a thick cuticle, sunken stomata and **CAM** photosynthesis (stomata open at night); *Opuntia* has spines for leaves and does photosynthesis in flat stems. Mammals of cold regions have shorter ears and limbs to reduce heat loss (**Allen's rule**); seals have thick fat (**blubber**). People who move quickly to high altitudes (above 3500 m, such as Rohtang Pass) get **altitude sickness**; the body adjusts by making more red blood cells, lowering the binding affinity of haemoglobin and breathing faster. Desert lizards bask in the sun when cool and move to shade when hot (behavioural adaptation).

## 2. Population attributes

- **Density** (N): the number per unit area. Sometimes another measure fits better: percent cover or biomass (one huge banyan tree vs 200 carrot grass plants), or indirect signs like pug marks and faecal pellets for tigers.
- **Birth rate** and **death rate**: expressed **per capita** (per individual). If 20 lotus plants make 8 new plants in a year, the birth rate is 8/20 = 0.4 per lotus per year.
- **Sex ratio**: for example, 60% females and 40% males.
- **Age pyramid**: pre-reproductive, reproductive and post-reproductive individuals as stacked bars. **Triangular** (broad base) = expanding; **bell-shaped** = stable; **urn-shaped** (narrow base) = declining.

## 3. Population growth

Four processes change N: **natality** (B, births) and **immigration** (I) add; **mortality** (D, deaths) and **emigration** (E) remove.

**N<sub>t+1</sub> = N<sub>t</sub> + [(B + I) − (D + E)]**

**Exponential growth.** With unlimited resources, each species grows at its full potential, its **intrinsic rate of natural increase (r)**. dN/dt = rN, which integrates to **N<sub>t</sub> = N<sub>0</sub> e<sup>rt</sup>**. The graph is **J-shaped**. Values of r (NCERT examples): Norway rat 0.015, flour beetle 0.12; India's population in 1981 had r = 0.0205.

**Logistic growth.** Resources are limited, so a habitat can support only a maximum number, its **carrying capacity (K)**. Growth slows as N nears K: **dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K**. The curve is **S-shaped (sigmoid)**: a lag phase, then acceleration, deceleration and an **asymptote** at K. This is the **Verhulst-Pearl logistic growth**, and it is more realistic.

<div class="myth">

**"A population at carrying capacity has no births."** Births and deaths go on; they simply balance, so N stays near K.

**Life history variation.** Populations evolve to maximise their reproductive fitness, which is reflected in r. Some breed **only once** in their lifetime (Pacific salmon, bamboo); others **many times** (most birds and mammals). Some make **many small offspring** (oysters, pelagic fishes); others **few large ones** (birds, mammals).

## 4. Population interactions

| Interaction | Species A | Species B | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mutualism | + | + | lichens (fungus + alga or cyanobacterium), mycorrhizae (fungus + plant roots), fig and its wasp, plant-pollinator partnerships |
| Competition | − | − | flamingoes and fishes for zooplankton in South American lakes; goats and Abingdon tortoise |
| Predation | + | − | tiger and deer; also herbivores eating plants |
| Parasitism | + | − | cuckoo in a crow's nest, liver fluke, *Cuscuta*, lice and ticks |
| Commensalism | + | 0 | orchid on a mango branch, barnacles on a whale, cattle egret and cattle, clown fish and sea anemone |
| Amensalism | − | 0 | one species harmed, the other unaffected |

**Predation** transfers energy up trophic levels, keeps prey populations in check and helps maintain species diversity by reducing competition among prey. When the prickly pear cactus was brought into Australia in the early 1920s it spread over millions of hectares, and it was controlled only after a cactus-eating moth (a predator) from its native range was introduced. When the starfish *Pisaster* was removed from an American Pacific coast community, more than 10 invertebrate species became extinct within a year because of increased competition. Prey defend themselves: camouflage (frogs, insects), poison (the **Monarch butterfly** is distasteful because of a chemical it takes up from a weed as a caterpillar), thorns (*Acacia*, cactus), and chemicals such as the **cardiac glycosides of *Calotropis***. Nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine and opium are plant defences too.

**Competition** happens when closely related or unrelated species share a limited resource; one species can also reduce another's feeding efficiency (interference). **Gause's competitive exclusion principle**: two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot coexist indefinitely; the weaker one is eliminated. On the Scottish rocky coast, the larger barnacle *Balanus* excludes the smaller *Chthamalus* from the lower intertidal zone (Connell). Species avoid competition by **resource partitioning**: MacArthur showed that five closely related warbler species on the same tree coexist because they forage in different parts of it.

**Parasitism**: the parasite lives on or in a host and depends on it for food and shelter. Many parasites are host-specific and have adaptations: loss of unnecessary sense organs, adhesive organs or suckers, loss of digestive system, high reproductive capacity. The **human liver fluke** needs two intermediate hosts (a snail and a fish). **Ectoparasites** feed from outside (lice on humans, ticks on dogs, copepods on marine fish, *Cuscuta*, which has no chlorophyll and no leaves, on hedge plants). **Endoparasites** live inside (tapeworm, liver fluke, *Plasmodium*). **Brood parasitism**: the cuckoo lays eggs in the crow's nest, and its eggs resemble the host's in size and colour.

**Mutualism** also includes sexual deceit: the Mediterranean orchid ***Ophrys*** has a petal that resembles a female bee, so male bees "pseudocopulate" with it and carry pollen. Fig species each have their own wasp pollinator, which lays eggs in the fig fruit, and the developing seeds feed the larvae.
