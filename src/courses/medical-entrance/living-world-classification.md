**Biology · Class 11 · about 32 minutes.** By the end you will be able to say what makes something alive, write and rank scientific names correctly, name the taxonomical aids, sort any organism into Whittaker's five kingdoms and explain where lichens, viruses, viroids and prions fit.

<div class="bigidea">Biologists sort millions of living things by putting similar ones in the same box, then putting similar boxes in bigger boxes. Each box is a taxon, each level is a rank, and a two-word name gives every species one label worldwide.</div>

## 1. What makes something alive

- **Growth** is an increase in mass and cell number. Plants grow all their life; animals grow up to an age. It is *not* a defining feature, because a mountain or a crystal can grow by adding material from outside. Living things grow from inside.
- **Reproduction** makes more of the same kind. It is also *not* defining: mules, sterile worker bees and infertile couples do not reproduce, yet they are alive.
- **Metabolism**, the sum of all chemical reactions in the body, is defining. No non-living object has it (reactions in a test tube are not metabolism of a living thing).
- **Cellular organisation** is defining. Every living thing is one or more cells.
- **Consciousness**, the ability to sense the surroundings and respond, is found in all living things, from bacteria to humans. Humans alone are aware of themselves.

<div class="myth">

**"Growth and reproduction define life."** Both have exceptions. NEET calls metabolism, cellular organisation and consciousness the defining properties.

</div>

## 2. Naming: binomial nomenclature

Common names change from place to place, so each species gets one **scientific name** of two words, a system popularised by **Carolus Linnaeus**. Plant names follow the **ICN** (International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi and plants) and animal names the **ICZN**.

Rules: the names are in Latin or Latinised form. The first word is the **genus** and starts with a capital letter; the second is the **specific epithet** and starts with a small letter. Printed names are in italics; handwritten names are underlined separately. The author's name may follow in short form, for example *Mangifera indica* Linn.

## 3. The taxonomic hierarchy

The seven obligate ranks, from the biggest box down, are **kingdom, phylum (division in plants), class, order, family, genus and species**. A taxon at any rank is a real group (Mammalia, Poaceae). Pick an organism and climb its ladder.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="taxon-ladder"><figcaption>The seven ranks of a human, a housefly, a mango and a wheat plant. Mango and wheat share a kingdom and a division but split at class (dicot versus monocot); a human and a housefly split at phylum.</figcaption></figure>

- **Species** is the basic unit: individuals with basic similarities that can breed among themselves. *Panthera leo* (lion), *Panthera tigris* (tiger) and *Panthera pardus* (leopard) are three species of one genus.
- **Family** groups related genera: cats (*Felis*) and big cats (*Panthera*) are in Felidae. **Order** groups families: Felidae and Canidae (dogs) are in Carnivora.
- Higher up, the number of shared characters falls, so a kingdom is the hardest taxon to describe in a sentence.

**Taxonomy** is identification, naming and classification; **systematics** (a word from *systema*, arrangement) adds evolutionary relationships.

## 4. Taxonomical aids

| Aid | What it is |
|---|---|
| Herbarium | dried, pressed plant specimens on sheets with labels, kept for future use |
| Botanical garden | living plants grown for reference, each labelled with its name and family (Kew in England, the Indian Botanical Garden at Howrah, NBRI Lucknow) |
| Museum | preserved plants and animals: specimens in jars of preservative, insects pinned in boxes, larger animals stuffed, skeletons |
| Zoological park | wild animals kept in protected surroundings close to their natural home |
| Key | pairs of contrasting statements (couplets, each half a lead) used to identify; one key per taxon |
| Flora, manual, monograph, catalogue | written records: a flora lists the plants of an area, a monograph covers one taxon |

## 5. Whittaker's five kingdoms (1969)

The criteria were **cell type** (prokaryote or eukaryote), **body organisation** (unicellular or multicellular), **mode of nutrition**, **reproduction** and **phylogenetic relationships**.

<div class="steps" data-ms="8000">
<div class="step"><b>Monera (bacteria).</b> Prokaryotes with a cell wall (no cellulose). Shapes: coccus, bacillus, vibrio, spirillum. <b>Archaebacteria</b> live in extremes: halophiles (salty), thermoacidophiles (hot springs), methanogens (marshes and the guts of cattle, making methane in biogas). Their wall differs from other bacteria. <b>Eubacteria</b> (true bacteria) include cyanobacteria (blue-green algae such as <i>Nostoc</i> and <i>Anabaena</i>; heterocysts fix nitrogen; they form blooms), chemosynthetic autotrophs (oxidise nitrates, nitrites, ammonia) and heterotrophs (decomposers, curd, antibiotics, nitrogen fixers, and disease agents). Mycoplasma have no wall, can live without oxygen and are the smallest living cells. Reproduction is mainly by fission.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Protista.</b> Single-celled eukaryotes. <b>Chrysophytes</b>: diatoms and desmids; diatom walls of silica fit like a soapbox and pile up as diatomaceous earth; chief producers of the oceans. <b>Dinoflagellates</b>: marine, two flagella (one along the body, one around it), cellulose plates; <i>Gonyaulax</i> causes red tides with toxins. <b>Euglenoids</b>: no wall but a protein pellicle; two flagella; photosynthetic in light, heterotrophic in the dark. <b>Slime moulds</b>: saprophytic, form a plasmodium, spores with true walls survive for years. <b>Protozoa</b>: heterotrophs.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Protozoa, four groups.</b> Amoeboid (pseudopodia; <i>Amoeba</i>, <i>Entamoeba</i>), flagellated (<i>Trypanosoma</i>, sleeping sickness), ciliated (<i>Paramecium</i>, a gullet and coordinated cilia), sporozoans (infectious spore stage; <i>Plasmodium</i>, malaria).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Fungi.</b> Heterotrophs with a chitin wall (except yeast, unicellular). Body of hyphae forming a mycelium; coenocytic hyphae have many nuclei and no septa. Saprophytes, parasites or symbionts (lichens with algae, mycorrhiza with roots). Classes: <b>Phycomycetes</b> (aseptate, zoospores or aplanospores; <i>Mucor</i>, <i>Rhizopus</i>, <i>Albugo</i>), <b>Ascomycetes</b> (sac fungi, septate, conidia, ascospores in asci; <i>Penicillium</i>, <i>Aspergillus</i>, <i>Claviceps</i>, <i>Neurospora</i>, yeast; morels and truffles are edible), <b>Basidiomycetes</b> (no asexual spores, basidiospores on basidia; mushrooms, <i>Agaricus</i>, rusts <i>Puccinia</i>, smuts <i>Ustilago</i>), <b>Deuteromycetes</b> (imperfect fungi: sexual stage unknown; <i>Alternaria</i>, <i>Colletotrichum</i>, <i>Trichoderma</i>).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Plantae.</b> Eukaryotes with chlorophyll and a cellulose wall, mostly autotrophs; a few are partly heterotrophic (insectivorous bladderwort and Venus fly trap; parasitic <i>Cuscuta</i>). Life cycle with alternation of generations.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Animalia.</b> Eukaryotic, multicellular, no cell wall, holozoic nutrition; store food as glycogen or fat. Most move and follow a definite growth pattern.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Outside the kingdoms.</b> <b>Viruses</b> (Ivanowsky saw them in tobacco mosaic, 1892; Beijerinck called the fluid <i>contagium vivum fluidum</i>; Stanley crystallised them): protein coat (capsid of capsomeres) around either RNA or DNA, never both; plant viruses mostly single-stranded RNA, bacteriophages mostly double-stranded DNA. <b>Viroids</b> (Diener, 1971): free RNA with no protein coat (potato spindle tuber). <b>Prions</b>: misfolded protein (mad cow disease, CJD). <b>Lichens</b>: an alga (phycobiont, makes food) with a fungus (mycobiont, gives shelter and water); they do not grow in polluted air.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Viruses are the smallest living cells."** Viruses are not cells at all; they are inert outside a host. Mycoplasma is the smallest living cell.

</div>

## 6. Worked example

**Sort these:** *Nostoc*, *Euglena*, *Agaricus*, *Gonyaulax*, *Trypanosoma*. *Method:* first ask prokaryote or eukaryote; then one cell or many; then nutrition. *Nostoc* is a prokaryote: Monera. *Euglena*, *Gonyaulax* and *Trypanosoma* are single-celled eukaryotes: Protista (euglenoid, dinoflagellate, flagellated protozoan). *Agaricus* is multicellular with a chitin wall: Fungi, Basidiomycetes.

<div class="keypoints">

- Defining features of life: metabolism, cellular organisation, consciousness; growth and reproduction have exceptions.
- Binomial name: genus capital, epithet small, italics or underlined separately; ICN for plants, ICZN for animals.
- Seven ranks: kingdom, phylum or division, class, order, family, genus, species; shared characters fall going up.
- Aids: herbarium (dried plants), botanical garden, museum, zoo, key (couplets), flora and monograph.
- Five kingdoms (Whittaker 1969) by cell type, body, nutrition, reproduction and phylogeny.
- Archaebacteria in extremes (methanogens make biogas); cyanobacteria fix nitrogen in heterocysts; Mycoplasma has no wall.
- Fungi classes by spores: phycomycetes (aseptate), ascomycetes (ascospores), basidiomycetes (basidiospores), deuteromycetes (no sexual stage known).
- Viruses: RNA or DNA in a coat; viroids: free RNA; prions: protein; lichens: alga plus fungus, pollution indicators.

</div>
