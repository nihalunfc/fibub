**Biology · Class 11 · about 34 minutes.** By the end you will be able to trace air from the nostrils to the alveoli, explain breathing as a pressure change, work out any lung capacity from the volumes, read the oxygen dissociation curve, and say how O₂ and CO₂ travel in the blood.

<div class="bigidea">Air moves only from high pressure to low pressure, and each gas diffuses only from its high partial pressure to its low one. Breathing changes the volume of the chest to make those pressure differences; blood then carries the gases between lungs and tissues.</div>

## 1. Respiratory organs

Animals use what suits their size and habitat: **body surface** diffusion (sponges, cnidarians, flatworms), moist **skin** (earthworms; frogs also breathe through skin), **tracheal tubes** (insects), **gills** (most aquatic arthropods and molluscs, fishes) and **lungs** (land vertebrates).

**Human path:** external nostrils → nasal chamber → **pharynx** (shared with food) → **larynx** (cartilaginous sound box) → **trachea** → right and left primary bronchi (the trachea divides at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra) → secondary and tertiary bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → **alveoli**. The **epiglottis**, a flap of elastic cartilage, covers the larynx during swallowing. The trachea and bronchi are held open by **incomplete cartilage rings**.

The lungs are wrapped in a double **pleura** with **pleural fluid** between, which reduces friction. From nostrils to terminal bronchioles is the **conducting part** (it filters, warms and moistens air); the alveoli and their ducts are the **exchange part**. The chest is an airtight box: vertebral column behind, sternum in front, ribs at the sides and the dome-shaped **diaphragm** below.

Five steps of respiration: breathing (pulmonary ventilation), diffusion across the alveolar membrane, transport by blood, diffusion between blood and tissues, and use of O₂ by cells (cellular respiration).

## 2. Mechanism of breathing

<div class="steps" data-ms="7500">
<div class="step"><b>Inspiration.</b> The diaphragm contracts and flattens, enlarging the chest along its antero-posterior axis, and the <b>external intercostal muscles</b> contract, lifting ribs and sternum (chest deeper, dorso-ventral axis). Lung volume rises, pressure inside falls below atmospheric, and air rushes in.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Expiration.</b> The diaphragm and intercostals relax; the chest and lungs return to their smaller size, pressure inside rises above atmospheric, and air is pushed out. Quiet expiration is passive.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Forced breathing.</b> Abdominal muscles (and internal intercostals) add strength to breathing in and out when needed.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Rate.</b> A healthy adult breathes 12 to 16 times a minute. A <b>spirometer</b> measures the volumes of air moved.</div>
</div>

## 3. Lung volumes and capacities

| Volume | Meaning | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal volume (TV) | air in or out in a normal breath | about 500 mL (6 to 8 L a minute) |
| Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) | extra air you can breathe in by force | 2500 to 3000 mL |
| Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) | extra air you can breathe out by force | 1000 to 1100 mL |
| Residual volume (RV) | air left after the most forceful expiration | 1100 to 1200 mL |

**Capacities** add volumes: IC = TV + IRV; EC = TV + ERV; **FRC** = ERV + RV; **VC** = ERV + TV + IRV; **TLC** = RV + ERV + TV + IRV = VC + RV.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="lung-volumes"><figcaption>A spirometer trace: three normal breaths, one deepest breath in, a normal breath, one deepest breath out. Pick a capacity to see which volumes it adds. RV never leaves the lungs, so a spirometer cannot measure it.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="brth-capacity"></div>

<div class="myth">

**"After the deepest breath out, the lungs are empty."** The residual volume (about 1.1 to 1.2 L) always stays. That is why vital capacity is smaller than total lung capacity.

</div>

## 4. Exchange of gases

Gases diffuse down their **partial pressure** gradients (pO₂, pCO₂: the share of the total pressure due to each gas). Solubility matters too: CO₂ is 20 to 25 times more soluble than O₂, so it crosses the membrane easily despite a small gradient.

| Partial pressure (mm Hg) | Air | Alveoli | Blood to lungs (deoxygenated) | Blood from lungs (oxygenated) | Tissues |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| O₂ | 159 | 104 | 40 | 95 | 40 |
| CO₂ | 0.3 | 40 | 45 | 40 | 45 |

The diffusion membrane has three layers: the thin squamous epithelium of the alveolus, the endothelium of the capillary, and the basement substance between them. Its total thickness is much less than a millimetre.

Switch between rest and exercise, a thick membrane and thin air at 5000 m, and read the oxygen level at the end of the capillary.

## 5. Transport of gases

**Oxygen:** about **97%** is carried by haemoglobin in red cells as oxyhaemoglobin, and about 3% dissolved in plasma. Each haemoglobin molecule carries up to four O₂. Binding depends mainly on pO₂, and also on pCO₂, H⁺ and temperature.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="o2-dissociation"><figcaption>The oxygen dissociation curve is sigmoid. High pCO₂, high H⁺ and higher temperature (as in working tissues) move it right, so haemoglobin lets go of oxygen; the alveoli favour loading.</figcaption></figure>

In the alveoli, high pO₂, low pCO₂, fewer H⁺ ions and lower temperature favour oxyhaemoglobin. In the tissues, the opposite favours release. Every 100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers about **5 mL of O₂** to the tissues.

**Carbon dioxide:** about **20 to 25%** as **carbamino-haemoglobin**, about **70%** as **bicarbonate**, about **7%** dissolved in plasma. **Carbonic anhydrase**, plentiful in red cells, speeds both ways of CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺. In the tissues (high pCO₂) bicarbonate forms; in the lungs (low pCO₂) it turns back into CO₂, which is breathed out. Every 100 mL of deoxygenated blood delivers about **4 mL of CO₂** to the alveoli.

## 6. Regulation and disorders

The **respiratory rhythm centre** in the **medulla** sets the rhythm. The **pneumotaxic centre** in the **pons** can shorten inspiration and so change the rate. A **chemosensitive area** next to the rhythm centre responds to CO₂ and H⁺ and speeds up breathing to remove them. Receptors in the **aortic arch** and **carotid artery** also sense CO₂ and H⁺. Oxygen plays only a small part in normal regulation.

- **Asthma:** inflamed bronchi and bronchioles; difficulty breathing and wheezing.
- **Emphysema:** a long-term disorder in which alveolar walls are damaged, so the exchange surface shrinks; cigarette smoking is a major cause.
- **Occupational respiratory disorders:** in stone-breaking and grinding work, dust overwhelms the body's defences, causing inflammation and **fibrosis** (growth of fibrous tissue) and serious lung damage. Masks protect workers.
