**Biology · Class 12 · about 28 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how Bt crops and RNAi protect plants, how insulin is made by genetic engineering, how gene therapy and molecular diagnosis work, what transgenic animals are used for, and the main ethical and legal questions.

<div class="bigidea">The tools of the last chapter become products here. Put a bacterium's toxin gene into a crop and it protects itself; put a human gene into a bacterium and it makes human insulin; put a working gene into a patient's cells and they make a missing enzyme. Each use raises new questions about safety, ownership and fairness.</div>

## 1. Biotechnology in agriculture

Three ways to feed more people: agrochemical-based farming, organic farming, and farming with **genetically modified (GM) crops**. GM plants can:

- tolerate abiotic stresses (cold, drought, salt, heat);
- need fewer chemical pesticides (pest-resistant crops);
- reduce post-harvest losses;
- use minerals more efficiently, so soil fertility lasts longer;
- have better nutritional value, for example **Golden rice**, rich in vitamin A.

GM is also used to make tailor-made plants that supply starch, fuels and medicines to industry.

## 2. Bt cotton

*Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) makes proteins that kill certain insects: lepidopterans (tobacco budworm, armyworm), coleopterans (beetles) and dipterans (flies, mosquitoes). The protein forms **crystals** during a particular phase of growth.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="bt-toxin"><figcaption>Bt toxin is harmless until an insect's alkaline midgut activates it. Compare the caterpillar gut with the human stomach and the bacterium itself.</figcaption></figure>

The toxin is made as an inactive **protoxin**. In the **alkaline pH** of the insect's gut, the crystals dissolve and the protoxin turns into the active toxin, which binds to the surface of **midgut epithelial cells** and creates **pores**, so the cells swell and burst (lyse) and the insect dies.

The toxin is coded by genes called ***cry***. Genes **cryIAc and cryIIAb** control **cotton bollworms**; **cryIAb** controls the **corn borer**. These genes were put into cotton to make Bt cotton.

<div class="myth">

**"Bt crops are poisonous to everyone who eats them."** The toxin needs an alkaline gut and specific receptors to work. Human stomachs are acidic and lack those receptors, which is why each Cry protein affects only certain insect groups.

</div>

## 3. Pest resistance by RNA interference

The nematode ***Meloidogyne incognita*** infects tobacco roots and greatly reduces yield. **RNA interference (RNAi)** stops it. RNAi occurs naturally in all eukaryotes as a defence: a **complementary dsRNA** binds and prevents translation of a specific mRNA (**silencing**). The source of this dsRNA can be a virus with an RNA genome or mobile genetic elements (transposons) that copy through RNA.

Using ***Agrobacterium* vectors**, nematode-specific genes were put into the host plant so that the plant made both **sense and antisense RNA**. The two pair into dsRNA, start RNAi, and silence the nematode's mRNA. The parasite could not survive inside the transgenic host, which was protected.

## 4. Biotechnology in medicine: insulin

Adult-onset diabetes is controlled by insulin taken regularly. Earlier insulin came from the pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs, and some patients developed **allergies** or other reactions to the foreign protein. Insulin has **two short chains, A and B, joined by disulphide bridges**. In the body it is made as **pro-hormone (proinsulin)** with an extra stretch, the **C peptide**, which is removed during maturation. The challenge was to get insulin assembled into its mature form.

In **1983**, an American company (Eli Lilly) prepared two DNA sequences for the A and B chains, put them into plasmids of *E. coli*, made the two chains separately, and joined them by disulphide bonds to form **human insulin (Humulin)**. This was the first recombinant medicine of its kind. About 30 recombinant therapeutics are now approved worldwide, 12 of them sold in India (NCERT figures).

## 5. Gene therapy

**Gene therapy** corrects a gene defect diagnosed in a child or embryo by putting normal genes into the person's cells or tissues, to take over from the faulty gene.

The first clinical gene therapy was given in **1990** to a **4-year-old girl with adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency**. The ADA enzyme is crucial for the immune system; its absence comes from deletion of the gene. Treatments such as bone marrow transplant or enzyme replacement therapy (injecting functional ADA) are not fully curative. In gene therapy, **lymphocytes** from the patient's blood are grown in culture, a functional **ADA cDNA** is introduced using a **retroviral vector**, and the cells are returned to the patient. Since these cells are not immortal, the treatment must be repeated periodically. If the gene isolated from marrow cells producing ADA is introduced into cells at an **early embryonic stage**, it could be a **permanent cure**.

## 6. Molecular diagnosis

Conventional methods (serum and urine analysis) cannot detect a disease early, when the pathogen or mutation is still rare. Molecular methods can:

- **PCR**: amplifies very low amounts of DNA, so it detects **HIV** in suspected AIDS patients and **mutations** in genes of suspected cancer patients, and other genetic disorders.
- **Probes**: a single-stranded DNA or RNA tagged with a radioactive molecule is allowed to hybridise with its complementary DNA in cells; the clone with the mutated gene does not appear on the photographic film (**autoradiography**), because the probe does not match it.
- **ELISA**: based on **antigen-antibody interaction**; detects either the pathogen's antigens (proteins, glycoproteins) or the antibodies made against it.

Molecular diagnosis and DNA fingerprinting both end in a gel. Press play and compare the lanes.

## 7. Transgenic animals

Animals whose DNA has been changed to carry and express an extra (foreign) gene are **transgenic**. Rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows and fish have been made, but **over 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice**. Uses:

1. **Normal physiology and development**: to study how genes are regulated and how they affect growth (for example insulin-like growth factor).
2. **Study of disease**: models for human diseases such as cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's.
3. **Biological products**: for example human protein (**α-1-antitrypsin**) used to treat **emphysema**. In **1997** the first transgenic cow, **Rosie**, produced human protein-enriched milk (**2.4 g per litre**). The milk contained human **α-lactalbumin** and was nutritionally more balanced for human babies than ordinary cow milk.
4. **Vaccine safety**: transgenic mice are used to test the safety of the **polio vaccine**, before use on humans.
5. **Chemical safety (toxicity) testing**: animals made more sensitive to toxic substances give results faster.

## 8. Ethical issues

Changing organisms can have unpredictable results when they enter the ecosystem. India's **Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC)** decides on the validity of GM research and the safety of introducing GM organisms for public services.

**Patents and biopiracy.** India has about **200,000 varieties of rice**, and **27 documented varieties of Basmati** grown in India. In **1997** an American company got patent rights on Basmati rice through the US Patent and Trademark Office, for a "new" variety derived from Indian farmers' varieties crossed with semi-dwarf ones. Similar attempts have been made to patent uses of **turmeric** and **neem**. **Biopiracy** is the use of bio-resources by multinational companies and other organisations without proper authorisation from the countries and people concerned, and without compensating them. Industrialised nations are rich financially but poor in biodiversity and traditional knowledge, while developing nations hold much of the world's biodiversity. India's Parliament has cleared the **second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill**, which takes such issues into account, including patent terms, emergency provisions and research and development initiatives.

<div class="keypoints">

- GM crops: stress tolerance, less pesticide, fewer post-harvest losses, better mineral use, nutrition (Golden rice, vitamin A).
- Bt: inactive protoxin, activated in alkaline insect gut, pores in midgut cells; cryIAc and cryIIAb (bollworms), cryIAb (corn borer).
- RNAi: sense + antisense RNA → dsRNA silences *Meloidogyne incognita* mRNA in tobacco; *Agrobacterium* vectors.
- Insulin: A and B chains made separately in *E. coli* and joined by disulphide bonds (1983); proinsulin has a C peptide.
- Gene therapy: 1990, ADA deficiency, lymphocytes + ADA cDNA via retrovirus; repeated unless done at an early embryonic stage.
- Diagnosis: PCR (HIV, mutations), probes and autoradiography, ELISA (antigen-antibody).
- Transgenic animals: over 95% mice; α-1-antitrypsin (emphysema); Rosie (1997, 2.4 g/L, α-lactalbumin); polio vaccine testing.
- GEAC; Basmati (27 varieties) patent of 1997; turmeric, neem; biopiracy; Indian Patents Bill second amendment.
