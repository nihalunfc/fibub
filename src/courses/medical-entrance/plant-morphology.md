**Biology · Class 11 · about 34 minutes.** By the end you will be able to identify any root, stem or leaf modification from its origin, describe a flower part by part, read and write a floral formula, and name the features and uses of Fabaceae, Solanaceae and Liliaceae.

<div class="bigidea">Morphology is about origin, not looks. A spine can be a leaf (cactus) or a stem (Citrus); a tendril can be a leaf (pea) or a stem (cucumber). Ask where a part grows from, and the name follows.</div>

## 1. Root

- **Tap root** (dicots, from the radicle, e.g. mustard), **fibrous roots** (monocots like wheat: the primary root is short-lived and many roots grow from the stem base), **adventitious roots** (from any part other than the radicle: grass, *Monstera*, banyan).
- **Regions from the tip:** root cap, meristematic region, region of elongation, region of maturation (with root hairs that absorb water).
- **Modifications:** storage in tap roots of carrot and turnip and adventitious roots of sweet potato; **prop roots** hanging from banyan branches; **stilt roots** from lower nodes of maize and sugarcane; **pneumatophores** that grow upward for air in *Rhizophora* in swamps.

## 2. Stem

The stem has **nodes** (where leaves arise) and **internodes**, and bears buds. Modifications:

| Job | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Underground storage | potato (tuber), ginger, turmeric (rhizome), *Zaminkand*, *Colocasia* (corm) | they have nodes and buds; a root never does |
| Tendrils | cucumber, pumpkin, watermelon, grapevine | from axillary buds |
| Thorns | *Citrus*, *Bougainvillea* | from axillary buds; protect |
| Photosynthesis | *Opuntia* (flat), *Euphorbia* (fleshy, cylindrical) | stems take over the leaf's job in dry places |
| Spread | runners and stolons (grass, mint, jasmine), offsets (*Pistia*, *Eichhornia*), suckers (banana, pineapple, *Chrysanthemum*) | vegetative propagation |

## 3. Leaf

Parts: **leaf base** (swollen as a **pulvinus** in legumes; sheathing in monocots), **petiole** and **lamina**. **Venation** is reticulate (net, most dicots) or parallel (most monocots). A leaf is **simple** if the lamina is whole or cut but not to the midrib, and **compound** if cut into leaflets: **pinnate** (leaflets along a rachis: neem) or **palmate** (leaflets from one point: silk cotton). A bud sits in the axil of a leaf, never in the axil of a leaflet; that is how you tell them apart.

**Phyllotaxy**: alternate (one leaf per node: china rose, mustard, sunflower), opposite (two: *Calotropis*, guava), whorled (more than two: *Alstonia*).

**Modifications:** tendrils (pea), spines (cacti), fleshy storage leaves (onion, garlic), **phyllode** (flat petiole doing the leaf's job in Australian *Acacia*), and traps in insect-eating plants (pitcher plant, Venus flytrap).

## 4. Inflorescence and flower

**Racemose:** the main axis keeps growing and flowers open from the base upward (acropetal). **Cymose:** the main axis ends in a flower, so growth is limited and flowers open from the top downward (basipetal).

<div class="steps" data-ms="8000">
<div class="step"><b>Symmetry.</b> Actinomorphic (any cut through the centre gives halves: mustard, datura, chilli), zygomorphic (one plane only: pea, gulmohur, bean, <i>Cassia</i>), asymmetric (<i>Canna</i>). Trimerous, tetramerous or pentamerous by number of parts per whorl.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Ovary position.</b> Hypogynous: ovary superior, other parts below it (mustard, china rose, brinjal). Perigynous: half-inferior, parts at the rim of a cup (plum, rose, peach). Epigynous: ovary inferior, fused with the thalamus (guava, cucumber, ray florets of sunflower).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Aestivation</b> (how petals overlap in the bud). Valvate: edges only touch (<i>Calotropis</i>). Twisted: each overlaps the next (china rose, lady's finger, cotton). Imbricate: no fixed order (<i>Cassia</i>, gulmohur). Vexillary: one large standard over two wings over two keel petals (pea, bean).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Androecium.</b> Epipetalous (stamens on petals: brinjal), epiphyllous (on tepals: lily). Filaments joined in one bunch: monoadelphous (china rose); two bunches: diadelphous (pea); more: polyadelphous (<i>Citrus</i>).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Gynoecium.</b> Apocarpous: carpels free (lotus, rose). Syncarpous: carpels joined (mustard, tomato).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Placentation.</b> Marginal (along one side of a one-chambered ovary: pea), axile (central axis of a many-chambered ovary: china rose, tomato, lemon), parietal (on the wall: mustard, <i>Argemone</i>), free central (central axis with no septa: <i>Dianthus</i>, <i>Primrose</i>), basal (one ovule at the base: sunflower, marigold).</div>

## 5. Fruit and seed

A **fruit** is a ripened ovary; one formed without fertilisation is **parthenocarpic**. Its wall (pericarp) has epicarp, mesocarp and endocarp. In a **drupe** the endocarp is stony: mango (fleshy edible mesocarp) and coconut (fibrous mesocarp).

A **dicot seed** (pea, gram): seed coat (outer testa, inner tegmen), **hilum** (scar of the stalk), **micropyle**, an embryo axis and two cotyledons; usually non-endospermic (castor is endospermic). A **monocot seed** (maize): endospermic; the endosperm is separated from the embryo by a protein-rich **aleurone layer**; one large shield-shaped cotyledon, the **scutellum**; the plumule is covered by the **coleoptile** and the radicle by the **coleorhiza**.

<div class="myth">

**"Every underground part is a root."** Potato, ginger, turmeric and *Colocasia* are stems: they have nodes, buds and scale leaves. Sweet potato and carrot are roots.

</div>

## 6. Floral formula and three families

A floral formula writes a flower in one line: **Br** bracteate, **⊕** actinomorphic, **%** zygomorphic, **⚥** bisexual, **K** calyx, **C** corolla, **P** perianth (when sepals and petals look alike), **A** androecium, **G** gynoecium, a number for how many, brackets for joined parts, and a line under G for a superior ovary (above G for inferior). Pick a family.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="floral-formula"><figcaption>Floral diagrams of Fabaceae, Solanaceae and Liliaceae. Rings from outside: calyx or perianth, corolla, androecium, gynoecium. In Fabaceae, nine of the ten stamens are joined.</figcaption></figure>

**Worked example.** A flower has 5 joined sepals, 5 joined petals, 5 stamens on the petals and a superior ovary of 2 joined carpels with a swollen placenta. *Reading it:* ⊕ ⚥ K(5) C(5) A5 G(2): **Solanaceae** (brinjal, tomato, potato).

<div class="keypoints">

- Prop roots (banyan), stilt roots (maize, sugarcane), pneumatophores (Rhizophora); sweet potato root, potato stem.
- Stem tendrils (cucumber, grape) and thorns (Citrus, Bougainvillea) come from axillary buds; pea tendrils are leaves.
- Opuntia phylloclade, Australian Acacia phyllode; offsets in Pistia and Eichhornia; suckers in banana.
- Phyllotaxy: alternate (china rose), opposite (Calotropis, guava), whorled (Alstonia).
- Ovary: hypogynous (mustard), perigynous (rose, plum), epigynous (guava, cucumber).
- Aestivation: valvate (Calotropis), twisted (china rose, cotton), imbricate (Cassia), vexillary (pea).
- Placentation: marginal (pea), axile (tomato, lemon), parietal (mustard), free central (Dianthus), basal (sunflower).
- Maize seed: aleurone layer, scutellum, coleoptile, coleorhiza.
- Fabaceae % K(5) C1+2+(2) A(9)+1 G1; Solanaceae ⊕ K(5) C(5) A5 G(2); Liliaceae Br ⊕ P(3+3) A3+3 G(3).

</div>
