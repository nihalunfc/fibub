**Biology · Class 11 · about 30 minutes.** By the end you will be able to compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, describe every organelle by its membranes, contents and job, and spot the traps NEET builds from this chapter.

<div class="bigidea">Every living thing is made of cells, and every cell comes from another cell. A cell is a membrane-bound bag of chemistry; eukaryotic cells divide that bag into rooms (organelles) so that each job has its own space.</div>

## 1. Cell theory

- **Matthias Schleiden** (1838, plants) and **Theodor Schwann** (1839, animals) concluded that living things are made of cells. Schwann also noted that animal cells have a thin outer layer (now the plasma membrane) and that plant cells have a cell wall as well.
- **Rudolf Virchow** (1855) completed the theory: cells arise only from pre-existing cells (*omnis cellula e cellula*).

So the modern cell theory has two parts: all living organisms are made of cells and their products, and all cells come from pre-existing cells.

<div class="myth">

**"Schleiden and Schwann's cell theory was complete."** It did not explain where new cells come from. That was Virchow's contribution, and NEET often tests exactly who added what.

</div>

## 2. Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

| Feature | Prokaryotic (bacteria, cyanobacteria, mycoplasma) | Eukaryotic (protists, fungi, plants, animals) |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | No nuclear envelope; DNA lies in the cytoplasm (nucleoid) | True nucleus with a double envelope |
| Membrane-bound organelles | None | ER, Golgi, lysosomes, mitochondria, plastids and more |
| Ribosomes | 70S (50S + 30S) | 80S in the cytoplasm (60S + 40S); 70S inside mitochondria and plastids |
| Extra DNA | Plasmids (small circular DNA, often carrying antibiotic resistance) | Mostly none (but mitochondria and plastids have their own DNA) |
| Typical size | 1 to 2 µm (Mycoplasma about 0.3 µm, the smallest cells) | 10 to 100 µm |

**Inside a bacterial cell:** the **cell envelope** has three layers: the outer **glycocalyx** (a loose slime layer or a tough capsule), the **cell wall** and the **plasma membrane**. The Gram stain separates bacteria with a thick wall that keeps the stain (Gram-positive) from those that lose it (Gram-negative). **Mesosomes** are infoldings of the membrane that help in wall formation, DNA replication and respiration. Movement uses **flagella** (filament, hook, basal body); **pili** and **fimbriae** help attach, not swim. **Inclusion bodies** store reserve material in the cytoplasm without a membrane.

## 3. A tour of the eukaryotic cell

Choose a cell and an organelle. The line under the drawing gives its membranes, whether it has its own DNA, and its job.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="cell-tour"><figcaption>A simplified plant and animal cell (not to scale). Chloroplasts, a large central vacuole and a cellulose wall are plant features; centrioles are found in animal cells.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="steps" data-ms="7500">
<div class="step"><b>Plasma membrane.</b> The <b>fluid mosaic model</b> (Singer and Nicolson, 1972): a lipid bilayer with proteins floating in it. Proteins can be peripheral (on the surface) or integral (buried). In the human red blood cell membrane, protein is about 52% and lipid about 40%. Small and fat-soluble molecules diffuse through; others need carrier proteins (passive) or pumps that use ATP (active transport, for example the Na⁺/K⁺ pump).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Cell wall.</b> Non-living. Plants: cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins and proteins; algae: cellulose, galactans, mannans and calcium carbonate. The middle lamella (mostly calcium pectate) glues neighbouring cells; plasmodesmata connect their cytoplasm.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Endomembrane system.</b> ER, Golgi, lysosomes and vacuoles work together and share membranes. Mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes are <b>not</b> part of it.</div>
<div class="step"><b>ER and Golgi.</b> Rough ER (with ribosomes) makes proteins for export; smooth ER makes lipids and steroids. The Golgi (Camillo Golgi, 1898) has a cis (forming) face near the ER and a trans (maturing) face; it packages proteins and is where glycoproteins and glycolipids form.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Lysosomes and vacuoles.</b> Lysosomes bud from the Golgi and hold acid hydrolases (lipases, proteases, carbohydrases, nucleases) active at acidic pH. The plant vacuole, bounded by the tonoplast, can fill up to 90% of the cell; the tonoplast moves ions against their gradient. Amoeba has a contractile vacuole for osmoregulation.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Mitochondria.</b> Double membrane; the inner one folds into cristae, which hold the enzymes of the electron transport chain. The matrix has circular DNA, RNA, 70S ribosomes and the enzymes of the Krebs cycle. They divide by fission.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Plastids.</b> Chloroplasts (green, photosynthesis), chromoplasts (yellow, orange, red pigments such as carotene) and leucoplasts (colourless stores: amyloplasts for starch, elaioplasts for oils and fats, aleuroplasts for proteins). Chloroplast: double membrane, thylakoids stacked as grana in a stroma with its own DNA and 70S ribosomes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Ribosomes.</b> No membrane; made of RNA and protein (George Palade first saw them). S is the Svedberg unit, a measure of how fast a particle sinks in a centrifuge, so 50S + 30S makes 70S, not 80S.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Cytoskeleton, cilia and centrioles.</b> Microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments give shape, support and movement. Cilia (short, many) and flagella (long, few) share a 9 + 2 axoneme of microtubules, growing from a basal body. Centrioles: nine evenly spaced triplets with a central hub and spokes (the cartwheel); the pair forms the centrosome and organises the spindle.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Nucleus.</b> Robert Brown (1831) described it. Double envelope with pores; inside, chromatin (DNA with histone proteins) and one or more nucleoli where ribosomal RNA is made. Mature red blood cells of mammals and the sieve tube cells of plants have no nucleus.</div>

## 4. Chromosomes by centromere position

At division, chromatin condenses into chromosomes. Each has a **centromere** (primary constriction) with disc-shaped **kinetochores** where spindle fibres attach.

| Type | Centromere | Arms |
|---|---|---|
| Metacentric | middle | two equal arms |
| Sub-metacentric | slightly off the middle | one shorter, one longer |
| Acrocentric | close to one end | one very short, one very long |
| Telocentric | at the end | one arm |

Some chromosomes have a **secondary constriction** at a fixed place, which gives a small piece called a **satellite**.

## 5. Microbodies

Small single-membrane vesicles containing enzymes, found in both plant and animal cells (for example, peroxisomes, which break down hydrogen peroxide, and glyoxysomes in germinating oily seeds).

<div class="keypoints">

- Cell theory: Schleiden and Schwann (all organisms are cellular), completed by Virchow (cells come from cells).
- Prokaryotes: no nuclear envelope, no membrane-bound organelles, 70S ribosomes, cell envelope of glycocalyx, wall and membrane, mesosomes; Mycoplasma about 0.3 µm.
- Fluid mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972): lipid bilayer with integral and peripheral proteins.
- Endomembrane system = ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles; not mitochondria, plastids or peroxisomes.
- Mitochondria and plastids: double membrane, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes.
- Cilia and flagella 9 + 2; centrioles 9 triplets in a cartwheel; ribosomes have no membrane (Svedberg units do not add).
- Chromosomes: metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, telocentric by centromere position.

</div>
