**Biology · Class 12 · about 28 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how an ecosystem captures, passes on and loses energy, calculate productivity and the energy at any trophic level, list the steps of decomposition and what speeds or slows them, and draw and explain every kind of ecological pyramid.

<div class="bigidea">An ecosystem is living things plus their physical surroundings working as one unit. Energy enters as sunlight, is fixed by plants, and flows one way up the food chain, with most of it lost as heat at each step. Nutrients, by contrast, are recycled by decomposers. Almost every question here is about that one-way flow of energy or the work of decomposers.</div>

## 1. Structure and function

An ecosystem can be as large as the biosphere or as small as a pond; it can be **natural** (forest, grassland, desert, pond, lake, wetland, river, estuary) or **man-made** (crop fields, an aquarium). Its parts are **biotic** (producers, consumers, decomposers) and **abiotic** (water, soil, light, temperature, nutrients). Plants grow at different heights in a forest, which is called **stratification** (trees, shrubs, herbs and grasses form vertical layers).

A **pond** shows every function in small: **productivity** (phytoplankton and plants fix energy), **decomposition** (bacteria and fungi break down the dead), **energy flow** (one way, from the sun up the food chain), and **nutrient cycling** (minerals return to the water and sediments).

## 2. Productivity

**Primary production** is the amount of biomass or organic matter produced per unit area over a time period by plants during photosynthesis, expressed as weight (g m⁻²) or energy (kcal m⁻²). The **rate** of biomass production is **productivity** (g m⁻² yr⁻¹ or kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹).

- **Gross primary productivity (GPP)**: the total rate of photosynthesis, all the organic matter made.
- Plants use part of it in respiration (R).
- **Net primary productivity (NPP) = GPP − R**: what is left for herbivores and decomposers.
- **Secondary productivity**: the rate at which consumers form new organic matter.

Productivity depends on the plant species, the environment (light, temperature, water) and nutrients. The annual NPP of the whole biosphere is about **170 billion tons** of organic matter (dry weight). Although oceans cover about **70%** of the surface, they make only about **55 billion tons** of it, because open oceans are poor in nutrients.

## 3. Decomposition

Decomposers break complex organic matter into inorganic substances such as CO₂, water and nutrients. The raw material is **detritus**: dead plant remains (leaves, bark, flowers) and dead animals, including faecal matter.

<div class="steps" data-ms="7500">
<div class="step"><b>Fragmentation.</b> Detritivores, such as earthworms, break detritus into smaller pieces, which gives microbes more surface to work on.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Leaching.</b> Water-soluble inorganic nutrients move down into the soil and get precipitated as unavailable salts.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Catabolism.</b> Bacterial and fungal enzymes break the detritus into simpler inorganic substances.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Humification.</b> A dark, amorphous substance called <b>humus</b> forms. It is highly resistant to microbes, decomposes very slowly, and, being colloidal, holds nutrients like a reservoir.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Mineralisation.</b> Humus is slowly broken down by some microbes, releasing inorganic nutrients.</div>
</div>

These steps happen at the same time on the same detritus. Decomposition is mainly **oxygen-requiring**. Its rate depends on:

- **chemical composition** of detritus: slower if it is rich in **lignin and chitin**, faster if rich in **nitrogen and water-soluble substances like sugars**;
- **climate**: **warm and moist** conditions favour it; **low temperature** and **anaerobiosis** (no oxygen) slow it, which lets organic matter build up (as in bogs).

<div class="myth">

**"Humus decomposes quickly and so feeds plants at once."** Humus is resistant and breaks down very slowly. That slowness is what makes it a long-term store of nutrients and gives soil its structure.

</div>

## 4. Energy flow

Except for deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystems, the **sun is the only source of energy** for all ecosystems. Of the sunlight reaching Earth, less than 50% is **photosynthetically active radiation (PAR)**, and plants capture only **2 to 10% of the PAR**. That small amount supports the whole living world.

Energy flows **one way**: from the sun to producers, then to consumers. It is never recycled, because at each transfer much is lost as heat (by the second law of thermodynamics, organisms need a constant energy supply to keep their order). On average only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next: **Lindeman's 10 per cent law**.

- **Producers** (autotrophs) are at the first trophic level. **Primary consumers** (herbivores) eat them; **secondary consumers** (primary carnivores) eat herbivores; **tertiary consumers** eat them. A species can sit at more than one level (we eat both plants and animals).
- **Grazing food chain (GFC)**: begins with living producers, e.g. grass → goat → man.
- **Detritus food chain (DFC)**: begins with dead organic matter; its organisms (decomposers, mainly fungi and bacteria, which are **saprotrophs**) secrete enzymes that digest it outside their bodies.
- In **aquatic** ecosystems the GFC is the major path of energy; in **terrestrial** ecosystems much more energy flows through the DFC. Chains link into a **food web**.
- **Standing crop**: the mass of living material at a trophic level at a particular time, measured as biomass or number per unit area.

## 5. Ecological pyramids

Placing the trophic levels as stacked bars, producers at the base, gives an ecological pyramid. Three kinds:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="energy-pyramid"><figcaption>Energy pyramids follow the 10 per cent law and are always upright. Number and biomass pyramids can be inverted (a tree, the sea). Widths are on a log scale.</figcaption></figure>

| Pyramid | Usually | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| Number | upright (grassland: many grasses, fewer insects, fewer birds, few hawks) | inverted for a tree with many insects on it |
| Biomass | upright on land | **inverted in the sea**: a small standing crop of phytoplankton supports a larger biomass of fish, because phytoplankton reproduce very fast |
| Energy | **always upright** | none: energy is lost as heat at every step |

**Limits of pyramids**: they do not account for a species occupying two or more trophic levels; they assume a simple food chain, which rarely exists in nature, not a food web; and they give no place to saprophytes, although these play a vital role.

<div class="keypoints">

- Ecosystem functions: productivity, decomposition, energy flow, nutrient cycling; stratification is vertical layering.
- NPP = GPP − R; biosphere NPP about 170 billion tons a year, oceans about 55 billion tons despite 70% of the surface.
- Decomposition: fragmentation (detritivores), leaching, catabolism, humification, mineralisation; faster with N and sugars, warm and moist; slower with lignin and chitin, cold and no oxygen.
- Sun is the only energy source (except hydrothermal vents); plants fix 2 to 10% of PAR; energy flow is one way; 10 per cent law.
- GFC starts with living plants; DFC with detritus; GFC dominates in water, DFC on land.
- Number pyramid inverted for a tree; biomass pyramid inverted in the sea; energy pyramid always upright.
- Pyramids ignore multi-level species, food webs and saprophytes.

</div>
