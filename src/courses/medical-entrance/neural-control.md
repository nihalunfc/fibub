**Biology · Class 11 · about 32 minutes.** By the end you will be able to sort the neural system into its parts, draw a neuron and name its types, explain the resting and action potentials ion by ion, describe what happens at a synapse, place every brain part and its job, and trace a reflex arc.

<div class="bigidea">A neuron is a living battery: pumps keep it charged (inside negative), and a stimulus opens gates that let ions rush through for a split second. That flicker of charge runs along the axon like a falling row of dominoes, and at the end a chemical carries the message across a gap to the next cell.</div>

## 1. The neural system

Simple animals such as *Hydra* have a net of neurons; insects have a brain with ganglia. In humans:

- **Central neural system (CNS):** brain and spinal cord, where information is processed and controlled.
- **Peripheral neural system (PNS):** all the nerves linked to the CNS (cranial and spinal nerves). Its fibres are **afferent** (carrying impulses from tissues and organs to the CNS) or **efferent** (from the CNS to tissues and organs).
- The PNS has a **somatic** part (CNS to skeletal muscles) and an **autonomic** part (CNS to involuntary organs and smooth muscles), which is further split into **sympathetic** and **parasympathetic** systems.
- The **visceral** neural system is the part of the PNS (nerves, fibres, ganglia and plexuses) that carries impulses between the CNS and the viscera.

## 2. The neuron

A neuron has a **cell body** (with the usual organelles and **Nissl's granules**), short branched **dendrites** that bring impulses towards the cell body (they also have Nissl's granules), and one long **axon** that carries impulses away. The axon ends in branches with bulb-like **synaptic knobs** full of **synaptic vesicles** holding **neurotransmitters**.

| Type | Processes | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Multipolar | one axon, two or more dendrites | cerebral cortex |
| Bipolar | one axon, one dendrite | retina |
| Unipolar | cell body with only one axon | usually in the embryo |

**Myelinated** fibres are wrapped by **Schwann cells** that form a **myelin sheath**, broken by gaps called **nodes of Ranvier** (spinal and cranial nerves). **Non-myelinated** fibres are enclosed by a Schwann cell that does not form a myelin sheath (common in the autonomic and somatic systems).

## 3. Resting potential and the nerve impulse

At rest the axon membrane is **fairly permeable to K⁺**, **nearly impermeable to Na⁺**, and impermeable to the negatively charged proteins inside. The axoplasm has much K⁺ and protein and little Na⁺; the fluid outside has much Na⁺ and little K⁺. The **sodium–potassium pump** uses ATP to send **3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in**. So the outside is positive and the inside negative: the membrane is **polarised**, and the difference is the **resting potential**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="action-potential"><figcaption>Membrane potential at one point of an axon. A stimulus that reaches threshold fires a full spike every time (all or none); a weaker one fades away. Move the time marker to see which ions move in each phase.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="steps" data-ms="8000">
<div class="step"><b>Depolarisation.</b> A stimulus at point A makes the membrane freely permeable to <b>Na⁺</b>. Na⁺ rushes in, so the inside becomes positive and the outside negative: the polarity is reversed. The potential difference at A now is the <b>action potential</b>, which is the nerve impulse.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Spread.</b> Just ahead, at point B, the outside is still positive and the inside negative. Current flows on the inner surface from A to B, and on the outer surface from B to A. This depolarises B, then the next point, so the impulse travels along the axon.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Repolarisation.</b> The rise in Na⁺ permeability lasts only a moment. It is quickly followed by a rise in <b>K⁺</b> permeability; K⁺ moves out and the resting potential returns within a fraction of a second.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Ready again.</b> The fibre can respond to a new stimulus. The pump keeps the ion concentrations in balance over time.</div>
</div>

**Worked example.** A toxin blocks the Na⁺ channels that open on stimulation. *Reasoning:* without the Na⁺ rush there is no depolarisation, so **no action potential** forms and no impulse is sent, even though the resting potential is unchanged.

<div class="myth">

**"A stronger stimulus makes a bigger impulse."** Once threshold is reached, the spike is the same size (all or none). A stronger stimulus can make impulses come more often, not larger.

</div>

## 4. Synapses

A **synapse** is where a **pre-synaptic** neuron meets a **post-synaptic** neuron, with or without a gap, the **synaptic cleft**.

- **Electrical synapses:** the membranes are very close and current flows straight across, so transmission is faster. They are rare.
- **Chemical synapses:** a fluid-filled cleft separates the cells. When an impulse reaches the axon terminal, synaptic vesicles move to the membrane, fuse with it and release **neurotransmitters** (such as acetylcholine) into the cleft. These bind **receptors** on the post-synaptic membrane, ion channels open and a new potential starts, which may be **excitatory** or **inhibitory**.

Step through the events at a chemical synapse.

<div class="steps" data-fig="@bio-synapse-steps">
<div class="step"><b>Action potential arrives</b> at the axon terminal.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Ca²⁺ channels open</b> and calcium rushes into the terminal.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Vesicles fuse</b> with the membrane and release the neurotransmitter.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Transmitter diffuses</b> across the synaptic cleft.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Receptors open</b> ligand-gated channels on the next neuron: a postsynaptic potential.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The signal ends</b> when enzymes break down the transmitter or it is taken back up.</div>
</div>

## 5. The brain

The brain sits in the skull inside three **meninges**: the outer **dura mater**, the thin middle **arachnoid** and the inner **pia mater**, which touches the brain.

<div class="steps" data-ms="8500">
<div class="step"><b>Cerebrum</b> (forebrain). The largest part. A deep cleft splits it into left and right <b>cerebral hemispheres</b>, joined by the <b>corpus callosum</b>. The outer <b>cerebral cortex</b> is folded <b>grey matter</b> (cell bodies) with motor areas, sensory areas and large <b>association areas</b> (linking senses, memory, communication). Inside is <b>white matter</b>: myelinated fibre tracts.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Thalamus</b> (forebrain). Wrapped by the cerebrum; the main relay and coordinating centre for sensory and motor signals.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Hypothalamus</b> (forebrain). At the base of the thalamus; controls body temperature, hunger and thirst, and has neurosecretory cells that make hypothalamic hormones. With the <b>limbic system</b> (amygdala, hippocampus and nearby deep structures) it governs sexual behaviour, emotions (excitement, pleasure, rage, fear) and motivation.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Midbrain.</b> Between the thalamus and hypothalamus above and the pons below. The <b>cerebral aqueduct</b> runs through it; its back has four round lobes, the <b>corpora quadrigemina</b>.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Hindbrain.</b> <b>Pons</b>: fibre tracts linking parts of the brain. <b>Cerebellum</b>: a deeply folded surface that fits in very many neurons. <b>Medulla oblongata</b>: joins the spinal cord and holds centres for breathing, heart and blood-vessel reflexes, and gastric secretion.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Brain stem.</b> Midbrain + pons + medulla oblongata; it links the brain and the spinal cord.</div>
</div>

## 6. Reflex action

A **reflex** is an automatic response to a stimulus at the periphery, made without conscious thought, through part of the CNS. Its pathway, the **reflex arc**, needs at least one **afferent** (sensory) neuron and one **efferent** (motor) neuron in series. The afferent neuron brings the signal from the sense organ through the **dorsal root** into the spinal cord; the efferent neuron carries the reply to the effector (a muscle or gland). The **knee-jerk reflex** is the classic example.

<div class="keypoints">

- CNS = brain + spinal cord; PNS = cranial + spinal nerves; afferent to CNS, efferent from CNS; somatic and autonomic (sympathetic, parasympathetic).
- Neuron: cell body with Nissl's granules, dendrites in, axon out, synaptic knobs with vesicles.
- Multipolar (cortex), bipolar (retina), unipolar (embryo); myelin from Schwann cells, nodes of Ranvier.
- Rest: inside negative; K⁺ high inside, Na⁺ high outside; pump 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in.
- Impulse: Na⁺ in (depolarisation), K⁺ out (repolarisation); current inside from A to B; all or none.
- Electrical synapses fast and rare; chemical synapses release neurotransmitters into the cleft; effects excitatory or inhibitory.
- Meninges: dura, arachnoid, pia. Corpus callosum joins hemispheres; thalamus relay; hypothalamus temperature, hunger, thirst, hormones; limbic system emotions.
- Midbrain: cerebral aqueduct, corpora quadrigemina; pons links; cerebellum folded; medulla breathing and heart centres; brain stem = midbrain + pons + medulla.
- Reflex arc: afferent neuron via dorsal root, efferent neuron to effector; knee jerk.

</div>
