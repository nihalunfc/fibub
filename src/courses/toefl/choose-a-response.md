**Module 3 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will know how Listen and Choose a Response works, the six kinds of sentence it uses, and a four-step method to pick the natural reply even when it never says yes or no.

<div class="bigidea">Choose a Response is not about matching words; it is about matching purpose. Work out what the speaker is doing (asking, requesting, suggesting, sharing news) and pick the reply a real person would give.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

You hear **one short sentence**, spoken by one person. Then you see **four written replies** and choose the one that fits best. There is no long recording and nothing to remember for later, but it moves fast. You hear the sentence once, so your attention has to be fully on it from the first word.

ETS lists this task first among the Listening tasks. ETS does not publish how many of these items each person gets, and because the test adapts, it varies. What we know: each item is quick, so steady accuracy across many of them adds up.

## 2. What it really tests

It tests whether you understand **spoken English in social use**: the polite, indirect way people actually talk on campus and in daily life. Three things make it harder than it looks:

1. **The best reply is often indirect.** "Are you coming tonight?" may be answered with "I have to finish my lab report." No "yes", no "no", yet the meaning is clear.
2. **Form and function differ.** "Why don't we...?" looks like a question about reasons but is a suggestion. "Do you know where...?" looks like a yes/no question but is a request for directions.
3. **Wrong replies repeat words.** If you hear "moved", a wrong option will contain "moved" in another sense.

Everyday analogy: think of a friend who asks "Is it cold in here?" They usually do not want a temperature. They want you to close the window. This task tests whether you hear the request behind the words.

## 3. The six kinds of sentence

| The speaker... | Example | A natural reply |
|---|---|---|
| Asks a yes/no question | Did you finish the reading? | I'm halfway through. |
| Asks a wh- question | When does the bookstore close? | At six, I think. |
| Makes a request | Could you email me the slides? | They're on the course page. |
| Makes an offer | Want me to save you a seat? | That would be great. |
| Makes a suggestion | How about meeting online? | Fine by me. |
| Shares news or a feeling | My laptop crashed. | Oh no. Did you have a backup? |

Two small rules save many points. **"Do you mind...?"** is answered with "Not at all" or "Go ahead" when you agree. **Either-or questions** ("in person or online?") are answered by choosing one, never with "yes".

## 4. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Catch the first words.** The opening tells you the type: *Could you, Would you mind* (request), *Why don't we, How about, Let's* (suggestion), *When, Where, Who, How long* (wh- question), *Did, Are, Have* (yes/no question). A statement means news, a problem or an opinion.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Name the purpose in two words.** Say it silently: "asking time", "wants help", "bad news", "suggesting plan". This turns sound into meaning before the options distract you.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Imagine the reply.** Before you read the options, think of what you would say. Even a rough idea ("sympathy", "a time", "agree") is enough.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Pick the option that does the job, then check the traps.** Cross out replies that repeat words with a new meaning, answer a different question word (a place for "when"), or could follow almost any sentence ("That's interesting").

</div>
</div>

## 5. Worked example

You hear: **"Have you registered for next semester yet?"**

Options:

- A. I registered my bike with campus police.
- B. The system was down all morning.
- C. Next semester starts in the fall.
- D. Yes, I like this semester.

**The thinking.** Step 1: "Have you..." is a yes/no question. Step 2: purpose, "checking if done". Step 3: I expect "yes", "not yet" or a reason. Step 4: A repeats "registered" for a bike: trap. C repeats "next semester" but gives a start date nobody asked for. D starts with "yes" but then talks about liking this semester, which does not answer. B gives a reason the speaker could not register: an indirect "not yet". **Answer: B.**

Notice that the right answer shares **no key words** with the question. That is very common. On this task, a reply that echoes the question is more often a trap than the answer.

## 6. Traps to expect

- **Sound-alikes and double meanings.** "This printer keeps jamming" / "I like strawberry jam".
- **Wrong question word.** "Why is the gym crowded?" / "It opens at seven" answers *when*.
- **Wrong time.** The question is about tomorrow; the option is about last year.
- **General truths.** "Calculators are expensive" is true but does not accept or refuse.
- **Filler replies.** "That's very interesting" could follow anything, so it rarely wins.

<div class="strategy">

**Prefer the human answer.** If two options seem possible, choose the one a polite person would really say to a classmate. TOEFL replies are friendly, natural and specific: sympathy for bad news, congratulations for good news, a reason with a refusal, a plan with an agreement. Decide fast and move on; this task rewards rhythm.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The answer must contain yes or no."** It often does not. Natural English answers yes/no questions indirectly all the time: "I wish I could, but I have a lab" means no; "I've seen worse" means it was not that hard. Judge whether the reply answers the meaning, not whether it contains a magic word.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- Requests: *Could you..., Would you mind..., Can I borrow...*
- Offers: *Want me to..., Would you like me to..., Shall I...*
- Suggestions: *Why don't we..., How about..., Let's..., Maybe we should..., You should...*
- Agreeing: *Sure, Go ahead, Fine by me, Good idea, That would be great*
- Polite refusals: *I wish I could, but..., Sorry, I need it myself, I'm afraid...*
- Reactions: *Really?, Again?, Oh no, That's fantastic, Me neither*

</div>

## 7. Practice

There are 44 original items here, covering indirect answers, requests, offers, suggestions, news, wh- questions, either-or questions and polite disagreement. Each round mixes them, so repeat the drill until you get every round right.

<div class="keypoints">

- You hear one short sentence once, then choose the best of four written replies.
- Match the purpose (request, offer, suggestion, question, news), not the words.
- Indirect replies are often correct: a reason can mean yes or no.
- "Do you mind...?" takes "Not at all"; either-or questions take a choice, not "yes".
- Options that repeat the speaker's words are often traps.
- Imagine your own reply before you read the options.

</div>
