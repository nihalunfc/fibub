**Module 2 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will know when to skim, when to scan and when to read slowly, how signal words speed you up, how to pace the Reading section, and how to build real reading speed in a few weeks.

<div class="bigidea">Fast readers do not read every word faster. They choose the right speed for each job: skim for the big idea, scan for one fact, and slow down only for the sentence that answers the question.</div>

## 1. Why speed matters

The 2026 TOEFL Reading section takes about 30 minutes for about 50 items, across Complete the Words, Read in Daily Life and Read an Academic Passage. The test is adaptive, so the exact time and number of items can vary a little, and the screen shows your timer. Either way, that is well under a minute per item on average, including reading time.

Many learners lose points not because they cannot understand, but because they run out of time and rush the last questions. Better speed gives you calm time to check the hard ones.

An everyday picture: think of a supermarket. You do not read every label in every aisle. You look at the aisle signs (skim), walk to the right shelf, look for your brand (scan), and only then read the label carefully. Reading for a test works the same way.

## 2. Three reading speeds

| Speed | When to use it | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **Skim** (fast) | First look at a passage or email | Read the title, first sentences and any bold words; ignore details |
| **Scan** (very fast, targeted) | Finding a name, number, date or key word | Move your eyes quickly down the text looking for one thing |
| **Careful read** (slow) | The sentence that answers a question | Read every word, including small ones like "not", "only", "until" |

Skilled readers switch between these all the time. Research on reading fluency (Grabe 2009) shows that good second-language readers read with a purpose and adjust their speed to it, rather than reading everything at one slow pace.

## 3. The method for the test

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Skim the text (20 to 60 seconds, depending on length).** Title, first paragraph, first sentence of each later paragraph. Know the topic and the route.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Read the question and name its type** (lesson 2.5). Detail and vocabulary questions are fast. Main idea questions use your skim.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Scan for the key word or its synonym.** Use your map to go to the right paragraph. Do not reread from the top.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Read the target sentences carefully.** Slow down here: this is where points are won or lost.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Answer and move on.** If you are stuck after about a minute, eliminate what you can, choose your best answer and go to the next question. A blank is always wrong.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Signal words: read the road signs

Signal words tell you what comes next before you read it. When you see "however", you know a contrast is coming. When you see "for example", you know the next part is less important than the idea it supports. This prediction makes your reading faster and your understanding stronger.

**Worked example.** Read this original sentence pair: "The new medicine worked well in the laboratory. However, ..." Before you read on, you already know the next part will be negative: something did not work as well, perhaps in real patients. If a question asks about a problem with the medicine, you know to look right after "However".

Another: "Wind power is clean. In addition, ..." The next part will be another advantage. If a question asks for a disadvantage, it will not be here; look for "but", "however" or "on the other hand" instead.

| Signal | What comes next | Speed tip |
|---|---|---|
| however, but, yet, nevertheless, although | A contrast or turn | Slow down: questions love turns |
| for example, for instance, such as | An example | You can skim examples unless asked |
| therefore, as a result, so, thus | A result | Often the key point of the paragraph |
| in addition, moreover, also | Another point, same direction | Skim unless the question lists points |
| first, next, finally | Steps in order | Note the order |
| in other words, that is | The same idea, said more simply | Useful for vocabulary questions |

## 5. Pacing the section

You cannot always move back and forth freely in an adaptive test, so pace each item as it comes:

- **Complete the Words**: fill each gap in a few seconds. Fast, but check endings.
- **Daily-life texts**: a short skim, then quick scans. These are usually your fastest points.
- **Academic passages**: a one-minute map, then answer. Spend more time here than on daily-life texts.

Never let one question take three or four minutes. That time is stolen from questions you could have answered easily.

<div class="strategy">

**The one-minute rule.** If a question has taken about a minute and you still are not sure, cut it short: cross off options that are clearly wrong, choose the best of the rest, and move on with a calm mind. Steady pace across the whole section beats perfection on one item.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Speed reading means moving my eyes faster over every word."** Pushing your eyes faster without understanding just means reading badly at high speed. Real speed comes from two things: knowing words so well that you recognize them instantly, and choosing what to read closely. Both grow with practice, not with tricks.

</div>

## 6. Build speed in three weeks

Reading speed improves with lots of easy reading. Nation (2009) recommends regular timed reading of texts with almost no unknown words, so that your brain practices fluency rather than decoding.

1. **Every day, 15 minutes of easy reading** in English: news for learners, graded readers, simple science articles. You should know about 98 of every 100 words.
2. **Three times a week, a timed read.** Choose a 300-word text, time yourself, then answer two or three questions about it. Keep a note of your time and aim to cut it slowly without losing accuracy.
3. **Once a week, a full practice set** from this module under time pressure.

<div class="phrases">

Habits that slow you down, and what to do instead:

- **Saying every word in your head** → read groups of words ("in the early morning") as one unit
- **Going back to reread** → keep going; the next sentence often explains
- **Stopping on every unknown word** → guess from context and continue
- **Translating into your language** → think in English pictures and ideas
- **Reading questions last** → for scanning tasks, read the question first

</div>

## 7. Practice

The drill has three parts: skim a paragraph and choose its main idea (aim for about 30 seconds each), scan a timetable for one fact (aim for 10 to 15 seconds each), and predict what comes after a signal word.

<div class="keypoints">

- The Reading section is about 30 minutes for about 50 items, so speed matters.
- Use three speeds: skim for the main idea, scan for one fact, read carefully only where the answer is.
- Signal words predict the next idea; slow down at contrasts and results.
- Pace every item; after about a minute, eliminate, choose and move on.
- Real speed comes from daily easy reading and timed practice over several weeks.
