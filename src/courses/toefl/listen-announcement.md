**Module 3 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will know what TOEFL announcements sound like, the four things every announcement contains, and how to avoid the old-rule and wrong-number traps.

<div class="bigidea">Every announcement answers four questions: what is happening, what changed, what the rules are, and what you should do. Listen for those four and you will have the answers before you see the questions.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

You hear **one speaker** making an announcement, for example a professor at the start of class, a library or health center employee, a recreation center, an event organizer or a bus driver. It is short. Then you answer **several questions** about it.

ETS does not publish how many announcements or questions each person gets, and the number can vary as the test adapts. What you can count on: announcements are full of **times, days, places, prices and rules**, and many of them describe a **change**.

## 2. What it really tests

Announcements test whether you can pick out **practical information** from a stream of speech and understand **why** it is being said. Question types:

- **Purpose:** What is the main purpose of the announcement?
- **Detail:** When will the pool reopen? What must students show after ten p.m.?
- **Reason:** Why was the exam moved?
- **Inference:** What does the speaker imply about late-night studying?
- **NOT questions:** Which of these is NOT required?
- **Attitude:** What is the professor's attitude toward late emails?

Everyday analogy: an airport gate announcement. You do not need every word. You need four things: is my flight affected, what changed (gate or time), what are the rules (boarding groups), and what do I do (go to gate twelve). TOEFL announcements work exactly the same way.

## 3. The four things in every announcement

| Part | Listen for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic / purpose** | First sentence; *a quick change, attention, I'm from...* | "A quick change to the schedule." |
| **The change** | *instead of, now, will no longer, moved to, starting* | "Thursday instead of Tuesday" |
| **Rules and conditions** | *you will need to, only, must, no..., if...* | "Show your student card after ten p.m." |
| **Action for listeners** | *please, remember, bring, sign up, come to* | "Please bring your own mat." |

Some announcements add a **reason** (*because, as, since*) and a **tip** (*lines are shortest right after ten*). Both get tested.

## 4. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Use the title line.** "An announcement from the campus library" tells you to expect hours, rooms and rules. Predict two or three words you might hear.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Catch the purpose in the first sentence.** Speakers announce their topic right away: "Attention, swimmers. The pool will be closed..." Note it in three words.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Mark every change as OLD → NEW.** When you hear *instead of* or *now*, write both values with an arrow: "Tue → Thu", "2h → 3h". The question will ask for the new one; the old one will be an option.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Pair each number with its label.** Never write a number alone. "$1 food ticket", "free entry", "15 min green bus", "5 min walk". Most wrong options are real numbers from the talk attached to the wrong thing.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Catch the instruction and the "if".** "Please..." and "If you..." lines tell listeners what to do. They are tested often, especially "If X happens, what should listeners do?"

</div>
</div>

## 5. Worked example

Imagine a recreation center announcement:

> "Attention, swimmers. The indoor pool will be closed from Monday to Wednesday next week for its yearly cleaning. Open swim hours will return on Thursday morning. During the closure, we are offering free yoga classes in Studio Two. No sign-up is needed, but please bring your own mat, as we only have a few to lend."

Notes: *pool closed Mon-Wed (cleaning) → back Thu a.m. / free yoga Studio 2, no sign-up, bring own mat (few to lend)*.

**Q: Why will the pool be closed?** "For its yearly cleaning." Not repairs, not a competition.

**Q: When does open swim return?** **Thursday morning.** Wednesday is the last closed day, a likely trap.

**Q: What are swimmers advised to do if they go to yoga?** **Bring their own mat.** "Sign up online" is a trap: the speaker said *no* sign-up is needed. "Pay a small fee" is a trap: the classes are free.

Look at how the traps were built: one from the wrong end of a date range, one from a negative sentence turned positive, one from an idea that was never said. Your notes beat all three.

## 6. Traps to expect

- **Old rule vs new rule.** "Three hours instead of two": two is the trap.
- **Negatives flipped.** "No sign-up is needed" becomes an option "Sign up first".
- **Number swaps.** Entry is free; food tickets are one dollar. The option "One dollar to enter" mixes them.
- **"Both sides" style wording.** "One page, both sides" is one sheet, not two pages.
- **Mentioned but not required.** NOT questions list three real requirements and one that never appeared. Tick off what you heard.

<div class="strategy">

**For NOT questions, use elimination.** Read each option and ask "Did I hear this?" Cross out the three you heard. The one left is the answer. Your notes make this fast: if a rule list has four items in your notes, the option that is not in them wins.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Announcements are easy, so I can relax."** They sound simple, but they are dense: one minute can hold five numbers and three rules. Many test takers lose points here by remembering the right number for the wrong thing. Treat every number as a pair: number plus label.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- Starting: *Attention..., Before we begin..., One more thing..., A quick reminder...*
- Change: *instead of, now, starting Sunday, will no longer, has been moved to*
- Rules: *you will need to, must, only, no..., not allowed*
- Instructions: *please..., remember to..., make sure you..., bring...*
- Conditions: *if it rains..., in case..., unless...*
- Reasons: *because of, due to, as, since*

</div>

## 7. Practice

Eight original announcements from professors, the library, the health center, the recreation center, an event and a campus shuttle. Write OLD → NEW and number-plus-label notes as you listen.

<div class="keypoints">

- One speaker makes a short announcement; several questions follow.
- Every announcement has a topic, a change, rules and an action for listeners.
- Write changes as OLD → NEW: the new value is the answer.
- Pair every number with its label; wrong options swap them.
- Answer NOT questions by ticking off what you heard.

</div>
