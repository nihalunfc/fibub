**Module 2 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will be able to take a word apart into its prefix, root and suffix, choose the right member of a word family for any gap, and avoid the spelling slips that cost points in Complete the Words.

<div class="bigidea">Most English academic words are built from parts. If you know the common prefixes, suffixes and spelling rules, half a word is usually enough to rebuild the whole thing correctly.</div>

## 1. Why word building matters for TOEFL Reading

In Complete the Words (lesson 2.1), you see the first part of a word and type the rest. Very often, the part you must type is an ending: **-tion**, **-ment**, **-ed**, **-ly**, **-ous**. So the task is partly a test of word endings. If you know that "develop" becomes "development" (noun), "developing" (verb form) and "developed" (past), you only need to decide which form the sentence wants.

Word building also helps in the rest of the Reading section. When an academic passage uses a word you have never seen, such as "unpredictable", you can often work out the meaning: un (not) + predict (say before) + able (can be). That is a vocabulary question answered without a dictionary.

An everyday picture: words are like LEGO. A small set of pieces (about twenty common prefixes and twenty common suffixes) can build thousands of words. Learn the pieces once, and every new word becomes easier.

## 2. Prefixes: the front piece changes the meaning

A prefix goes at the start of a word and changes its meaning, but not usually its part of speech. "Clear" and "unclear" are both adjectives.

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| un-, in-, im-, il-, ir-, non- | not | unclear, inaccurate, impossible, illegal, irregular, nonprofit |
| re- | again, back | rebuild, return, reconsider |
| pre- / post- | before / after | preview, postwar |
| mis- | wrongly | misread, misunderstand |
| over- / under- | too much / not enough | overestimate, underfunded |
| inter- | between | international, interact |
| trans- | across | transatlantic, transport |
| sub- | below | subsurface, subway |
| co- | together | coauthor, cooperate |
| anti- | against | antibacterial |
| multi- | many | multilingual |

A tip on the "not" prefixes: **im-** comes before b, m and p (impossible, imbalance), **il-** before l (illegal), **ir-** before r (irregular). Most others take **un-** or **in-**, and you simply have to learn which.

## 3. Suffixes: the back piece changes the job

A suffix goes at the end and usually changes the part of speech. This is the key to Complete the Words, because the grammar slot tells you which job the word must do.

| Job | Common suffixes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a thing or idea) | -tion, -sion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -ance, -ence, -ure | production, decision, development, darkness, ability, importance, existence, pressure |
| Noun (a person) | -er, -or, -ist, -ian | employer, inventor, scientist, historian |
| Verb | -ize, -ify, -en, -ate | modernize, simplify, strengthen, create |
| Adjective | -ous, -ful, -less, -able, -ible, -al, -ic, -ive | dangerous, useful, careless, reliable, possible, seasonal, economic, persuasive |
| Adverb | -ly | rapidly, frequently |

A word family is all the forms of one root: **decide** (verb), **decision** (noun), **decisive** (adjective), **decisively** (adverb). Research on vocabulary (Bauer and Nation 1993) shows that learners who know one member of a family can often recognize the others, which makes word families an efficient way to grow vocabulary.

## 4. Spelling patterns that matter most

Complete the Words needs exact spelling. These rules cover most of the slips learners make:

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Silent e: drop it before a vowel ending.** make → making, use → usable, create → creation. Keep it before a consonant ending: use → useful, care → careless.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Consonant + y: change y to i.** easy → easier, study → studied, happy → happiness. But keep the y before -ing: studying, carrying.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Short vowel + one consonant: double it.** drop → dropped, plan → planning, begin → beginning (stress on the last part, be-GIN). Do not double when the stress is earlier: visit → visited, open → opened.

</div>
<div class="step">

**-ful has one l, but -fully has two.** beautiful → beautifully, careful → carefully.

</div>
<div class="step">

**i before e, except after c** (for the "ee" sound). believe, field, but receive, ceiling. There are exceptions (science, their), so learn the common ones by heart.

</div>
</div>

## 5. Worked example: three gaps, one method

Take each gap and ask two questions: what job does the slot need, and what does the meaning need?

> The **(1) disco____** of the new species surprised scientists. It lives in a **(2) remar_____** small area, and its habitat is **(3) threa_____** by new roads.

**Gap 1.** After "The" and before "of": a noun. The letters "disco" plus four missing letters. The verb "discover" is in the right family but has only three letters after "disco" and is the wrong job. The noun is "discovery": disco + very, four letters. Count, then match the job.

**Gap 2.** Before "small", which is an adjective: you need an adverb that describes it. remar + 5 → "remarkably". Root "remark", adjective "remarkable", adverb "remarkably". The -ly ending is the job marker.

**Gap 3.** After "is" and before "by": a passive. You need a past participle: threa + 5 → "threatened". Root "threat", verb "threaten", past "threatened". One t at the end of "threaten", because the stress is on the first syllable (THREAT-en).

The lesson from gap 1 is the most important: **count the missing letters and match the job in the word family**. That stops most mistakes.

## 6. Traps

| Trap | Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adjective where a noun is needed | "a big different" | "a big difference" | After "a big" you need a noun |
| Noun where an adverb is needed | "melting rapid" | "melting rapidly" | It describes the verb |
| Wrong "not" prefix | "unpossible" | "impossible" | im- before p |
| Wrong doubling | "visitted", "begining" | "visited", "beginning" | Double only when the stress is on the last syllable |
| -ful slip | "beautifuly" | "beautifully" | beautiful + ly |
| US/UK mix | "behaviour" then "color" | "behavior", "color" | Choose one spelling and stay with it; this course uses US spelling |

<div class="strategy">

**Learn by family, not by single word.** When you meet a new academic word, write its family in four columns: noun, verb, adjective, adverb (analyze, analysis, analytical, analytically). Five families a day, reviewed after one day, three days and a week, will add hundreds of words before test day.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Spelling does not matter in a reading test."** In Complete the Words it matters a lot: you produce the word yourself, so a misspelled word is very likely to be marked wrong. The good news is that the spelling rules in this lesson cover most of the errors learners make, and the rest is a short list of common words to learn by heart.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Endings that tell you the job at a glance:

- **-tion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -ance, -ence** → noun
- **-ist, -er, -or** → person
- **-ize, -ify, -en** → verb
- **-ous, -ful, -less, -able, -al, -ic, -ive** → adjective
- **-ly** → usually adverb (but friendly, lonely and lovely are adjectives)

</div>

The drill mixes four kinds of item: prefix meanings, suffix jobs, the right form of a word for a gap, and spelling choices. Read each solution, even when you are right: it names the rule.

<div class="keypoints">

- Many academic words are prefix + root + suffix; knowing the parts lets you rebuild a cut word.
- Prefixes change meaning (un-, re-, pre-, mis-, over-); suffixes change the job (noun, verb, adjective, adverb).
- Choose the family member that fits the grammar slot: noun after "the", adverb before an adjective, past participle after "is ... by".
- Learn the five spelling rules: silent e, y to i, doubling, -fully, i before e.
- Count the missing letters and check the word family before you type.

</div>
