**Module 5 · about 17 minutes.** By the end you will know the six features that make English easy to understand (word stress, sentence stress, linking, weak forms, thought groups and intonation) and how to practise each one in a few minutes a day.

<div class="bigidea">Being understood depends more on rhythm and stress than on perfect individual sounds. Get the beat right and listeners forgive a lot.</div>

## 1. Why this matters for both speaking tasks

In **Listen and Repeat**, you copy a sentence's sounds, stress and rhythm. If you cannot hear weak forms and linking, you lose words; if you cannot produce stress, your repetition sounds flat. In **Take an Interview**, clear delivery decides whether the listener can follow your ideas at all.

Good news: you do not need a native accent. Speakers with many different accents are easy to understand because their **stress, rhythm and pausing** are clear. This lesson focuses on those, in the order that helps most.

Everyday analogy: think of music. You can play a song on a cheap keyboard with a few wrong notes and people still recognise it, as long as the rhythm is right. Play every note perfectly at the same length and loudness, and nobody knows the tune.

## 2. Word stress: one strong syllable

Every English word with two or more syllables has **one syllable that is longer, louder and higher**: PHO-to-graph, pho-TOG-ra-pher, pho-to-GRAPH-ic. Stressing the wrong syllable can make a word impossible to recognise, even when every sound is correct.

Useful patterns (with exceptions, but they help):

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| -tion, -sion: stress just before | in-for-MA-tion, de-CI-sion |
| -ic, -ics: stress just before | e-co-NOM-ics, sci-en-TIF-ic |
| -ity: stress just before | u-ni-VER-si-ty, a-BIL-i-ty |
| -ee, -eer: stress the ending | em-ploy-EE, vol-un-TEER |
| Two-syllable noun / verb pairs | a REC-ord / to re-CORD, a PRES-ent / to pre-SENT |

Unstressed syllables are not just quieter. Their vowel often shrinks to a short "uh" sound (called schwa): the "o" in "today", the "a" in "about". Shrinking these vowels is what makes the stressed one stand out.

## 3. Sentence stress and rhythm

In a sentence, **content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words, "not") carry beats, and **function words** (a, the, of, to, can, was, him) squeeze between them. English roughly keeps the beats evenly spaced, so the more small words between two beats, the faster they are said.

*"The LIB-rary OP-ens at NINE."* Three beats. *"The LIB-rary is GO-ing to be OP-en un-til NINE."* Four beats, but the small words between them are much quicker.

One beat in each thought group is strongest: usually the **last new content word**. Move it, and the meaning moves too:

- I didn't take **your** notebook. (I took someone else's.)
- I didn't **take** your notebook. (I only borrowed it.)
- I didn't take your **notebook**. (I took your pen.)

## 4. Linking and weak forms

Speakers do not pause between words. They join them:

- **Consonant to vowel:** "turn it off" sounds like "tur-ni-toff"; "opens at" like "open-zat".
- **Vowel to vowel:** a light w after o or u ("go-w-out"), a light y after ee, i or ay ("the-y-end", "my-y-umbrella").
- **Same sound meets same sound:** say it once, a little longer: "bus stop", "need to".
- **t or d between consonants** often disappears: "nex(t) day", "las(t) week".

Weak forms are the short versions of function words: "can" → "kn", "for" → "fer", "to" → "tuh", "and" → "n", "of" → "uv", "them" → "em". Casual reductions go further: *gonna* (going to), *wanna* (want to), *hafta* (have to), *whaddaya* (what do you). **Recognise these; you never have to say them.** In your own speech, light natural weak forms help your rhythm, and a clear full form is always acceptable.

Everyday analogy: linking is like the way a good cook moves from one step to the next without putting the knife down each time. The steps are all there; they just flow.

## 5. Thought groups and intonation

A **thought group** is a short chunk of meaning said in one breath, with one main stress and a small pause after it: *"When I moved here / I didn't know anyone / so I joined a running club."* Never pause between "the" and its noun, or between a verb and a short object.

**Intonation** is the music of the voice:

- **Statements and wh- questions fall** at the end: "I'm studying biology." "Where's the lab?"
- **Yes/no questions rise:** "Is the lab open?"
- **Lists rise on each item and fall on the last:** "paper ↗, pens ↗ and a calculator ↘".
- **Unfinished parts stay up** ("When the alarm rings ↗, leave the building ↘").

In an interview answer, ending your points with a clear fall sounds confident. Rising at the end of every sentence can sound like you are asking for approval.

## 6. The method: a daily five-minute routine

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Choose one sentence.** Take one from the Listen and Repeat drill or from something you read today.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Mark it.** Underline the stressed syllable in long words, circle the main stress, draw a slash at each thought group and an arrow for the final pitch.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Tap the beat.** Tap the table on each stressed syllable while you say it slowly. Squeeze the small words between taps.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Link and speed up.** Join the words that link. Say it three times, faster each time, keeping the taps even.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Record and compare.** Record yourself with the speaking timer. Listen: can you hear the beat, the endings and the final fall?

</div>

## 7. Worked example

Sentence: **"If you have any questions, just send me an email."**

- Word stress: QUES-tions, e-MAIL.
- Content words: QUES-tions, SEND, e-MAIL (and "any" lightly). Main stress: e-MAIL.
- Thought groups: *If you have any questions / just send me an email.*
- Linking: "have any" → "ha-veny"; "send me an email" → "sen-me-a-nemail".
- Weak forms: you → "ya", an → "n".
- Intonation: stays up slightly after "questions" (more is coming), falls on "email".

Said with these features, the sentence takes about three seconds and sounds natural. Said with every word strong and separate, it takes five seconds and sounds like a list.

<div class="strategy">

**Fix the biggest problem first.** If you are not sure what to work on, use this order: (1) word stress in common long words, (2) final consonants and endings (-s, -ed), (3) pausing in thought groups, (4) sentence stress, (5) linking, (6) intonation. Spend a week on each, five minutes a day, and record yourself at the start and end of the week to hear the change.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I need to lose my accent to get a high score."** You do not. Raters listen for whether you are clear and easy to follow, not for a particular accent. Many accents are perfectly clear. What makes speech hard to follow is wrong word stress, missing endings and pauses in strange places, and all of those can be fixed.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

**Stress patterns of words you will use often in interviews**

- *imPORtant, interESTing* (or IN-ter-est-ing), *eCONomy, ecoNOMic, techNOLogy, enVIronment*
- *opPORtunity, uniVERsity, comMUnity, eduCAtion, inforMAtion*
- *deVELop, deCIDE, sugGEST, preFER, reCOMmend*
- *COMfortable, VEGetable, DIFFerent* (often said in three syllables)

<div class="keypoints">

- Rhythm and stress matter more for clarity than perfect individual sounds.
- Every long word has one strong syllable; learn the patterns (-tion, -ic, -ity).
- Content words carry the beats; small words are weak and squeezed between.
- Linking and weak forms are natural: hear them all, use the light ones, never force slang.
- Speak in thought groups and end statements with a falling pitch.
- Five minutes a day of marking, tapping and recording works better than occasional long sessions.

</div>
