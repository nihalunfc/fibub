**Module 3 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will know what the TOEFL Listening section looks like, which five skills every task tests, and a five-step method you can use on any recording.

<div class="bigidea">Good test listening is active: you predict what is coming, listen for the words that signal what matters, and keep the speaker's purpose in mind, because every recording plays only once.</div>

## 1. What the Listening section looks like

On the 2026 TOEFL iBT, Listening takes **about 29 minutes** and has **about 47 items**. Because the test is adaptive, the exact number and time can change a little from person to person; the test screen shows your time. There are four task types:

| Task | What you hear | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Listen and Choose a Response | One short thing a person says | Pick the best reply |
| Listen to a Conversation | Two people talking, often on campus | Answer questions |
| Listen to an Announcement | A notice from a professor or campus office | Answer questions |
| Listen to an Academic Talk | A short lecture | Answer questions |

ETS does not publish exactly how many items of each type you will get, so do not plan around a fixed count. Plan around the skills.

Two things matter for strategy. First, **each recording plays once**. Second, plan as if you **will not see the questions until after the audio** (the test screen shows exactly how each task works). So do not wait to "hear the answer". Listen for the things questions are usually about.

## 2. The five skills every task tests

Whatever the task, the questions come from a short list. Learn the list and you know what to listen for.

1. **Main idea (gist).** What is this mostly about? Usually stated near the start.
2. **Detail.** A fact, number, time, place or reason.
3. **Purpose.** Why did the speaker say something, or why is this person talking at all?
4. **Inference.** What does the speaker mean but not say? What will happen next?
5. **Attitude.** How does the speaker feel: pleased, worried, doubtful, unhappy?

Think of a football commentator. They do not describe every step a player takes. They say who has the ball, what changed, and how the game is going. You listen the same way: follow the story, notice changes, feel the mood.

## 3. The method: five steps for any recording

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Read the title line and predict.** "A student and a librarian" means a problem with a book, a room or a card. "A biology class" means a term, an example and maybe an experiment. Ten seconds of prediction makes the first sentences much easier.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Catch the topic in the first lines.** Conversations state the problem early ("I can't find..."). Talks state the topic early ("Today I want to talk about..."). If you miss everything else, keep this.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Listen for signal words.** *But, actually, instead, however* mean a change. *First, second, finally* mean a list. *Because, so, that's why* mean a reason. *For example, imagine* mean an example. Questions love these moments.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Track the final decision.** Plans change. The answer is almost always the last version: the new time, the second suggestion, the corrected number.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Notice the tone.** Is the voice pleased or annoyed? A sentence like "Well, that's one way to spend the weekend" means the opposite of its words. Attitude questions test this.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Listen in your head to this short exchange:

> **Nina:** Are we still meeting at the café at three?
> **Omar:** That was the plan, but the café is closed today. Let's use the study room in the engineering building instead. I booked it from three thirty.

**Question:** Where and when will they meet?

**The thinking.** "That was the plan, but" is a big signal: the café and three o'clock are now old information. "Instead" confirms the new place. "From three thirty" is the new time. **Answer: the engineering study room at three thirty.** The wrong options on the test would be "the café at three" (the old plan) and "the café at three thirty" (half old, half new). This trap appears on almost every listening test in the world, so learn to love the word *but*.

## 5. Question types and their traps

| Question sounds like | It tests | Classic trap |
|---|---|---|
| What is the conversation mainly about? | Main idea | A detail that was mentioned, but only once |
| What does the man say about...? | Detail | The right word with the wrong fact attached |
| Why does the professor mention...? | Purpose | What the thing is, instead of why it was said |
| What does the woman imply? | Inference | A big guess the speaker never supported |
| What will the student probably do next? | Inference | An early plan that was later dropped |
| How does the speaker feel about...? | Attitude | Taking an ironic sentence at face value |

**Repeated words are a warning, not a clue.** Test writers often put a word you heard into a wrong option. If an option repeats an exact word but the meaning does not match what was said, be suspicious.

<div class="strategy">

**Answer the question, not the recording.** After the audio, read the question carefully and decide what kind it is (main idea, detail, purpose, inference, attitude). Then go to the matching part of your memory or notes. Eliminate options that are false, not mentioned, or true but not what was asked. Never leave a blank: a careful guess may be right; a blank never is.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I must understand every word to answer well."** You do not. Native speakers miss words all the time and still follow the meaning. Focus on the topic, the signal words, the final decision and the tone. If one word passes you by, let it go and keep listening; worrying about it makes you miss the next sentence.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- Change: *but, actually, instead, however, on second thought, I'd rather*
- List and order: *first, second, next, after that, finally*
- Reason and result: *because, since, so, that's why, as a result*
- Example: *for example, for instance, imagine, take...*
- Main point: *the key thing is, what I want you to remember, so basically*
- Attitude: *I'm not sure, honestly, I doubt, that's great, I'm afraid*

</div>

## 6. Train your ear every day

Test skills grow from daily listening. Fifteen minutes a day beats two hours once a week.

- **Campus podcasts and university lectures.** Many universities post free lectures. Listen to five minutes, pause, and say the main idea in one sentence.
- **Shadowing.** Play a short clip and speak along a half-second behind. It trains your ear for linking and reduced sounds such as *gonna* and *wanna*.
- **Predict and check.** Before each clip, guess three words you will hear from the title. Then check.
- **Use speed.** If your player has a speed control, try 0.9x for new material and 1.1x for review.

Using the TOEFL drills below, listen once only, the way the test works. Then press play again and listen for the parts you missed. That second listen is where your ear learns.
