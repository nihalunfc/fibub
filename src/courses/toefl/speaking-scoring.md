**Module 5 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will understand, in plain terms, what TOEFL speaking scores reward, how the 2026 score scale works, and how to rate your own recordings so your practice targets the right things.

<div class="bigidea">Raters reward speech that is easy to follow, accurate enough to be clear, and full of relevant, connected ideas. Practise all three, in that order of attention.</div>

## 1. The official guides, and this summary

ETS publishes **scoring guides** (sometimes called rubrics) and sample materials for TOEFL speaking on its TOEFL pages at ets.org. Those are the official description, and they can change, so read them there. This lesson does not reproduce them. What follows is fibub's plain-language summary, made to help you practise and check yourself.

ETS has long used a combination of trained human raters and automated scoring for TOEFL speaking. The exact mix and process are described by ETS; check ets.org for the current details.

## 2. The score scale in 2026

From 21 January 2026, every TOEFL iBT section, including Speaking, is reported on a **1 to 6 band scale**, in half bands, aligned with the CEFR levels. Your overall score is the average of the four sections, rounded to the nearest half band. For two years after January 2026, score reports also show a comparable score on the old 0 to 120 scale. See [Scores: the 1 to 6 bands, CEFR and the old 0 to 120](/learn/courses/toefl/scores-and-bands/) for the details and the calculator.

## 3. What raters listen for: three areas

TOEFL speaking has long been described in terms of three areas. In plain words:

**Delivery: how easy you are to understand.** Clear sounds and endings, the right word stress, a steady pace, pauses between thought groups rather than in the middle of them, natural intonation. Not an accent: clarity.

**Language use: the grammar and words you control.** Accurate verb forms and sentence structure, a range of structures (not only simple sentences), and words that say exactly what you mean. Small errors that do not block meaning matter much less than errors that confuse the listener.

**Topic development: the ideas.** Answering the exact question, giving reasons and specific examples, and connecting your ideas so the listener can follow from one to the next.

Everyday analogy: a delivery driver bringing you a parcel. Delivery is whether the van arrives smoothly; language use is whether the address and parcel are right; topic development is whether what is inside is what you ordered. You need all three to be happy.

For **Listen and Repeat**, fibub's plain reading is simpler: the goal is to say the sentence back fully, accurately and clearly. Every missed or changed word is lost. For **Take an Interview**, all three areas matter in every answer.

## 4. How the areas look at different levels

These descriptions are fibub's own, written to help you place your practice answers. They are not official band descriptors.

| | Lower range | Middle range | Upper range |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Delivery** | Often hard to follow; long pauses; many words unclear | Mostly clear, with some unclear words, flat rhythm or hesitation | Clear and smooth throughout; small slips do not slow the listener |
| **Language use** | Very simple or memorised phrases; errors often block meaning | Basic structures mostly accurate; limited range; some errors noticeable | Wide range, mostly accurate; precise word choice |
| **Topic development** | Off topic, very short or a single repeated idea | Answers the question with a reason, but support is general or loosely connected | Clear answer, specific support, ideas connected from start to finish |

Most learners find their answers sit in different columns for different areas. That is useful: it tells you exactly what to practise next.

## 5. The method: rate your own recordings

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Record.** Use the speaking timer with "Record myself" ticked. The recording stays on your device.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Listen once for delivery.** Pretend you are a stranger. Mark any word you would not understand and any pause in the middle of a chunk.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Listen again for language.** Note each error: tense, article, word choice. Circle the ones that would confuse a listener.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Check development.** Did your first sentence answer the question? Can you name your reason and your example? Did you close?

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Pick one fix and redo.** Choose the single biggest problem and record the same question again. Compare the two recordings.

</div>
</div>

## 6. Worked example: rating one answer

**Question:** "Should students work part-time while they study?"

**Answer (transcribed from a learner recording):** "Yes, I think student should work part-time. Because, um, they can get money. And also experience. For example... um... my friend, she work in a café, and she learn many things. So, yes, I think it is good."

**Rating.** *Delivery:* understandable, but there are pauses inside ideas ("For example... um... my friend") rather than between them. *Language use:* repeated errors with verb endings ("student should", "she work", "she learn") and a sentence fragment ("Because..."); simple vocabulary. *Topic development:* a clear position and two reasons, but the example is vague ("learn many things"). Overall: middle range.

**One fix:** make the example specific. "My friend Ana works at a café twelve hours a week, and she has learned to deal with difficult customers, which helped her in a job interview last month." That one change improves development and gives the language a chance to show range. Fix the endings next.

<div class="strategy">

**Self-rating checklist**

- [ ] Delivery: could a stranger understand every word without effort?
- [ ] Delivery: were my pauses between ideas, not inside them?
- [ ] Language: were my verb forms right, especially -s and -ed?
- [ ] Language: did I use at least one longer structure (because, although, if, which)?
- [ ] Development: did my first sentence answer the exact question?
- [ ] Development: did I give a specific example and a closing sentence?

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Speaking faster sounds more fluent, so it scores higher."** Speed is not fluency. Rushing blurs your endings, removes your pauses and leads to more mistakes, all of which make you harder to follow. A steady pace with short pauses between thought groups sounds more fluent and leaves room to think.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

**What to listen for in your own recordings**

- Delivery: *stress on the right syllable, -s and -ed audible, pauses at commas and between ideas, falling pitch at the end of points*
- Language: *past tense for stories, "would" for imagined situations, a/the before nouns, linking words (because, although, so, which)*
- Development: *answer in sentence one, "The main reason is...", "For instance...", "So overall..."*

</div>

- Speaking is reported on the 1 to 6 band scale from January 2026.
