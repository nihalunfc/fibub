**Module 3 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will know what TOEFL conversations sound like, the problem-options-decision pattern most of them follow, and how to answer detail, purpose, inference and attitude questions about them.

<div class="bigidea">A TOEFL conversation is a small story with a shape: someone has a problem, options are discussed, and a decision is made. Follow that shape and the questions almost answer themselves.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

You hear **two people talking**, often on a university campus: a student and a professor, a librarian, a housing officer, an adviser, or two classmates. A line on screen tells you who is talking. The conversation is short, with natural turns. After it ends, you answer **several questions** about it, each with four options.

ETS does not publish exactly how many conversations or questions you will get; it changes because the test adapts. Expect each conversation to have a handful of questions, and expect the topics to be ordinary campus life: books, rooms, deadlines, jobs, classes, money, projects.

## 2. What it really tests

It tests whether you can **follow people's goals** in real talk. Not just the facts, but why someone came, what they want, what changed, and how they feel. Four question types cover nearly everything:

- **Main idea / purpose of the conversation:** Why does the student go to see the professor?
- **Detail:** When is the report due? What does the librarian offer?
- **Inference:** What will the student probably do next? What does the man imply?
- **Attitude and function:** How does the woman feel? Why does she say "Don't undersell it"?

Everyday analogy: think of overhearing two people at a bus stop. In thirty seconds you know what the problem is, whether it is solved, and if they are happy about it. You do not remember every word. That is exactly the kind of understanding this task rewards.

## 3. The pattern: problem, options, decision

Most campus conversations follow the same three-part shape:

| Part | Sounds like | Typical question |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem** (first 1-2 turns) | "I can't find...", "My heater hasn't worked...", "I wasn't sure if..." | Why does the student go to the office? |
| **Options** (middle) | "You can..., or I can...", "What if we...?", "Have you tried...?" | What does the librarian suggest? Why does the student not choose X? |
| **Decision / next step** (last 1-2 turns) | "Then I'll...", "Okay, I'll text Sam", "Bring a printed copy" | What will the student do next? |

When you know this shape, you know where to put your attention: **the first turns** give the purpose, **the middle** gives the details and the traps, and **the end** gives the inference answer.

## 4. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Read who is talking and predict the problem.** "A student and a housing office employee" means a room problem. "Two students" means a class, a project or a plan. Predicting makes the first turn easy to catch.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Lock in the problem.** In the first two turns, note in three or four words why this conversation is happening: "book checked out", "lab moved Friday". This answers the main purpose question.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Track options and who rejects what.** When a second idea appears, note it with an arrow: "hold → reserve copy". Listen for *but, actually, I'd rather, honestly*: they mark a rejected idea.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Catch the ending.** The last two turns usually contain the decision and the next step. Many test takers relax at the end. Do the opposite.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Hear the feeling.** Note one word for each speaker's mood when it shows: "relieved", "worried", "helpful". Attitude questions use exactly these.

</div>
</div>

## 5. Worked example

Imagine this short conversation:

> **Maya:** Jordan, did you see the email? They moved our biology lab from Wednesday to Friday morning.
> **Jordan:** Friday morning? I work at the café on Fridays until noon. And the lab report is due on Friday now too, right?
> **Maya:** No, actually, the report deadline stays the same. It's still due next Monday.
> **Jordan:** Oh, that's a relief. Okay, I'll text Sam and ask him to swap shifts.

**Q1. What is the conversation mainly about?** The first turn names the problem: the lab moved. Answer: a change to the time of a lab. (Not "a new job": the job is only part of why the change is a problem.)

**Q2. When is the report due?** Jordan guesses Friday, but Maya says "No, actually" and gives Monday. Answer: **next Monday.** "Friday" is the trap: a wrong idea that gets corrected.

**Q3. How does Jordan feel about the report?** He says "that's a relief". Answer: **relieved.**

**Q4. What will Jordan do next?** Last turn: "I'll text Sam and ask him to swap shifts." Answer: **ask a co-worker to switch shifts.**

Four questions, and each one came from one part of the shape: problem, correction, feeling, next step.

## 6. Traps to expect

- **The corrected idea.** Someone says something wrong, and the other person corrects it. The wrong version appears as an option.
- **The rejected suggestion.** "You could place a hold, but there are four people ahead of you." The hold is mentioned, but it is not the plan.
- **True but not asked.** An option repeats a real fact from the conversation that does not answer this question.
- **Over-inference.** "She'll look at the syllabus before deciding" does not mean she has signed up. Do not go further than the speakers did.
- **Literal reading of idioms.** "Don't undersell it" is not about selling. Listen to the context: the adviser then lists the job's value.

<div class="strategy">

**Write the arc, not the words.** Your notes for a conversation can fit on three short lines: *P:* (problem), *O:* (options, with arrows for changes), *D:* (decision). Add numbers and days next to what they belong to. That is enough to answer nearly every question.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The first thing they suggest is the answer."** Often it is the opposite. TOEFL conversations are written so that plans change: the first suggestion is rejected, the first date is moved, the first guess is corrected. Wait for the final version before you lock in an answer.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- Stating the problem: *I was wondering if..., The thing is..., I'm having trouble with...*
- Offering options: *You could..., Another option is..., Have you thought about...*
- Rejecting politely: *Honestly, I'd rather..., That might be hard because..., The catch is...*
- Deciding: *Then I'll..., Okay, let's..., In that case...*
- Feelings: *That's a relief, I'm worried that..., That sounds fun, I'm sorry to hear that*

</div>
