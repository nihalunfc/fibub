**Module 5 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will know how Listen and Repeat works, why memory alone is not enough, and a five-step method to catch a whole sentence and say it back clearly, small words and all.

<div class="bigidea">You cannot repeat what you did not understand. Listen for meaning in chunks, keep the rhythm, and the words come back with it.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

The Speaking section is the shortest part of the TOEFL iBT: **about 8 minutes and about 11 items**, in two task types. The first is **Listen and Repeat**. You hear a sentence, and then you say it back, exactly, clearly and naturally. The sentences are the kind you would hear on a campus or in daily life: directions, notices, a comment about a class.

ETS does not publish the exact time per sentence on the pages we checked, so this course does not guess. The test screen tells you what to do and when. In this lesson we practise with **3 seconds to get ready and 10 seconds to speak** per sentence. Those are fibub's practice times, not official ones.

Because the test adapts and later items can be harder, expect sentences to vary in length. Short sentences test clear sounds. Longer ones test whether you can hold a whole idea in your head.

## 2. What it really tests

It looks like a memory game. It is really a **listening and pronunciation** task.

1. **Listening in chunks.** Nobody can remember fifteen separate words. But you can remember three meaning chunks: *"The new parking rules / will start / at the beginning of next month."* If you understand the meaning, you only need to rebuild it.
2. **Grammar you can hear.** If your grammar knows that "students" needs "have" and "yesterday" needs "-ed", it fills the small words in for you. That is why stronger English means better repeating.
3. **Clear, natural speech.** Stress on the right words, smooth linking, endings you can hear and a natural fall at the end of a statement.

Everyday analogy: a waiter takes an order for a table of four without writing it down. They do not memorise sounds; they remember "two pastas, one salad, one soup, no onions". Meaning is much easier to carry than noise.

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Get ready before it plays.** Sit up, breathe out, and focus on the speaker. You hear the sentence once, so the first word counts.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Listen for meaning, in chunks.** Ask "who / does what / where or when?" Picture it. A picture is easier to hold than a list of words.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Catch the beat.** Notice which words are strong (the content words) and where the speaker pauses. Copying the rhythm brings the weak words back with it.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Say it straight away, chunk by chunk.** Start speaking when the screen tells you to. Use the same chunks and pauses you heard. Do not rush: clear and complete beats fast.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Keep the small words and endings.** As you speak, make sure "the", "a", "to", "of", "-s" and "-ed" are there. End with falling pitch for a statement, rising pitch for a yes/no question.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

You hear: **"Students who live off campus can apply for a parking permit online."**

**Step 2, meaning.** Who? *Students who live off campus.* Does what? *Can apply for a parking permit.* How? *Online.* The picture: a student at home, applying on a laptop for a car permit.

**Step 3, beat.** Strong words: STUdents, LIVE, CAMpus, apPLY, PARKing, PERmit, ONline. Weak words: who, off (lightly), can, for, a. Pauses: after "campus".

**Step 4, say it.** *"Students who live off campus / can apply for a parking permit online."*

**Step 5, check.** Common mistakes on this sentence:

| What the learner said | What went wrong |
|---|---|
| Students live off campus can apply parking permit online. | Dropped "who", "for" and "a": the small words |
| Student who live off campus can apply for parking permit online. | Dropped plural "-s" and the article "a" |
| Students who live off campus can get a permit on the internet. | Changed words: the meaning is close, but it is not a repetition |
| Students who live... um... can apply... online. | Lost the middle chunk; pausing inside a chunk |

The third one is worth noticing. Changing words may feel clever, but in this task the goal is the sentence you heard.

## 5. Why sentences slip away, and how to stop it

**Weak forms.** In natural speech, "can" sounds like "kn", "for" like "fer", "to" like "tuh", "of" like "uv". If you expect strong forms, you will not hear them at all. The next lesson, [Stress, rhythm and linking](/learn/courses/toefl/pronunciation/), trains this.

**Linking.** "Apply for a" sounds like "a-ply-fer-ruh". The word boundaries blur. Knowing the common links helps you split the sound back into words.

**Long subjects.** "Anyone who wants to join the hiking club" is all subject. Learners often lose the verb that follows. Mark it in your head: subject chunk, then *should come*.

**Numbers and names.** Days, times and numbers carry a lot of meaning and are usually stressed. Hear "the first Sunday of every month" as one picture.

Everyday analogy: copying a phone number. You do not hear "6-0-4-5-5-5-1-2-3-4"; you hear "six-oh-four / five-five-five / one-two-three-four". Chunks are how memory works.

<div class="strategy">

**Train it like a muscle.** Ten minutes a day works better than one hour on Sunday.

- **Shadowing:** play a sentence and speak along with it, a split second behind. Then speak without it.
- **Dictation:** type exactly what you heard (the drill below does this). Every missing "the" shows you a weak form you are not hearing yet.
- **Build up:** start with six-word sentences, then add a chunk at a time.
- **Record and compare:** use the speaking timer below with "Record myself" ticked, then listen. Is every ending there?

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"If I have a good memory, I will do well on Listen and Repeat."** Memory helps a little, but it fills up after about seven unrelated words. What really lets you repeat long sentences is understanding: grammar that predicts the small words and listening that hears chunks. Improve your English and your "memory" improves with it.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

**Words that hide in fast speech** (listen for them):

- Articles and prepositions: *a, an, the, to, of, for, at, in, on*
- Helping verbs: *can, has, have, was, were, will, would, could*
- Endings: *-s, -es* (plurals and he/she verbs), *-ed* (past), *-ing*
- Pronouns: *him* (often "im"), *her* ("er"), *them* ("em"), *you* ("ya")

</div>

## 6. Practice

**Drill.** Each sentence comes with three questions: type it word for word (dictation), find the main stress, and one question on linking, thought groups or intonation. Play the sentence first and do not open the transcript until you have answered. The voice is your browser's speech synthesis, so its stress may be flatter than a real speaker's; use your own knowledge of English for the stress questions. Thirty sentences and 90 items in all.

**Timed repeating.** Read the sentence once during preparation, look away, then say it from memory when the timer turns. It trains the chunk-and-rhythm habit without audio. Practice times, not official ones.

<div class="keypoints">

- Speaking is about 8 minutes and about 11 items; Listen and Repeat comes first.
- Listen for meaning in chunks (who, does what, where or when), not word by word.
- Copy the rhythm: strong content words, weak small words, pauses between chunks.
- Keep every small word and ending; do not swap in your own words.
- If you lose part of it, say what you caught clearly; never stay silent.
- Practise daily with shadowing, dictation and recording yourself.

</div>
