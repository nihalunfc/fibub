**Module 4 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will know what the Write an Email task asks, how to plan an email in one minute, how to choose the right tone for the reader, and how to check your work. You will see two model answers at different levels and practice with three timed tasks.

<div class="bigidea">A good test email is a useful real email: it does every job the task lists, in a tone that suits the reader, in an order that is easy to follow.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

Write an Email is one of the three task types in the Writing section of the 2026 TOEFL iBT (about 23 minutes and about 12 items in all). You read a short description of a real-life situation: a problem with a service, a request to a professor, a message to a neighbor or a club. The task tells you who to write to and lists the points your email should cover, usually two or three. You type your email in a box.

ETS does not publish the exact time or a word limit for this task on the pages we checked; the test screen shows the time you have. For **practice**, this lesson uses **7 minutes** and a target of about **80 to 150 words**. These are fibub's practice settings, not official rules. They are enough to cover three points properly without padding.


Pick a situation and slide Formality from very casual to very formal.

## 2. What it really tests

The email task checks whether you can use written English to get something done:

1. **Doing the task.** Every point in the task is covered, clearly.
2. **Development.** Each point has enough detail to be useful: what, when, why, what you want.
3. **Organization.** A greeting, a purpose sentence, one short paragraph per point, a closing.
4. **Language use.** Correct grammar, a good range of words, and a tone that fits the reader.

An everyday picture: think of a delivery driver with three parcels. If one parcel is missing, the delivery failed, however fast the driver was. Your three task points are the parcels.

## 3. The method: plan in one minute, write in five, check in one

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Read the task and underline the points.** Find who you are writing to and the two or three things you must do (for example: explain, give a reason, ask). Number them in your head.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Decide the tone.** Professor, manager, company, official: polite and formal ("Dear Dr. Lin", "Best regards"). Friend, classmate, neighbor you know: friendly but correct ("Hi Jordan", "Thanks again").

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Make a quick plan.** One line per point, with one detail each. "1 returned camera, Tuesday. 2 cap in bag, assistant checked. 3 check records, remove fee."

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Write: greeting, purpose, one paragraph per point, closing.** Say why you are writing in the first sentence. Give each point two or three sentences. Use linking words to join ideas.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Check.** Are all the points there? Re-read for missing words, verb endings ("he goes", "I returned"), articles and spelling. Spell check is off, as in the test.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example: planning

> **Task (original).** You borrowed a camera from the media center for a class project. When you returned it, the staff said the lens cap was missing, and you were charged a $35 fee. You are sure you returned the lens cap. Write an email to Ms. Patel, the media center manager. In your email, explain what happened, say why you think the fee is a mistake, and ask her to do something.

**Reader and tone:** a manager; you disagree, but you want her help. Polite and firm.

**Points:** (1) what happened, (2) why the fee is a mistake, (3) what you want her to do.

**Plan:**
1. borrowed March 3, returned March 10, then saw $35 fee
2. put cap in bag; student assistant checked the bag
3. check return record or camera bag; remove fee

Now look at two answers written from this plan.

<div class="model">

**Model A: a clear, basic answer (likely to score in the middle range)**

Dear Ms. Patel,

I am writing about a fee on my account. I borrowed a camera from the media center on March 3 and I returned it on March 10. Yesterday I saw a $35 fee for a missing lens cap.

I think this is a mistake. I put the lens cap in the camera bag when I returned it. Also the student at the desk checked the bag.

Can you please check again and remove the fee? Thank you.

Best regards,
Amir Haddad

</div>

**Why it works, and what holds it back.** *Task completion:* all three points are there. *Development:* each point has a basic detail, but the reasons are short ("I think this is a mistake"). *Organization:* clear paragraphs, a purpose sentence, a proper greeting and closing. *Language use:* accurate but simple; the same patterns repeat ("I borrowed... I returned... I saw... I put"), and "Also the student" needs a comma.

<div class="model">

**Model B: a developed, precise answer (likely to score near the top)**

Dear Ms. Patel,

I am writing about a $35 charge for a missing lens cap that appeared on my student account this week. I borrowed one of the center's cameras for my documentary project on March 3 and returned it on March 10.

I believe the charge was made in error. Before returning the camera, I placed the lens cap in the front pocket of the camera bag, and the student assistant at the desk opened the bag and checked the contents in front of me. It is possible that the cap was moved to another bag after I left.

Would you mind checking the return record for March 10, and perhaps the other camera bags? If the cap is found, I would be grateful if you could remove the fee. Please let me know if you need any more information.

Thank you for your help.

Best regards,
Amir Haddad

</div>

**Why it scores higher.** *Task completion:* all three points, each fully done, and the request is specific (check the record and the other bags, then remove the fee). *Development:* the reason has evidence (the assistant checked the bag) and a possible explanation (the cap was moved). *Organization:* one clear job per paragraph, smooth links ("Before returning", "If the cap is found"). *Language use:* a wider range of accurate structures ("appeared on my account", "Would you mind checking", "I would be grateful if you could") in a polite, firm tone.

Notice that Model B is not full of rare words. It is better because it is more **specific** and more **connected**, not because it sounds fancy.

## 5. Tone: choosing the right voice

| Reader | Greeting | Closing | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Professor, manager, official | Dear Dr. Lin, / Dear Ms. Patel, | Best regards, / Sincerely, | "I would be grateful if you could..." |
| Company you do not know | Dear Customer Service Team, | Kind regards, | "I am writing to report a problem with..." |
| Classmate, neighbor, club member | Hi Jordan, / Hello Maya, | Thanks, / See you soon, | "Would it be possible to...?" |
| Close friend | Hi Sam! | Take care, | "Do you want to...?" |

Polite does not mean weak. "Could you please remove the fee?" is a clear request. Rude words or threats make the email worse in every area, because they do not do the job well.

## 6. Common traps

- **A missing point.** The most common and most costly problem. Tick each point off your plan.
- **Copying the task.** "I want to explain what happened" is not explaining what happened. Use your own words and real details.
- **No purpose sentence.** Readers should know why you are writing within one line.
- **Wrong register.** "Hey dude" to a professor, or "Yours faithfully" to a friend.
- **Vague requests.** "Please do something" or "Let me know your thoughts" when you need a specific action.
- **Padding.** Long greetings ("I hope this email finds you very well and that your family is...") add words, not quality.

<div class="strategy">

**Self-check before you finish**

- [ ] Every point in the task is covered, in the order given
- [ ] The first sentence says why I am writing
- [ ] Greeting and closing fit the reader
- [ ] Each point has at least one specific detail (a date, a reason, a number)
- [ ] My request says exactly what I want
- [ ] I checked verb endings, articles and spelling

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"A longer email always gets a higher score."** Length helps only when the extra words add useful detail. A 110-word email that covers every point clearly beats a 200-word email that repeats itself or misses a point. Aim for complete and specific; the words will follow.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Useful email phrases:

- **Purpose:** I am writing to ask about... / I am writing regarding... / I am contacting you because...
- **Explaining:** Last week, I... / The reason is that... / Unfortunately, ...
- **Requests:** Could you please...? / Would you mind + -ing...? / I would be grateful if you could... / Would it be possible to...?
- **Offering:** I would be happy to... / If it helps, I can...
- **Closing:** Thank you for your help. / I look forward to hearing from you. / Please let me know if you need any more information.

</div>
