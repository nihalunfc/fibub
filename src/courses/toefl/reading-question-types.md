**Module 2 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will recognize each kind of reading question from its first few words, know the method that fits it, and spot the wrong options that test makers like to use.

<div class="bigidea">Each type of reading question asks for a different kind of thinking. If you name the type first, you know where to look and what a right answer looks like before you read the options.</div>

## 1. Why question types matter

In both Read in Daily Life and Read an Academic Passage, the questions come in a small number of recognizable types. ETS describes questions on detail, inference, vocabulary and purpose, among others. It does not publish how often each type appears, and the adaptive test may show you a different mix from another test taker.

Knowing the types saves time. A detail question sends you to one sentence. A main idea question asks you to think about the whole text. A vocabulary question needs only the sentence around one word. Using the wrong approach (for example, reading the whole passage again for a vocabulary question) wastes minutes.

An everyday picture: a doctor asks different questions to find different things. "Where does it hurt?" needs one exact answer. "How have you been feeling this month?" needs the big picture. Reading questions work the same way.


Tap a phrase from the question, then the passage phrase that means the same.

## 2. The six types at a glance

| Type | How it is worded | Where to look | A right answer... |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detail | "According to the passage...", "What does the notice say about..." | One sentence | Paraphrases what is written |
| Negative detail | "Which is NOT mentioned...", "All of the following EXCEPT" | Several places | Is the one option not in the text |
| Inference | "can be inferred", "suggests", "most likely", "can be concluded" | One or two sentences | Must be true, but is not written |
| Vocabulary | "The word X is closest in meaning to..." | The word's sentence | Could replace the word without changing the meaning |
| Purpose | "Why does the author mention...", "The function of the sentence is..." | The sentences around it | Names the job (example, contrast, support, limit) |
| Main idea | "mainly about", "main purpose", "best summarizes" | The whole text | Covers all of it, not one part |

## 3. A method for each type

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Detail.** Find the key word in the question (a name, a number, a term). Scan for it, or for a synonym. Read that sentence and choose the option that says the same thing in different words.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Negative detail (NOT, EXCEPT).** Treat each option as a small detail question. Find it in the text and cross it off. The last one standing is the answer. Do not choose an option just because you remember it; check.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Inference.** Find the related sentences, then ask: what else must be true if this is true? Choose the option that is a small, safe step from the text, never a big leap or an outside opinion.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Vocabulary.** Read the full sentence. Cover the word, think of your own word that fits, then find the option closest to yours. Put the chosen option into the sentence and read it: it must keep the meaning.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Purpose and main idea.** For purpose, read the sentence before the detail: the detail usually supports, explains or contrasts with it. For main idea, use your paragraph map and choose the option that covers every paragraph.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example: one paragraph, four types

> Sea otters live in cold ocean water, yet unlike seals and whales they have no thick layer of fat. Instead, they rely on the densest fur of any animal. The fur traps a layer of air that keeps the skin dry. For this reason, otters spend hours each day grooming; a coat that becomes dirty or matted no longer holds air, and the animal quickly loses heat.

**Detail: According to the paragraph, how do sea otters stay warm?** Key words "stay warm". The text: "they rely on the densest fur ... The fur traps a layer of air". The right option will paraphrase this, for example "Their thick fur holds air next to the skin". A trap: "a thick layer of fat", which uses words from the text but describes seals and whales.

**Purpose: Why does the author mention seals and whales?** The sentence says "unlike seals and whales they have no thick layer of fat". The mention sets up a contrast that leads to the real point: otters use fur instead. Right answer: "to contrast them with otters".

**Inference: What can be inferred about an otter whose fur is covered in oil?** Not written directly. But the text says a dirty coat "no longer holds air, and the animal quickly loses heat". Oil makes fur dirty, so the otter would lose heat quickly. Small, safe step. An option like "it would die within an hour" is too big a leap.

**Vocabulary: "matted" is closest in meaning to...** Cover the word: "a coat that becomes dirty or ___ no longer holds air". Your own guess: "stuck together" or "messy". The closest option: "tangled". Check: "a coat that becomes dirty or tangled". The meaning holds.

## 5. Wrong-answer patterns

Test makers write wrong options with a purpose. Learn to recognize them:

| Pattern | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Word match | Copies words from the text but says something different | "a thick layer of fat" for otters |
| True but not asked | A real fact from the text that answers another question | The pool's weekend hours, when the question asks about weekdays |
| Too strong | Adds "always", "only", "never", "all" | "Language completely changes what people see" |
| Opposite | Reverses the text | "Weekend hours change" when they do not |
| Outside knowledge | Sounds true in real life but is not in the text | "Silver was cheap in Europe" |
| Wrong meaning of a word | Uses another dictionary meaning | "fixed" = "repaired" instead of "set" |

<div class="strategy">

**Name it, then answer it.** Before you read the options, say the type to yourself: "detail", "inference", "purpose". Then predict what the answer will look like. When you finally read the options, you are checking against your prediction instead of being pulled by attractive wrong answers.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Inference questions need me to guess or use my own ideas."** No. A TOEFL inference is not a guess. It is something that must be true because of what the text says. If an option needs any information that is not in the passage, or you could argue against it using the passage, it is not the inference the question wants. The right answer is usually a small, careful step.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Question wording and what it signals:

- **"according to", "states", "mentions"** → detail
- **"NOT", "EXCEPT", "LEAST likely"** → negative detail (cross off three)
- **"infer", "imply", "suggest", "most likely", "conclude"** → inference
- **"closest in meaning to", "refers to"** → vocabulary or reference
- **"why does the author", "in order to", "function of"** → purpose
- **"mainly about", "main purpose", "best summarizes"** → main idea

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Reading questions come in six main types: detail, negative detail, inference, vocabulary, purpose and main idea.
- The first words of the question tell you the type and where to look.
- Detail and vocabulary need one sentence; main idea needs the whole text.
- An inference must be true from the text: a small, safe step, never a guess.
- Know the wrong-answer patterns: word match, true but not asked, too strong, opposite, outside knowledge.

</div>
