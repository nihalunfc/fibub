**Module 4 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will know what the Academic Discussion task asks, a five-step method to write a strong post quickly, and how to add an idea your classmates did not. You will see two model posts at different levels and practice with three timed tasks.

<div class="bigidea">Your post is one voice in a class conversation: answer the professor's question clearly, add something new, and explain it well enough that a classmate could reply.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

Write for an Academic Discussion is the third task type in the Writing section of the 2026 TOEFL iBT (about 23 minutes and about 12 items in all). You see an online discussion board for a university class. A professor asks a question about a topic from the course, such as city planning, education, the environment or media. Two students have already posted short answers, often with different views. You write your own post that takes part in the discussion.

ETS does not publish the exact time or a word limit for this task on the pages we checked; the test screen shows the time. For **practice**, this lesson uses **10 minutes**, and aims for **about 100 words or more**, which is common advice for a post that is properly developed. These are practice settings, not official rules.

You do not need special knowledge of the subject. Every question can be answered from everyday experience and common sense.

Step through a body paragraph and watch each move (Point, Explain, Example, Link) add one sentence.

<div class="steps" data-fig="@lang-peel-steps">
<div class="step"><p><strong>Point.</strong> Open with one sentence that states the idea of the paragraph.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><strong>Explain.</strong> Say why it is true or how it works, in one or two sentences.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><strong>Example.</strong> Give one specific example that proves the point.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><strong>Link.</strong> Close by tying the paragraph back to the question.</p></div>
</div>

Notice that the Point and the Link say nearly the same thing; the Explain and Example in between do the proving.

## 2. What it really tests

1. **Answering the question.** Your position on exactly what the professor asked.
2. **Development.** Reasons, explained, with a specific example or detail.
3. **Contribution.** Something that moves the discussion on, not a copy of what was said.
4. **Organization.** A clear opening position, connected ideas, a closing line.
5. **Language use.** Accurate grammar and a range of academic but natural vocabulary.

An everyday picture: imagine joining friends who are debating where to eat. If you only say "I agree with Sam", nobody learns anything. If you say "I agree with Sam, and the new place also has a student discount on weekdays", you have moved the decision forward. Your post should do the second thing.


Test your linking words: tap the job each card does. A wrong tap tells you why.

## 3. The method: five steps in about ten minutes

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Read the professor's question twice (about 1 minute).** Find the exact question: "Should cities...?", "Which is better...?", "Is this good or bad...?" Then read both student posts and note each one's main reason in two or three words.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Choose a position and a new reason.** Pick the side you can explain best, not the one you think sounds smartest. Find one reason or angle that neither student used. A middle position ("yes, but gradually") is fine if it is clear.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Plan four lines.** Position. Reason. Example or explanation. Link to a classmate or a closing line.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Write (about 6 minutes).** Open with your position in one sentence. Develop your reason with an example, a cause and effect, or a comparison. Connect to a classmate's idea (agree and add, or disagree politely). Close with one sentence that restates your view.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Check (about 1 minute).** Did you answer the exact question? Is there a new idea? Then fix verb endings, articles, plurals and spelling.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

> **Professor Huang (original):** Some universities now require every first-year student to take a course on personal finance: budgeting, loans, saving. Others think students should choose their own courses. Should a personal finance course be required? Explain your view.
>
> **Lena:** Yes. Many students take out loans without understanding interest. A required course would prevent serious mistakes.
>
> **Omar:** I think it should be optional. Students already have heavy schedules, and people can learn about money online for free.

**Step 1.** The question: should the course be required? Lena: yes, to prevent loan mistakes. Omar: no, schedules are full and free videos exist.

**Step 2.** Position: required, but short. New angle: the students who most need it are the least likely to look for free videos; and a short course does not have to add to the schedule.

**Step 3.** Plan: (1) required, short, no extra credits. (2) people who need it most do not search for it. (3) example: a cousin with credit card debt. (4) answer Omar: one-unit course fits schedules.

Now two posts written from similar plans.

<div class="model">

**Model A: a clear, basic post (likely to score in the middle range)**

I agree with Lena that the course should be required. Many students do not know about money. For example, my friend got a credit card in his first year and he spent too much money. He did not understand interest, so he had a big debt. If he took a finance course, he would not have this problem. Omar says students can learn online, but many students do not do it. In conclusion, I think the course should be required because it helps students.

</div>

**Why it works, and what holds it back.** *Answering the question:* a clear position. *Development:* there is a personal example, which is good. *Contribution:* the example mostly repeats Lena's point (loans and interest); the answer to Omar ("many students do not do it") is a new idea but is not explained. *Organization:* logical, but "In conclusion" is heavy for a short post. *Language use:* mostly accurate, with simple, repeated words ("money", "problem"); "If he took... he would not have" should be "If he had taken... he would not have had", because it is about the past. About 85 words.

<div class="model">

**Model B: a developed post that adds to the discussion (likely to score near the top)**

In my view, a personal finance course should be required, as long as it is short and does not add to students' total credit load. Omar is right that excellent free videos exist, but the students who most need them are often the least likely to go looking for them. My cousin is a good example: in her first week of college she signed up for a credit card at a campus booth, and by the summer she owed over two thousand dollars, simply because nobody had explained how interest grows. A single required unit, perhaps six online sessions, would reach students like her before they make that kind of mistake, without making anyone's schedule heavier. For these reasons, I think a short, practical course would be worth far more than the time it takes.

</div>

**Why it scores higher.** *Answering the question:* clear position with a sensible condition. *Contribution:* answers Omar with a new point (the people who need help do not search for it) and solves his schedule worry (a short unit). *Development:* a specific example with time, place and amount, linked directly to the point. *Organization:* position, response to a classmate, example, solution, closing, all connected. *Language use:* a wide range of accurate structures ("as long as", "the least likely to", "simply because nobody had explained"), natural academic tone. About 135 words.

## 5. Ways to add a new idea

When both classmates have taken the obvious reasons, try one of these angles:

| Angle | Question to ask yourself | Example (bike lanes) |
|---|---|---|
| A different group of people | Who else is affected? | Children walking to school are safer |
| Time | What happens in the short term versus the long term? | Shops may lose customers at first, then gain walkers |
| Cost | Who pays, and is it worth it? | Bike lanes cost less than widening roads |
| A condition | When would the answer change? | Yes, but only on streets with other bus options |
| A solution | Can you fix the other side's worry? | Delivery hours early in the morning |
| Personal experience | What happened to you or someone you know? | My city's new lane changed how I get to work |

<div class="strategy">

**Self-check before you finish**

- [ ] My first sentence answers the professor's exact question
- [ ] I added at least one idea that neither classmate gave
- [ ] My main reason is explained with an example or detail
- [ ] I connected to a classmate (agree and add, or disagree politely)
- [ ] My post has a closing sentence
- [ ] About 100 words or more (practice target), checked for errors

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I must not use 'I' in academic writing."** That rule belongs to some formal essays, not to this task. A class discussion board is a conversation, so "I think", "In my experience" and "I agree with Lena, and I would add..." are natural. What matters is that your opinion is clear and supported, and that the tone stays respectful and neutral (no slang, no insults).

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Useful discussion phrases:

- **Position:** In my view, ... / I believe that... / I would argue that... / I partly agree with...
- **Adding to a classmate:** Building on Lena's point, I would add that... / Omar raises a good point, and...
- **Polite disagreement:** I see why Theo feels this way; however, ... / While Mia is right that..., I think...
- **Explaining:** This is because... / As a result, ... / This means that...
- **Examples:** For example, ... / A good example is... / In my experience, ...
- **Closing:** For these reasons, ... / Overall, I think... / That is why I believe...

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- You read a professor's question and two students' posts, then write your own post; the test screen shows the time.
- Practice with 10 minutes and about 100 words or more (practice settings, common advice).
- Method: read the question twice, choose a position and a new reason, plan four lines, write, check.
- Add something new: a different group, time, cost, condition, solution or personal experience.
- One or two reasons explained with a specific example beat a long list; "I" and classmates' names are fine.
