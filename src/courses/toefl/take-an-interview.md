**Module 5 · about 20 minutes.** By the end you will know how Take an Interview works, what makes an answer strong, and how to give a clear view with a reason and an example every time, with four practice interviews and model answers at two levels.

<div class="bigidea">Every interview answer is a tiny argument: say what you think, say why, prove it with something real, and finish cleanly.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

Take an Interview is the second Speaking task. An **interviewer asks you a series of questions on one topic**, and you answer each one by speaking. The topic might be study habits, your city, technology, work or free time. The questions usually move from easy and personal ("Where do you usually study?") to more thoughtful ("Should universities require attendance?").

ETS does not publish the exact preparation and speaking time for each question on the pages we checked. The screen shows you when to speak and how long you have. In this lesson we practise with **5 to 15 seconds to prepare and 45 seconds to speak** per question. These are fibub's practice times, chosen to build a good habit, not official numbers.

The whole Speaking section is about 8 minutes, so each answer is short. That is why structure matters: you have no time to wander.

## 2. What it really tests

Three things, which match the way speaking has long been described in TOEFL scoring (see [How TOEFL speaking is scored](/learn/courses/toefl/speaking-scoring/)):

1. **Topic development.** Do you answer the exact question, with a reason and a specific example, in a connected way?
2. **Language use.** Can you use a range of grammar and vocabulary accurately: past tenses for experiences, "would" for imagined situations, linking words for reasons and contrast?
3. **Delivery.** Are you easy to understand, at a steady pace, with clear stress and sensible pauses?

Everyday analogy: a job interview. "Why do you want this job?" is not answered with "Jobs are important." The interviewer wants your view, your reason and a real story that proves it.

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Catch the question type.** Is it about you (experience, habit, preference) or an opinion on an issue? Does it have two parts ("where... and why")? Hold every part in mind.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Choose fast.** In the preparation time, pick a side and one example. If you have no strong view, pick the side with the easiest example.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Answer in your first sentence.** Give your view in your own words, ideally with the main reason: "I'd say a big city is better, mainly for the job opportunities."

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Support it.** One reason, then one specific example: a real person, a place, a time, a number, what happened.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Close cleanly.** Return to your view in one short sentence. If time remains, add a detail before closing, or admit the other side briefly ("Of course..., but...").

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

**Interviewer:** "Some universities now record every lecture and put it online. Do you think students should still be required to attend class in person?"

**The thinking (in about 10 seconds).** Type: opinion on an issue. Side: yes, mostly required, because you can ask questions at once. Example: last term in calculus, I asked about one step and understood it in seconds. Close: recordings for review, class for learning.

**The answer.** "I think attendance should still be required for most classes. The main reason is that in a live class you can ask a question the moment you're confused. For example, last term in calculus I didn't understand one step in a proof, so I put my hand up, and the professor explained it in about thirty seconds. With a video, I would have been stuck for days. Of course, recordings are great for review before an exam, but they work best alongside class, not instead of it."

That is six sentences, about 40 seconds at a calm pace: view, reason, example with detail, concession, close.

## 5. Model answers at two levels

Interview topic: **study habits**. Two questions, each answered at two levels. Read them aloud to feel the difference.

**Question A: "Where do you usually study, and why do you like that place?"**

<div class="model">

**Model 1: a basic answer (likely to score in the middle range)**

I usually study in my room. I like it because it is quiet. My room is small but it is comfortable. I have a desk and a lamp. Sometimes my brother is noisy, but usually it is okay. So I like to study in my room.

</div>

**Why it scores as it does.** *Topic development:* both parts are answered, but the reason ("quiet") is not developed, and the noisy brother actually weakens it. *Language use:* accurate but very simple: short sentences, repeated "I like", "my room". *Delivery:* likely clear, but the short, similar sentences can sound flat.

<div class="model">

**Model 2: a developed answer (likely to score near the top)**

Most days I study at a small café near my apartment. It might sound strange, but the background noise actually helps me focus. At home I keep picking up my phone or wandering into the kitchen, whereas at the café I put my phone in my bag, order one coffee and give myself a deadline: I can't leave until I've finished a chapter. Last week I got through an entire unit of biology that way in one afternoon. So for me, a little noise and a little pressure work much better than total silence.

</div>

**Why it scores higher.** *Topic development:* where and why are both clear; the reason is explained by a contrast (home versus café) and a specific result (a whole unit in one afternoon). *Language use:* a range of structures ("whereas", "I can't leave until I've finished", "It might sound strange, but") and precise words ("wandering", "deadline", "pressure"). *Delivery:* natural thought groups and a clear final point.

**Question B: "Is it better to study alone or with other people?"**

<div class="model">

**Model 1: a basic answer (likely to score in the middle range)**

I think studying with other people is better. Because you can learn from your friends. For example, when I don't understand, my friend can explain. And it is more fun. Studying alone is boring. So I prefer to study with people.

</div>

**Why it scores as it does.** *Topic development:* a position and reasons, but the example is general ("when I don't understand") with no real event. *Language use:* "Because you can learn..." is a sentence fragment; vocabulary is basic ("fun", "boring"). *Delivery:* fine if clearly said, but there is little to carry the listener along.

<div class="model">

**Model 2: a developed answer (likely to score near the top)**

It depends on the stage, but overall I'd say working with others is more valuable. Reading and memorising are easier alone, but checking your understanding really needs other people. For instance, before my chemistry final, four of us met in the library every Thursday and took turns explaining one topic each. I thought I understood reaction rates until I had to explain them, and then I realised I had a gap. My friends caught it in five minutes. So I'd recommend doing the first reading alone and then testing yourself in a group.

</div>

**Why it scores higher.** *Topic development:* a clear (and thoughtful) position, a reason, and a specific example that proves exactly the reason given (explaining exposes gaps). *Language use:* accurate past tenses, "I thought... until...", "took turns", "realised I had a gap". *Delivery:* the connectors ("For instance", "then", "So") mark the thought groups for the listener.

## 6. Question types and traps

| Question type | Example | Trap to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Personal habit | What do you do to relax? | Listing five activities with no detail |
| Two-part | Where do you study, and why? | Forgetting the second part |
| Preference | Alone or in a group? | "Both are good" with no choice |
| Past experience | Tell me about a skill you learned. | Wrong tense: "When I was child I learn..." |
| Opinion on an issue | Should attendance be required? | "Some people think..." and never giving your view |
| Imagined situation | If your school had extra money...? | "If I will have..." instead of "If I had..." |
| Effect on you | How would a car-free street affect people like you? | Drifting into global facts instead of your life |

<div class="strategy">

**Self-check after each practice answer**

- [ ] My first sentence answered the exact question (every part)
- [ ] I gave a reason, not just a repeat of my view
- [ ] My example had a who, when, where or a number
- [ ] I used at least one linking word for reason, example or contrast
- [ ] My tenses matched the question (past for experiences, "would" for imagined)
- [ ] I finished with a clear closing sentence, not mid-thought

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I should prepare a perfect answer about myself and use it for every question."** Memorised answers usually miss the exact question, and they sound recited: flat rhythm, no natural pauses. Prepare flexible pieces instead: two or three true stories from your life (a skill you learned, a challenge at school, a place you love) that you can bend to fit many questions, plus the phrases below.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- Giving your view: *I'd say..., In my view..., Personally, I think..., I'm in favour of..., I'm not convinced that...*
- Buying a second: *That's an interesting question. I'd say..., Let me think... I'd probably go with...*
- Reasons: *The main reason is that..., This is mainly because..., On top of that...*
- Examples: *For instance..., For example, last year..., A good example is when...*
- Contrast and concession: *Of course..., but..., Although..., That said..., On the other hand...*
- Imagined situations: *If I had the choice, I would..., I'd probably...*
- Closing: *So overall..., That's why I think..., So for me...*
- Recovering: *What I mean is..., Let me put it another way...*

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- An interviewer asks several questions on one topic; you answer each by speaking.
- Answer the exact question in your first sentence, including every part.
- Support with one reason and one specific example (who, when, where, a number).
- Match tenses to the question: past for experiences, "would" for imagined situations.
- Close with a short return to your view; never stop mid-thought.
- Prepare flexible true stories and phrases, not memorised speeches.

</div>
