**Module 4 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will know what the Build a Sentence task looks like, why word order in English follows a small number of firm rules, and a five-step method for putting any set of word chips in the right order. Then you will practice on more than seventy original items.

<div class="bigidea">English word order is a fixed frame: subject, then verb, then the rest in chunks. Find the frame first and the other words fall into place.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

Build a Sentence is the first task in the Writing section of the 2026 TOEFL iBT. The whole Writing section takes about 23 minutes and has about 12 items, made of three task types: Build a Sentence, Write an Email and Write for an Academic Discussion. ETS does not publish how many items of each type you get or how long each one takes, so watch the timer on the test screen.

On the screen you see a short exchange, like two students talking on campus. The first person says something. The second person's reply is not finished: you see some words as movable chips, and you put them in the right order to complete the reply. The exchanges are about everyday campus and study life: a lost book, a class schedule, a group project.

Here is an original example in the same style:

> **A:** Why are you leaving so early?
> **B:** [ my / starts / class / at / eight ]

The correct reply is "My class starts at eight." In our drills, every word is a chip and you tap them in order. Capital letters and full stops are left out, so the first word does not give itself away.

## 2. What it really tests

Build a Sentence looks like a puzzle, but it tests whether you control the grammar of English sentences:

1. **The basic frame.** Subject, verb, object, then place and time.
2. **Helper verbs.** Where "have", "is", "can" and "will" go, and what form the main verb takes after them.
3. **Questions inside sentences.** "Do you know where the lab is?" (not "where is the lab").
4. **Small words.** Adverbs (usually, never, already), prepositions and linking words.
5. **Meaning.** The reply has to answer the first speaker in a natural way.

An everyday picture: building a sentence is like packing a car boot. Big, fixed things go in first (the subject and verb), then the bags that fit around them (objects, places, times). If you start with the small bags, nothing fits.

The good news is that English word order is much stricter than in many languages. A small set of rules covers almost every item, and you can practice them until they are automatic.

## 3. The method: five steps per item

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Read what the first speaker said.** It tells you what kind of reply you need: an answer, a reason, a time, a polite refusal. "Why...?" usually means a reason ("because", "so"). "When...?" means a time.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Find the subject and the main verb.** Look for a pronoun or noun (I, she, the library) and a verb that agrees with it (am, went, has posted). Put them together first: that is the backbone.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Build the verb group.** Put helper verbs in order: modal, then have, then be, then the main verb ("should have been finished"). Put "not", "never", "already" or "usually" after the first helper.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Add the chunks.** Objects come right after the verb ("put it"), then place ("on your desk"), then time ("this morning"). Keep chunks together: "on your desk", "the whole class", "more interesting than I expected".

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Read it as a reply.** Say the whole sentence in your head as an answer to the first speaker. Is every chip used once? Does it sound natural? If not, look again at step 3 or 4.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

> **A:** Has the schedule for finals been posted?
> **B:** [ was / it / on / posted / yesterday / the / website ]

**Step 1.** A asks a yes/no question about the schedule. B will give news about it.

**Step 2.** Subject: "it" (the schedule). Verbs: "was" and "posted". Together: "it was posted", a passive, because the schedule did not post itself.

**Step 3.** The verb group is "was posted". There is no "not" or adverb to place.

**Step 4.** The place chunk is "on the website" (preposition + the + noun stay together). The time is "yesterday". Place before time: "on the website yesterday". Time at the start also works: "Yesterday it was posted on the website".

**Step 5.** "It was posted on the website yesterday." That answers A's question naturally, and every chip is used.

Notice what you did not do: you did not try orders at random. You found the frame first, and the frame left only one or two possible places for each remaining word.

## 5. The rules that decide most items

| Rule | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Subject before verb in statements | **She said** I was late | She said was I late |
| Adverb after the first helper | I **have never** been there | I have been never there |
| Frequency adverb before the main verb | We **usually meet** on Tuesdays | We meet usually on Tuesdays |
| Verb and object stay together | He speaks **English** very well | He speaks very well English |
| Place, then time | I put it **on your desk this morning** | I put it this morning on your desk (possible but less usual) |
| Relative clause after its noun | The book **that you lent me** was great | The book was that you lent me great |
| too / enough | **too late** to call; **easy enough** | late too; enough easy |
| Modal + have + past participle | someone **must have jammed** it | must jammed have |
| Passive | it **is being renovated** | it being is renovated |
| had better + base verb | you **had better finish** | you had better to finish |

## 6. Common traps

**The helpful-looking extra.** Some chips look like they start the sentence, such as "because" or "if". "Because" and "if" clauses can come first or second, so do not decide on them until the main clause is built.

**Two good orders.** Sometimes a time or place phrase can go at the start or the end ("Before seven, it is usually empty" and "It is usually empty before seven"). Both are correct English. In our drills, we accept every natural order we know of.

**Meaning traps.** "I used to drive but now I cycle" and "I used to cycle but now I drive" use the same chips. Both are grammatical, so let the first speaker decide which makes sense.

**Chunk splitting.** Words that belong together, like "the whole class" or "more ... than", get separated when you place chips one by one. Build small chunks in your head first.

<div class="strategy">

**Fast and safe.** Spend your first few seconds finding the subject-verb backbone. Then add one chunk at a time, reading from the start each time. If you are stuck, move on and come back: an adaptive test rewards accuracy, so one careful answer beats two rushed ones. Never leave an item unanswered; an order that keeps the subject-verb frame is often right.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Word order is just about feeling; you either hear it or you do not."** Not true. Native speakers do hear it, but learners can get the same result from a handful of rules: subject plus verb, helper verbs in order, adverb after the first helper, verb and object together, place then time. Learn the table above, practice with the drills, and the "feeling" grows from the rules.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Signals in the chips and what they usually mean:

- *have / has / had* + *-ed or 3rd form* → perfect tense ("have lived", "had left")
- *is / was / been* + *-ed* → passive ("was posted", "has been accepted")
- *never / already / always / usually* → after the first helper, or before a one-word verb
- *than* → a comparison chunk: "more ... than", "better than", "quieter than"
- *that / who / which* → a relative clause that follows a noun
- *too* + adjective + *to* → "too late to call"; adjective + *enough* → "easy enough"
- *if / because / although / when* → a second clause; build the main clause first

</div>

## 7. Practice

Start with the drill below. Each round gives you a mix of exchanges; tap the chips in order, then check. Read the solution even when you are right: it names the rule, and that is what you need on test day.
