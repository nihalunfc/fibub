**Module 4 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will be able to build direct questions, turn them into polite indirect questions, and spot the inversion and tag traps that catch many test takers. Then you will practice on more than eighty original items.

<div class="bigidea">A direct question swaps the subject and the helper verb ("is it?"). The moment the question goes inside another sentence ("Do you know where...?"), it swaps back to statement order.</div>

## 1. Why this gets its own lesson

In Build a Sentence (lesson 4.1), the reply you build is often a question, or a sentence with a question inside it. Campus life is full of these: "Do you know when the library closes?", "I wonder why the trip was canceled", "Could you tell me which room we booked?" They are also where learners lose the most points, because the rules are different from many other languages and from direct questions in English itself.

The good news: there are only five patterns to learn. Once you know them, these items become some of the fastest on the test.

## 2. Pattern 1: direct questions

A direct question uses this frame:

**(question word) + helper verb + subject + main verb + rest**

- What time **does the session start**?
- How long **have you been** waiting?
- Which chapter **should we** read first?

If the statement has no helper verb, add "do", "does" or "did", and the main verb goes back to its base form: "The bus runs on weekends" → "How often **does** the bus **run** on weekends?"

An everyday picture: the helper verb is like a door you have to open before you walk in. In a question it comes first; the subject walks through after it.

## 3. Pattern 2: subject questions (no "do")

When "who", "what" or "which" is the **subject**, you do not need "do / did". The verb follows directly, like a statement:

- **Who told** you about the change? (someone told you)
- **What happened** at the meeting?
- **Which bus goes** downtown?

Compare an object question, where the question word is the thing the action happens to:

- **Who did you call** last night? (you called someone)

Ask yourself: is the question word doing the action? If yes, no "do".

## 4. Pattern 3: indirect questions

An indirect question sits inside a polite frame or a statement:

| Frame | Example |
|---|---|
| Could you tell me... | Could you tell me **where the registrar's office is**? |
| Do you know... | Do you know **when the next bus leaves**? |
| I wonder... | I wonder **why he left**. |
| I have no idea... | I have no idea **how much it costs**. |
| Can you remind me... | Can you remind me **which room we booked**? |

After the question word, use **statement order** (subject, then verb), and drop "do / does / did":

- Where **is the office**? → ...where **the office is**
- When **does the bus leave**? → ...when **the bus leaves**
- Why **did he leave**? → ...why **he left**

For yes/no questions, use **if** or **whether**:

- **Is it open** late? → Do you know **if it is open** late?
- **Did everyone pass**? → I wonder **whether everyone passed**.

Notice the punctuation: "Could you tell me..." and "Do you know..." are questions (question mark). "I wonder..." and "I have no idea..." are statements (full stop).

## 5. Pattern 4: reported questions

When you report what someone asked, use the same statement order, and move tenses back:

- "Where is the lab?" → She asked **where the lab was**.
- "Have you finished?" → She asked me **whether I had finished**.

## 6. Pattern 5: tags and inversion

**Question tags** check something you think is true. Positive sentence, negative tag, with the same helper verb:

- The lab closes at six, **doesn't it**?
- You were at the talk, **weren't you**?

**Inversion** happens after a negative or limiting word at the start of a sentence. It is more formal, and it uses question order even though it is a statement:

- **Rarely have I** seen such a full hall.
- **Not only did she** finish early, but she also helped me.

## 7. The method for any question item

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Is there a frame?** Look for "could you tell me", "do you know", "I wonder", "I am not sure", "asked". If yes, the rest is an indirect question: statement order.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Find the question word.** Where, when, why, how long, which room, whether, if. Put it right after the frame (or first, in a direct question).

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Choose the order after it.** Direct question: helper verb, then subject. Indirect question: subject, then verb. Subject question: verb straight away.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Check the helper verbs.** Any "do / does / did" left inside an indirect question? Remove it in your head (the chips will not include it). Is the main verb in the right form?

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Read it aloud in your head.** "Do you know when the next bus leaves?" If it sounds like two questions joined together ("do you know when does..."), something is in the wrong place.

</div>
</div>

## 8. Worked example

> **A:** The museum is free for students.
> **B:** [ remind / we / can / show / you / what / need / me / to ]

**Step 1.** "can you remind me" is a frame, so an indirect question follows.

**Step 2.** The question word is "what".

**Step 3.** Indirect question: subject + verb after "what": "what we need to show".

**Step 4.** No "do" is left. The hidden direct question was "What do we need to show?"; the "do" disappeared.

**Step 5.** "Can you remind me what we need to show?" Natural, and every chip is used.

<div class="strategy">

**Spot the frame first.** In any set of chips, look for "you know", "tell me", "wonder", "sure", "asked". If you see one, decide right away: statement order inside. This one habit fixes most question items. Then check that there is exactly one helper verb before the subject in direct questions, and none in front of the subject in indirect ones.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"A question always has the verb before the subject."** Only a direct question does. Once the question sits inside another sentence ("I wonder...", "Could you tell me..."), it returns to statement order. And when "who" or "what" is the subject, even a direct question keeps statement order: "Who told you?" Many strong speakers still make the "Do you know where is it?" mistake in speech, but in writing, and in Build a Sentence, it counts as wrong.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Polite frames that take statement order:

- *Could you tell me* + where / when / how / if ...?
- *Do you know* + what time / which / whether ...?
- *Can you remind me* + what / why ...?
- *I wonder* + why / whether ... .
- *I am not sure* + what time / how ... .
- *I have no idea* + how much / where ... .
- *She asked (me)* + if / whether / where ... .

</div>

## 9. Practice

The drill mixes indirect questions, direct questions and quick "which is correct" checks. Each indirect question tells you what the hidden direct question was, so you can see what changed.

When you speak English this week, try to use one polite indirect question a day: "Do you know if...", "Could you tell me where...". Using the pattern out loud makes it automatic when you see the chips.

<div class="keypoints">

- Direct question: question word + helper verb + subject + main verb.
- Subject questions ("Who told you?") have no "do / did".
- Indirect questions use statement order and drop "do / does / did": "Do you know when the bus leaves?"
- Yes/no questions become "if" or "whether" + statement order.
- Tags repeat the helper verb with the opposite sign; negative words at the start ("Rarely", "Not only") take question order.

</div>
