**Module 4 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will understand, in plain words, what makes a TOEFL writing answer stronger or weaker, how the three task types differ in the way they are marked, and how to score your own practice answers so you know exactly what to fix.

<div class="bigidea">Raters ask four questions about your email or post: did you do the task, did you develop your ideas, is it easy to follow, and is the English accurate and varied? Fix them in that order.</div>

## 1. Your Writing score in the 2026 test

The Writing section gives you a band from **1 to 6**, like the other three sections, and your four section bands are averaged into your overall band (lesson 1.3 explains this). Writing is about 23 minutes and about 12 items, made of three task types.

The three task types are not marked the same way:

| Task | How it is marked, in plain terms |
|---|---|
| Build a Sentence | Each item has a correct answer: the words are in a correct order or they are not |
| Write an Email | Your email is rated on quality, using ETS's scoring guide for the task |
| Write for an Academic Discussion | Your post is rated on quality, using ETS's scoring guide for the task |

ETS has long used a combination of trained human raters and automated scoring for TOEFL writing, and it publishes **scoring guides** (sometimes called rubrics) and sample materials on its TOEFL pages at ets.org. Read them there: they are the official description, and this lesson does not reproduce them. What follows is fibub's plain-language summary, made to help you practice and check your own work.

## 2. The four areas, in plain words

For the email and discussion tasks, it helps to think about four areas. They overlap, but each one points to a different kind of fix.

**1. Task completion: did you do what was asked?**
Every point in the email task covered. In the discussion, the professor's exact question answered, with a contribution of your own. A suitable tone for the reader. An everyday picture: a recipe with one missing ingredient. However well you cook, the dish is not what was ordered.

**2. Development: did you take your ideas far enough?**
Reasons that are explained, not just named. Specific details: a date, a number, a short example from real life. An everyday picture: a photo versus a sketch. "Bike lanes are good" is a sketch. "When my city added a lane on Main Street, I started cycling and saved forty minutes a day" is a photo.

**3. Organization: is it easy to follow?**
A clear start (purpose or position), ideas in a logical order, linking words that show how ideas connect (because, however, as a result), a closing. An everyday picture: road signs. The reader should never wonder where they are or where they are going.

**4. Language use: is the English accurate and varied?**
Correct grammar, especially the frequent things (verb endings, tenses, articles, plurals). A range of words and sentence types. Errors matter more when they make the meaning unclear. An everyday picture: a clean window. Small spots are fine; a crack across the middle stops people seeing through it.

## 3. What a stronger and a weaker answer look like

Here are short original extracts from two discussion posts on the same question ("Should cities replace car lanes with bike lanes?").

<div class="model">

**Weaker extract**

Bike lanes is good for city. Many people use bike. Also it is good for health and environment and less cars. I agree with Priya because she is right. Bike lanes are good.

</div>

*Task completion:* a position, but the post just echoes Priya. *Development:* reasons are named (health, environment) but none is explained. *Organization:* ideas are listed with no logic; the last line repeats the first. *Language use:* agreement errors ("bike lanes is"), missing articles ("for city"), "less cars" instead of "fewer cars", repeated words.

<div class="model">

**Stronger extract**

I support bike lanes, but I think cities should add them gradually, one street at a time. That way, shop owners like the ones Ben mentions can see whether they actually lose customers. In my neighborhood, a café owner was worried at first, but after a new lane opened, she told me her lunchtime sales went up because more cyclists stopped by.

</div>

*Task completion:* a clear position that answers the question and responds to a classmate. *Development:* a specific example that supports the point. *Organization:* position, reason, example, connected with "That way" and "but". *Language use:* accurate, with varied structures ("whether they actually lose", "after a new lane opened").

The stronger extract is not longer because of difficult words. It is stronger because it **does more**: a new idea, a real example, a clear link.

## 4. How to score your own practice answer

You cannot get an official score at home, but you can find your weakest area. After each timed task, wait ten minutes, then read your answer as a stranger would.

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Task check.** List the points the task asked for. Put a tick next to each one your answer covers. Any missing point is your first fix.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Development check.** Underline every reason. Does each one have a "because", a detail or an example after it? Mark any reason that stands alone.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Organization check.** Can you write the job of each paragraph or sentence group in two words ("problem", "request", "my example")? Circle your linking words. Are there any jumps?

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Language check.** Read once only for verbs (ending, tense), once for articles and plurals. Count the errors. Then count how many times you used your most common word ("good", "very", "thing").

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Choose one fix.** Pick the weakest area and rewrite only that part. One focused fix per task improves faster than trying to fix everything.

</div>
</div>

<div class="strategy">

**Self-scoring checklist (fibub's practice list, not ETS's guide)**

- [ ] Task: every point covered, the exact question answered, tone fits the reader
- [ ] Development: each reason explained with a detail or example
- [ ] Organization: clear start, logical order, linking words, closing
- [ ] Language: verb endings, tenses, articles and plurals checked; no word used more than three times
- [ ] Discussion only: at least one idea that neither classmate gave

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Raters count big words, so I should use as many advanced words as possible."** Rare words used wrongly make your writing less clear, and that costs you. What helps is **precise** words used correctly ("a $35 charge appeared on my account" rather than "a bad thing happened"). Choose the clearest word you are sure of, then add range with sentence structures you control, such as "If I had known..." or "which meant that...".

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Quick upgrades that add range without risk:

- *good* → helpful, effective, worthwhile, reliable
- *bad* → harmful, inconvenient, costly, unfair
- *a lot of* → many, a large number of, a great deal of
- *I think* → In my view, I would argue that, It seems to me that
- *so* → as a result, which means that, this is why
- *but* → however, although, while it is true that

</div>
