**Module 2 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will know how to read the short everyday texts in TOEFL Reading, from notices to emails to timetables, find the answer quickly, and avoid the traps these questions use.

<div class="bigidea">Daily-life texts are short and practical. Every one exists to make the reader do or know something, so ask "who wrote this, for whom, and what do they want?" and the questions become easy.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

Read in Daily Life is one of the three task types in the 2026 TOEFL Reading section (about 30 minutes, about 50 items, adaptive). You read a short text of the kind people meet every day, especially on a college campus, and answer a few multiple-choice questions about it. ETS does not publish exact counts per task, so the test screen tells you how many questions each text has.

The texts are things like:

- **Notices and signs**: building closures, new rules, changes to opening hours
- **Emails and messages**: from a professor, an office, a store, a classmate
- **Schedules and timetables**: buses, events, office hours
- **Advertisements**: rooms for rent, jobs, products, events
- **Online posts**: forum questions, lost-and-found posts, reviews and replies

The language is everyday English, not academic. The challenge is not difficult words. It is reading precisely: noticing a changed time, an exception, a condition, or who must do what.

## 2. What it really tests

These questions test whether you can use a text the way you would in real life. If a notice says the laundry room reopens at 3:00, can you plan your afternoon? If an email gives two choices and a deadline, do you know what happens if you do nothing?

An everyday picture: think of a text from your landlord. You do not study it like a poem. You look for what matters to you: what is happening, when, and what you need to do. Daily-life questions reward exactly that practical reading.

Typical questions ask:

- **Purpose**: Why was this written? (to announce, to remind, to request, to warn, to apologize)
- **Detail**: When, where, how much, who?
- **Condition**: What happens if...? Who can...? Who cannot?
- **Inference**: What can we conclude about...?
- **Vocabulary**: The word "provided" is closest in meaning to...

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Identify the text type and the header (5 seconds).** Is it an email, a notice, an ad, a schedule? Read the title, subject line, sender and receiver. This tells you the purpose before you read a word of the body.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Skim the body once for the main message (20 to 30 seconds).** What is changing or happening? What does the writer want? Do not stop on details yet.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Read the question and find its key word.** A time, a place, a person, a condition ("if", "only", "after").

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Scan back to the exact line and read it carefully.** Read the sentence before and after too. Most traps sit in the words right around the answer: "until", "except", "but", brackets with old information.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Match meaning, not words.** The correct option usually uses different words from the text (a paraphrase). An option that copies the text's words exactly is often a trap.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Read this original notice.

> **Science Library: Exam Season Hours**
> From April 20 to May 8, the Science Library will stay open until 2:00 a.m. on weekdays (normally midnight). Weekend hours are not changing. After 10:00 p.m., only students with a valid ID can enter, and guests must leave. The third-floor silent area will be reserved for students with exams that week.

**Question 1: What is the main purpose of the notice?**
(A) To announce longer opening hours during exams (B) To tell guests they cannot use the library (C) To close the third floor (D) To change weekend hours

Thinking: the title says "Exam Season Hours", and the first sentence gives the change. (A) is the purpose. (B) is a true detail, but only after 10:00 p.m., so it is not the main purpose. (D) is the opposite of the text: "Weekend hours are not changing."

**Question 2: A guest visits with a student at 11:00 p.m. on a Wednesday in late April. What happens?**
(A) The guest must leave (B) The guest may stay until 2:00 a.m. (C) The library is closed (D) The guest needs to sign in

Thinking: key word "11:00 p.m." Scan: "After 10:00 p.m., only students with a valid ID can enter, and guests must leave." So (A). (B) mixes up the closing time with the guest rule. (C) is the trap for people who remember "midnight", which is the normal time, not the exam-season time.

**Question 3: What can be inferred about the third-floor silent area?**
(A) Some students may not be able to use it during exam season (B) It is closed (C) It is open only on weekends (D) It is new

Thinking: "reserved for students with exams that week". So students without exams that week cannot use it: (A). The text never says it is closed or new.

## 5. Traps these questions use

| Trap | What it looks like | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| Old information | "6:30 a.m. (7:00 a.m. before)" | Brackets and words like "previously", "normally", "used to" mark the old fact |
| True but not asked | A real detail that answers a different question | Re-read the question's key word before choosing |
| Too strong | "only", "never", "all" when the text says "usually" or "preferred" | Match the strength of the text exactly |
| Same word, different meaning | The text says "fixed times" (set); an option says "repaired" | Check the word's meaning in its sentence |
| Plan that changes | "We first planned Friday, but the meeting is now on Monday" | The final arrangement is the answer |
| Default action | "If we do not hear from you, we will cancel it" | Note what happens if the reader does nothing |

<div class="strategy">

**Read like the person the text was written for.** Before each question, imagine you are the student getting this email or standing in front of this notice. Ask: what do I have to do, by when, and what happens if I do not? Most daily-life questions are some version of those three.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Daily-life texts are easy, so I can read them quickly and move on."** They are short, but they are full of small conditions: "until", "except", "only after", "if you do not reply". Test makers like these details because careless readers miss them. Read the question's target line slowly, even if you skim the rest.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Words that change the meaning in practical texts:

- **Time limits:** until, by, no later than, from ... to, starting, effective
- **Exceptions:** except, unless, other than, not including, apart from
- **Conditions:** if, only if, as long as, provided that
- **Changes:** now, no longer, instead, previously, normally, will be moved to
- **Strength:** must, may, should, preferred, required, optional, encouraged

</div>

## 6. Practice

Eight original texts with three or four questions each: notices, emails, a shuttle timetable, a room ad, a forum post and a flyer. Try to finish each text in about two minutes. Read the solutions: they show the exact words that prove each answer.

To practice outside this course, read real everyday texts in English: your bank's emails, a city's website, store return policies, transit timetables. After each one, ask yourself the three questions: what must I do, by when, and what happens if I do not?

<div class="keypoints">

- Read in Daily Life uses short practical texts: notices, emails, schedules, ads and posts.
- Read the header first: text type, sender, receiver and subject reveal the purpose.
- Skim once for the main message, then scan back to the exact line for each question.
- Watch the small words: until, except, only, if, previously, preferred.
- The right answer usually paraphrases the text; copied words are often a trap.

</div>
