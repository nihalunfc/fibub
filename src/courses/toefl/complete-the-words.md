**Module 2 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will know what the Complete the Words task looks like, what it really tests, and a five-step method for filling each gap fast and accurately. Then you will practice on more than a hundred original gaps.

<div class="bigidea">Complete the Words gives you a short academic paragraph in which some words have lost their second half. Grammar tells you what kind of word fits, meaning tells you which word, and spelling decides whether you get the point.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

Complete the Words is one of the three task types in the Reading section of the 2026 TOEFL iBT. The Reading section takes about 30 minutes and has about 50 items in all, and it is adaptive, so the test screen shows the exact time and number of questions you get. ETS does not publish how many items each task type has, so do not trust anyone who gives you an exact count.

On the screen you see a short paragraph, often on a school subject such as biology, history or geography. The first sentence is usually complete, so you can understand the topic. After that, some words are cut: you see only the first part, followed by empty spaces for the missing letters. You type the missing letters to complete each word.

Here is an original example in the same style:

> Honeybees live in large groups called colonies. Each colony has one queen, who lays all the eg__. The worker bees collect nectar and pollen fr__ flowers and bring th__ back to the hive.

In our practice, a cut word looks like **fir__** or **incr____**: the letters you can see, then one underscore for each missing letter. You can type the whole word or only the missing letters; both are accepted.

## 2. What it really tests

Complete the Words looks like a spelling game, but it tests three things at once:

1. **Grammar.** Is the gap a noun, a verb, an adjective? Singular or plural? Past or present?
2. **Vocabulary.** Do you know common academic words well enough to recognize them from half a word?
3. **Reading for meaning.** Does your word make sense in the sentence and in the paragraph?

An everyday picture: it is like reading a text message where your friend's phone cut off the end of some words. "See you at the stat___ at six" is easy, because you know the situation (a train station), the grammar (a noun after "the") and the spelling. The test works the same way, but with academic topics.

Many of the cut words are small, frequent words: "from", "their", "which", "because", "about". Others are common academic words: "increase", "important", "species", "process", "evidence". You do not need rare vocabulary. You need to know frequent words very well, including their exact spelling.

## 3. The method: five steps per gap

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Read the first sentence fully.** It is usually complete and tells you the topic. Ten seconds here saves time on every gap.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Name the slot.** Look at the words just before and after the gap. After "the" or "a" you need a noun or adjective. After "to" or "can" you need a base verb. After "very" you need an adjective or adverb. After "they" in a past story you need a past verb.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Use the letters you have.** "incr" plus four letters, in a noun slot: "increase". The number of underscores tells you how long the rest of the word is, so "fir__" has two letters missing: "first", not "firm" or "fire".

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. Check the meaning.** Read the whole sentence with your word in it. Does it make sense with the topic? "The experiment showed a clear increase in temperature" is logical; "a clear incredible" is not.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Check the ending and spelling, then move on.** Plural -s? Past -ed? Third person -s ("it grows")? Double letters? Then go to the next gap. Do not spend more than a few seconds on any single gap.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Read this original paragraph and watch the thinking for each gap.

> Glaciers are huge bodies of ice that move slowly over land. They form in pla___ where more snow falls in winter th__ melts in summer. Over many years, the snow is press__ into solid ice. Because glaciers are so heavy, th__ can carve deep valleys into the rock.

**Gap 1: "pla___"** (three letters missing). Slot: after "in", before "where": a noun, and "where" tells you it is a place. Letters: pla + 3. "places" fits the meaning and length. Check: "They form in places where more snow falls". Correct.

**Gap 2: "th__"** (two letters missing). Slot: after "more snow falls in winter", a comparison is coming: "more ... than". Letters: th + 2 = "than". Not "that" or "then": "more snow falls than melts" is the only logical sentence.

**Gap 3: "press__"** (two letters missing). Slot: after "the snow is": a passive verb needs a past participle. press + ed = "pressed". Check the length: two letters, "ed". Correct.

**Gap 4: "th__"** (two letters missing). Slot: the subject of "can carve". It refers back to "glaciers", which is plural: "they". Not "this" (wrong number) or "then" (not a subject).

Notice that gaps 2 and 4 both start with "th" and have two letters missing, yet the answers differ. Grammar is what told them apart.

## 5. Common traps

| Trap | Example | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|
| Right word, wrong form | "The plant grow__" → "growed" | Know irregular forms; here the answer is "grows" (third person -s) |
| Ignoring the letter count | "fir__" → "firm" | Count the underscores: two missing letters, so "first" |
| Wrong small word | "th__" → "that" when "than" is needed | Look for "more ... than", "rather than", "less ... than" |
| Spelling slips | "becuase", "enviroment", "goverment" | Practice the 200 most common academic words until they are automatic |
| Plural forgotten | "many species" vs "a specie" | "species" is the same in singular and plural |
| Meaning not checked | "The river carries soil to the coa__" → "coal" | Re-read: rivers carry soil to the "coast" |

<div class="strategy">

**Fast and safe.** Fill every gap on your first pass, even with a guess. If you have time at the end of the paragraph, read it once more as a whole: wrong words often "sound" wrong when you read the full sentence. Never leave a gap empty; a blank is always wrong, and a guess with the right grammar is often right.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I need to learn lots of difficult words for this task."** Not really. Most cut words are frequent words that appear on almost every page of academic writing. Knowing 2,000 common words perfectly, with their spelling and endings, helps far more than knowing 200 rare ones. Research on vocabulary learning (Nation 2013) shows that meeting the same frequent words again and again, spread over days, is what makes them stick.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Grammar clues that tell you the slot:

- *the / a / an / this / their* + ___ → noun (or adjective + noun)
- *to / can / will / must / should* + ___ → base verb (to grow, can survive)
- *is / are / was / were* + ___ed → passive (was pressed, were built)
- *very / more / most / so* + ___ → adjective or adverb (very fertile, more reliable)
- *more ... / less ... / -er* + ___ → "than"
- *it / he / she / the plant* + ___ → verb with -s in the present (it grows)

</div>

## 6. How to practice

Short daily practice beats one long session. Ten minutes a day of these drills, plus reading one short academic article (science news, an encyclopedia entry), builds the habit of predicting words. When you read normally, try covering the end of a word with your finger and guessing it before you look. That is exactly the skill the task rewards.

Start with single sentences. Each one has one cut word; the hint in the solution tells you the grammar slot.

Now try whole paragraphs, the way the test shows them. Read the paragraph first, then complete each numbered gap.

If you keep missing the same kind of word (endings like -tion, -ment, -ly, or spellings with double letters), go straight to lesson 2.2 on word building.
