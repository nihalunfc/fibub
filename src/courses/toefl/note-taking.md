**Module 3 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will have a simple note-taking system for TOEFL Listening: what to write, what to skip, a set of symbols, and four layouts that match the way talks and conversations are built.

<div class="bigidea">Notes are hooks for your memory, not a copy of the talk: a few key words, every number with its label, and every change, laid out to match the talk's structure.</div>

## 1. Why notes matter on the TOEFL

You are allowed to take notes during the TOEFL, at a test center and in the Home Edition (check ets.org for the current rules on what you may write on). Each recording plays once, so you cannot go back to check a detail. For a short choose-a-response sentence you need no notes. For conversations, announcements and talks, a few good notes can save several points: especially on numbers, lists and changed plans.

But bad notes cost points too. If you try to write everything, you stop listening, and you miss the next idea. The goal of this lesson is notes that take **almost no attention** and still catch what questions ask.

Everyday analogy: notes are like the photos you take on a trip. You do not film every second. You snap the moments you want to remember, and later each photo brings back the whole scene.

## 2. What to write and what to skip

| Write | Skip |
|---|---|
| The topic in 3-5 words | Greetings and small talk |
| Key terms and a short meaning | Full sentences |
| Every number with its label | Words you already know will be repeated |
| Changes: OLD → NEW | Examples in detail (just the name + what it shows) |
| List items and their count | Spelling: abbreviations are fine |
| The final decision or takeaway | Things you cannot catch: leave a gap and keep listening |

## 3. Symbols that save time

Use a small set and practise them until they are automatic. Do not invent new ones on test day.

| Symbol | Meaning | Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| → | leads to, changes to, so | ✗ | not, wrong, rejected |
| ↑ / ↓ | increase / decrease | ✓ | correct, agreed, chosen |
| = | means, is | ? | unsure, question |
| ≠ | different from | ex | example |
| b/c | because | vs | compared with |
| w/ | with | P / S | problem / solution |

Shorten words by keeping the start and the main consonants: *experiment* → *expt*, *students* → *stds*, *professor* → *prof*, *temperature* → *temp*.

## 4. The method: four layouts

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**1. Top line: who and what.** Before the audio, write the speakers or the class from the title line: "S + librarian" or "Biology". When the topic comes, add it in a few words: "night-singing robins".

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Choose a layout in the first twenty seconds.** Steps → numbered list (1, 2, 3). Comparison → two columns. Problem → P / causes / S lines. Conversation → P / O / D (problem, options, decision).

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Write key words, not sentences.** Three or four words per idea. Nouns, numbers and verbs carry the meaning; skip *the, is, very*.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Label every number and mark every change.** "$1 food ticket", "2-3 wks sun-dry". "Tue → Thu". These two habits prevent most wrong answers.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. After the audio, look before you answer.** Spend two seconds scanning your notes for the part each question asks about. Your notes are in the same order as the talk, so the question order usually follows them too.

</div>
</div>

## 5. Worked example

Imagine this part of a talk:

> "Let me compare two ways to store energy. The first is batteries. They respond in less than a second and can be built almost anywhere. But they are expensive, and usually last ten to fifteen years. The second is pumped water storage. Water is pumped up to a lake on a hill and flows down through turbines when power is needed. It can last fifty years or more, but you need the right land, with two lakes at different heights."

Signal: "Let me compare two" → two columns.

| Batteries | Pumped water |
|---|---|
| fast (<1 s) | pump up hill → down turbines |
| anywhere | 50+ yrs |
| $$$ | needs land, 2 lakes ≠ height |
| 10-15 yrs | |

**Q: How long do batteries usually last?** Look across: **10-15 years.** The trap, "fifty years or more", sits in the other column, and your layout keeps it there.

**Q: What is the main limit of pumped water storage?** Last line of the second column: **it needs land with two lakes at different heights.**

That took about twenty-five words of notes. A transcript would have taken eighty, and you would have missed the end.

## 6. Common note-taking mistakes

- **Writing too much.** If your pen is behind the speaker, you are writing sentences. Drop to key words.
- **Numbers without labels.** "15" means nothing later. Was it minutes, dollars or years?
- **Only noting the first plan.** Always add the arrow when a plan changes.
- **Copying examples in full.** Write "ex: honey" and an arrow to the idea it explains.
- **Stopping when you miss something.** Leave a gap, write "?", and catch the next idea.

<div class="strategy">

**Practise with a timer, then shrink.** Take notes on a two-minute talk. Count your words. Next time, aim for fewer words with the same answers right. Most strong test takers end up with 20-40 words for a short talk. Also practise reading your notes back: if you cannot explain the talk from them a minute later, change what you write, not how much.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Good listeners don't need notes."** For a short conversation that may be true. But even strong listeners forget which number went with which item a minute later, especially under test pressure. Light notes cost little attention and protect exactly the details that wrong options are built from.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- Change the layout to a list: *first, there are three..., the steps are...*
- Change it to two columns: *let me compare, the first... the second..., whereas*
- Change it to P / C / S: *the problem is, causes, so what can we do*
- Add a number label: *costs, lasts, takes, about, up to, per*
- Add an arrow: *instead, now, moved to, actually, but*

</div>

## 7. Practice

Six original recordings full of steps, lists, numbers and comparisons. Take notes with the layouts above, then answer from your notes. Try the drill once with notes and once without, and compare your scores.

<div class="keypoints">

- Notes are memory hooks: topic, key terms, labelled numbers and changes.
- Pick a layout early: list for steps, two columns for comparisons, P / C / S for problems, P / O / D for conversations.
- Write key words, not sentences; use a small set of symbols.
- Always label numbers and mark OLD → NEW changes.
- If you miss something, leave a gap and keep listening.
