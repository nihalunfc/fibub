**Module 2 · about 17 minutes.** By the end you will know how to read a short textbook-style passage quickly, build a mental map of it, and use that map to answer questions with evidence from the text.

<div class="bigidea">An academic passage is an argument in small steps. Find the topic, then what each paragraph does (explain, give an example, contrast, show a problem), and every question becomes a short trip back to the right place.</div>

## 1. What the task looks like

Read an Academic Passage is the third task type in the 2026 TOEFL Reading section (about 30 minutes, about 50 items in all, adaptive). You read a short passage like a page from an introductory textbook, on topics such as biology, history, geology, psychology, economics or art. Then you answer multiple-choice questions about it. ETS does not publish the exact length or number of questions per passage, and the test screen shows what you get.

You do not need any knowledge of the subject. Everything you need is in the passage, and questions that seem to need outside knowledge are really testing whether you stick to the text.

## 2. What it really tests

Academic reading questions test four skills:

- **Finding details**: can you locate a stated fact?
- **Inferring**: can you see what must be true even if it is not written?
- **Understanding vocabulary in context**: what does this word mean here?
- **Following the author's purpose and structure**: why is this example here? What does this paragraph do?

An everyday picture: reading an academic passage is like following a friend's directions across town. You do not need to memorize every street name, but you must know the route: "go north, then turn at the park, then look for the blue building". Once you know the route (the structure), you can find any street (any detail) quickly.

Lesson 2.5 goes deeper into each question type. This lesson is about reading the passage itself.

## 3. How academic passages are built

Most short academic passages follow one of a few patterns. Spotting the pattern in the first few lines tells you where answers will be.

| Pattern | How it moves | Signal words |
|---|---|---|
| Problem and solution | A problem, then ways it was solved | problem, challenge, to solve this, one answer |
| Cause and effect | Something happened, then its results | because, as a result, led to, therefore |
| Comparison and contrast | Two things, how they are alike and different | similarly, unlike, whereas, however |
| Claim and evidence | An idea, then studies or examples that support it | research shows, for example, experiments suggest |
| Change over time | Before, then a change, then after | before, by the 1800s, within decades, today |
| Idea and limitation | Benefits, then problems or exceptions | however, critics, not perfect, though |

## 4. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Step 1. Read the title and the first paragraph (about 30 seconds).** These give the topic and often the main idea. Ask: what question is this passage answering?

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 2. Read the first sentence of every other paragraph.** It usually says what that paragraph does. Make a quick mental map: "P1 = what countershading is. P2 = it works in water too, plus other uses."

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 3. Read the rest at normal speed, without stopping on unknown words.** Guess from context and keep going. You will come back only to the parts a question asks about.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 4. For each question, use your map to go to the right paragraph.** Find the exact sentence and read one sentence before and after it.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Step 5. Choose the option the text proves.** Eliminate options that are too strong, off topic, true but not asked, or the opposite of the text. If you cannot point to the words that prove it, it is probably wrong.

</div>
</div>

## 5. Worked example

Read this original passage.

> **Tea and Trade**
> In the eighteenth century, tea became one of the most valuable goods shipped from China to Europe. Because Chinese merchants accepted mainly silver in payment, European traders had to send large amounts of silver east each year.
> This imbalance worried governments, which feared that their supplies of silver would run low. One response was to try growing tea elsewhere. In the nineteenth century, plantations were established in India and Sri Lanka, where the climate in the hills suited the plant. Within a few decades, these regions were producing more tea for Europe than China was.

**Map:** P1 = tea was valuable, and Europe paid in silver. P2 = the problem this caused, and the solution (growing tea elsewhere). Pattern: problem and solution, plus change over time.

**Detail question: Why did European traders send silver to China?**
Go to P1: "Because Chinese merchants accepted mainly silver in payment". The answer is that Chinese merchants wanted silver as payment. A trap option might say "because silver was cheap in Europe": it sounds reasonable but the text never says it.

**Vocabulary question: The word "imbalance" is closest in meaning to... (A) unequal flow (B) injury (C) weight (D) argument.**
Use word building (lesson 2.2): im (not) + balance. In context, silver kept flowing one way: (A).

**Inference question: What can be inferred about China's share of tea sold to Europe by the late nineteenth century?**
P2's last sentence: India and Sri Lanka "were producing more tea for Europe than China was". So China's share fell. The text does not say China stopped selling tea, so an option like "China sold no tea to Europe" is too strong.

**Purpose question: Why does the author mention the climate in the hills?**
It explains why India and Sri Lanka could grow tea, so it supports the solution. Purpose questions ask what job a detail does, not what it says.

## 6. Unknown words: four context clues

You will meet words you do not know. That is normal, even for strong readers. Use the sentence around the word:

1. **Definition**: "dispersal, the movement of seeds away from the parent plant".
2. **Example**: "predators, such as wolves and eagles".
3. **Contrast**: "unlike the arid interior, the coast receives heavy rain" (so arid = dry).
4. **Cause or result**: "The bridge was so fragile that it collapsed under light snow" (fragile = easily broken).

<div class="strategy">

**Evidence or nothing.** For every answer you choose, put your finger (or your eyes) on the exact words in the passage that prove it. If you cannot find them, look at the other options again. This one habit removes most wrong answers, especially the attractive ones that "sound true" from general knowledge.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I must understand every word of the passage to answer the questions."** You do not. Strong readers skip words they do not know, guess from context and keep the main line of the argument. Questions focus on specific sentences; you only need full understanding of those. Stopping to puzzle over every hard word costs time you need for the questions.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

Signal words that show what a sentence does:

- **Adding:** also, in addition, furthermore, moreover
- **Contrasting:** however, but, yet, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless
- **Giving an example:** for example, for instance, such as
- **Cause and result:** because, since, as a result, therefore, thus, led to
- **Concluding:** in short, overall, as a result, for these reasons
- **Hedging (not 100% sure):** may, might, suggests, appears, is thought to

</div>

## 7. Practice

Six original passages, each with four or five questions of mixed types. Before you look at the questions, give yourself one minute to map the passage. Then answer, and always check the solution for the evidence sentence.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="drill" data-set="rap" data-n="5"><figcaption>Read an Academic Passage: six original passages, 28 questions.</figcaption></figure>

To build this skill outside the course, read one short encyclopedia article or science news story a day. Before you read, cover everything except the title and first sentences of each paragraph, and predict what each paragraph will say. Then read and check.

<div class="keypoints">

- Academic passages are short textbook-style texts; all the information you need is in the passage.
- Map first: title, first paragraph, first sentence of each paragraph.
- Spot the pattern: problem and solution, cause and effect, contrast, claim and evidence, change over time.
- Guess unknown words from definitions, examples, contrasts and results nearby.
- Choose only answers you can prove with words from the passage.

</div>
