**Module 1 · about 10 minutes · read first: [1.1](/learn/courses/sat/how-the-digital-sat-works/).** By the end you will know what your score report means and how to pick a realistic target.

<div class="bigidea">Your SAT score is not a simple count of right answers. Each section is a 200 to 800 score that depends on which questions you got right, so strong first modules and careful work on harder questions pay off most.</div>

## 1. Three numbers on your report

- **Reading and Writing**: 200 to 800.
- **Math**: 200 to 800.
- **Total**: the two added together, 400 to 1600.

Scores go up in steps of 10. The average total in the USA is a little over 1000; scores above 1400 put you among roughly the top few percent. Your report also shows a **percentile**: the share of test takers who scored at or below you.

## 2. Why "how many right" is not the whole story

The digital SAT uses a method called **item response theory**. Before a question ever counts, it is tried out on thousands of students, so the College Board knows how hard it is. When you take the test, your score is the ability level that best explains your pattern of answers.

Think of a high-jump competition. Clearing a bar at 1.5 m says more about you than clearing ten bars at 1 m. In the same way, getting harder questions right lifts your score more than getting easy ones right.

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><b>Module 1</b> has easy, medium and hard questions. It gives the test a first estimate of your level.</div>
<div class="step">A strong estimate routes you to the <b>harder Module 2</b>. Its questions can lift you to the top of the scale.</div>
<div class="step">A weaker estimate routes you to the <b>easier Module 2</b>. Its questions can still raise your score, but the top of the scale is out of reach.</div>
<div class="step">The final section score combines <b>both modules</b>: which questions you answered, how hard they were, and whether you got them right.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I should rush Module 1 to save time for Module 2."** Time does not carry over: each module has its own clock. Use all 32 or 35 minutes of Module 1 to get as many right as you can, because that decides how high your score can go.

</div>

## 3. Setting a target

1. List the colleges you want. Each one publishes the **middle 50% range** of SAT scores of the students it admitted (often on its admissions page or in the Common Data Set).
2. Your target is at or above the middle of that range for your top-choice college.
3. Take a full practice test (Part D) to get your starting score. The gap between that and your target tells you how much study time you need (Lesson 1.4).

Many colleges look at your best section scores across different test dates ("superscoring"), and you can choose which scores to send. Rules differ by college, so check each one.

## 4. When you get your scores

Scores usually appear in your College Board account within a few days to two weeks of the test. You can send four score reports free when you register; check the College Board website for current fees and for fee waivers if cost is a problem.

<div class="keypoints">

- Two section scores of 200 to 800 add up to a total of 400 to 1600.
- Scores depend on which questions you got right and how hard they were, not just how many.
- Module 1 decides which Module 2 you get, so give it full care; there is no wrong-answer penalty.
- Pick a target from the middle 50% score ranges of the colleges you want.

</div>
