**Module 9 · about 15 minutes · read first: [1.1 How the digital SAT works](/learn/courses/sat/how-the-digital-sat-works/).** By the end you will test any sentence for completeness and join sentences four correct ways.

<div class="bigidea">A complete sentence (an independent clause) has a subject and a verb and makes sense alone. Two of them can be joined in only four ways: a period, a semicolon, a comma plus a joining word (and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor), or a colon when the second explains the first.</div>

## 1. Is it complete?

Cover everything else and read the words alone. "The octopus has three hearts." Complete. "After decades of research into how bees navigate." Not complete: it leaves you waiting for the main point. That is a fragment.

## 2. Joining two sentences

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">Two sentences: "The octopus has three hearts" and "two pump blood through the gills".</div>
<div class="step">Wrong: "hearts, two pump" (comma splice) or "hearts two pump" (run-on).</div>
<div class="step">Right: "hearts. Two pump", "hearts; two pump", or "hearts, and two pump".</div>
<div class="step">With "however", "therefore" or "for example" between them, use a semicolon or period before and a comma after: "hearts; however, two..."</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Independent clause</dt><dd>a subject and verb that can stand alone as a sentence</dd>
<dt>Dependent part</dt><dd>a phrase or clause that cannot stand alone ("after the storm", "which surprised everyone"); it attaches with a comma or nothing</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Punctuation tells a reader where one complete thought ends. A comma splice makes the reader stumble; the SAT asks you to remove that stumble.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<details class="try"><summary>Try it: "The bridge opened in 1883 ______ it was the longest suspension bridge in the world." Which work: ". It", "; it", ", it", ", and it"?</summary>

". It", "; it" and ", and it" all work. ", it" alone is a comma splice. On the SAT only one correct choice will be offered.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"Long sentences are run-ons."** Length has nothing to do with it. A run-on is two complete sentences joined wrongly; a long sentence can be perfectly correct.

</div>
