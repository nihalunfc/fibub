**Module 7 · about 15 minutes · read first: [7.2 Text structure and purpose](/learn/courses/sat/text-structure-purpose/).** By the end you will place two authors on the same map and say where they meet and where they part.

<div class="bigidea">Reduce each text to one line: the author's claim. Then compare the two lines. Most cross-text answers are "Text 2 would disagree because..." or "both would agree that...".</div>

## 1. Compare the claims

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">Text 1: "Containers made trade cheap." Claim: containers caused it.</div>
<div class="step">Text 2: "Containers mattered, but only with standard ports, cranes and trucks." Claim: containers plus a system caused it.</div>
<div class="step">Agree: containers played a part.</div>
<div class="step">Disagree: whether containers alone explain it. Text 2 would answer Text 1 by pointing to the rest of the system.</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Agreement</dt><dd>a point both texts support, even if they disagree on others</dd>
<dt>Response</dt><dd>how one author would answer the other, using their own text's evidence</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Real research is a conversation: one study claims something, another adds a condition or a counter-example. Reading pairs is reading that conversation.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<details class="try"><summary>Try it: Text 1 says four-day weeks hurt learning; Text 2 finds scores did not fall in some districts. How would Text 2 respond?</summary>

By pointing out that, at least in some districts, the shorter week did not lower test scores. Not by saying four-day weeks are always better: Text 2 does not claim that.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"The two texts always disagree."** Often they partly agree. Look for the concession words ("mattered, but", "true, however").

</div>
