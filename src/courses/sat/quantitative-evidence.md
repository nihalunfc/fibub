**Module 6 · about 15 minutes · read first: [4.4 Scatterplots and two-way tables](/learn/courses/sat/two-variable-data/).** By the end you will read a claim, find the numbers that test it, and ignore the rest.

<div class="bigidea">Read the claim first, then the table. Every correct answer is both accurate (it matches the data) and relevant (it supports the exact claim). Most wrong answers are accurate but irrelevant.</div>

## 1. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">Read the last sentence first: what claim or goal is the answer supposed to support?</div>
<div class="step">Find the rows and columns the claim is about. Ignore the others.</div>
<div class="step">Decide what comparison the claim needs: a change over time, a larger and a smaller value, or two groups.</div>
<div class="step">Pick the option that makes that comparison with the right numbers.</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Accurate</dt><dd>the numbers in the option match the table or graph</dd>
<dt>Relevant</dt><dd>the numbers are the ones the claim needs</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Newspapers do this every day: "cycling rose after the bike lane" needs before-and-after counts, not just this year's total.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<details class="try"><summary>Try it: a table shows test scores for three classes. The claim: "Class B improved the most." What do you need?</summary>

Each class's change (later score minus earlier score), and evidence that B's change is the biggest. A single score for B is not enough.

</details>

<div class="myth">

**"Pick the option with the most numbers."** More numbers is not better evidence. The best option often uses just two values, the two the claim compares.

</div>
