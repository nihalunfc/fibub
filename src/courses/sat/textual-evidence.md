**Module 6 · about 15 minutes · read first: [6.1 Central ideas](/learn/courses/sat/central-ideas/).** By the end you will match evidence to a claim piece by piece.

<div class="bigidea">Break the claim into its parts, then pick the evidence that matches every part. Evidence about the right topic but the wrong link is the most common trap.</div>

## 1. Support and weaken

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">Claim: city blackbirds sing higher <i>because</i> traffic noise drowns low notes.</div>
<div class="step">Parts: (1) pitch, (2) traffic noise, (3) one causes the other.</div>
<div class="step">Strong support links all three: in the same city, birds sing higher on loud streets than in quiet parks.</div>
<div class="step">To weaken, find evidence that breaks the link: birds sing just as high in a quiet city.</div>
</div>

## 2. Illustrating quotations

When the question asks which quotation "illustrates the claim", list the claim's describing words (for example "patience" and "humour") and check each option against both.

<details class="try"><summary>Try it: claim, "the explorer's diary shows both fear and wonder". Which is better: "The ice cracked all night and I did not sleep" or "The ice cracked all night, and though I trembled, I have never heard music so strange"?</summary>

The second. It shows fear ("I trembled") and wonder ("never heard music so strange"). The first shows only fear.

</details>

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Support</dt><dd>makes the claim more likely to be true</dd>
<dt>Weaken</dt><dd>makes it less likely, often by showing the outcome without the cause, or another cause</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Scientists do this all the time: a good test compares a group with the cause to a similar group without it.</dd>
</dl>

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"An option that repeats the claim's words supports it."** Repeating words proves nothing. Evidence must be new information that connects the claim's parts.

</div>
