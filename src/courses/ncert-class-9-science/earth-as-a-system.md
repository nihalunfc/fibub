<div class="bigidea">The Earth is one connected system. Energy comes in from the Sun and leaves as heat. Matter (water, carbon, nitrogen) does not come in or leave: it goes round and round, through air, water, rock and living things. Change one part and every other part responds. People are now changing the carbon cycle faster than nature can keep up.</div>

## 1. The Earth’s four spheres

A system is a set of parts that work together. The Earth has four main parts, called **spheres**:

- **Lithosphere** (rock): the solid crust and the soil on it. It holds minerals and fossil fuels, and its slow movement gives mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes.
- **Hydrosphere** (water): oceans, rivers, lakes, ice and underground water. About 97% of all water is salty ocean; only about 2.5% is fresh, and most of that is frozen or underground.
- **Atmosphere** (air): a layer of gases, 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, a little argon, and very small amounts of carbon dioxide (about 0.04%) and water vapour. Most of the air lies within the lowest 15 km.
- **Biosphere** (life): every living thing, from bacteria in soil to whales and trees. It is a thin skin that lives inside the other three spheres.

They are not separate boxes. A fish uses oxygen that came from the air and dissolved in water. A tree’s roots take minerals from rock and water from the ground, and its leaves release oxygen and water vapour into the air. A volcano pushes gases into the air and ash onto land.

## 2. Energy in, energy out

Almost all the energy that runs the Earth’s weather, oceans and life comes from the **Sun**. (Heat from inside the Earth is thousands of times smaller.) At the top of our air, sunlight carries about **1361 W** on every square metre facing the Sun. But the Earth is a sphere that turns, so over the whole surface and over day and night the average is a quarter of that: about **340 W/m²**.

Not all of it is kept:

- About **30%** is reflected straight back to space by clouds, ice, sea and light-coloured ground. The fraction reflected is called the **albedo** (Earth’s is about 0.30). Fresh snow reflects about 80%, a dark forest about 10%.
- About **70%** is absorbed. It warms the ground, sea and air, melts ice, evaporates water and powers photosynthesis.

If the Earth kept taking in energy and sent none out, it would keep getting hotter. A steady temperature means **energy in = energy out**. The warm Earth sends the absorbed energy back to space as **infrared** (heat) radiation, which we cannot see.

```math
\text{absorbed} = \tfrac{1}{4}\,S\,(1-\text{albedo}) = 340\times(1-0.30)\approx 238\ \text{W/m}^2
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>S</dt><dd>sunlight on a surface facing the Sun, about 1361 W/m²</dd>
<dt>¼</dt><dd>because a sphere has 4 times the area of the flat disc that catches the light</dd>
<dt>albedo</dt><dd>the fraction reflected, from 0 (reflects nothing) to 1 (reflects everything)</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>The factor (1 − albedo) is the share that stays. A planet with more ice has a higher albedo, absorbs less, and so is cooler. Wearing white on a summer day works the same way.</dd>

## 3. The greenhouse effect

If the Earth had no atmosphere, balancing 238 W/m² would give an average surface temperature of about **−18 °C**: frozen oceans. The real average is about **15 °C**. The difference of 33 °C is the **greenhouse effect**, and without it we would not be here.

Here is how it works. Sunlight is mostly visible light, which passes easily through air. The warm ground sends back **infrared** heat. A few gases, the **greenhouse gases** (water vapour, carbon dioxide CO₂, methane CH₄, nitrous oxide), absorb that infrared and send it out again in every direction, including down. A car parked in the sun with its windows shut warms up in the same way: light gets in, heat is slow to get out. (A real greenhouse works mainly by stopping warm air from leaving; the atmosphere works by catching infrared. The name is only an analogy.)

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-earth-greenhouse">
<div class="step">Sunlight, mostly visible light, travels through space and reaches the Earth.</div>
<div class="step">About 30% bounces straight back: off clouds, ice and the sea. It never warms the Earth.</div>
<div class="step">The other 70% is absorbed by land and sea, which warm up.</div>
<div class="step">The warm surface sends out the energy again as infrared (heat) rays. Without gases to stop them, they would all escape.</div>
<div class="step">Greenhouse gas molecules (CO₂, CH₄, H₂O) absorb some of it and send it out in all directions. Some goes up to space, but some comes back down and warms the surface again.</div>
<div class="step">Add more of these gases (from burning fuel, cattle, rice fields) and more heat is sent back down. The surface settles at a higher temperature: this is global warming.</div>
</div>

**“The greenhouse effect is a pollution problem.”** The natural effect keeps the planet livable. The problem is the **extra** greenhouse gas that humans add, which strengthens the effect and warms the planet further. Too little would freeze us; too much overheats us.

## 4. The water cycle

Water moves round and round without ever being used up. The Sun’s heat powers the loop:

1. **Evaporation:** the Sun warms the sea, lakes and wet soil, and liquid water turns to vapour. Leaves also give out vapour through tiny pores: **transpiration**.
2. **Condensation:** the vapour rises, cools, and turns into tiny droplets that make clouds.
3. **Precipitation:** droplets join, grow heavy and fall as rain, hail or snow.
4. **Runoff and storage:** water flows in streams and rivers back to the sea, soaks into the ground as groundwater, or is stored as ice for thousands of years.

A hotter Sun speeds up the whole cycle: more evaporation, more clouds, heavier rain. A drop of water stays in the air for only about nine days on average but may stay in the deep ocean for thousands of years.

**“Rain water is a new supply.”** Water is not made or lost; it is recycled. The water in your glass has probably been a cloud, a river and a dinosaur’s drink many times over.

## 5. The carbon cycle

Carbon is in every living thing, in limestone, in coal and oil, and in the CO₂ of the air. It moves between them in a cycle.

- **Photosynthesis** takes CO₂ from the air (or dissolved in the sea) and makes it into glucose and other food in plants, algae and some bacteria.
- **Food chains** pass the carbon from plants to animals.
- **Respiration** in every living cell, **decay** of dead matter by bacteria and fungi, and **burning** of wood or fuel all return CO₂ to the air.
- Over millions of years some dead plants and tiny sea creatures were buried and turned into **fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas). Their carbon was locked away from the air.
- **Oceans** take in a lot of CO₂ and release some back. Shells and limestone lock up carbon as well.

For thousands of years these flows nearly balanced and the air stayed at about **280 ppm** (parts per million) of CO₂. Since about 1750 we have been burning fossil fuels and clearing forests, adding carbon far faster than nature takes it back. The air now has about **425 ppm**, over 50% more. About half of what we add is taken in by the oceans and land plants; the rest stays in the air, so the amount keeps rising.

## 6. The nitrogen cycle

Nitrogen is a key part of **proteins** and DNA, so every living thing needs it. The air is 78% nitrogen gas (N₂), but plants and animals cannot use it directly, because the two atoms in N₂ are held by a very strong triple bond. Nitrogen has to be **fixed**: changed into compounds such as nitrates and ammonia that plants can absorb.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-earth-nitrogen">
<div class="step">The air holds a huge store of nitrogen gas, but it is locked away from most living things.</div>
<div class="step">Fixation. Lightning splits N₂ and makes nitrates that rain brings down. Bacteria such as <i>Rhizobium</i> in the root nodules of pulses (gram, peas, groundnut) fix nitrogen. Fertiliser factories do it using the Haber process.</div>
<div class="step">Plants take nitrates from the soil through their roots and use them to build proteins.</div>
<div class="step">Animals eat plants (or other animals) and get their nitrogen as protein.</div>
<div class="step">Waste and dead bodies are broken down by decomposers into ammonia. Soil bacteria change the ammonia into nitrates again, ready for plants.</div>
<div class="step">Denitrifying bacteria turn some nitrates back into nitrogen gas, which returns to the air. The cycle is complete.</div>
</div>

Farmers in India have used this for centuries: growing a pulse in turn with a cereal (**crop rotation**) leaves nitrogen in the soil. Fertiliser such as urea also adds it, but too much washes into rivers and lakes, where it makes algae overgrow and use up the oxygen that fish need.

## 7. Why the wind blows: the Sun heats Earth unevenly

The Sun does not heat all places equally. The tropics get more than the poles, and **land warms and cools faster than water**. Warm air is lighter, so it rises, leaving a region of **low pressure** at the surface. Cooler air from a high-pressure area flows in to replace it. That moving air is **wind**. The air that rose spreads out at the top, cools, sinks over the cooler place, and a loop forms.

- **Sea breeze** (day): land is hotter, air over it rises, cool air blows from the sea to the land.
- **Land breeze** (night): land cools faster, so the sea is the warmer side and air blows from land to sea.
- **Monsoon** is the same idea over a whole subcontinent and a whole season. In summer (June to September) India’s land is far hotter than the Indian Ocean, so moist air flows in from the sea and brings the rains. In winter the land is cooler than the sea and the dry wind blows the other way.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-earth-wind"><figcaption>Slide the land temperature above or below the sea’s and watch the loop of air reverse. Use the buttons to jump to sea breeze, land breeze and the two monsoons.</figcaption></figure>

Rising air is also why clouds form: the rising, moist air cools and the water vapour condenses. The monsoon is the water cycle and the wind system working together, and crops, rivers and the economy of India depend on it.

## 8. Weather and climate

**Weather** is what the air is like at one place at one time: today’s temperature, wind, cloud and rain. It can change in hours. **Climate** is the **usual pattern of weather over a long time**, usually 30 years or more: Chennai is hot and humid, Shimla is cool, Rajasthan is dry.

A single hot day does not prove that the climate is changing, and a cold morning does not disprove it. To see climate you average many years.

Climate depends on latitude (nearness to the equator), height (it gets about 6.5 °C colder per 1000 m climbed), nearness to the sea (the sea keeps coastal places milder) and ocean currents and winds.

## 9. Climate change and what we can do

Global average temperature is about **1.1 °C higher** than in 1850–1900 (IPCC, 2011–2020 average), and most of the extra heat has gone into the oceans. Scientists have shown that burning fossil fuels, cutting forests and farming practices that release CO₂ and methane are the main cause. The effects include:

- Melting glaciers and ice sheets, and sea level rising slowly (about 20 cm since 1900) as water warms and ice melts, a risk for coastal cities such as Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata.
- More frequent extreme heat, heavy rain and floods in many places, and changes in the monsoon pattern that make farming harder.
- Stress on coral reefs and on species that cannot move or adapt quickly.

What can we do? The actions fall into two groups. **Cut emissions:** use less electricity and fuel, switch to solar, wind and hydro power, walk, cycle and use trains and buses, waste less food, eat less meat. **Help nature absorb more:** protect forests and wetlands, plant native trees, restore soil. India has set a target of net-zero emissions by 2070, and each of us has a share through the choices we make daily.

<div class="keypoints">

- The Earth is a system of four connected spheres: lithosphere (rock), hydrosphere (water), atmosphere (air) and biosphere (life). They exchange energy and matter all the time.
- Energy comes from the Sun: about 340 W/m² on average, of which about 30% is reflected (albedo 0.30) and 70% is absorbed. Energy in equals energy out at a steady temperature.
- The natural greenhouse effect (water vapour, CO₂, CH₄) warms Earth from about −18 °C to 15 °C. Extra gases from human activity strengthen it: global warming.
- Water cycle: evaporation and transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff and storage; powered by the Sun. Only about 2.5% of water is fresh.
- Carbon cycle: photosynthesis takes CO₂ in; respiration, decay and burning return it. Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests have raised CO₂ from about 280 ppm to about 425 ppm.
- Nitrogen cycle: fixation (lightning, root bacteria, factories), uptake by plants, passing along the food chain, decay to ammonia, nitrification, and denitrification back to N₂.
- Uneven heating makes wind; land warms faster than sea, giving sea and land breezes and the monsoon.
- Weather is short-term; climate is the 30-year pattern. We can cut emissions and help nature absorb more carbon.

</div>
