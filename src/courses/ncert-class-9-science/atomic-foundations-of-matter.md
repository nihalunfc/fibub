**Science · Class 9 · Chapter 9 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you will be able to state and use the laws of chemical combination, explain Dalton’s atomic theory, write symbols and ions, write chemical formulae by the criss-cross method, find molecular and formula unit masses, and use the mole and Avogadro’s number to count atoms by weighing.

<div class="bigidea">A chemical reaction only rearranges atoms; none appears and none is lost, so the total mass stays the same. Because atoms of each element have a fixed mass and join in fixed ratios, every pure compound has a fixed formula and a fixed composition. The mole lets us count these invisible atoms by weighing a lump of matter.</div>

## 1. Laws of chemical combination

Around 1800, chemists with accurate balances found two simple rules about how substances react.

### Law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier, 1789)

In a chemical reaction, **mass is neither created nor destroyed**: the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.

Burning a candle seems to make it vanish, but the wax joins with oxygen to make carbon dioxide gas and water vapour. If you could catch all the gases, the total mass would be the same. See it with a flask on a balance: the same reaction in a sealed and in an open flask.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-matter-flask"><figcaption>Baking soda and vinegar make carbon dioxide gas. In the sealed flask nothing can escape, so the balance never changes. In the open flask the gas floats away and the reading falls by exactly the mass of the gas. Mass has not vanished: it has left the flask.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“When wood burns, matter disappears.”** The ash left is much lighter than the wood, but the missing mass left as carbon dioxide and water vapour, which we cannot see. Measure everything, gases included, and the total is unchanged.

</div>

### Law of constant proportions (Proust, 1799)

A pure chemical compound always contains the **same elements in the same proportion by mass**, whatever its source or how it was made. In water, hydrogen and oxygen are always in the ratio **1 : 8** by mass: 9 g of water always gives 1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen, whether it came from a tap, the sea (after distilling) or a lab.

## 2. Dalton’s atomic theory

How could these laws be explained? In 1808 John Dalton proposed that matter is made of atoms. His main ideas, in modern words:

1. All matter is made of very small particles called **atoms**.
2. Atoms of one element are identical in mass and properties.
3. Atoms of different elements differ in mass and properties.
4. Atoms combine in **small whole-number ratios** to make compounds.
5. In a chemical reaction atoms are **rearranged**, never created or destroyed.

Idea 5 explains conservation of mass; idea 4 (with fixed atomic masses from 2 and 3) explains constant proportions: H₂O always has 2 H atoms (2 × 1) and 1 O atom (16), so the mass ratio is 2 : 16 = 1 : 8.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-matter-dalton">
<div class="step">Atoms: everything, even a drop of water, is made of tiny particles.</div>
<div class="step">Atoms of one element are alike: every oxygen atom has the same mass.</div>
<div class="step">Atoms of different elements differ: H is 1 u, C is 12 u, O is 16 u, Na is 23 u.</div>
<div class="step">Compounds have fixed numbers of each atom: every water molecule has 2 H and 1 O.</div>
<div class="step">In a reaction the atoms only change partners: 4 H and 2 O go in, 4 H and 2 O come out, so the mass is the same.</div>
</div>

<div class="history">

<ul class="timeline">
<li><b>c. 400 BCE</b> Democritus in Greece and Kanada in India imagine matter made of tiny indivisible particles (atoms), by pure thought.</li>
<li><b>1789</b> Antoine Lavoisier: mass is conserved in reactions.</li>
<li><b>1799</b> Joseph Proust: a compound has a fixed composition.</li>
<li><b>1808</b> John Dalton turns the idea of atoms into a scientific theory.</li>
</ul>

</div>

**What we know now.** Dalton was right about the big picture, but atoms can be divided into protons, neutrons and electrons (previous chapter), and atoms of one element can have different masses (isotopes). The laws still hold for chemical reactions, because these only move electrons and never change the nucleus.

## 3. Symbols and the atomic mass unit

Chemists write each element with a **symbol**: one capital letter, or a capital and a small letter. Many come from English names (H, O, C, Ca, Cl), some from Latin or other old names (Na for natrium, K for kalium, Fe for ferrum, Cu for cuprum, Ag for argentum, Au for aurum).

Atoms are far too light to weigh in grams (a hydrogen atom is about 1.7 × 10⁻²⁴ g), so we use the **atomic mass unit (u)**, defined as 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. On this scale:

| Element | Symbol | Atomic mass (u) |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H | 1 |
| Carbon | C | 12 |
| Nitrogen | N | 14 |
| Oxygen | O | 16 |
| Sodium | Na | 23 |
| Magnesium | Mg | 24 |
| Aluminium | Al | 27 |
| Sulphur | S | 32 |
| Chlorine | Cl | 35.5 |
| Potassium | K | 39 |
| Calcium | Ca | 40 |

(Chlorine is 35.5 because it is an average over its isotopes, as you saw in the previous chapter.)

## 4. Ions: charged atoms

An atom with a full outer shell is stable. Metals tend to **lose** outer electrons to become **positive ions (cations)**; non-metals tend to **gain** electrons to become **negative ions (anions)**. The charge on the ion is the same as its valency.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-matter-ions"><figcaption>Press Transfer: sodium (2, 8, 1) gives its single outer electron to chlorine (2, 8, 7). Both ions now have a full outer shell. The opposite charges attract, and in a crystal each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite kind in a 1 : 1 ratio.</figcaption></figure>

| Ion | Name | Ion | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na⁺ | sodium | Cl⁻ | chloride |
| K⁺ | potassium | O²⁻ | oxide |
| Mg²⁺ | magnesium | S²⁻ | sulphide |
| Ca²⁺ | calcium | N³⁻ | nitride |
| Al³⁺ | aluminium | OH⁻ | hydroxide |
| NH₄⁺ | ammonium | NO₃⁻ | nitrate |
| H⁺ | hydrogen | SO₄²⁻ | sulphate |
| | | CO₃²⁻ | carbonate |

Groups like NH₄⁺, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻ and CO₃²⁻ are **polyatomic ions**: several atoms joined together that carry a charge as a unit.

## 5. Writing chemical formulae

A chemical formula shows which atoms (or ions) there are in a compound and in what ratio. A compound is neutral overall, so **the positive and negative charges must add up to zero**. This is why the criss-cross method works.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-matter-criss">
<div class="step">Write the symbols side by side, the positive ion first, with their charges: Al³⁺ and O²⁻.</div>
<div class="step">Criss-cross: the number in Al’s charge (3) becomes the subscript of O; the number in O’s charge (2) becomes the subscript of Al.</div>
<div class="step">Al₂O₃. A subscript of 1 is never written.</div>
<div class="step">Check: 2 × (+3) = +6 and 3 × (−2) = −6. The total is 0.</div>
<div class="step">If the two numbers share a factor, cancel it: Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ give Mg₂O₂, which is divided by 2 to give MgO.</div>
<div class="step">A polyatomic ion is put in brackets when its subscript is more than 1: Al³⁺ with SO₄²⁻ gives Al₂(SO₄)₃.</div>
</div>

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>Subscript</dt><dd>the small number after a symbol: how many of that atom (or ion) are in the formula. H₂O: 2 H atoms, 1 O atom.</dd>
<dt>Brackets</dt><dd>they keep a polyatomic ion together: (NH₄)₂SO₄ has 2 NH₄ groups, so 2 N and 8 H.</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>The formula gives the ratio in which the ions or atoms combine so that the total charge is zero. Criss-cross is just a quick way to balance charges.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

## 6. Molecular mass and formula unit mass

The **molecular mass** of a molecule is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in it, in u. For water, H₂O: 2 × 1 + 16 = **18 u**. For carbon dioxide, CO₂: 12 + 2 × 16 = **44 u**.

Ionic compounds like NaCl and CaCO₃ do not exist as separate molecules; they are lattices of ions. For them we find the **formula unit mass**, in the same way: NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = **58.5 u**; CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 3 × 16 = **100 u**.

## 7. The mole and Avogadro’s number

A chemist wants to react exactly 2 hydrogen atoms with 1 oxygen atom. Counting atoms one by one is impossible, so we count in a big batch, like a dozen for bananas or a ream for paper. The chemist’s batch is the **mole (mol)**:

```math
1\ \text{mol} = 6.022\times 10^{23}\ \text{particles}\quad(\text{Avogadro's number},\ N_A)
```

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-matter-mole-scale"><figcaption>Slide up from one to a dozen, a lakh, a crore, all the people on Earth, and finally a mole. Counting a mole at one per second would take about 2 × 10¹⁶ years, over a million times the age of the universe. So we do not count: we weigh.</figcaption></figure>

Here is why weighing works. The mass of one atom of carbon is 12 u. The **molar mass** is defined so that **1 mole of any substance weighs its atomic or molecular mass, in grams**:

- 1 mol of carbon atoms = 12 g.
- 1 mol of water molecules = 18 g.
- 1 mol of NaCl = 58.5 g.

So 12 g of carbon holds 6.022 × 10²³ atoms. To find the amount of a substance and the number of particles in a sample:

```math
n = \frac{m}{M}\qquad N = n \times N_A
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>n</dt><dd>amount of substance, in moles</dd>
<dt>m</dt><dd>given mass in grams</dd>
<dt>M</dt><dd>molar mass in grams per mole (the same number as the molecular mass in u)</dd>
<dt>N</dt><dd>number of particles (atoms, molecules or formula units)</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>M is the bridge between the world of atoms (u) and the world of the balance (g). Going from grams to moles divides by M; going from moles to particles multiplies by N<sub>A</sub>.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

**Worked example.** How many molecules are in 4.5 g of water?

1. M = 18 g/mol. n = 4.5 ÷ 18 = 0.25 mol.
2. N = 0.25 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.5 × 10²³ molecules**.
3. Atoms: each molecule has 3 atoms, so 4.5 × 10²³ atoms.

<div class="keypoints">

- Conservation of mass: total mass before = total mass after (count any gas). Constant proportions: a pure compound has the same elements in the same mass ratio, e.g. water H : O = 1 : 8.
- Dalton: matter is made of atoms; atoms of one element are alike; they join in whole-number ratios; reactions only rearrange atoms. (Atoms are divisible and isotopes exist, but the laws still hold in chemistry.)
- Atomic mass unit u = 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom. Ions: metals lose electrons to form cations, non-metals gain to form anions; the charge equals the valency.
- Criss-cross: swap the charge numbers into subscripts, cancel common factors, use brackets for polyatomic ions, check that the charge adds to zero.
- Molecular (or formula unit) mass = sum of atomic masses, e.g. H₂O 18 u, CO₂ 44 u, NaCl 58.5 u.
- 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles; its mass in grams equals the atomic or molecular mass. n = m/M and N = n × N<sub>A</sub>.

</div>
