**Biology · Class 9 · Chapter 11 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you can name the ways living things make new individuals, describe what happens in a flower from pollination to seed, and explain why two parents give more variety than one.

<div class="bigidea">Every living thing comes from another living thing. Some make copies of themselves from one parent; others mix two parents’ contributions. Both ways keep the species going, but only the second gives variety.</div>

## 1. Why do organisms reproduce?

An individual cannot live forever. A tree gets old, a cell wears out, a rat is caught by a cat. If nothing replaced them, the species would vanish. **Reproduction** is the making of new individuals of the same kind, and it is how a species continues.

Reproduction is not needed for the survival of the *individual* (a person who never has children is perfectly healthy). It is needed for the survival of the *species*.

It also relies on a fact you met in the cell chapter: **cells come from cells** by cell division. Reproduction always begins with cell division. See it happen first:

<!--fig:tool cell-division-->

There are two broad kinds.

- **Asexual reproduction:** one parent, no gametes, and the offspring are identical copies (clones).
- **Sexual reproduction:** usually two parents; special cells called **gametes** (male and female) fuse, and the offspring are a mix of both.

## 2. Asexual reproduction

In asexual reproduction a single parent makes new individuals. It is quick, needs no partner, and works well where conditions stay the same. Bacteria, amoeba, yeast, bread mould, potato and ginger all use it.

### Binary fission

An amoeba or a bacterium divides into two equal parts. The nucleus divides first, then the cytoplasm pinches in between.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-rep-fission">
<div class="step">One amoeba with one nucleus.</div>
<div class="step">The nucleus divides into two.</div>
<div class="step">The cell pinches in the middle.</div>
<div class="step">Two daughter cells separate.</div>
<div class="step">Each divides again: 2 become 4.</div>
</div>

Because each round doubles the number, a few hours of fission can give millions of bacteria. That is why a small infection grows so fast, and why milk left out of the fridge turns sour by the next day.

In **multiple fission** (as in *Plasmodium*, the malaria parasite) the nucleus divides many times first and then the cell splits into many small cells at once.

### Budding, fragmentation, spores and regeneration

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-rep-modes"><figcaption>Pick a mode and press Play. Budding: a bud grows on yeast and breaks off. Fragmentation: a Spirogyra filament breaks and each piece grows. Spores: a bread-mould sporangium bursts and the spores land and grow. Regeneration: a cut flatworm regrows the missing parts.</figcaption></figure>

- **Budding:** a small bulge (bud) grows on the parent, gets a copy of the nucleus, and breaks off. Yeast and *Hydra* do this.
- **Fragmentation:** the body breaks into pieces, and each piece grows into a full organism. Spirogyra, a green alga you see in ponds as slimy threads, does this.
- **Spores:** tiny, light cells in a case called a sporangium. They have a tough coat, so they survive dry air, and when one lands on a damp, food-rich spot it grows. Bread mould (*Rhizopus*), the black fuzz on stale bread in the monsoon, is the example. Ferns and mushrooms use spores too.
- **Regeneration:** some animals, like *Planaria* flatworms, can regrow missing body parts, and each piece of a cut worm may become a complete worm. (A lizard regrowing a tail is also regeneration, but it is not a way of making a new individual.)

### Vegetative propagation

In plants, a new plant can grow from a **root, stem or leaf** of the parent. This is **vegetative propagation**.

- **Potato:** each “eye” on a tuber is a bud. Cut the potato into pieces, each with an eye, and each piece grows into a plant.
- **Ginger:** the underground stem (rhizome) has buds at its joints.
- **Bryophyllum (patharchatta):** tiny plantlets with roots grow in the notches on the leaf edge and fall off.
- **Cuttings:** rose, sugarcane and money plant are grown from a cut stem with a node planted in damp soil.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-rep-vegetative"><figcaption>Choose a plant and drag the time slider from 0 to 20 days. Watch shoots rise from the buds and roots grow from the node or notch.</figcaption></figure>

Gardeners and farmers also use **layering** (bending a branch into the soil so it roots while still attached) and **grafting** (joining a cut stem of one plant onto a rooted plant of a related kind, as with mango and rose). Growing a whole plant from a few cells in a lab is called **tissue culture**.

Why use it? Plants like banana, rose and sugarcane do not make seeds easily or the seeds grow slowly, and vegetative propagation gives a plant with exactly the same good fruit or flower as the parent, and flowers and fruit earlier.

### Advantages and drawbacks of asexual reproduction

| Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|
| One parent is enough; no need to find a mate | All the offspring are clones, with no variation |
| Fast; many offspring from one parent | If conditions change or a disease comes, all may die |
| Good traits are kept exactly | Offspring crowd near the parent |

## 3. Sexual reproduction: why mix two parents?

In sexual reproduction two **gametes** fuse in **fertilisation** to make a **zygote**, the first cell of the new individual. The male gamete and the female gamete come from different parents (or, in some plants, different parts of one plant). Offspring get a mix of features from both parents, so they differ from each other and from their parents: **variation**.

Variation is valuable. If the climate changes or a new disease arrives, some of the varied individuals may be able to cope. See what happens when a disease strikes:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-rep-variation"><figcaption>Switch between one-parent and two-parent offspring, then press “Disease strikes”. The disease harms only the red trait. Clones are all red and all die; varied offspring mostly survive.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“Asexual reproduction is a worse method.”** Neither is “better”. Asexual reproduction is perfect when the surroundings stay the same, because it is fast and needs no partner. Sexual reproduction pays off when surroundings change, thanks to variation.

</div>

## 4. Sexual reproduction in flowering plants

The flower is the reproductive organ of a flowering plant.

### Parts of a flower

- **Sepals** (green, outer): protect the bud. All together, the calyx.
- **Petals** (usually bright): attract insects. All together, the corolla.
- **Stamens** (male): each has an **anther** (makes pollen grains) on a **filament**.
- **Carpel or pistil** (female): the **stigma** (sticky top), the **style** (tube) and the **ovary** with one or more **ovules**, each holding an egg cell.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-rep-flower"><figcaption>A cut-open flower. Choose a part to highlight it and read what it does.</figcaption></figure>

A flower that has both stamens and carpels (such as hibiscus, mustard and pea) is a **bisexual** flower. One with only stamens or only carpels (such as papaya, cucumber and maize) is **unisexual**.

### Pollination

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.

- **Self-pollination:** to the stigma of the same flower (or another flower on the same plant).
- **Cross-pollination:** to a flower on a different plant of the same kind. This gives more variation.

The carriers (pollinating agents) are insects such as bees and butterflies, wind, water, and sometimes birds and bats. Flowers that depend on insects have bright petals, scent and nectar and sticky pollen. Flowers pollinated by wind, like maize and grasses, are small and dull and make huge amounts of light pollen.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-rep-pollination"><figcaption>Choose self, insect or wind pollination and press Play to watch the pollen travel.</figcaption></figure>

### Fertilisation: the pollen tube

When a pollen grain lands on a matching stigma, it germinates and grows a **pollen tube** down the style, all the way to an ovule. The male gametes travel down this tube. One fuses with the egg cell: this is **fertilisation**, and it forms the **zygote**.

<div class="step">A male gamete fuses with the egg: the zygote.</div>

Flowers do not always get this far: pollen of the wrong kind of plant will not grow a tube.

### After fertilisation: seed and fruit

The zygote divides to make an **embryo**. The ovule becomes the **seed** with its seed coat and stored food. The ovary becomes the **fruit**. The other flower parts wither.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-rep-seed">
<div class="step">The flower is fertilised: the zygote starts to divide.</div>
<div class="step">Petals and stamens wither; the ovary swells.</div>
<div class="step">The ovule becomes the seed and the ovary becomes the fruit.</div>
<div class="step">Seeds are spread: wind, water, animals or bursting pods.</div>
<div class="step">In a suitable place, a seed germinates.</div>
</div>

**Seed dispersal** keeps the seedlings away from the shade and crowding of the parent. Think of drumstick and maple seeds with wings, coconut floating on sea water, burr-like seeds sticking to animal fur, and mustard pods that burst and shoot out the seeds.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c9s-rep-seeds"></div>

### Germination

A seed is a plant waiting for the right moment. With **water, air (oxygen) and a suitable temperature** it **germinates**: it soaks up water and swells, the seed coat splits, a **root** comes out first, then the **shoot**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-rep-germ"><figcaption>Drag “Days after sowing” from 0 to 10 and watch a bean seed swell, put out a root, then send up a hooked shoot that opens into leaves.</figcaption></figure>

## 5. Sexual reproduction in animals

Animals also have two kinds of gametes.

- **Sperm** (male gametes): tiny, with a tail to swim, and made in huge numbers.
- **Egg or ovum** (female gamete): large, round, with stored food, and made in smaller numbers.

When a sperm fuses with an egg, that is **fertilisation**, and the fused cell is the **zygote**. The zygote divides again and again and grows into an **embryo**, in which organs gradually form.

<div class="step">The fertilised egg is the zygote.</div>
<div class="step">The zygote divides into 2, then 4, then 8 cells.</div>

### Where do fertilisation and development take place?

- **External fertilisation:** eggs and sperm are released into water and meet there. Fish and frogs do this. The animal lays hundreds or thousands of eggs, because most are eaten or fail to grow.
- **Internal fertilisation:** the sperm enters the female’s body and fertilises the egg inside. This is the case for birds, reptiles and mammals.
  - Birds, reptiles and some other animals then lay **eggs** protected by a shell, and the young develop outside the body (a hen sits on her eggs).
  - Mammals such as cows, cats and humans keep the embryo in the mother’s body (in the **uterus**, or womb), which feeds and protects it until birth. Mammals have few young at a time and look after them for a long time.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-rep-where"><figcaption>Compare a frog, a hen and a cow or human: where the egg is fertilised, where the young develop, how many there are and how much care they get.</figcaption></figure>

In human beings, the male reproductive organs make sperm and the female organs make eggs. Fertilisation takes place inside the female body, and the embryo develops in the uterus for about nine months. (How the body changes at adolescence is a topic of a later class.)

### Asexual and sexual reproduction compared

| | Asexual | Sexual |
|---|---|---|
| Parents | One | Usually two |
| Gametes | Not involved | Male and female gametes fuse |
| Offspring | Identical to the parent (clones) | A mix: they show variation |
| Speed | Fast, many offspring | Slower |
| Examples | Amoeba, yeast, potato | Flowering plants, birds, humans |

<div class="keypoints">

- Reproduction makes new individuals so that the species continues; it always involves cell division.
- Asexual reproduction: one parent, no gametes, offspring identical. Methods: binary and multiple fission, budding, fragmentation, spores, regeneration, vegetative propagation.
- Vegetative propagation uses a root, stem or leaf: potato eyes, ginger rhizome, bryophyllum leaf, cuttings; also layering, grafting and tissue culture.
- Sexual reproduction: male and female gametes fuse (fertilisation) to form a zygote; offspring show variation, which helps a species cope with change.
- A flower has sepals, petals, stamens (anther and filament, male) and a carpel (stigma, style and ovary with ovules, female).
- Pollination moves pollen from anther to stigma (self or cross; by insects, wind, water). The pollen tube carries the male gametes to the egg for fertilisation.
- After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed and the ovary the fruit. A seed germinates with water, air and warmth: root first, then shoot.
- In animals, sperm are small and many; eggs are large and fewer. Fertilisation may be external (frog, fish) or internal (hen, mammals); development may be in an egg or in the mother’s body.
- The zygote divides into many cells and forms an embryo.

</div>
