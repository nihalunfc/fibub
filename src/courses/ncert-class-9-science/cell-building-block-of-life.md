**Science · Class 9 · Chapter 2 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you will be able to state the cell theory, work out a microscope’s magnification, compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, name every organelle and its job, explain diffusion and osmosis (turgid, flaccid, plasmolysis) and say why cells stay small.

<div class="bigidea">Every living thing, from a banyan tree to the bacteria in curd, is made of cells. A cell is a tiny walled-off factory: its parts (organelles) each do one job, its skin (membrane) decides what enters and leaves, and its small size keeps it well fed.</div>

## 1. Discovering the cell and the cell theory

Cut a thin slice of onion and look at it through a microscope: you see neat boxes packed together like bricks in a wall. Those boxes are **cells**. In 1665 Robert Hooke looked at a slice of cork and called the little boxes “cells” because they reminded him of the small rooms (cells) of a monastery. Later, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek saw living cells in pond water.

Start with the zoom below. Move through the four levels: the whole onion, a thin peel, one cell, and the parts inside it.

The sizes are typical; an onion peel cell is about 0.2 mm long, which is why it is just visible to a sharp eye but only clear under a microscope.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="history">

- **1665** Robert Hooke names “cells” in cork.
- **1674 to 1683** Antonie van Leeuwenhoek sees bacteria and living single cells.
- **1831** Robert Brown finds the nucleus in plant cells.
- **1838 to 1839** Matthias Schleiden (plants) and Theodor Schwann (animals) say all living things are made of cells.
- **1855** Rudolf Virchow adds that new cells come only from existing cells.

</div>

Put together, the **cell theory** says:

1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function of life.
3. All new cells come from cells that already exist (by cell division).

Some organisms are a single cell (an *Amoeba*, a bacterium) and are called **unicellular**. A banyan tree or a human is **multicellular**: trillions of cells, with different types doing different jobs. Think of a cricket team: every player is a unit, but the team wins only when they work together.

<div class="myth">

**“Cells are only found in plants and animals.”** Bacteria, yeast in your dosa batter and the algae in a pond are all cells too. Even a single cell is a complete living thing: it feeds, grows, respires, gets rid of waste, and reproduces.

</div>

## 2. The microscope and magnification

A cell is much too small to see. A **compound microscope** uses two sets of lenses. The **objective** (near the slide) makes a first magnified image and the **eyepiece** (where you look) magnifies it again. So the powers multiply:

```math
\text{total magnification} = \text{eyepiece power} \times \text{objective power}
```

An eyepiece of 10× and an objective of 40× give 10 × 40 = 400×.

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>Magnification</dt><dd>how many times bigger the image looks than the real object</dd>
<dt>Field of view</dt><dd>the circle of the slide that you can see; it shrinks as magnification rises</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Image size = real size × magnification. A 10 µm cell at 400× looks 4000 µm = 4 mm long. It is the same cell: only the picture is bigger.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

Pick lenses and a cell size and watch how many cells fit in the field of view. High power shows more detail but a smaller patch, like zooming into a map.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-cell-mag"><figcaption>The round view is what you see through the eyepiece. The black bar is 100 µm long. Raise the objective power and the same cells grow larger while fewer of them fit in the circle.</figcaption></figure>

**Units for tiny things.** 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres, also called microns). A red blood cell is about 7 µm across; a bacterium 1 to 2 µm. Nanometres (nm) are 1000 times smaller still: 1 µm = 1000 nm.

**Making a temporary slide of onion peel.** Peel a thin, transparent layer from the inner side of a scale leaf, float it in a drop of water on a slide, add a drop of safranin or iodine stain (cell parts are nearly transparent, so the stain makes them show), lower a cover slip at an angle so no air bubbles are trapped, and look first at low power, then at high power. Never use the coarse focus wheel at high power: you may crack the slide.

## 3. Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

Cells come in two broad designs.

- A **prokaryotic** cell (such as a bacterium) is small, has **no true nucleus** and **no membrane-bound organelles**. Its DNA lies loose in a region of the cytoplasm called the **nucleoid**. “Pro-karyote” means “before a nucleus”.
- A **eukaryotic** cell (plants, animals, fungi, *Amoeba*) is usually larger and has a **true nucleus** wrapped in a membrane, plus organelles each wrapped in its own membrane. “Eu-karyote” means “true nucleus”.

Think of a one-room shop (prokaryote) against a factory with separate rooms for each job (eukaryote).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-cell-types"><figcaption>Pick a cell. The list shows which features it has. A bacterium has a wall and free DNA but no true nucleus; an animal cell has a nucleus and organelles but no wall; a plant cell has everything, plus chloroplasts and a large vacuole.</figcaption></figure>

## 4. Plant cells and animal cells

Both are eukaryotic, so both have a nucleus, cytoplasm and a cell membrane. The main differences:

| Feature | Plant cell | Animal cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall (cellulose) | present, outside the membrane | absent |
| Shape | fixed, often boxy | changeable, often round or irregular |
| Chloroplasts (plastids) | present in green parts | absent |
| Vacuole | one large central vacuole | none or several small ones |
| Centrioles | usually absent in higher plants | present (help in cell division) |

The **cell wall** is a tough, rigid layer of **cellulose**. It gives the plant cell its shape, protects it and lets plants stand tall without bones. It is fully permeable: water and salts pass through it freely. The **cell membrane** inside it is the one that decides what gets in.

<div class="myth">

**“The cell wall and the cell membrane are the same.”** The membrane is a thin, living, selective skin found in every cell. The wall is a thick, non-living, rigid layer that only plants, fungi and bacteria have, outside the membrane.

</div>

## 5. The organelles and their jobs

**Organelles** are the little “organs” of a cell. Tap each part on the factory map.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-cell-factory"><figcaption>Pick an organelle to see its job and its matching part of a factory. The picture is a simplified sketch, and the analogy is a memory aid.</figcaption></figure>

- **Cell membrane (plasma membrane):** a very thin skin made of fats (lipids) and proteins. It is **selectively permeable**: it lets some things pass and blocks others, like a security gate.
- **Nucleus:** surrounded by a double membrane with tiny pores. It holds the **DNA** in thread-like **chromosomes**, which carry the instructions for the cell and for heredity. A dark spot inside is the **nucleolus**, where ribosomes are made.
- **Cytoplasm:** the jelly-like fluid between the membrane and the nucleus, in which all organelles float and many reactions happen.
- **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER):** a network of folded tubes and sheets. **Rough ER** has ribosomes on it and makes proteins. **Smooth ER** has none; it makes fats and helps to remove poisons (the liver cell has a lot).
- **Ribosomes:** tiny granules, free or on the rough ER, where proteins are built from amino acids.
- **Golgi apparatus:** a stack of flat sacs that modifies, packs and sends out proteins and other products in small bags called **vesicles**.
- **Lysosomes:** bags of digestive enzymes that break down worn-out parts, food and germs; if a cell is damaged they may burst and digest the cell, so they are called “suicide bags”.
- **Mitochondria:** the “power houses”. They release energy from food using oxygen and store it as **ATP**. They have two membranes, and the inner one is folded.
- **Plastids:** found in plants. **Chloroplasts** contain green **chlorophyll** and carry out photosynthesis; **chromoplasts** hold other colours (the orange of a carrot); **leucoplasts** are colourless and store starch, oil or protein.
- **Vacuoles:** sacs of fluid. In plants one large central vacuole stores water, sugar and salts and keeps the cell firm; in animals they are small, and a food vacuole in *Amoeba* digests food.

Follow one protein through the cell, from its instruction to the outside.

## 6. Moving in and out: diffusion and osmosis

A cell is alive only if things keep entering (food, oxygen, water) and leaving (carbon dioxide, wastes). Two basic processes need no energy from the cell.

**Diffusion** is the movement of particles from a region of **higher concentration** to a region of **lower concentration**, until they are spread evenly. Think of the smell of tadka spreading from the kitchen to the whole house. The particles are always moving; it is only the net movement that goes from crowded to empty.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-cell-diffusion"><figcaption>Lift the partition and watch the graph of the number in the left box fall to about half. Raise the temperature and the particles move faster, so diffusion is quicker. After mixing, particles still cross both ways but in equal numbers.</figcaption></figure>

Oxygen enters cells and carbon dioxide leaves by diffusion. Diffusion is fast over tiny distances and very slow over long ones, which is another reason cells are small.

**Osmosis** is the diffusion of **water** through a **selectively permeable membrane**, from a region of **higher water concentration** (a weaker solution) to a region of **lower water concentration** (a stronger solution).

Compare the outside solution with the cell sap:

- **Hypotonic** solution (weaker than the cell): water goes **in**. The plant cell swells and becomes **turgid** (firm). The cell wall stops it bursting. An animal cell, with no wall, can swell and burst.
- **Isotonic** solution (same strength): no net movement.
- **Hypertonic** solution (stronger than the cell): water goes **out**. The cell becomes **flaccid** (limp) and, in a plant cell, the membrane pulls away from the wall: **plasmolysis**.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c9s-cell-potato"></div>

Everyday examples: raisins swell in water; limp salad leaves become crisp in water; pickling uses a lot of salt to draw water out of microbes and vegetables, which preserves them; rubbing salt on a leech or a slug makes it lose water. Plants wilt on a hot day or when too much fertiliser is applied, because the soil water becomes stronger than the root cell sap.

<div class="myth">

**“Osmosis is when salt moves into the cell.”** In osmosis it is the **water** that moves, through a membrane that lets water pass but holds back the dissolved substances. Water moves toward the stronger solution.

</div>

Cells also move materials **against** a gradient using energy (active transport) and swallow bigger particles by wrapping the membrane around them (endocytosis, as in *Amoeba* eating); these are mentioned only so you know the words.

## 7. Why cells are small: surface area and volume

Food and oxygen enter through the **surface**, but they must reach the whole **volume**. Take a cube-shaped cell of side *s*:

```math
\text{surface area} = 6s^2, \qquad \text{volume} = s^3, \qquad \frac{\text{surface area}}{\text{volume}} = \frac{6}{s}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>6s²</dt><dd>the six square faces: the “skin” through which things enter</dd>
<dt>s³</dt><dd>the amount of living stuff that needs feeding</dd>
<dt>6/s</dt><dd>the surface available for each unit of volume</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>When a cell doubles in size, its skin becomes 4 times bigger but its “stomach” becomes 8 times bigger. The big cell is hungrier than its skin can feed.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-cell-sav"><figcaption>Make the cube bigger and the ratio bar shrinks. Press the cut button to split the cube into 8 smaller ones: the volume is the same but the total surface area doubles. That is why cells divide when they grow, and why large animals are built from many small cells, not a few giant ones.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c9s-cell-sav"></div>

Cells that must exchange a lot have clever shapes: root hair cells are long and thin to reach more soil water; the lining of the small intestine is folded into tiny fingers (villi); the inner membrane of a mitochondrion is folded. All of these increase surface area without increasing volume. A few cells, like a bird’s egg yolk or a nerve cell, are large but they get around the problem with special arrangements.

When a cell does reach its size limit, it divides in two. Starting from one cell, the number after each round is 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on.

<div class="keypoints">

- Cell theory: all living things are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit; new cells come only from existing cells.
- Total magnification = eyepiece × objective; image size = real size × magnification. 1 mm = 1000 µm.
- Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no true nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have both.
- Plant cells have a cellulose wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole; animal cells do not.
- Nucleus: DNA and control. Mitochondria: energy (ATP). Ribosomes: proteins. Rough ER: proteins; smooth ER: fats. Golgi: packing. Lysosomes: digestion. Plastids: photosynthesis and storage. Vacuole: storage and firmness. Membrane: selective gate.
- Diffusion is movement from high to low concentration; osmosis is the diffusion of water through a selectively permeable membrane.
- Hypotonic: water in, turgid. Isotonic: no net change. Hypertonic: water out, flaccid, plasmolysis.
- Surface area ÷ volume = 6/s for a cube, so it falls as the cell grows: this keeps cells small.

</div>
