**Biology · Class 9 · Chapter 12 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you can explain why living things are classified, name the ranks from kingdom to species, write a scientific name correctly, place any organism in one of five kingdoms, describe the main plant and animal groups, and use or build a dichotomous key.

<div class="bigidea">There are millions of kinds of living things. Sorting them into groups by shared features gives every organism a place and a name, so that anyone, anywhere can know exactly which organism is being talked about.</div>

## 1. Why do we classify?

Walk through a vegetable market. The seller does not pile everything together: leafy greens in one place, roots in another, fruits somewhere else. You can find a tomato in seconds. A library does the same with books.

Scientists have named about 2 million kinds of living things and find new ones every year. Without a system, studying them would be hopeless. **Classification** is arranging living things into groups according to their similarities and differences. It helps us to:

- **Name and identify** an organism and find it again.
- **Study** a group by studying a few of its members.
- **Understand relationships** and how living things changed over time.

But the choice of feature matters. Try grouping the same twelve organisms in different ways:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-div-sort"><figcaption>Group twelve living things by habitat, by backbone, or by number of legs. Notice that “lives in water” puts a fish, a dolphin and a crab together although they are very different inside, while “has a backbone” brings together animals that share many other features.</figcaption></figure>

Good classification uses **basic, important features** first (such as the kind of cell, or whether there is a backbone), not easy-to-see but unimportant features (such as colour or where it lives). Scientists use the features that tell most about how living things are related.

## 2. The hierarchy: from kingdom to species

Classification is a **ladder of groups inside groups**. The biggest group is the **kingdom**; each is split into smaller and smaller groups:

**Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species**

A handy way to remember it: *King Philip Came Over For Good Soup*.

Each step down contains fewer organisms that are more alike. The **species** is the smallest group: organisms so alike that they can breed with each other and produce fertile young. Slide the zoom control to move from the Bengal tiger up to the animal kingdom:

## 3. Naming living things: binomial nomenclature

A plant called “jamun” in Kerala may be called “jambul” somewhere else, and “black plum” by someone writing English. Common names change from place to place. In the 18th century the Swedish botanist **Carl Linnaeus** gave every species a two-word Latin name used all over the world. It is the **binomial name**.

The rules:

1. The name has two parts: the **genus** first, then the **species**.
2. The genus starts with a **capital letter**; the species is all **small letters**.
3. In print the name is written in *italics*; written by hand, each word is **underlined separately**.

Examples: *Panthera tigris* (tiger), *Mangifera indica* (mango), *Homo sapiens* (human), *Azadirachta indica* (neem).

Notice that the tiger and lion are both *Panthera*: same genus, close relatives. The banyan (*Ficus benghalensis*) and peepal (*Ficus religiosa*) share the genus *Ficus*. Compare any two of these living things rank by rank:

<div class="myth">

**“Scientific names are just fancy names for the same thing.”** A scientific name carries information: the first word tells you the genus, so you already know a lot about *Panthera leo* if you know *Panthera tigris*. And one species has only one scientific name worldwide.

## 4. The five kingdoms

In 1969 the American scientist **R. H. Whittaker** proposed a classification into five kingdoms. It uses four big features:

- Does the cell have a **nucleus**? (If not, it is a prokaryote; if yes, a eukaryote.)
- Is the body **one cell or many**?
- Is there a **cell wall**?
- How does it get **food**: make it (photosynthesis), absorb it, or eat it?

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-div-cells"><figcaption>Choose a kingdom to see a typical cell and its features: Monera (no nucleus), Protista (a nucleus, one cell), Fungi (cell wall, absorb food), Plantae (cellulose wall, make food) and Animalia (no wall, eat food).</figcaption></figure>

| Kingdom | Nucleus | Cells | Cell wall | Food | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Monera** | No | One | Usually | Make or absorb | Bacteria, cyanobacteria |
| **Protista** | Yes | One | Mostly none | Make or eat | Amoeba, *Paramecium*, *Euglena* |
| **Fungi** | Yes | One or many | Yes | Absorb from dead matter | Yeast, mushroom, bread mould |
| **Plantae** | Yes | Many | Yes (cellulose) | Make (photosynthesis) | Moss, fern, mango, rice |
| **Animalia** | Yes | Many | No | Eat | Insects, fish, humans |

<div class="myth">

**“Fungi are plants.”** Mushrooms grow from the ground, but they have no chlorophyll and cannot make food. They absorb it from dead matter, and so have their own kingdom. Viruses are not placed in any of the five kingdoms at all: they are not made of cells.

## 5. The plant kingdom

Plants are classified by a few big features.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-div-plants">
<div class="step">Algae (Thallophyta): simple body, no root, stem or leaf.</div>
<div class="step">Bryophytes: a stem-like and leaf-like body, no vascular tissue.</div>
<div class="step">Pteridophytes: true roots, stems, leaves, vascular tissue, no seeds.</div>
<div class="step">Gymnosperms: seeds that are not enclosed in a fruit.</div>
<div class="step">Angiosperms: flowers and seeds enclosed in fruits.</div>
</div>

Angiosperms are also split by the number of **seed leaves (cotyledons)**:

- **Monocots** have one seed leaf, parallel veins in the leaves, and fibrous roots (rice, wheat, maize, banana, coconut).
- **Dicots** have two seed leaves, net-like veins, and a taproot (mango, gram, rose, neem).

## 6. The animal kingdom

Animals are many-celled, have no cell wall, and eat food. They are grouped by their **body plan**: how the body is built, whether it has a backbone, how many segments or limbs and so on.

**Invertebrates** (no backbone): sponges (Porifera), Cnidaria (jellyfish, Hydra), flatworms, roundworms, annelids (earthworm), arthropods (insects, spiders, crabs: jointed legs, the biggest group), molluscs (snails, octopus) and echinoderms (starfish).

**Vertebrates** (backbone) in the phylum Chordata, in five classes: fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-div-animals"><figcaption>Choose a group to see a simple drawing, its key feature and examples from India. Compare the vertebrate groups on skin, breathing and reproduction.</figcaption></figure>

| Vertebrate class | Body covering | Breathing | Reproduction | Body temperature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fishes | Scales | Gills | Eggs in water | Cold-blooded |
| Amphibians | Moist skin | Gills then lungs and skin | Eggs in water | Cold-blooded |
| Reptiles | Dry scales | Lungs | Eggs on land | Cold-blooded |
| Birds | Feathers | Lungs | Hard-shelled eggs | Warm-blooded |
| Mammals | Hair or fur | Lungs | Mostly live young, milk | Warm-blooded |

(“Cold-blooded” here means the body temperature changes with the surroundings.)

## 7. Dichotomous keys: a tool to identify

A **dichotomous key** (dichotomous means “in two parts”) is a set of paired statements or yes/no questions about features you can see. Each answer sends you either to the next question or to a name. It is how field guides identify a plant, a bird or a snake.

Rules for a good key:

- Use features that are **clear and can be seen** (parallel veins, thorns), not vague ones (“pretty”).
- Each question must **split** the group into two.
- Start with the broadest features and narrow down.

With **q** questions on every path a key can separate at most **2<sup>q</sup>** organisms. So 3 questions separate up to 8, and 6 questions up to 64.

<div class="keypoints">

- Classification arranges living things into groups by similarities, using basic features, so we can name, study and compare them.
- The ranks, big to small: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. A species can breed and produce fertile young.
- Binomial name: genus (capital) then species (small), italic or underlined: *Mangifera indica*. Introduced by Linnaeus; one name worldwide.
- Five kingdoms (Whittaker): Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia, based on nucleus, cell number, cell wall and mode of nutrition.
- Plant groups: algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes (vascular tissue, no seeds), gymnosperms (naked seeds), angiosperms (flowers and fruits; monocot and dicot).
- Animals: invertebrates (sponges, Cnidaria, flatworms, roundworms, annelids, arthropods, molluscs, echinoderms) and vertebrates (fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals).
- A dichotomous key uses paired yes/no questions about visible features; q questions can separate up to 2<sup>q</sup> organisms.

</div>
