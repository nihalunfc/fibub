**Science · Class 9 · Chapter 6 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you will be able to add forces and decide if they are balanced, state and apply Newton's three laws, explain inertia, calculate momentum and force with F = ma, and use conservation of momentum for collisions, recoil and rockets.

<div class="bigidea">A force is a push or a pull. If the forces on a body add up to zero, its motion does not change. If they do not, the body accelerates in the direction of the net force: a = F/m. Forces always come in pairs on two different bodies, and in any collision or explosion the total momentum, mass × velocity, stays the same.</div>

## 1. Forces: balanced and unbalanced

A **force** is a push or a pull. It can start a body moving, stop it, change its speed or its direction, or change its shape (squeezing a sponge). The SI unit is the **newton (N)**. A small apple weighs about 1 N.

A force has a size and a direction, so it is a **vector**. When several forces act on one body we **add them as vectors** to find the **net force** (the resultant). Forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract.

- **Balanced forces** add up to zero. The body does not accelerate: if it was at rest it stays at rest, and if it was moving it keeps moving at the same velocity. A book on a table: the table's upward push balances its weight.
- **Unbalanced forces** have a net force that is not zero. The body accelerates in the direction of the net force.

Set the two pushes on this block. Make them equal, then unequal, then turn on the rough floor:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-force-balance"><figcaption>The block weighs 10 kg. Net force = right push minus left push. Equal pushes: balanced, no acceleration. Unequal: the block accelerates by F/m. On the smooth floor, even a block that is already moving keeps moving when the net force is zero. On the rough floor, friction holds the block still for small pushes (up to 30 N) and slows it once it slides.</figcaption></figure>

**Friction** is the force that opposes sliding between two surfaces. It acts opposite to the (attempted) motion. It is why a rolling ball slows down, and why you cannot walk on ice.

<div class="myth">

**“A moving body must have a force acting on it in the direction of motion.”** No: a body moves at constant velocity with zero net force, such as a hockey puck on ice, or a car cruising at steady speed on a flat road (the engine's forward force exactly balances friction and air drag). Force is needed to **change** the motion, not to keep it going.

</div>

## 2. Newton's first law and inertia

**Newton's first law:** a body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, **unless an unbalanced force acts on it**.

This tendency to keep its state of rest or motion is called **inertia**. You feel it every day:

- When a bus **brakes**, you lurch **forward**: your body tends to keep moving at the old speed.
- When a bus **starts**, you tip **backwards**: your body tends to stay at rest.
- Dust flies out of a carpet when you beat it: the carpet moves, the dust stays.
- Mud flies off a spinning wheel along the tangent.

**Mass is the measure of inertia.** The greater the mass, the greater the inertia, and the harder it is to start or stop: an empty auto-rickshaw is much easier to push than a loaded truck. The SI unit of mass is the **kilogram**. Mass is the same everywhere, but **weight** (the pull of gravity, W = mg) changes: a 60 kg student weighs about 588 N on Earth and only about 96 N on the Moon, where g ≈ 1.6 m/s². Her mass is still 60 kg.

<div class="myth">

**“Mass and weight are the same thing.”** Mass is the amount of matter (kg) and measures inertia; it never changes with place. Weight is the gravitational force on the mass (N). A shop's "weighing machine" really measures weight and shows it in kilograms, which is fine on Earth.

## 3. Momentum

A cricket ball and a tennis ball both move at 20 m/s. Which is harder to stop? The cricket ball: it has more mass. A fast, light bullet can be as hard to stop as a slow, heavy truck. We need a quantity that combines mass and velocity: **momentum**.

```math
p = m v
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>p</dt><dd>momentum, in kg m/s</dd>
<dt>m</dt><dd>mass, in kg</dd>
<dt>v</dt><dd>velocity, in m/s (momentum points the same way as the velocity)</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Momentum is the "quantity of motion": how hard a body is to stop. Twice the mass or twice the speed gives twice the momentum.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

A 0.16 kg cricket ball at 30 m/s has p = 0.16 × 30 = 4.8 kg m/s. A 1000 kg car at 15 m/s has 15 000 kg m/s.

## 4. Newton's second law: F = ma

**Newton's second law:** the rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the unbalanced force applied, and takes place in the direction of the force.

Step through how this becomes the famous equation:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-force-derive">
<div class="step">Momentum is mass times velocity. Our 2 kg trolley has p = 2 kg m/s at 1 m/s and p = 14 kg m/s when it reaches 7 m/s.</div>
<div class="step">The change in momentum is Δp = mv − mu = m(v − u) = 14 − 2 = 12 kg m/s. It happened in t = 2 s.</div>
<div class="step">The second law says F is proportional to the <b>rate</b> of change of momentum, Δp/t. A bigger change, or the same change in less time, needs a bigger force.</div>
<div class="step">Choose units so that the constant of proportionality is 1 (this defines the newton). Then F = m(v − u)/t. But (v − u)/t is the acceleration a, so <b>F = ma</b>. 1 N is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s².</div>
<div class="step">Check with numbers: a = (7 − 1)/2 = 3 m/s², F = 2 × 3 = 6 N. Using momentum: Δp/t = 12/2 = 6 N. The two ways agree.</div>
</div>

```math
F = ma \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F = \frac{m(v-u)}{t}
```

Race two trolleys: the reference one has 10 N pushing 5 kg. Change the force and the mass of the other and see the acceleration change:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-force-fma"><figcaption>a = F/m. Double the force on the same mass and the acceleration doubles. Double the mass with the same force and the acceleration halves. The dots are 0.5 s apart.</figcaption></figure>

**Using F = ma.** Find the **net** force (add all forces with signs), then divide by the mass. A 1000 kg car with an engine force of 3000 N against 1000 N of friction and drag has a net force of 2000 N and so a = 2 m/s². The unit chain: N = kg m/s², so N ÷ kg = m/s².

<div class="problem" data-problem="c9s-force-fma"></div>

**Why heavy things fall as fast as light ones.** The weight of a body is W = mg, so its acceleration is a = W/m = mg/m = g. The mass cancels. That is why a heavy ball and a light ball, without air resistance, hit the ground together. See it in [Free fall](/learn/free-fall/).

**Soft hands.** In the force formula F = Δp/t, a longer time means a smaller force for the same change of momentum. A fielder catching a fast ball pulls her hands back to stretch the stopping time. A high jumper lands on a thick foam mat, a car has a crumple zone, and a seat belt and an airbag lengthen the stopping time of a passenger. Same momentum to remove, smaller force.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-force-soft"><figcaption>The ball’s momentum change is always 4.8 kg m/s. The coloured rectangle on the force-time graph always has the same area, 4.8 N s (this area is called the impulse). Moving the hands back widens the rectangle and so lowers it: a 2 cm stop needs 3600 N, a 50 cm stop only 144 N.</figcaption></figure>

## 5. Newton's third law

**Newton's third law:** to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. More carefully: whenever body A exerts a force on body B, B exerts a force on A that is **equal in size and opposite in direction**. The two forces are a pair. They act at the **same time**, and **on different bodies**.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-force-pairs">
<div class="step">Hook two spring balances together and pull. Both read the same, 20 N. Neither can pull harder than the other is pulling back.</div>
<div class="step">A skater on roller skates pushes a wall. The wall pushes her back with an equal force and she rolls away. The wall did not move because it is fixed to the Earth.</div>
<div class="step">A swimmer pushes water backwards. The water pushes the swimmer forwards by the same amount.</div>
<div class="step">A balloon: the balloon pushes air out of its neck, and the air pushes the balloon the other way, so it flies off.</div>
<div class="step">A rocket throws burning gas downwards and the gas pushes the rocket up. It needs nothing to push against, so rockets work in the vacuum of space. (This is how ISRO’s launch vehicles climb.)</div>
<div class="step">Weight and the table's push on a book are equal and opposite, but they are <b>not</b> an action-reaction pair, because both act on the same book. The partner of the Earth pulling the book is the book pulling the Earth.</div>
</div>

**Why don't action and reaction cancel?** Because they act on **different bodies**. Forces only cancel when they act on the **same** body. When you push a heavy trolley, you push it and it pushes you back equally, but the push on the trolley moves the trolley and the push on you is balanced by your feet on the ground.

**Same force, different effects.** When a gun fires, the force on the bullet and the force on the gun are equal. The accelerations differ because the masses differ: a = F/m. The light bullet accelerates enormously and the heavy gun only a little.

<div class="myth">

**“The reaction is weaker because the object is lighter.”** The two forces are always exactly equal in size. A fly hitting a bus windscreen pushes on the bus with the same force as the bus pushes on the fly. The fly feels a huge acceleration (small mass), the bus feels a tiny one.

</div>

## 6. Conservation of momentum

When bodies interact (collide, push apart, explode) they exert equal and opposite forces on each other for the same time. So the **changes in their momenta are equal and opposite** and the **total momentum is unchanged**, provided no outside (external) force acts.

> The total momentum of an isolated system before an interaction = the total momentum after it.

For two bodies: 

```math
m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2
```

Momentum is a vector, so use a sign for direction (take right as +, left as −).

**Collisions.** Two trolleys meet. If they stick together afterwards,

```math
v = \frac{m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2}{m_1 + m_2}
```

The red and blue bars change in the collision, but the green total is the same. Try heavy against light, and a head-on collision where the totals cancel.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c9s-force-stick"></div>

**Recoil of a gun.** Gun and bullet are at rest, so the total momentum is zero. After firing, the bullet has momentum mv forwards, so the gun must have the same momentum backwards: MV = mv. So V = mv/M. A 20 g bullet at 400 m/s from a 4 kg gun gives p = 0.02 × 400 = 8 kg m/s, and the gun recoils at V = 8/4 = 2 m/s. The heavier the gun, the smaller the kick: that is why a rifle (held against the shoulder, making a bigger effective mass) kicks less than the same bullet fired from a light pistol.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-force-recoil"><figcaption>Press Fire. The bullet’s momentum (blue bar) and the gun’s (orange, backwards) are equal and opposite so the total stays 0. Change the bullet and the gun masses and see how the recoil speed changes: a heavy gun recoils slowly.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c9s-force-recoil"></div>

**Rockets.** A rocket and its fuel are at rest on the pad: total momentum zero. The engine throws hot gas **downwards** at a very high speed, every second. The gas gains downward momentum, so the rocket gains an equal **upward** momentum. The rocket also gets lighter as it burns fuel, so the same thrust gives a larger acceleration. The same idea is behind a balloon darting about the room, a boat when you jump off it, and a hose that kicks back when the water gushes out.

**Where momentum is not conserved.** The rule needs **no outside force**. A car skidding to a stop on a road loses momentum, because the road's friction is an outside force. The car's momentum goes to the Earth, which is so heavy that its change of speed cannot be seen.

<div class="keypoints">

- A force is a push or pull (vector, unit newton). Add forces as vectors to find the net force. Balanced forces: net force zero, no change in motion. Unbalanced: acceleration in the direction of the net force.
- First law: a body stays at rest or in uniform motion unless an unbalanced force acts. Inertia is that tendency; mass measures it.
- Mass (kg) is constant; weight W = mg (N) depends on g.
- Momentum p = mv (kg m/s, a vector).
- Second law: F = ma, from F = change in momentum ÷ time. 1 N = 1 kg m/s². A longer stopping time means a smaller force (soft hands, seat belts, airbags).
- Third law: forces come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different bodies, at the same time; they do not cancel.
- Conservation of momentum (no outside force): m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂. Stick together: v = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁ + m₂).
- Recoil: MV = mv. Rockets work by throwing gas backwards; they need no air.

</div>
