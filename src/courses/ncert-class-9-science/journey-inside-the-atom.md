**Science · Class 9 · Chapter 8 · about 45 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe how our picture of the atom changed from Thomson to Rutherford to Bohr, name and count the particles inside an atom, use atomic number and mass number, tell isotopes from isobars, write the electron arrangement of the first 20 elements and work out valency.

<div class="bigidea">Atoms are not the smallest things there are. Each has a tiny, heavy, positive nucleus (protons and neutrons) with light electrons in shells around it. The number of protons decides the element; the electrons in the outer shell decide how it behaves in chemistry.</div>

## 1. Atoms have parts: from Thomson to Bohr

For a long time people thought atoms were solid and could not be divided. In 1897 J. J. Thomson found that metal atoms can give out tiny negative particles, much lighter than any atom: **electrons**. So atoms must have parts. But an atom is neutral overall. Where is the positive charge?

### Thomson’s model (1904): the plum pudding

Thomson pictured the atom as a ball of positive charge with electrons stuck in it, like plums in a pudding (or watermelon seeds in the red fruit). The total positive and negative charges cancel, so the atom is neutral.

### Rutherford’s gold-foil experiment (1909 to 1911)

Ernest Rutherford’s team fired fast alpha particles (heavy, positive; they are helium nuclei) at a very thin gold foil and watched where they landed on a screen. If Thomson was right, the spread-out positive charge could only bend the alpha particles a tiny bit. But the result was a surprise:

- **Most** passed straight through.
- **A few** were bent through large angles.
- **About 1 in 8000** bounced almost straight back. Rutherford said this was like firing a cannon shell at tissue paper and having it bounce back.

What Rutherford worked out:

1. Most of the atom is **empty space** (most alphas pass).
2. All the positive charge and almost all the mass sit in a tiny centre, the **nucleus** (a few alphas are turned back; positive repels positive).
3. The electrons circle outside the nucleus.

How tiny? The nucleus is about 100 000 times narrower than the atom.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-atom-scale"><figcaption>Zoom the atom up to the size of a stadium: the nucleus is only a marble in the middle. In the picture the marble is drawn far too large; at the true scale you could not see it at all.</figcaption></figure>

**But there was a problem.** An electron going round in a circle is always changing direction, and a charge that is accelerated should give out energy, lose speed, and spiral into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second. Atoms should not last. Yet they do.

### Bohr’s model (1913)

Niels Bohr kept the nucleus but made a bold rule: electrons move only in certain fixed circular paths, called **shells** (or orbits, or energy levels). While an electron stays in one shell it does **not** lose energy, so it never spirals in. Each shell has a fixed energy, with shell 1 closest to the nucleus having the least.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-atom-bohr">
<div class="step">Rutherford’s problem: a circling electron should lose energy and spiral in. Atoms would collapse.</div>
<div class="step">Bohr’s fix: electrons are allowed only in fixed shells and lose no energy in them.</div>
<div class="step">Normally the electron sits in the lowest shell, shell 1. The atom is steady.</div>
<div class="step">If the atom takes in exactly the right amount of energy (from heat or light), the electron jumps up to a higher shell.</div>
<div class="step">It falls back and gives out that energy as light. This is why sodium street lamps glow yellow and neon signs glow red: each element gives out its own colours.</div>
</div>

## 2. The three particles of the atom

| Particle | Where | Charge | Mass (relative to a proton) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton (p) | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron (n) | nucleus | 0 | about 1 |
| Electron (e⁻) | in shells outside | −1 | about 1/1836 |

A neutral atom has **equal numbers of protons and electrons**, so the + and − charges cancel. Because the electron is about 1800 times lighter than a proton, nearly all of the atom’s mass is in its nucleus.

## 3. Atomic number and mass number

- The **atomic number (Z)** is the number of protons in the nucleus. It is the “identity card” of an element: all atoms with 6 protons are carbon, with 8 are oxygen. In a neutral atom, Z is also the number of electrons.
- The **mass number (A)** is the number of protons plus neutrons (the **nucleons**, the particles in the nucleus).

```math
A = Z + N \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N = A - Z
```

where N is the number of neutrons. We write an atom as <sup>A</sup><sub>Z</sub>X, for example <sup>23</sup><sub>11</sub>Na: sodium with 11 protons, 12 neutrons and (if neutral) 11 electrons.

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>Z</dt><dd>atomic number: protons in the nucleus (and electrons in a neutral atom)</dd>
<dt>A</dt><dd>mass number: protons + neutrons (a whole number, not the same as the average atomic mass in the periodic table)</dd>
<dt>N</dt><dd>neutron number, A − Z</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Z says <i>which element</i>; A says <i>how heavy this atom is</i>. Change Z and you change the element.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

## 4. Isotopes and isobars

### Isotopes

Hydrogen atoms nearly all have 1 proton and no neutron. But a tiny share have 1 neutron (deuterium) or 2 (tritium). All are hydrogen, since they all have 1 proton. Atoms of the **same element (same Z) that have different mass numbers (different numbers of neutrons)** are **isotopes**.

Since the electrons (and so the chemistry) are the same, isotopes of an element behave almost identically in reactions. Only mass-dependent properties differ slightly.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-atom-isotopes"><figcaption>Choose an element. Notice the proton count never changes, only the neutrons. For chlorine, drag the slider to change how much of each isotope there is and watch the average mass change: with the real 75 : 25 mix it is 35.5, which is why chlorine’s atomic mass is not a whole number.</figcaption></figure>

**Uses of isotopes** (this list is a typical summary):

- Carbon-14: finding the age of ancient wood, bone and cloth (radiocarbon dating).
- Cobalt-60: radiation for treating cancer and for sterilising medical equipment.
- Iodine-131: treating thyroid problems.
- Uranium-235: fuel in nuclear reactors.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c9s-atom-avgmass"></div>

### Isobars

**Isobars** are atoms of **different elements** (different Z) that have the **same mass number**. Argon-40 (18 protons), potassium-40 (19) and calcium-40 (20) all have A = 40. They have different numbers of protons and electrons, so they are different elements with different chemistry.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-atom-isobars"><figcaption>Isobars: the top-left number (A) is the same, the bottom-left number (Z) is different, so the counts of protons, neutrons and electrons all differ. Compare with isotopes, where Z stays fixed.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“Isotopes and isobars are the same thing.”** They are opposites. Isotopes keep the same **Z** (same element, same place in the periodic table; the Greek “topos” means place) and change A. Isobars keep the same **A** (the same weight; “baros” means weight) and change Z.

</div>

## 5. How electrons fill the shells

Electrons are arranged in shells around the nucleus, the shell closest to the nucleus filling first. For the first 20 elements:

- shell 1 holds up to **2** electrons,
- shell 2 holds up to **8**,
- shell 3 holds up to **8**,
- then shell 4 starts (potassium and calcium).

The **electron arrangement** of an atom lists the electrons per shell: sodium (11 electrons) is 2, 8, 1; chlorine (17) is 2, 8, 7. The last shell with electrons in it is the **outer shell** (valence shell), and its electrons are the **valence electrons**.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-atom-fill">
<div class="step">Hydrogen has one electron: shell 1 holds it. Arrangement 1.</div>
<div class="step">Helium has two: shell 1 is full. Arrangement 2. A full outer shell makes helium unreactive.</div>
<div class="step">Lithium has three: the first two fill shell 1, so the third starts shell 2. Arrangement 2, 1.</div>
<div class="step">Neon has ten: 2 in shell 1 and 8 in shell 2. Both shells are full: 2, 8. Another unreactive gas.</div>
<div class="step">Sodium has eleven: the 11th electron has no room in the full shells, so it starts shell 3. Arrangement 2, 8, 1.</div>
<div class="step">Argon has eighteen: 2, 8, 8. Full again and unreactive.</div>
<div class="step">Potassium (19) is 2, 8, 8, 1 and calcium (20) is 2, 8, 8, 2: the next electrons go into shell 4.</div>
</div>

## 6. Valency

Atoms with a **full outer shell** (2 for helium; 8 for neon, argon) are very stable. Other atoms react to get one, by losing, gaining or sharing electrons. The number of electrons an atom gains, loses or shares to get a full outer shell is its **valency** (combining capacity).

- 1 to 3 outer electrons (metals): they are easier to **lose**. Valency = number of outer electrons. Na (2, 8, 1) loses 1: valency 1. Mg (2, 8, 2): 2. Al (2, 8, 3): 3.
- 5 to 7 outer electrons (non-metals): they are easier to **gain**. Valency = 8 − outer electrons. O (2, 6): 8 − 6 = 2. Cl (2, 8, 7): 1.
- 4 outer electrons (carbon, silicon): they **share**; valency 4.
- Full outer shell (He, Ne, Ar): valency 0, unreactive.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-atom-valency"><figcaption>Pick an element and press Show. Sodium’s single outer electron leaves, giving Na⁺ with the full arrangement 2, 8. Chlorine takes one in, giving Cl⁻ with 2, 8, 8. Each ion now has a full outer shell, like neon and argon.</figcaption></figure>

When an atom loses electrons it becomes a positive ion (a **cation**); when it gains electrons it becomes a negative ion (an **anion**). The nucleus does not change: Na has 11 protons whether it is Na or Na⁺.

<div class="keypoints">

- Thomson: plum pudding. Rutherford: tiny dense positive nucleus (from the gold-foil result: most alphas pass, a few bend, very few bounce back). Bohr: electrons in fixed shells that do not lose energy.
- Proton: +1, nucleus. Neutron: 0, nucleus. Electron: −1, outside, about 1/1836 of a proton’s mass.
- Atomic number Z = protons (= electrons in a neutral atom). Mass number A = protons + neutrons; neutrons = A − Z.
- Isotopes: same Z, different A (and the same chemistry). Isobars: different Z, same A.
- Shells fill 2, 8, 8 (then shell 4 for K and Ca). The outer shell holds the valence electrons.
- Valency: outer electrons if 1 to 3 (lose); 8 minus outer electrons for 5 to 7 (gain); 4 shares; full shell gives 0.
- Ions: losing electrons makes a positive ion; gaining makes a negative ion. The number of protons does not change.

</div>
