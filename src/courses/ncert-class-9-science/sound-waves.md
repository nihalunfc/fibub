**Physics · Class 9 · Chapter 10 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you can explain how sound travels, use `$v = f\lambda$`, tell pitch from loudness, work out the distance to a cliff or the sea bed from an echo, and follow a sound through your ear.

<div class="bigidea">Sound is a vibration that travels as a chain of squeezes and stretches through a material. Time that chain over a distance and you can find speed, or find distance: that is how echoes, bats and SONAR work.</div>

## 1. What is sound? A longitudinal wave

Pluck a rubber band or tap a steel plate. It vibrates. The vibrating thing pushes the air next to it, that air pushes the air beyond it, and so on. Nothing travels all the way from the source to your ear except the **disturbance**. The air near you only shakes a tiny bit back and forth, like people in a stadium passing on a wave: the wave goes round the stadium but no person does.

The pushes of air have a special pattern.

- **Compression (C):** a place where particles are squeezed close together. Air pressure here is a little higher than normal.
- **Rarefaction (R):** a place where particles are spread apart. Pressure is a little lower.

The particles move **back and forth along the same line the wave travels in**. A wave like that is a **longitudinal wave**. (A wave on a rope or on water moves particles up and down across the direction of travel: a transverse wave. Sound in air is never transverse.)

Watch it happen. Each dot is a bit of air. Watch the red dot: it never leaves home, it only swings left and right, while the bunched-up part travels on.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-snd-slinky"><figcaption>Air particles in slow motion. C marks a compression (dots crowded, pressure high) and R a rarefaction (dots spread out, pressure low). The green graph under it is the air pressure along the wave. Press Play, then raise the frequency and see the wavelength shrink.</figcaption></figure>

Three facts to remember:

1. Sound needs a **medium** (air, water, steel, anything with particles). There is no sound in a vacuum, which is why astronauts outside a spacecraft cannot hear each other without radios.
2. The wave carries **energy**, not matter.
3. A graph of pressure against distance has crests where there is a compression and troughs where there is a rarefaction, so a longitudinal wave is often drawn like a transverse one.

<div class="myth">

**“Sound is air rushing from the speaker to my ear.”** No. If it were, a speaker would blow your hair back every time. Air particles only vibrate about a fixed spot; the compressions and rarefactions are what travel.

</div>

## 2. Describing a wave: wavelength, frequency, amplitude, speed

Every sound wave can be described with a few measurements.

| Quantity | What it is | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Wavelength (λ)** | the distance from one compression to the next (or one rarefaction to the next) | metre (m) |
| **Frequency (f)** | how many complete vibrations (or waves passing a point) per second | hertz (Hz) |
| **Time period (T)** | the time for one complete vibration, `$T = 1/f$` | second (s) |
| **Amplitude (A)** | the biggest displacement of a particle from its rest position (how big the squeeze is) | metre, or any pressure unit |
| **Speed (v)** | the distance the wave travels in one second | metre per second (m/s) |

If a tuning fork vibrates 256 times in one second, its frequency is 256 Hz and the time for one vibration is 1/256 s, about 0.0039 s.

### The wave equation

In one time period T the wave travels one wavelength, so speed = distance ÷ time = λ ÷ T. And 1/T is f. So:

```math
v = f \lambda
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>v</dt><dd>speed of the wave in m/s (set by the medium)</dd>
<dt>f</dt><dd>frequency in Hz (set by the source)</dd>
<dt>λ</dt><dd>wavelength in m (the one that adjusts: λ = v ÷ f)</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>f waves pass you every second and each is λ long, so the front moves f × λ metres every second.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

## 3. Pitch and loudness

Two simple everyday facts: a mosquito whines high and a bus horn rumbles low; a whisper is soft and a firecracker is loud. These two differences have two different causes.

- **Pitch** is how high or low a note *sounds*. It depends on **frequency**. A small, tight string or a short pipe vibrates fast and gives a high pitch; a long, thick string vibrates slowly and gives a low pitch. The tabla’s small right drum is higher than the big left bayan.
- **Loudness** is how strong a sound *seems*. It depends on **amplitude**: the harder you hit the drum, the bigger the vibration and the louder the sound. Loudness is measured with the **decibel (dB)** scale: a whisper is about 30 dB, normal talk about 60 dB, and heavy traffic about 80 dB. Long exposure to sounds above about 85 dB can damage hearing.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-snd-wave"><figcaption>Move the frequency slider: the waves bunch up and the pitch meter rises, but the height of the waves does not change. Move the amplitude slider: the waves get taller and the loudness meter rises, but the number of waves does not change. Use “Hear it” to listen (keep your volume low).</figcaption></figure>

The more amplitude, the more energy the wave carries. Move away from the source and the same energy spreads over a bigger area, so the sound gets quieter: loudness also depends on how far you are. (The [Decibel distance calculator](/science/decibel-distance/) shows this.)

The **quality (timbre)** of a sound is the third difference. The same note on a sitar and on a flute sound different because each makes a different mix of extra vibrations along with the main one. The [Audio spectrum analyser](/science/audio-spectrum-analyser/) can show that mix for your own voice.

## 4. Speed of sound in different media

Sound is handed on by the particles bumping into their neighbours. The closer and the stiffer the particles, the faster the hand-over, so sound is **slowest in gases, faster in liquids, fastest in solids**.

| Medium | Speed (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Air (20 °C) | 343 m/s |
| Sea water | 1500 m/s |
| Steel | 5000 m/s |

Warm air carries sound faster than cold air (about 0.6 m/s more for each °C), because its particles move faster. Press your ear to a long iron railing while a friend taps it far away: the sound through the metal reaches you first, and the sound through the air arrives a moment later.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-snd-race"><figcaption>Three sound pulses race over the same distance. Change the distance and compare the finishing times. Time = distance ÷ speed.</figcaption></figure>

**Thunder and lightning.** Light is so fast that you see lightning at once, but sound takes about 3 s to cover each kilometre. If the thunder arrives 6 s after the flash, the storm is about 6 × 343 ≈ 2 km away.

## 5. Reflection of sound and echoes

Sound reflects off hard, smooth surfaces in the same way a ball bounces off a wall: it turns back. The angle at which it strikes equals the angle at which it leaves.

An **echo** is a reflected sound that you hear as a separate repeat of the original. For this to work, two things are needed: a large, hard surface (a cliff, a big wall, a hill), and enough distance.

Our ears keep hold of a sound for about **0.1 s** (persistence of hearing). A reflected sound that arrives sooner than that blends into the original and you do not notice a separate echo. In 0.1 s sound goes 34.3 m, there and back, so the wall must be at least **17 m** away.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-snd-echo">
<div class="step">You clap near a cliff 85 m away. The sound spreads out as a wave.</div>
<div class="step">The sound travels to the cliff at about 343 m/s.</div>
<div class="step">The cliff reflects the sound back.</div>
<div class="step">The echo returns to you. The total path is there and back: 2d.</div>
<div class="step">Put the numbers in: 2d = v × t, so d = v × t ÷ 2.</div>
</div>

```math
d = \frac{v \times t}{2}
```

Why divide by 2? Because the time you measure covers the journey *to* the wall and *back*.

### Reverberation

In a large hall, a temple or a stadium, sound reflects from the walls, ceiling and floor again and again. If the reflections arrive less than 0.1 s apart they merge with the original, so the sound seems to **linger**. This is **reverberation**. A little makes music sound rich; too much makes speech muddy.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-snd-reverb"><figcaption>Slide the wall away. Close walls give reflections inside the shaded 0.1 s zone (reverberation); far walls give a separate echo. Switch to a soft wall and the repeats shrink.</figcaption></figure>

Halls reduce reverberation by using **soft, rough materials** that absorb sound: carpets, curtains, cushioned seats, perforated boards, and the audience itself.

### Uses of reflection

- **Megaphones, loudspeaker horns and bugles** send sound forward as a beam by reflecting it from their conical walls.
- **Stethoscopes**: the doctor listens to the heart, whose sound travels along the tube by repeated reflections.
- **Curved sound boards behind a stage** push the sound toward the audience.
- **Curved ceilings** in old halls reflect sound to all corners.

## 6. The range of hearing: infrasound and ultrasound

A healthy young person hears frequencies from about **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz (20 kHz)**. Older people lose the high end.

- **Infrasound**: below 20 Hz. Elephants and whales use it for long-distance calls; earthquakes make it too.
- **Ultrasound**: above 20 kHz. Dogs, bats, dolphins and porpoises hear and use it; many animals hear much more than we do.

<!--fig:tool hearing-range-->

Some animals make good use of this. **Bats** fly in the dark by sending out squeaks and listening to the echoes from insects and walls. Dolphins do the same under water.

## 7. Applications of ultrasound and SONAR

Ultrasound is useful because it goes in a narrow beam, carries a lot of energy, and is reflected by even small objects. People use it for:

- **Cleaning** in a bath of liquid: the vibrations shake dirt out of watch parts, jewellery and electronic circuits.
- **Medical imaging (sonography):** echoes from body tissues build a picture of a baby in the womb, the heart or the liver, with no X-rays.
- **Finding flaws in metal**: bridges, railway tracks, pipes and aircraft parts are scanned for hidden cracks.
- **Breaking kidney stones** by focused beams, and **SONAR** at sea.

### SONAR

**SONAR** stands for **SO**und **N**avigation **A**nd **R**anging. A ship sends an ultrasonic pulse down, a receiver listens for the echo from the sea bed, a rock, a shoal of fish or a submarine, and the time of the echo gives the distance. The depth is d = v × t ÷ 2, where v is the speed of sound in sea water (about 1500 m/s).

Why ultrasound and not ordinary sound for SONAR? It travels in a narrow, controllable beam and is not confused with the many ordinary sounds of the sea and the ship.

## 8. The human ear

Our ear turns a pressure wave in the air into electrical signals for the brain. Follow one sound all the way through:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-snd-ear">
<div class="step">The pinna collects sound waves and funnels them in.</div>
<div class="step">The ear canal carries them to the eardrum.</div>
<div class="step">The eardrum vibrates with each compression and rarefaction.</div>
<div class="step">The hammer, anvil and stirrup pass on the vibration and strengthen it.</div>
<div class="step">In the fluid-filled cochlea, hair cells change vibration into electrical signals.</div>
<div class="step">The auditory nerve carries the signals to the brain, which makes sense of the sound.</div>
</div>

The **outer ear** is the pinna and canal, the **middle ear** is the eardrum and the three tiny bones (the smallest in the body), and the **inner ear** is the cochlea. Your brain also compares the signals from both ears to tell which direction a sound came from. Very loud noise damages the delicate hair cells, and they do not grow back: earphones should not be played at full volume for hours.

<div class="keypoints">

- Sound is a longitudinal wave: particles move back and forth along the direction of travel, forming compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure). It needs a medium and cannot travel in a vacuum.
- Wavelength λ (m), frequency f (Hz), time period T = 1/f, amplitude A, and speed v = f × λ. The frequency is set by the source; the speed by the medium.
- Pitch depends on frequency; loudness depends on amplitude (measured in decibels); quality (timbre) depends on the mix of vibrations.
- Speed of sound: solids > liquids > gases. About 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, 1500 m/s in sea water, 5000 m/s in steel. Warm air is faster than cold.
- Echo: a reflected sound heard as a separate sound, needs a hard surface at least 17 m away (delay 0.1 s or more); distance d = v × t ÷ 2.
- Reverberation: reflections less than 0.1 s apart blur into the sound; reduced by soft, absorbing materials.
- Hearing range is about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Below is infrasound, above is ultrasound.
- Ultrasound is used for cleaning, medical scans, flaw detection and SONAR; SONAR depth d = v × t ÷ 2.
- Sound passes through the pinna, ear canal, eardrum, three small bones and the cochlea, and the auditory nerve takes the signals to the brain.

</div>
