**Science · Class 9 · Chapter 5 · about 55 minutes.** By the end you will be able to tell pure substances from mixtures, work out the concentration of a solution, tell solutions, colloids and suspensions apart with the Tyndall effect, and choose the right method to separate any mixture: evaporation, centrifugation, a separating funnel, sublimation, chromatography, distillation, fractional distillation and crystallisation.

<div class="bigidea">Most things around us are mixtures: milk, sea water, air, ink, a plate of dal-rice. To separate a mixture, look for a property in which its parts differ (size, magnetism, solubility, boiling point, how fast they travel) and use that difference.</div>

## 1. Pure substances and mixtures

A **pure substance** has just one kind of particle: an **element** (one kind of atom, like copper or oxygen) or a **compound** (different atoms joined in a fixed ratio, like water, H₂O). It has a fixed composition and sharp, fixed melting and boiling points.

A **mixture** has two or more substances that are only mixed, not joined. The amounts can vary, and each part keeps its own properties.

- **Homogeneous mixture:** the same throughout, the parts cannot be seen: sugar in water, air, vinegar, brass (copper and zinc), tea after stirring.
- **Heterogeneous mixture:** the parts can be seen or are not evenly spread: sand and water, oil and water, salad, muddy puddle.

<div class="myth">

**“Water is a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen.”** Water is a compound: the hydrogen and oxygen atoms are chemically joined in a fixed ratio (2 to 1), with properties unlike either gas. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gas would keep the properties of each and could have any proportion.

</div>

## 2. Solutions and concentration

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The substance that dissolves is the **solute**; the one that does the dissolving, present in larger amount, is the **solvent**. In sweet tea, sugar is the solute and water the solvent. Solutions need not be liquid: air is a gas solution, brass is a solid solution.

Properties of a true solution:

- it is homogeneous, and the particles (smaller than 1 nm) cannot be seen even with a microscope;
- the particles do not settle however long it stands;
- it passes through filter paper unchanged;
- it does **not** scatter a beam of light.

**Concentration** tells how much solute there is in a given amount of solution. A **saturated** solution holds as much solute as it can at that temperature; an **unsaturated** one can still take more. The amount that dissolves in 100 g of solvent is the **solubility**, and for most solids it rises with temperature (hot tea dissolves more sugar than cold).

Two common ways to give concentration:

```math
\text{mass percent} = \frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100
\qquad
\text{mass by volume} = \frac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (mL)}} \times 100
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>Mass of solution</dt><dd>mass of solute + mass of solvent (not just the solvent)</dd>
<dt>Mass percent</dt><dd>grams of solute in every 100 g of solution</dd>
<dt>Mass by volume</dt><dd>grams of solute in every 100 mL of solution (written g per 100 mL or % w/v); used for liquids like saline</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>A 5% sugar solution has 5 g of sugar in each 100 g of solution. Double the sugar in the same solution and the concentration roughly doubles: it tastes twice as sweet.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Mass percent means the grams of solute in 100 g of water.”** It is the grams in 100 g of the whole **solution**. 10 g of salt in 90 g of water gives a 10% solution (10 g in 100 g), not an 11%.

## 3. Suspensions and colloids: the Tyndall effect

Not every mixture is a true solution. Look at the size of the particles:

| | True solution | Colloid | Suspension |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle size | under 1 nm | 1 nm to 1000 nm | over 1000 nm |
| Seen by eye? | no | no (cloudy or milky) | yes |
| Settles on standing? | no | no | **yes** |
| Passes through filter paper? | yes | yes | **no** |
| Scatters light (Tyndall effect)? | no | **yes** | yes (cloudy) |
| Examples | salt water, sugar water | milk, fog, ink, blood, butter, jelly | muddy water, chalk in water, flour in water |

A **colloid** is in between: a **dispersed phase** (small particles) spread through a **dispersion medium**. Milk is tiny fat droplets in water; fog is water droplets in air; smoke is solid particles in air; butter is water in fat.

The **Tyndall effect** is the scattering of light by the particles of a colloid, making the beam visible. A torch beam through a dusty room, sunlight through trees in a morning mist, and car headlights in fog all show it. A true solution is too fine to scatter light, so the beam passes through unseen.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-mix-tyndall"><figcaption>Choose a mixture, switch the torch on, and press “Let it stand”. The solution’s beam stays invisible; in the colloid the beam shows up; the suspension shows it too but its particles settle to the bottom when it stands.</figcaption></figure>

Shine a beam through different liquids with the tool below and see which scatter it.

<div class="myth">

**“A colloid is the same as a suspension.”** A suspension settles and can be filtered; a colloid does not settle and cannot be filtered out by ordinary filter paper. Both can scatter light, but only colloids stay evenly mixed over time.

</div>

## 4. Separating mixtures

The aim: get each component out, ideally pure. Pick the method by the property that is different.

### Evaporation and crystallisation (dissolved solids from liquids)

To get dissolved salt back from salt water, heat it. The water **evaporates** and the salt is left behind. Salt-pans on the Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coast do this with the sun. But heating to dryness can spoil a substance that breaks down (like copper sulphate crystals, or sugar from cane juice which would burn). Instead the solution is heated until it is **saturated**, then left to **cool slowly**. Because the solubility falls as it cools, the extra solute comes out as pure **crystals** and the impurities stay dissolved. This is **crystallisation**, used for alum, sugar and copper sulphate.

Below about 55 °C the dashed line (100 g dissolved) is above the curve, so the extra forms crystals.</figcaption></figure>

### Filtration, decantation and magnets

For a suspension such as sand in water, **filtration** with filter paper holds the solid (**residue**) and lets the liquid (**filtrate**) through. Simple **decantation** is pouring off the clear liquid after the solid has settled. A **magnet** pulls out iron filings from sand, and **winnowing** (blowing off light husk) and **handpicking** are used on grain.

### Centrifugation (tiny solid or liquid particles)

When the particles are too small to settle fast (a colloid like milk, or blood), spin the mixture. The strong outward pull makes the denser parts move to the far end of the tube and the lighter to the inner end. A dairy separates cream from milk; a lab separates blood cells from plasma; a washing machine spinner dries clothes.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-mix-centrifuge"><figcaption>Set the speed and press Start. Fat droplets (pink dots) are lighter than the watery milk so they stay near the centre as cream, while the denser watery part is thrown to the outer end. The readout gives the pull relative to gravity (RCF).</figcaption></figure>

Work out spin speed and force with the [Centrifuge calculator](/science/centrifuge-calculator/).

### Separating funnel (two liquids that do not mix)

Oil and water form two layers because they are **immiscible**; the denser one (water) is at the bottom. In a **separating funnel** you run the lower layer out through the tap and stop when the boundary reaches it. This is also how ether is separated from water in labs, and how oil is separated from water after a spill clean-up.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-mix-funnel"><figcaption>Press Shake: the two liquids break into droplets. Press “Let it stand” and they separate into layers. Slide the tap open to run off the water and stop before the oil leaves.</figcaption></figure>

### Sublimation (a solid that turns directly to gas)

A few solids, like **ammonium chloride, camphor, naphthalene and iodine**, change from solid to gas without melting. This is called **sublimation**. If such a solid is mixed with something that does not sublime (sand, salt), heat the mixture: the vapour condenses back to solid on a cold surface above.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-mix-subl">
<div class="step">A mixture of sand and ammonium chloride.</div>
<div class="step">Cover the dish with an inverted funnel, plug the stem with cotton, heat gently.</div>
<div class="step">The ammonium chloride turns directly to vapour.</div>
<div class="step">The vapour touches the cool funnel wall and turns straight back to a solid.</div>
<div class="step">Pure ammonium chloride collects on the funnel; sand stays in the dish.</div>
</div>

### Chromatography (dissolved substances that travel at different speeds)

A drop of black ink is placed on a pencil line near the bottom of a strip of paper that dips in water. The water climbs up the paper and carries the dyes with it, but each dye clings to the paper by a different amount, so they are carried different distances and **separate into spots**. This is **paper chromatography**; it is also used to test food colours, plant pigments and drugs.

The **Rf value** (retention factor) of a spot is:

```math
R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}
```

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-mix-chroma"><figcaption>Press Play. The solvent front climbs, and the yellow, red and blue dyes follow at different speeds: the one that sticks least goes furthest. The readout shows each dye’s distance and Rf.</figcaption></figure>

### Distillation (a liquid from a dissolved solid, or from another liquid)

In **simple distillation** a solution is boiled, and only the solvent turns to vapour. The vapour is cooled in a **condenser** and collected as pure liquid (the **distillate**). It is how distilled water is made from salt water, and how a colourless liquid is taken from ink or copper sulphate solution.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-mix-distill">
<div class="step">Set up the flask, thermometer, condenser with cold water running through it, and a receiver.</div>
<div class="step">Heat the flask: the water boils at about 100 °C; the salt stays behind.</div>
<div class="step">Water vapour rises into the condenser while the thermometer stays near 100 °C.</div>
<div class="step">The cold jacket condenses the vapour back into drops of liquid.</div>
<div class="step">Pure water drips into the receiver; salt is left in the flask.</div>
</div>

When two **miscible** liquids (liquids that mix completely) have boiling points close together, such as ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C), simple distillation gives a mixed distillate. We use **fractional distillation**: a **fractionating column** packed with glass beads is fixed above the flask. It is cooler near the top, so vapour of the higher-boiling liquid condenses and falls back, while the more volatile liquid goes on up. The thermometer shows when each liquid is coming over.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c9-mix-frac">
<div class="step">Ethanol and water mix completely; their boiling points differ (78 °C and 100 °C).</div>
<div class="step">On heating, both evaporate, but the vapour is richer in the more volatile ethanol.</div>
<div class="step">In the column, water vapour condenses and drips back down; ethanol vapour keeps rising.</div>
<div class="step">Ethanol reaches the top at 78 °C and is collected in the first receiver.</div>
<div class="step">When the temperature rises to 100 °C, change the receiver and collect water.</div>
</div>

Fractional distillation is used in industry too: **crude oil** into petrol, kerosene and diesel; **liquid air** into nitrogen, oxygen and argon; and in making alcohol.

<div class="myth">

**“Evaporation and distillation do the same thing.”** In evaporation the vapour is lost to the air and you keep the solid. In distillation the vapour is cooled and collected, so you get the pure liquid too.

</div>

## 5. Physical and chemical changes

While separating mixtures, no new substance is made: the methods use **physical** differences. A **physical change** alters form, state or appearance but not the substance: melting ice, boiling water, dissolving sugar, cutting paper, crushing a rock. It is usually easy to reverse.

In a **chemical change** one or more **new substances** are formed with new properties: rusting of iron, burning of wood or a candle, curdling of milk, cooking an egg, digestion, the baking of a cake. It is usually hard to reverse, and often has a signal such as a colour change, a gas, a solid forming, heat or light.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c9-mix-change"><figcaption>Pick an example and drag “How far it has gone”. In the physical changes (ice, sugar) the same particles just move or spread. In the chemical changes (rust, candle) the starting particles change into new kinds. The ticks apply the three tests to each.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“Dissolving is a chemical change because the solid disappears.”** Dissolving sugar in water is a physical change: the sugar molecules are still there, spread out between the water molecules, and you can get them back by evaporating the water. Nothing new has been made.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Pure substances (elements and compounds) have a fixed composition; mixtures can vary. Mixtures are homogeneous (even) or heterogeneous (uneven).
- A solution has a solute dissolved in a solvent. Mass percent = solute mass ÷ solution mass × 100; mass by volume = solute mass ÷ solution volume × 100.
- Solution particles are under 1 nm, colloid particles 1 to 1000 nm, suspension particles over 1000 nm. Colloids show the Tyndall effect and do not settle; suspensions settle and can be filtered.
- Methods: evaporation, crystallisation, filtration, centrifugation, separating funnel, sublimation, chromatography, distillation, fractional distillation. Choose by the property that differs.
- Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front, always between 0 and 1.
- Simple distillation separates a liquid from a dissolved solid; fractional distillation separates liquids whose boiling points are close.
- Physical change: no new substance, usually reversible. Chemical change: new substances, usually not easily reversible.

</div>
