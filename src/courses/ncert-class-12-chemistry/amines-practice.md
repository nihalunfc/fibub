**Practice · Class 12 Chemistry · Chapter 9.** Read the chapter lesson, [Amines](/learn/courses/ncert-class-12-chemistry/amines/), first. Try each question on paper before opening the answer. Marks go for the correct reagent, the product, and a short reason that mentions the lone pair, the +I effect or the number of N–H bonds.

<div class="bigidea">Most answers come from the lone pair on nitrogen. Is it free (basic, nucleophilic) or shared with a ring (weak base, activated ring)? And how many N–H bonds are there (hydrogen bonding, Hinsberg, carbylamine, acylation)?</div>

## How to set out answers

1. For a preparation, name the method and write the equation with reagents; check the carbon count (Hofmann: one fewer; nitrile: one more).
2. For a comparison of basic strength, say which effect wins: +I, solvation or resonance.
3. For diazonium chemistry, write the temperature (273 to 278 K) and the reagent for the replacement.

## 1-mark questions

1. Write the IUPAC name of C₂H₅NHCH₃.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>N-methylethanamine.</details>

2. Which reagent reduces nitrobenzene to aniline in industry?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Iron filings with a little HCl (or H₂/Pd).</details>

3. Name the reaction that converts a diazonium salt to chlorobenzene using CuCl.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Sandmeyer reaction.</details>

4. What colour is the dye formed when benzenediazonium chloride couples with phenol?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Orange (p-hydroxyazobenzene).</details>

5. Which class of amine does not react with benzenesulphonyl chloride?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Tertiary amines.</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

6. (2) Why is the boiling point of propan-1-amine (49 °C) higher than that of N,N-dimethylmethanamine (3 °C)?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Propan-1-amine is primary and has N–H bonds, so it forms hydrogen bonds between its molecules. The tertiary isomer has no N–H and only weak dipole forces.</details>

7. (2) Why is Gabriel phthalimide synthesis not used to prepare aniline?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The phthalimide anion would need to attack an aryl halide by nucleophilic substitution, and aryl halides (C–X on an sp² carbon with partial double-bond character) do not undergo this reaction.</details>

8. (2) Give one chemical test to distinguish ethylamine from aniline.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Azo-dye test: nitrous acid (NaNO₂/HCl) at 273 to 278 K with aniline gives a diazonium salt that couples with β-naphthol to give an orange-red dye. Ethylamine gives nitrogen gas with bubbles, but no dye. (Aniline also gives a white precipitate with bromine water; ethylamine does not.)</details>

9. (3) Explain why ethylamine is soluble in water but aniline is only slightly soluble.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ethylamine forms hydrogen bonds with water through N–H···O and O–H···N and its alkyl group is small. Aniline has a large hydrophobic benzene ring that outweighs the effect of the NH₂ group, and its lone pair is less available for hydrogen bonding because it is delocalised.</details>

10. (3) Arrange in decreasing order of basic strength in water: (CH₃)₃N, CH₃NH₂, (CH₃)₂NH, C₆H₅NH₂. Give the reasons.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > C₆H₅NH₂. Methyl groups push electrons onto N (+I) and raise basicity. In water the tertiary amine falls below the primary one because its ion is poorly hydrated and crowded. Aniline is weakest because the lone pair is delocalised into the ring.</details>

11. (3) Write equations for: (a) acetylation of aniline with ethanoyl chloride, (b) bromination of the product, (c) hydrolysis to give p-bromoaniline.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) C₆H₅NH₂ + CH₃COCl → C₆H₅NHCOCH₃ + HCl. (b) Br₂ in ethanoic acid brominates mainly at the para position: 4-bromoacetanilide (the acetyl group makes the ring less reactive so substitution stops at one Br). (c) Hydrolysis with acid or base gives 4-bromoaniline.</details>

12. (3) 12.1 g of benzamide (121 g/mol) is converted to aniline (93 g/mol) by Hofmann degradation with 80 % yield. Find the mass of aniline.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Moles of benzamide = 12.1/121 = 0.100 mol. Theoretical aniline = 0.100 × 93 = 9.3 g. Actual mass = 80 % × 9.3 = 7.44 g.

</details>

## 5-mark questions

13. (5) Convert (a) nitrobenzene to phenol, (b) aniline to fluorobenzene, (c) aniline to benzoic acid. Give all reagents.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Nitrobenzene → (Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl) aniline → (NaNO₂/HCl, 273 to 278 K) benzenediazonium chloride → (warm water) phenol.

(b) Aniline → (NaNO₂/HCl, 273 to 278 K) diazonium chloride → (HBF₄) diazonium tetrafluoroborate → (heat) fluorobenzene (Balz–Schiemann).

(c) Aniline → diazonium chloride → (CuCN/KCN) benzonitrile → (H₃O⁺, heat) benzoic acid.

</details>

14. (5) (a) How does the Hinsberg test distinguish 1°, 2° and 3° amines? (b) Why does the product of a primary amine dissolve in NaOH? (c) Write the equation for the carbylamine reaction of methylamine.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) The amine is shaken with benzenesulphonyl chloride in aqueous NaOH. A primary amine gives a sulphonamide that dissolves in NaOH (clear solution), which precipitates again on acidifying. A secondary amine gives an insoluble sulphonamide (a precipitate in NaOH). A tertiary amine does not react: it remains as an oily layer and dissolves in HCl.

(b) The sulphonamide RNH–SO₂C₆H₅ still has an N–H, made acidic by the SO₂ group, so NaOH removes it to give a soluble sodium salt.

(c) CH₃NH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3 KOH → CH₃NC + 3 KCl + 3 H₂O (methyl isocyanide, a foul smell).

</details>

## Case-based question

15. **Case.** A chemist wants to prepare 4-bromoaniline from benzene. She nitrates benzene, reduces the nitro compound to aniline, and then tries bromine water. She obtains only a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromoaniline.

(a) Name the first two steps and their reagents. (1)
(b) Why does bromine water give the tribromo product? (1)
(c) How could she avoid this? (1)
(d) Which diazonium reaction could she use to replace the NH₂ group later? (1)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Nitration (conc. HNO₃/conc. H₂SO₄) then reduction (Fe/HCl or Sn/HCl). (b) The –NH₂ group is strongly activating: it pushes electrons to all three ortho and para positions. (c) Acetylate the NH₂ first (acetanilide), brominate, then hydrolyse. (d) For example, H₃PO₂ removes it, CuCl gives a chloro compound, and so on.

</details>

## Assertion and reason

16. **Assertion (A):** Aniline does not undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation.
**Reason (R):** Aniline forms a salt with the AlCl₃ catalyst.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both true and R explains A: the nitrogen donates its lone pair to AlCl₃, giving a positively charged group on the ring that deactivates it. Option 1.</details>

17. **Assertion (A):** Diazonium salts of aromatic amines are more stable than those of aliphatic amines.
**Reason (R):** The positive charge of the diazonium group is delocalised into the benzene ring.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both true and R explains A. Option 1.</details>

18. **Assertion (A):** Tertiary amines are the strongest bases in aqueous solution.
**Reason (R):** They have three electron-donating alkyl groups.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A is false and R is true. In water the tertiary ammonium ion is poorly solvated and the nitrogen is crowded, so the secondary amine is the strongest in the methyl and ethyl series.</details>

- Diazonium salts: cold (273 to 278 K); replace N₂ by Cl, Br, CN, I, F, H, OH; couple to make azo dyes.
