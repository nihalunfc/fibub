**Practice · Class 12 Chemistry · Chapter 1.** Read the chapter lesson, [Solutions](/learn/courses/ncert-class-12-chemistry/solutions/), first. Try each question on paper before opening the answer. Board marks go for the law or formula, the substitution with units, and the final answer.

<div class="bigidea">Every solutions question starts with the same move: turn what you are given (grams, percent, volume) into <b>moles</b>. Then pick the right measure, molality for temperature effects, molarity for volumes, mole fraction for vapour pressure, and add the van’t Hoff factor if the solute splits.</div>

## How to set out a numerical

1. Write the formula in symbols: for example `$\Delta T_f = i K_f m$`.
2. Convert the data first: grams to moles, g of solvent to kg, mL to L, °C to K.
3. Substitute with units and keep the units through the working.
4. State the answer with its unit and, if asked, say whether it is sensible (for example, a molar mass found from a dissociating solute is too small).

## 1-mark questions

1. Define molality.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The number of moles of solute dissolved in 1 kg of the solvent.</details>

2. State Henry’s law.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>At constant temperature the mole fraction of a gas dissolved in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid.</details>

3. What is meant by an isotonic solution?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Solutions that have the same osmotic pressure at the same temperature, so there is no net osmosis between them.</details>

4. Name the colligative property that is best for finding the molar mass of a protein, and give one reason.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Osmotic pressure: it is large enough to measure even at very low concentrations, while the other effects would be too tiny.</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

5. (2) Why does a solution of a non-volatile solute have a lower vapour pressure than the pure solvent?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Solute particles occupy part of the surface, so fewer solvent molecules can escape into the vapour. Quantitatively, p = x₁p° with x₁ less than 1.</details>

6. (2) Why is molality preferred to molarity in colligative-property formulas?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Molality is defined with masses (moles per kg of solvent), so it does not change with temperature. Molarity uses the volume of the solution, which changes as the liquid expands or contracts.</details>

7. (3) Calculate the molality of a solution of 18 g of glucose (180 g/mol) in 162 g of water, and the mole fraction of glucose.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

n(glucose) = 18/180 = 0.10 mol. Mass of water = 0.162 kg, so `$m = 0.10/0.162 = 0.617$` mol/kg.

n(water) = 162/18 = 9.0 mol, so `$x_2 = 0.10/(0.10+9.0) = 0.011$`.

</details>

8. (3) 1.5 g of a non-electrolyte dissolved in 50 g of water raises its boiling point by 0.26 K (K<sub>b</sub> = 0.52 K kg/mol). Find the molar mass.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$m = \Delta T_b/K_b = 0.26/0.52 = 0.50$` mol/kg. Moles = 0.50 × 0.050 = 0.025 mol. `$M = 1.5/0.025 = 60$` g/mol.

</details>

9. (3) Distinguish between positive and negative deviations from Raoult’s law (A–B interactions, sign of ΔH and ΔV, vapour pressure).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Positive deviation: A–B attractions weaker than A–A and B–B; ΔH(mix) &gt; 0, ΔV(mix) &gt; 0; the vapour pressure is higher than the ideal value (ethanol + hexane). Negative deviation: A–B attractions stronger (for example hydrogen bonding between acetone and chloroform); ΔH(mix) &lt; 0, ΔV(mix) &lt; 0; vapour pressure lower than ideal.</details>

10. (3) Find the osmotic pressure at 300 K of a solution of 5.0 g of glucose (180 g/mol) in 250 mL of solution. R = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$C = (5.0/180)/0.250 = 0.111$` mol/L. `$\pi = CRT = 0.111\times0.0821\times300 = 2.74$` atm.

</details>

## 5-mark questions (model answers)

11. (5) Derive the relation between the relative lowering of vapour pressure and the molar mass of a non-volatile solute.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

By Raoult’s law, for the solvent `$p_1 = x_1p_1^\circ$`. Since `$x_1 = 1-x_2$`, `$\dfrac{p_1^\circ-p_1}{p_1^\circ} = x_2 = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}$`.

For a dilute solution `$n_2 \ll n_1$`, so `$x_2 \approx \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}=\dfrac{w_2/M_2}{w_1/M_1}$`.

Therefore `$\dfrac{p_1^\circ-p_1}{p_1^\circ}=\dfrac{w_2M_1}{M_2w_1}$` and `$M_2=\dfrac{w_2M_1}{w_1}\cdot\dfrac{p_1^\circ}{p_1^\circ-p_1}$`.

The lowering depends on the mole fraction of the solute only, not on its identity: the vapour pressure is a colligative property.

</details>

12. (5) What is osmotic pressure? Derive the expression for the molar mass of a solute from it, and state two uses of reverse osmosis.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Osmotic pressure π is the extra pressure that must be applied to a solution to stop solvent entering it through a semipermeable membrane.

For dilute solutions (van’t Hoff) `$\pi = CRT = \dfrac{n_2}{V}RT$`. With `$n_2 = w_2/M_2$` we get `$\pi V = \dfrac{w_2}{M_2}RT$`, so `$M_2 = \dfrac{w_2RT}{\pi V}$`.

Reverse osmosis: when a pressure larger than π is applied on the solution, solvent flows out of it. Uses: desalination of sea water; purification of drinking water in home water purifiers (also treatment of waste water and concentrating fruit juice).

</details>

13. (5) 1.22 g of benzoic acid (122 g/mol) dissolved in 100 g of benzene lowers its freezing point by 0.32 K (K<sub>f</sub> = 5.12 K kg/mol). Find the observed molar mass, the van’t Hoff factor and the percentage association (dimerisation).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`$m = 0.32/5.12 = 0.0625$` mol/kg. Moles = 0.0625 × 0.100 = 0.00625 mol. Observed `$M = 1.22/0.00625 = 195$` g/mol.

`$i = \dfrac{\text{normal M}}{\text{observed M}} = \dfrac{122}{195} = 0.625$`.

For dimerisation `$i = 1-\alpha/2$`, so `$\alpha = 2(1-0.625)=0.75$`: 75 % of the acid exists as dimers (it is associated through hydrogen bonds).

</details>

## Case-based question

14. **Salt on a winter road and in the ice-cream maker.** In a hill town, workers spread rock salt (NaCl) on an icy road. At a mela stall, a kulfi seller packs his pot in crushed ice mixed with salt. Take K<sub>f</sub> of water 1.86 K kg/mol and K<sub>b</sub> 0.52 K kg/mol. A brine of 58.5 g of NaCl (58.5 g/mol) in 1.0 kg of water is used; assume complete dissociation.

(a) (1) What is the molality of the brine, and the van’t Hoff factor?

(b) (1) Find the freezing point of the brine.

(c) (1) Explain why salted ice gets colder than pure ice.

(d) (1) Find the boiling point of the same brine at normal pressure.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) m = 1.0 mol/kg; NaCl gives 2 ions, so i = 2.

(b) `$\Delta T_f = iK_fm = 2\times1.86\times1.0 = 3.72$` K, so the brine freezes at −3.72 °C.

(c) Salt lowers the freezing point, so ice melts at a temperature below 0 °C. Melting absorbs heat from the pot, bringing the mixture (and the kulfi) down to that lower temperature.

(d) `$\Delta T_b = 2\times0.52\times1.0 = 1.04$` K, so it boils at 101.04 °C.

</details>

## Assertion-reason questions

Choose: (A) both true and R explains A; (B) both true but R does not explain A; (C) A true, R false; (D) A false, R true.

15. **A:** The boiling point of a solution of a non-volatile solute is higher than that of the pure solvent. **R:** The vapour pressure of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent at the same temperature.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) Both true and R explains A: the solution must be heated to a higher temperature before its (lower) vapour pressure reaches the outside pressure.</details>

16. **A:** 0.1 m glucose and 0.1 m NaCl have the same freezing point. **R:** Colligative properties depend only on the number of moles of solute.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(D) A is false: NaCl gives about twice as many particles, so its depression is about double. R, as stated, is incomplete: they depend on the number of <i>particles</i>, and NaCl dissociates.</details>

17. **A:** The solubility of oxygen in water decreases as the temperature rises. **R:** Dissolving a gas in a liquid is an exothermic process.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(A) Both true and R explains A: by Le Chatelier’s principle, heating shifts the dissolving equilibrium towards the gas, so less stays dissolved.</details>
