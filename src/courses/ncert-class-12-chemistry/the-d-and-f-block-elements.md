**Chemistry · Class 12 · Chapter 4 · about 80 minutes.** By the end you will be able to write the electron configuration of any first-row transition metal or ion, explain why these metals show many oxidation states, coloured ions, magnetism and catalysis, work out the spin-only magnetic moment, and describe the lanthanoid contraction and the actinoids.

<div class="bigidea">In a transition metal the 3d and 4s electrons have almost the same energy, and the d subshell is only partly full. That one fact explains the long list of properties: many oxidation states (different numbers of electrons can leave), colour (a d electron jumps between split d levels), magnetism (unpaired d electrons), and catalysis (a metal that can change its oxidation state easily).</div>

## 1. Where the d- and f-block elements sit

The periodic table has four blocks, named after the subshell that is being filled. The **d-block** is the middle of the table, groups 3 to 12, in four rows: the 3d series (Sc to Zn), 4d (Y to Cd), 5d (La or Lu to Hg) and 6d (Ac or Lr to Cn). The **f-block** is the two rows at the bottom: the **lanthanoids** (Ce to Lu, filling 4f) and the **actinoids** (Th to Lr, filling 5f).

A **transition element** is one that has an incomplete d subshell in its atom or in any of its common ions. By this strict rule, Zn, Cd and Hg (d¹⁰ everywhere) are not transition elements, though we study them with the d-block because they behave in a similar way. Scandium counts, because Sc has one d electron in the atom, even though Sc³⁺ is d⁰.

## 2. Electronic configuration

For the 3d series the general configuration is `$[Ar]\,3d^{1-10}\,4s^{1-2}$`. Electrons fill the 3d boxes one at a time first (Hund's rule) and then pair up. Slide through the series and change the charge to see how an ion loses electrons: **the 4s electrons leave first**, then the 3d.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-dblock-dfill"><figcaption>Choose an element from Sc to Zn and a charge from 0 to +3. Watch the 4s box empty before the 3d boxes do. The bar reads the spin-only magnetic moment from the number of unpaired electrons.</figcaption></figure>

Two elements break the pattern. Chromium is 3d⁵ 4s¹ (not 3d⁴ 4s²) and copper is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (not 3d⁹ 4s²):

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-dblock-exception">
<div class="step">The simple filling order gives chromium 3d⁴ 4s²: four unpaired electrons and a 4s box that is full.</div>
<div class="step">But the 3d and 4s levels are very close, so moving an electron from one to the other costs very little energy.</div>
<div class="step">One 4s electron moves into the empty 3d box.</div>
<div class="step">The result is 3d⁵ 4s¹. Six electrons now sit alone with parallel spins. A half-filled subshell gains extra stability from exchange energy and from its symmetry, which pays for the move.</div>
<div class="step">Copper does the same at the other end: 3d⁹ 4s² becomes 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, and a full d subshell is the extra-stable arrangement.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Ions lose electrons in the reverse order of filling, so 3d goes before 4s.”** Atoms fill 4s before 3d, but once the d orbitals have electrons, the 3d level drops below 4s. So when an atom becomes an ion the **4s electrons leave first**. Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶ with an empty 4s, not 3d⁴ 4s².

</div>

## 3. General properties of the transition elements

### 3.1 Physical properties: hard, strong, high melting

Transition metals are hard, shiny, good conductors and have high melting points. Both the (n−1)d and the ns electrons join in the metallic bond, and the more unpaired d electrons there are, the stronger the bond. So melting points and enthalpies of atomisation rise to a peak near the middle of each series (tungsten melts at 3695 K, the highest of all metals) and fall again. Manganese melts lower than its neighbours Cr and Fe because its d⁵ is so stable that its electrons bond less. Zn, Cd and Hg (full d subshells, no d bonding) melt lowest, and mercury is a liquid.

### 3.2 Atomic and ionic sizes

Across a series the radius first falls, then levels off and rises slightly. Each extra d electron shields the others poorly, so the pull of the nucleus wins at first; later the repulsion between the crowded d electrons wins. Going **down** a group the 4d metals are larger than the 3d metals, as expected. But 5d metals are hardly larger than 4d ones. That surprise is the lanthanoid contraction (section 5).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-dblock-size"><figcaption>Metallic radii of the three series against group number. Slide the group to compare. Zr and Hf, Nb and Ta, Mo and W are almost exactly the same size.</figcaption></figure>

### 3.3 Ionisation enthalpies

The first ionisation enthalpy of the 3d metals rises only slowly (Sc 633 to Zn 906 kJ/mol), because the electron is removed from the 4s level while the d electrons grow in number. The jumps show stability: removing a d electron from d⁵ (Mn²⁺ → Mn³⁺, 3248 kJ/mol) or from d¹⁰ (Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺, 1958 kJ/mol) is especially expensive. This is why Mn³⁺ is a strong oxidant and why Cu⁺ is unstable in water.

### 3.4 Oxidation states

Transition metals show many oxidation states. The 4s and 3d electrons are so close in energy that any of them can be used in bonding, and the states **differ by one unit** (Mn: +2, +3, +4, +5, +6, +7). In contrast, p-block elements change in steps of two (Sn +2, +4), because they lose the p pair or the s pair together.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-dblock-oxstates"><figcaption>Filled dots mark the common states, open rings the less common ones. Slide through the series: the highest state climbs to +7 at manganese and then falls.</figcaption></figure>

Three patterns to remember:

- The **maximum** state equals the total number of 4s and 3d electrons up to Mn (+3 for Sc to +7 for Mn). After Mn the d electrons are held tightly and the highest state falls: Fe up to +6, Co up to +4 (rare), Ni and Cu +2, Zn +2.
- The **stable +2** state is common to the whole series because after losing the 4s pair, the rest is unchanged. +3 is also common.
- The **highest states** occur in compounds with the most electronegative elements, oxygen and fluorine, for example MnO₄⁻ (Mn +7), CrO₄²⁻ (Cr +6), VO₂⁺ (V +5), because these small atoms can hold many bonds.

### 3.5 Standard electrode potentials and chemical reactivity

Whether a metal dissolves in dilute acid depends on E°(M²⁺/M). The value is set by three energies: atomisation of the solid, ionisation of the gaseous atom, and hydration of the ion. Walk through the energy cycle for Zn, Fe and Cu:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-dblock-cycle">
<div class="step">We want M(s) to become M²⁺(aq). We cannot do it in one step on paper, so we use the sum of three steps that we know.</div>
<div class="step">Step 1: atomise. Break the metal into gaseous atoms. This costs energy, ΔH(atomisation), and is bigger for metals with strong metallic bonds.</div>
<div class="step">Step 2: ionise. Remove two electrons, M(g) → M²⁺(g) + 2e⁻. This costs the sum of the first and second ionisation enthalpies, by far the biggest term.</div>
<div class="step">Step 3: hydrate. Water molecules cluster round the small, doubly charged ion and release a large amount of energy. The more strongly charged and smaller the ion, the larger the release.</div>
<div class="step">Add the three: the net enthalpy is lowest for Zn, in between for Fe and highest for Cu. A low net cost means the metal gives up its electrons easily (very negative E°); a high net cost means it does not (positive E° for copper).</div>
</div>

Here are the measured values, M²⁺/M and M³⁺/M²⁺. A bar below the dashed H⁺/H₂ line means the metal dissolves in dilute acid.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-dblock-e0"><figcaption>Switch between the two couples and pick an element for the reason behind its value. Copper is the only 3d metal with a positive M²⁺/M value. In the M³⁺/M²⁺ chart notice the high values for Mn and Co and the low value for Cr.</figcaption></figure>

The irregularities all come from the stable configurations. Mn³⁺ → Mn²⁺ is strongly favoured because Mn²⁺ is d⁵. Cr³⁺ is d³ (a special stability that crystal field theory explains in the next chapter), so Cr²⁺ readily becomes Cr³⁺ and acts as a reducing agent. Fe³⁺ (d⁵) is more stable than Fe²⁺ (d⁶), so Fe²⁺ solutions turn yellow-brown in air. Co³⁺ (d⁶) is a powerful oxidant and exists in water only as complexes.

### 3.6 Magnetic properties

An electron has spin, which makes it a tiny magnet. Paired electrons cancel; an **unpaired** electron does not. A substance with unpaired electrons is **paramagnetic** (drawn into a magnetic field); one with all electrons paired is **diamagnetic** (pushed out very weakly). To a good approximation the magnetic moment comes only from spin:

```math
\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \text{BM}
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>μ</dt><dd>spin-only magnetic moment, in Bohr magnetons (BM)</dd>
<dt>n</dt><dd>number of unpaired electrons</dd>
<dt>Values</dt><dd>n = 1: 1.73 BM; 2: 2.83; 3: 3.87; 4: 4.90; 5: 5.92</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Every unpaired electron adds its own tiny magnet; the formula says how strongly the group of them is pulled in a field.</dd>
</dl>

### 3.7 Formation of coloured ions

Ions with a partly filled d subshell are usually coloured. In water or in a complex the five d orbitals split into a lower and a higher group. A d electron can jump from the lower to the higher group when it absorbs light of energy equal to the gap. The light that is left over reaches your eye, so the solution looks like the **complementary colour** of the light absorbed. Sc³⁺ (d⁰) and Zn²⁺ (d¹⁰) have no such jump and are colourless. Mn²⁺ is a very pale pink, because its jump is forbidden by the spin rules, so it is weak.

### 3.8 Catalytic properties

Transition metals and their compounds are good catalysts for two reasons: they can change oxidation state easily (so they can pass electrons or atoms to the reactants and take them back), and the surface of the solid can hold reactant molecules (adsorption) and bring them close. Examples: Fe (Haber process for ammonia), V₂O₅ (contact process for sulphuric acid), Ni (hydrogenation of vegetable oils into ghee-like fats), Pt and Rh in the catalytic converter of a car, MnO₂ in the decomposition of H₂O₂.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-dblock-contact">
<div class="step">Without a catalyst SO₂ and O₂ need a lot of energy to react, so the path has one high hump and the reaction is very slow.</div>
<div class="step">V₂O₅ (vanadium +5) gives an oxygen atom to SO₂ and becomes V₂O₄ (vanadium +4). SO₃ forms.</div>
<div class="step">V₂O₄ is then oxidised by O₂ back to V₂O₅. The vanadium has moved between +4 and +5 and the cycle is complete.</div>
<div class="step">In total 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃. The vanadium oxide is the same at the end as at the start; it was only a go-between.</div>
<div class="step">The catalysed route has two lower humps instead of one big hump: the activation energy is smaller, so many more collisions succeed.</div>
</div>

### 3.9 Interstitial compounds and alloys

Small atoms (H, C, N, B) fit into the gaps between the metal atoms and form **interstitial compounds** such as TiC, Fe₃H and VH₀.₅₆. They are usually non-stoichiometric, very hard, have melting points higher than the pure metal, keep metallic conduction and are chemically inert. That is why steel, with a little carbon in iron, is much harder than iron.

**Alloys** are made when metals of similar size (within about 15 %) replace one another in the lattice, as Cr, V, Mn, Ni do in iron. They are harder and resist corrosion: steel and stainless steel (Fe, Cr, Ni, C), brass (Cu, Zn) used for temple bells and utensils, bronze (Cu, Sn) for statues. A well-known alloy of the f-block is **mischmetal**, about 95 % lanthanoid metals with 5 % iron and traces of S, C, Ca and Al, used in lighter flints and in magnesium alloys.

## 4. Some oxides and oxoanions (what is left in the syllabus)

The **oxides** of a metal change from basic to acidic as the oxidation state rises: CrO (basic), Cr₂O₃ (amphoteric), CrO₃ (acidic); MnO (basic), Mn₂O₃ and MnO₂ (amphoteric), Mn₂O₇ (strongly acidic). A higher oxidation state means a smaller, more highly charged metal ion that holds the oxide's electrons tighter, so the oxide behaves like a covalent non-metal oxide and gives acid with water.

## 5. The f-block: lanthanoids and actinoids

### 5.1 Lanthanoids

The fourteen elements Ce to Lu fill the 4f subshell, and with La they are called lanthanoids; the usual configuration is `$[Xe]\,4f^{1-14}\,5d^{0-1}\,6s^2$`. Their chemistry is dominated by **+3**. Some show another state because it gives a half-filled or full 4f subshell: Eu²⁺ (4f⁷) and Yb²⁺ (4f¹⁴), and Ce⁴⁺ (the 4f⁰ of the xenon core) and Tb⁴⁺ (4f⁷). Ce⁴⁺ is a good oxidising agent in titrations; Eu²⁺ is a reducing agent.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-dblock-lnact"><figcaption>Switch between the lanthanoids and the actinoids and slide through each row: configuration and oxidation states. The lanthanoids are almost all +3; the actinoids spread from +2 up to +7.</figcaption></figure>

**Lanthanoid contraction.** From La³⁺ to Lu³⁺ the ionic radius falls steadily (from about 103 to 86 pm), though one proton and one electron are added each time. The new electron goes into the inner 4f subshell, whose diffuse shape shields the nuclear charge poorly. The outer electrons feel the growing pull, and the whole ion shrinks.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@chem-lanthanide-contraction"><figcaption>Radii of the Ln³⁺ ions with the 4f electrons shown, and the pairs Zr and Hf, Nb and Ta, Mo and W, whose radii are almost the same.</figcaption></figure>

Consequences: (1) the 5d elements are no larger than the 4d elements above them, so Zr and Hf (also Nb and Ta) have nearly the same radius and almost identical chemistry, and are very hard to separate; (2) the lanthanoids themselves are hard to separate from one another (it needs ion exchange or solvent extraction); (3) the basic strength of the hydroxides falls from La(OH)₃ to Lu(OH)₃, because the smaller ion holds OH⁻ more tightly and gives it up less readily. A real-life Indian link: monazite sand on the beaches of Kerala and Odisha is a source of the lighter lanthanoids and of thorium.

### 5.2 Actinoids

The actinoids (Th to Lr) fill the 5f subshell. All are radioactive; Th, Pa and U occur in nature, and the rest are made in reactors and accelerators. The 5f orbitals reach out further than the 4f and lie close to 6d and 7s in energy, so more electrons can take part in bonding. The result is a **wide range of oxidation states** (+3 for all, up to +7 for Np and Pu), and the 5f shielding is also poor, giving an **actinoid contraction** that is larger than the lanthanoid contraction.

| Property | Lanthanoids | Actinoids |
|---|---|---|
| Subshell filling | 4f | 5f |
| Usual oxidation state | +3 (a few +2, +4) | +3, with +4, +5, +6, +7 common |
| Radioactivity | only Pm | all |
| Contraction | smaller | larger |
| Compounds | mostly colourless or pale | many coloured, form oxo-ions such as UO₂²⁺ |
| Basic strength | less basic hydroxides than actinoids | more basic |

<div class="keypoints">

- Transition element: incomplete d subshell in the atom or a common ion (Zn, Cd, Hg are not, strictly). 3d series: [Ar] 3d¹⁻¹⁰ 4s¹⁻²; Cr is 3d⁵4s¹, Cu is 3d¹⁰4s¹. Ions lose 4s electrons first.
- Strong metallic bonding (both d and s electrons) gives high melting points and atomisation enthalpies, with a peak near the middle of each series.
- 3d and 4s are close in energy, so many oxidation states that differ by 1. The maximum rises to +7 at Mn. Highest states occur with O and F.
- E°(M²⁺/M) is irregular because of atomisation, ionisation and hydration; Cu is positive. Mn³⁺, Co³⁺ are strong oxidants; Cr²⁺ is a reductant.
- μ = √(n(n + 2)) BM; paramagnetic means unpaired electrons.
- Colour comes from d–d jumps, with the colour seen complementary to the light absorbed; d⁰ and d¹⁰ ions are colourless.
- Catalysis: variable oxidation states and surface adsorption. Interstitial compounds are hard and inert; alloys form between metals of similar size.
- Lanthanoids: mostly +3; lanthanoid contraction (poor 4f shielding) makes 4d and 5d sizes alike (Zr = Hf) and makes lanthanoids hard to separate.
- Actinoids: 5f; all radioactive; oxidation states +3 to +7; larger contraction than lanthanoids.

</div>
