**Practice · Class 12 Chemistry · Chapter 6.** Read the chapter lesson, [Haloalkanes and Haloarenes](/learn/courses/ncert-class-12-chemistry/haloalkanes-and-haloarenes/), first. Try each question on paper before opening the answer. Marks go for the correct reagent, the mechanism or rule, the equation and the reason in one sentence.

<div class="bigidea">Ask three questions about any haloalkane reaction: how crowded is the carbon (1°, 2°, 3°), is the reagent a good nucleophile or a strong base, and what is the solvent? Those three answers decide between SN2, SN1 and elimination.</div>

## How to set out answers

1. Name the substrate type (1°, 2°, 3°, allylic, benzylic, aryl).
2. Name the mechanism and give one line of evidence (rate law, stereochemistry or carbocation stability).
3. Write a balanced equation with conditions over the arrow.
4. For stereochemistry, say inversion, racemisation or retention.

## 1-mark questions

1. Write the IUPAC name of (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Br.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>1-Bromo-2-methylpropane.</details>

2. Which has the higher boiling point: 1-bromobutane or 2-bromo-2-methylpropane?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>1-Bromobutane (102 °C against 73 °C): the straight chain has more surface contact, so stronger van der Waals forces.</details>

3. What is the product of ethyl bromide and silver cyanide?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ethyl isocyanide, C₂H₅NC (the covalent AgCN attacks through nitrogen).</details>

4. What is a racemic mixture?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>An equimolar (50 : 50) mixture of two enantiomers; it is optically inactive.</details>

## 2- and 3-mark questions

5. (2) Why is the C–I bond weaker than the C–Cl bond, and what is the effect on reactivity?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The iodine atom is much larger, so its overlap with carbon is poorer and the bond is longer (214 pm against 178 pm) and weaker (234 against 351 kJ/mol). Iodide is therefore a better leaving group, so iodoalkanes are the most reactive towards substitution.</details>

6. (2) Write the Finkelstein reaction for bromoethane and say why it goes to completion.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>C₂H₅Br + NaI → C₂H₅I + NaBr in dry acetone. NaBr is insoluble in acetone and precipitates, removing a product and driving the reaction forward.</details>

7. (2) Why is chlorobenzene less reactive than chloroethane toward nucleophiles?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The lone pair on Cl is delocalised into the ring, so the C–Cl bond has partial double bond character (shorter and stronger), and the carbon is sp². A phenyl cation is too unstable for SN1, and the ring blocks back-side attack for SN2.</details>

8. (3) Explain the difference in rate law between SN1 and SN2 reactions with an example of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>SN2 (for example CH₃Br + OH⁻) has the nucleophile and the haloalkane in the single slow step, so rate = k[RBr][OH⁻], second order. SN1 (for example (CH₃)₃CBr + H₂O) has a slow ionisation to a carbocation first, so rate = k[RBr], first order and independent of the nucleophile.</details>

9. (3) Arrange the following in increasing order of SN1 reactivity and give the reason: (CH₃)₃CBr, CH₃CH₂Br, (CH₃)₂CHBr.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>CH₃CH₂Br < (CH₃)₂CHBr < (CH₃)₃CBr. The tertiary carbocation is stabilised by three alkyl groups (hyperconjugation and the inductive effect), so it forms fastest; primary is the least stable.</details>

10. (3) What products are formed when 2-bromobutane reacts with (a) aqueous KOH and (b) alcoholic KOH? Name the rule for (b).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>(a) Substitution: butan-2-ol. (b) Elimination: mainly but-2-ene (with a little but-1-ene), following **Saytzeff's rule**: the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product.</details>

11. (3) Calculate the specific rotation of a mixture that has 75 % of an enantiomer with [α] = +40° and 25 % of its mirror image.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Net = 40 × (75 − 25)/100 = +20°. The 25 % of the (−) form cancels an equal amount of the (+) form, leaving an excess of 50 %.

</details>

## 5-mark questions (model answers)

12. (5) Describe the SN2 mechanism of the hydrolysis of (R)-2-bromobutane with OH⁻. State the kinetics, the stereochemical outcome and the effect of branching.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Mechanism:** OH⁻ attacks the carbon from the side opposite to Br (back-side attack). In the transition state, the carbon is partly bonded to both OH and Br and its other three groups are in one plane. Br⁻ leaves and the three groups flip over like an umbrella.

**Kinetics:** one step, bimolecular: rate = k[RBr][OH⁻], second order.

**Stereochemistry:** inversion of configuration (Walden inversion): (R)-2-bromobutane gives (S)-butan-2-ol.

**Branching:** more alkyl groups on the carbon block the back-side approach, so the rate falls in the order methyl > primary > secondary > tertiary.

</details>

13. (5) How would you convert (a) chlorobenzene to phenol, (b) chlorobenzene to p-nitrochlorobenzene, (c) aniline to chlorobenzene? Give reagents and conditions.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm gives sodium phenoxide; acidify to get phenol.

(b) Nitrating mixture (conc. HNO₃ and conc. H₂SO₄): chlorine is ortho, para-directing, so the para isomer is the major product (with some ortho, separated by crystallisation).

(c) Diazotise aniline with NaNO₂/HCl at 273–278 K to get the diazonium salt, then use Cu₂Cl₂/HCl (Sandmeyer reaction).

</details>

## Case-based question

A student adds a few drops of 2-chloro-2-methylpropane to water containing a trace of base and follows the reaction by watching the pH. She notes that doubling the concentration of the halide doubles the rate, but doubling the base concentration changes nothing. She then repeats with 1-chlorobutane and finds that doubling the concentration of either the halide or the base doubles the rate.

14. (a) Name the mechanism in each experiment. (b) Write the rate law for each. (c) Why does the tertiary halide not follow the second mechanism? (d) What product does the tertiary halide give with alcoholic KOH?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) 2-Chloro-2-methylpropane: SN1. 1-Chlorobutane: SN2.

(b) SN1: rate = k[halide]. SN2: rate = k[halide][OH⁻].

(c) Three methyl groups crowd the carbon and block the back-side attack that SN2 needs; they also stabilise the carbocation, so SN1 is easy.

(d) Alcoholic KOH gives elimination: 2-methylpropene.

</details>

## Assertion-reason questions

15. **Assertion (A):** Haloalkanes are insoluble in water though they are polar. **Reason (R):** Water molecules attract each other strongly by hydrogen bonds and haloalkane–water attractions are weaker.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both true, and R explains A.</details>

16. **Assertion (A):** SN1 reactions of an optically active tertiary halide give a racemic mixture. **Reason (R):** The carbocation intermediate is planar and the nucleophile attacks both faces equally.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both true, and R explains A.</details>

17. **Assertion (A):** 2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene reacts with aqueous NaOH at 368 K, but chlorobenzene needs 623 K. **Reason (R):** The nitro groups are electron-withdrawing and stabilise the negatively charged intermediate.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both true, and R explains A.</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- Decide between SN2, SN1 and elimination from the type of carbon, the reagent and the solvent.
- SN2: rate = k[RX][Nu⁻], inversion; SN1: rate = k[RX], racemisation.
- Aqueous KOH substitutes, alcoholic KOH eliminates (Saytzeff).
- Haloarenes are unreactive toward SN; ortho and para NO₂ groups help.
- Racemic mixture: equal enantiomers, no net rotation.

</div>
