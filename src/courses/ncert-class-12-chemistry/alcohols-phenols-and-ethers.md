**Chemistry · Class 12 · Chapter 7 · about 85 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name alcohols, phenols and ethers, classify an alcohol as primary, secondary or tertiary, explain their boiling points and acidity, predict the products of their main reactions and choose the right method to make each of them.

<div class="bigidea">All three families have one oxygen joined by single bonds. In an alcohol that oxygen carries an H (R–OH), so it can hydrogen bond, act as a weak acid and be replaced or removed. In a phenol the OH sits on a benzene ring, so the ring and the OH change each other: the OH becomes more acidic and the ring becomes more reactive. In an ether (R–O–R) the oxygen has no H, so the molecule is quiet and useful as a solvent.</div>

## 1. Classification and naming

An **alcohol** has an –OH group on a saturated (sp³) carbon. A **phenol** has –OH on a carbon of a benzene ring. An **ether** has an oxygen between two carbon groups. By the number of OH groups an alcohol is mono-, di- (glycol, as in antifreeze) or trihydric (glycerol). By the carbon that carries the OH it is **primary (1°)**, **secondary (2°)** or **tertiary (3°)**: the class is how many other carbons are bonded to that carbon.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-alc-classify"><figcaption>Choose a compound. The picture shows the carbon that holds the OH and the groups around it. Count the carbons to read off 1°, 2° or 3°; notice that phenol and ether look different.</figcaption></figure>

**Naming alcohols (IUPAC).** Find the longest chain that contains the carbon with OH, change the final “e” of the alkane to “ol”, and number so that the OH gets the lowest number. CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ is butan-2-ol. For two OH groups use “diol” (ethane-1,2-diol); keep the “e”. Common names: methyl alcohol, ethyl alcohol, isopropyl alcohol. **Phenols** are named as derivatives of phenol; the other groups are numbered from the carbon with OH (2-methylphenol is o-cresol). **Ethers** are named as alkoxy alkanes: the smaller group with O becomes the prefix. CH₃OCH₂CH₃ is **methoxyethane**; C₆H₅OCH₃ is methoxybenzene (anisole).

## 2. Making alcohols

Think of three routes: **add water** to an alkene, **add hydrogen** to a carbonyl (reduce it), or **add a carbon group** to a carbonyl (Grignard).

1. **Hydration of alkenes.** Alkene + water with an acid catalyst adds H and OH by **Markovnikov's rule** (OH goes to the carbon with fewer hydrogens). Propene gives propan-2-ol: `$\text{CH}_3\text{CH=CH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)CH}_3$`. Ethene gives ethanol.
2. **Reduction of carbonyl compounds.** Aldehydes give primary alcohols and ketones give secondary alcohols with H₂/Ni, NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄. Carboxylic acids and esters give primary alcohols with LiAlH₄ (NaBH₄ is too mild for them).
3. **Grignard reagents** (RMgX in dry ether, then water). Methanal gives a primary alcohol, other aldehydes give secondary alcohols and ketones give tertiary alcohols. This also builds a longer carbon chain.
4. **Fermentation** (industry, India): sugar from sugarcane molasses or starch is fermented by yeast: `$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \to 2\,\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\,\text{CO}_2$`. Ethanol for fuel blending in India is made this way.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c12c-alc-yield"></div>

<div class="myth">

**“Hydration of propene gives propan-1-ol.”** Markovnikov's rule sends the OH to the middle carbon, so the main product is propan-2-ol. A primary alcohol from an alkene needs the anti-Markovnikov route (hydroboration, see Beyond the syllabus).

</div>

## 3. Making phenols and ethers

**Phenols.** (a) From a haloarene: chlorobenzene with NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm gives sodium phenoxide, then acid gives phenol. (b) From benzenesulphonic acid by fusing with NaOH. (c) From a **diazonium salt** by warming with water (chapter 9). (d) **From cumene** (the industrial route): cumene is oxidised by air to cumene hydroperoxide, which with dilute acid splits into **phenol and propanone (acetone)**, two useful products from one process.

**Ethers.** (a) **Dehydration of alcohols**: excess ethanol with concentrated H₂SO₄ at **413 K** gives ethoxyethane; the same acid at **443 K** gives ethene instead. Heat decides ether or alkene. This works for primary alcohols only; secondary and tertiary ones give alkenes. (b) **Williamson synthesis**: sodium alkoxide + alkyl halide, `$\text{RO}^- + \text{R'X} \to \text{ROR'} + \text{X}^-$`. It can make unsymmetrical ethers. Step through how it works and why the halide must be primary:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-alc-williamson">
<div class="step"><b>Make the alkoxide.</b> Sodium removes the OH hydrogen as H₂ gas, leaving RO⁻Na⁺, a strong nucleophile.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Meet a primary halide.</b> The carbon bonded to Br is slightly positive and has little in its way.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Back-side attack.</b> The alkoxide oxygen attacks the carbon from the side opposite to Br, in one step (SN2).</div>
<div class="step"><b>The ether forms.</b> Br⁻ leaves. To make an unsymmetrical ether, put the bulky group on the alkoxide.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Why a tertiary halide fails.</b> It is too crowded for back-side attack, so the alkoxide acts as a base and removes an H: the product is an alkene.</div>
</div>

## 4. Physical properties

Boiling points rise with the number of carbons, but the **alcohol** is far above the alkane or ether of the same size, because its O–H bonds form **hydrogen bonds** between molecules. Slide through the series and compare:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-alc-hbond"><figcaption>Boiling points of alkanes, ethers and alcohols with 1 to 5 carbons. The gap between the alcohol and the ether of the same size is the extra energy needed to break hydrogen bonds. The dotted lines show hydrogen bonds between alcohol molecules.</figcaption></figure>

**Solubility.** The first three alcohols mix with water in any ratio because their OH forms hydrogen bonds with water. As the alkyl part grows, it repels water, so solubility drops. Phenol dissolves a little in water (about 8 g per 100 g) and ethers dissolve slightly because their O can accept hydrogen bonds from water, but ethers boil at about the same temperature as alkanes of similar mass. **Branching** lowers the boiling point of isomers (tert-butanol 83 °C, butan-1-ol 118 °C) because the molecules are rounder and touch less.

## 5. Reactions of alcohols

An alcohol has two reactive bonds. In the **O–H bond** the alcohol acts as a weak acid or nucleophile (the H is lost). In the **C–O bond** the OH is replaced or removed. The order of reactivity depends on which bond breaks:

| Bond broken | Reaction | Easiest class |
|---|---|---|
| O–H | with Na (H₂ gas), esterification | primary (least crowded, so the OH is most acidic and open) |
| C–O | with HX, PCl₅, SOCl₂, dehydration | tertiary (most stable carbocation) |

**With sodium:** `$2\,\text{ROH} + 2\,\text{Na} \to 2\,\text{RONa} + \text{H}_2$`, showing that alcohols are acidic.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c12c-alc-h2"></div>

**Esterification:** alcohol + carboxylic acid (H⁺ catalyst) ⇌ ester + water; acid chlorides or anhydrides do the same without equilibrium. Aspirin is made from salicylic acid and ethanoic anhydride. Try the equilibrium yourself:

<!--fig:tool esterification-->

**With hydrogen halides.** `$\text{ROH} + \text{HX} \to \text{RX} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$`. The reactivity order is **3° > 2° > 1°**, and HI > HBr > HCl. The **Lucas test** (conc. HCl with anhydrous ZnCl₂) uses this to tell the classes apart by how fast the alkyl chloride makes the mixture turbid.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-alc-lucas"><figcaption>Choose a class of alcohol and press play (or slide the time). A tertiary alcohol clouds almost at once, a secondary one after a few minutes, and a primary one stays clear at room temperature.</figcaption></figure>

**Dehydration.** With concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄ at 443 K an alcohol loses water to make an alkene; the ease is 3° > 2° > 1°, and where there is a choice the **more substituted alkene** forms (Saytzeff's rule). The steps are protonation of OH, loss of water to give a carbocation, then loss of H⁺:

<div class="steps" data-fig="@chem-alcohol-dehydration" data-title="Acid-catalysed dehydration of an alcohol" data-ms="6500">
<div class="step"><b>Start.</b> The alcohol and a strong acid.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Protonation.</b> O takes H⁺, so OH becomes H₂O, a good leaving group.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Loss of water.</b> The slow step leaves a carbocation (a tertiary one here).</div>
<div class="step"><b>Deprotonation.</b> A base removes H⁺ from the next carbon.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Product.</b> The alkene (2-methylpropene).</div>
</div>

**Oxidation.** What you get depends on the number of hydrogens on the carbon with OH:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-alc-oxid"><figcaption>Choose a class of alcohol and a mild or strong oxidant. A primary alcohol goes to an aldehyde and, with a strong oxidant, a carboxylic acid; a secondary alcohol stops at a ketone; a tertiary alcohol has no H to lose and is not oxidised.</figcaption></figure>

Mild reagents (PCC, or Cu at 573 K which only removes H₂) stop a primary alcohol at the aldehyde. Strong ones (acidified KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇) carry on to the acid. A breath-test for drivers uses this: orange K₂Cr₂O₇ turns green when ethanol vapour reduces it.

<div class="myth">

**“Alcohols are oxidised by taking away oxygen.”** Oxidation here means removing hydrogen (or adding O). The carbon with OH loses an H, and ends with a C=O. That is why a carbon with no H, a tertiary alcohol, cannot be oxidised.

</div>

## 6. Phenols: acidity and the ring

**Acidity.** Phenol (pKa 10) is about a million times more acidic than ethanol (pKa 16), and dissolves in NaOH (alcohols do not), but it is too weak to react with NaHCO₃. The cause is the **phenoxide ion**: its negative charge is not stuck on oxygen but spreads onto the ortho and para carbons of the ring.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-alc-resonance">
<div class="step"><b>Phenol.</b> The O is joined to the ring; one of its lone pairs lies alongside the ring's π cloud.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The lone pair moves in.</b> It is drawn into the ring; O becomes slightly positive.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Charge on an ortho carbon.</b> One resonance structure puts − on the ortho position.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Charge on the para carbon.</b> A second structure puts it opposite the OH.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The other ortho carbon.</b> A third structure; the ring is electron-rich at o and p positions, which also tells us where reagents will attack.</div>
</div>

**Substituent effects.** Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –Cl) spread the charge further and make phenol stronger; electron-donating groups (–CH₃) make it weaker. **Picric acid** (2,4,6-trinitrophenol, pKa 0.4) is as strong as a mineral acid. In alcohols, alkyl groups push electrons to O and **lower** the acidity: methanol > ethanol > propan-2-ol > tert-butanol.

The scale at the top shows how far the compounds sit from each other; each step of 1 in pKa is a factor of ten in acid strength.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c12c-alc-ionised"></div>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c12c-alc-stronger"></div>

**Electrophilic substitution.** The OH group pushes electrons into the ring (activating) and directs the incoming group to the **ortho and para** positions:

- **Nitration:** dilute HNO₃ at low temperature gives o- and p-nitrophenol (separated by steam distillation: the o-isomer has an internal hydrogen bond and is volatile); concentrated HNO₃ gives 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid).
- **Halogenation:** bromine in CS₂ at low temperature gives mainly o- and p-bromophenol; **bromine water** gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol with no catalyst needed.
- **Kolbe reaction:** sodium phenoxide with CO₂ under pressure, then acid, gives **salicylic acid**, the parent of aspirin.
- **Reimer–Tiemann reaction:** phenol with CHCl₃ and NaOH, then acid, gives **salicylaldehyde** (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde).

Other reactions: neutral FeCl₃ gives a **violet** colour (test for phenols); distillation with zinc dust removes the OH to give benzene; oxidation with chromic acid gives a yellow **benzoquinone**.

<div class="myth">

**“Phenol is an alcohol, so it reacts like one.”** Phenol does not give the Lucas test and its C–O bond does not break with HX, since it has partial double-bond character from resonance. It is also far more acidic than an alcohol.

</div>

## 7. Reactions of ethers

Ethers are among the least reactive organic compounds, which is why they are good solvents. Their one important reaction is **cleavage by HI or HBr**: `$\text{R–O–R} + 2\,\text{HI} \to 2\,\text{RI} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$`. With a mixed ether, the halogen goes to the **smaller** (less hindered) group when both are primary, and to the tertiary carbon if one group is tertiary (it goes by a carbocation). Anisole gives **phenol and iodomethane**, never iodobenzene, because the ring C–O bond is strong.

In anisole the OMe group activates the ring and directs ortho/para: bromination gives o- and p-bromoanisole (even without a catalyst), and Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation gives mainly the para product.

Old ethers form dangerous **peroxides** on standing in air. That is why bottles of diethyl ether carry a date and are stored away from light.

## 8. Telling them apart and uses

In the infrared spectrum alcohols and phenols show a **broad O–H band at 3200 to 3600 cm⁻¹** (broad because of hydrogen bonding) and a C–O band at 1050 to 1250 cm⁻¹; ethers show only the C–O band. Try the band finder:

Uses in daily life: methanol is a solvent and the start of formaldehyde; ethanol is the fuel blend (E20 petrol in India), solvent and the alcohol in sanitiser; ethane-1,2-diol is coolant and antifreeze; glycerol is in soaps and food; phenol is a disinfectant and the start of Bakelite and aspirin; diethyl ether was a surgical anaesthetic. Methanol is **poisonous** (it is oxidised in the body to methanal and methanoic acid) and denatured alcohol is made unfit to drink by adding such compounds. <div class="keypoints">

- Alcohol R–OH (sp³ C), phenol Ar–OH, ether R–O–R. Alcohols are 1°, 2° or 3° by the carbons on the C–OH carbon.
- Alcohols from alkene hydration (Markovnikov), reduction of C=O (NaBH₄, LiAlH₄, H₂/Ni) and Grignard reagents (HCHO gives 1°, RCHO 2°, ketone 3°).
- Phenol: cumene process (also gives propanone), haloarene + NaOH at 623 K, diazonium salt + warm water. Ethers: 413 K dehydration of a primary alcohol, or Williamson (alkoxide + primary halide, SN2).
- Hydrogen bonds make alcohols boil far above ethers and alkanes of the same size; solubility falls with chain length.
- Reactions of alcohols: Na (H₂), esterification, HX and Lucas test (3° > 2° > 1°), dehydration (443 K alkene, 413 K ether), oxidation (1° gives aldehyde then acid, 2° gives ketone, 3° none).
- Phenol is acidic because the phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance; –NO₂ raises acidity and –CH₃ lowers it. Alkyl groups lower the acidity of alcohols.
- Phenol: o/p-directing; Br₂ water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol; Kolbe gives salicylic acid; Reimer–Tiemann gives salicylaldehyde; neutral FeCl₃ gives violet.
- Ethers are cleaved by HI to alkyl iodides; anisole gives phenol and CH₃I. IR: broad O–H near 3300 cm⁻¹, C–O 1050 to 1250 cm⁻¹.

</div>
