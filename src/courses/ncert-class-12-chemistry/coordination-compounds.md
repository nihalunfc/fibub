**Chemistry · Class 12 · Chapter 5 · about 85 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain Werner's theory, name coordination compounds, find every isomer of a complex, and use valence bond theory and crystal field theory to predict shape, magnetism and colour.

<div class="bigidea">A coordination compound is a metal ion surrounded by molecules or ions (ligands) that each donate a lone pair to it. The metal acts as a Lewis acid, the ligands as Lewis bases. The number of ligands and how they are arranged in space decide the shape, the isomers, the colour and the magnetism.</div>

## 1. What is a coordination compound?

Mix CuSO₄ solution with ammonia and the pale blue turns deep blue. The new colour belongs to the ion [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, in which four NH₃ molecules are **bonded to** copper. A **double salt** such as Mohr's salt, FeSO₄·(NH₄)₂SO₄·6H₂O, behaves differently: dissolved in water it splits into all its simple ions (Fe²⁺, NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻) and gives their tests. A **complex** such as K₄[Fe(CN)₆] keeps its [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ unit: no free Fe²⁺ or CN⁻ test is given.

## 2. Werner's theory

Alfred Werner explained the cobalt(III) chloride–ammonia compounds in 1893. They have the same elements, but different colours, and silver nitrate removes a different amount of chloride from each. His ideas, in modern words:

1. A metal has two kinds of valence. The **primary valence** is its oxidation state, satisfied by negative ions that can be free in solution. The **secondary valence** is the **coordination number**, satisfied by ligands bonded directly to the metal. For Co(III) it is 6.
2. Groups bound by the secondary valence sit in fixed directions in space: six of them make an octahedron.
3. Primary valence is shown by dotted lines in the old picture; secondary valence by solid lines.

Step through the evidence:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-coord-werner">
<div class="step">Four compounds of CoCl₃ with different amounts of NH₃. Excess AgNO₃ precipitates 3, 2, 1 and 0 moles of AgCl per mole. Why would the same Cl behave so differently?</div>
<div class="step">Werner's answer: cobalt always binds six groups directly (secondary valence 6). A chloride that is one of those six is held by a covalent bond and is not free to meet Ag⁺.</div>
<div class="step">CoCl₃·6NH₃ is [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃: all six places hold NH₃, so all three Cl⁻ are free outside. Three moles of AgCl.</div>
<div class="step">CoCl₃·5NH₃ is [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂: one Cl takes a place inside the sphere, so only two are free.</div>
<div class="step">CoCl₃·4NH₃ is [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl: two Cl inside, one free. This compound also shows two isomers, as we will see: it proved that the six groups sit at the corners of an octahedron.</div>
<div class="step">CoCl₃·3NH₃ is [Co(NH₃)₃Cl₃]: all the Cl are inside and the complex is neutral. No chloride is free, so AgNO₃ gives no precipitate.</div>
</div>

## 3. Basic terms

- **Central atom or ion**: the metal that accepts electron pairs (Co³⁺, Fe²⁺, Ni).
- **Ligand**: an ion or molecule that donates a lone pair. **Unidentate** ligands bond through one atom (Cl⁻, NH₃, H₂O), **bidentate** through two (ethane-1,2-diamine, written en, and oxalate), **polydentate** through many (EDTA has six). A polydentate ligand that forms a ring with the metal is a **chelating ligand**; the ring is a **chelate** (from the Greek for claw), and chelates are unusually stable.
- An **ambidentate** ligand can bond through either of two different atoms: NO₂⁻ through N (nitro) or O (nitrito); SCN⁻ through S or N.
- **Coordination number**: the number of donor atoms bonded to the metal, not the number of ligands. [Co(en)₃]³⁺ has three ligands but coordination number 6.
- **Coordination sphere**: the metal and its ligands inside the square brackets. Ions outside are counter ions.
- **Oxidation number** of the metal: the charge it would have if all the ligands were removed with their electron pairs. The charge on the complex is the sum of the metal's state and the ligand charges.
- **Homoleptic** complexes have one kind of ligand ([Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺); **heteroleptic** have more than one ([Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺).

## 4. Naming coordination compounds

The rules in short: (1) the cation is named first, then the anion; (2) inside the complex, the ligands come first in **alphabetical order** (ignoring prefixes), then the metal; (3) prefixes di, tri, tetra count simple ligands (bis, tris for complicated ones such as bis(ethane-1,2-diamine)); (4) anionic ligands end in -ido (chlorido, cyanido, hydroxido, oxalato) while neutral ones keep their names, except aqua (H₂O), ammine (NH₃) and carbonyl (CO); (5) the oxidation state of the metal follows in Roman numerals; (6) if the complex is an **anion**, the metal name ends in -ate (ferrate, cuprate, argentate, aurate).

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-coord-naming">
<div class="step">Look at the formula [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl. The bracket is the complex; Cl outside is the counter ion.</div>
<div class="step">The cation (the complex) is named first, the anion chloride is last.</div>
<div class="step">Inside the complex, list the ligands alphabetically: ammine (a) before chlorido (c).</div>
<div class="step">Add the number prefixes: four ammine, two chlorido gives tetraamminedichlorido.</div>
<div class="step">Find the oxidation state of cobalt: x + 4(0) + 2(−1) = +1, so x = +3. Write cobalt(III).</div>
<div class="step">Put it together: tetraamminedichloridocobalt(III) chloride.</div>
</div>

## 5. Isomerism

Isomers have the same formula but different arrangements. There are two big families.

### 5.1 Structural isomerism

- **Ionisation isomers** swap a ligand with a counter ion: [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ (violet, gives a white BaSO₄ precipitate) and [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br (red, gives a pale yellow AgBr precipitate).
- **Hydrate isomers** differ in how much water is inside the sphere (the chromium chloride isomers above).
- **Linkage isomers** have an ambidentate ligand bonded through a different atom: [Co(NH₃)₅(NO₂)]²⁺ (yellow, nitro) and [Co(NH₃)₅(ONO)]²⁺ (red, nitrito).
- **Coordination isomers** swap ligands between a cationic and an anionic complex: [Co(NH₃)₆][Cr(CN)₆] and [Cr(NH₃)₆][Co(CN)₆].

### 5.2 Stereoisomerism: geometrical

**Geometrical** isomers have the same bonds but a different arrangement round the metal. Square planar MA₂B₂ and octahedral MA₄B₂ give **cis** (same side) and **trans** (opposite). Octahedral MA₃B₃ gives **fac** (three alike on one face) and **mer** (three in a line round the middle). Tetrahedral complexes show no geometrical isomerism because all four corners are next to each other.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-coord-geoiso"><figcaption>Pick a complex and an isomer, and drag to turn. Cisplatin, the cis form of [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], is a cancer drug; the trans form is not.</figcaption></figure>

### 5.3 Stereoisomerism: optical

An **optical** isomer is a non-superimposable mirror image (an enantiomer), so the molecule is **chiral**. The pair rotates plane-polarised light by equal angles in opposite directions. Octahedral complexes with three bidentate ligands, like [Co(en)₃]³⁺, and cis-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺ are chiral. The trans form of [Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺ has a mirror plane and is not.

<div class="myth">

**“Cis and trans isomers are optical isomers of each other.”** Cis and trans differ in the *position* of the groups, not in handedness. Both [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] forms have a mirror plane (the plane of the square), so neither is chiral. They are **geometrical** isomers. Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images of each other.

## 6. Bonding in coordination compounds

### 6.1 Valence bond theory

The metal ion provides empty orbitals that **hybridise**; each ligand donates a lone pair into one. The type of hybridisation gives the shape. Step through [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (and pick other complexes with the buttons):

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-coord-vbt">
<div class="step">Start with the free metal ion: its d electrons sit one to a box first (Hund's rule).</div>
<div class="step">A strong-field ligand (CN⁻, NH₃) forces the d electrons to pair up, freeing 3d boxes. A weak-field ligand (F⁻, Cl⁻) does not.</div>
<div class="step">The empty orbitals (outlined) mix: two 3d + 4s + three 4p = d²sp³ (inner orbital, six of them) or 4s + three 4p + two 4d = sp³d² (outer orbital).</div>
<div class="step">Each ligand donates a lone pair into one hybrid orbital. Six pairs fill six hybrids.</div>
<div class="step">Count the unpaired electrons left in the 3d boxes. [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ has none: diamagnetic, low spin.</div>
</div>

Valence bond theory explains shape and magnetism but not colour, and it cannot say why one ligand is strong and another weak. Crystal field theory can.

### 6.2 Crystal field theory

Crystal field theory (CFT) treats ligands as negative point charges. Before the ligands arrive the five d orbitals have the same energy. When six ligands approach along the x, y and z axes, the two orbitals that point along the axes (the **e_g** pair, d(x²−y²) and d(z²)) are pushed up by repulsion; the three that point between the axes (the **t₂g** set) are pushed down. The gap is the **crystal field splitting energy Δₒ** (o for octahedral).

```math
\text{t}_{2g}: -0.4\,\Delta_o \text{ each}, \qquad \text{e}_g: +0.6\,\Delta_o \text{ each}, \qquad \text{CFSE} = (-0.4\,n_{t_{2g}} + 0.6\,n_{e_g})\,\Delta_o
```

<div class="meaning">

<dl>
<dt>Δₒ</dt><dd>energy gap between t₂g and e_g in an octahedral field</dd>
<dt>P</dt><dd>pairing energy: the cost of putting two electrons in one orbital</dd>
<dt>CFSE</dt><dd>crystal field stabilisation energy: how far the electrons sit below the average d level</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>If Δₒ is small, electrons would rather spread out over all five orbitals than pay P; if Δₒ is large, they pair up in the lower three. It is like choosing between a long walk to a free seat (Δₒ) and sharing a seat (P).</dd>
</dl>

</div>

For d⁴, d⁵, d⁶ and d⁷ there are two ways to fill: **high spin** (weak field, Δₒ < P: electrons go into e_g before pairing) and **low spin** (strong field, Δₒ > P: t₂g fills first). Slide the number of d electrons and switch the field:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-coord-cft"><figcaption>Choose the d count and the field. The model places the electrons, counts the unpaired ones and works out the magnetic moment and CFSE. Switch to tetrahedral to see the order flip.</figcaption></figure>

In a **tetrahedral** complex there are four ligands, and none points straight at an orbital. The order is reversed (e below t₂) and the splitting is much smaller, Δₜ = (4/9)Δₒ, which is never large enough to beat P. So tetrahedral complexes are always high spin.

### 6.3 Colour and the spectrochemical series

A complex with a partly filled d set absorbs a photon when an electron jumps from t₂g to e_g: Δₒ = hc/λ. The colour you see is the complement of what is absorbed. Ligands arranged by how large a Δₒ they cause form the **spectrochemical series**:

I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO

<div class="keypoints">

- Werner: primary valence (oxidation state, ionisable) and secondary valence (coordination number, ligands bonded to the metal). AgNO₃ precipitates only the free chloride.
- Ligands: unidentate, bidentate, polydentate (chelating), ambidentate. Coordination number = number of donor atoms. Common shapes: linear (2), tetrahedral and square planar (4), octahedral (6).
- Naming: cation first; ligands alphabetical then metal; di, tri, tetra (bis, tris for complex ligands); anionic ligands end in -ido; anionic complex: metal ends in -ate; oxidation state in Roman numerals.
- Isomers: structural (ionisation, hydrate, linkage, coordination) and stereo (geometrical cis/trans, fac/mer; optical). [Co(en)₃]³⁺ is chiral.
- VBT: d²sp³ (inner, low spin, strong field) or sp³d² (outer, high spin, weak field); sp³ tetrahedral; dsp² square planar.
- CFT: octahedral t₂g (−0.4Δₒ) and e_g (+0.6Δₒ). Δₒ > P: low spin; Δₒ < P: high spin. Δₜ = 4/9 Δₒ: tetrahedral always high spin. μ = √(n(n + 2)).
- Colour from d–d jumps, Δₒ = hc/λ; spectrochemical series I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < ox < H₂O < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO.

</div>
