**Chemistry · Class 12 · Chapter 10 · about 95 minutes.** By the end you will be able to describe the structure of glucose (open and ring), classify sugars as reducing or non-reducing, explain zwitterions and the peptide bond, describe the four levels of protein structure, say how an enzyme works, list the vitamins with their deficiency diseases, and explain how the DNA bases pair.

<div class="bigidea">Living things are built from four families of big molecules, and each one is made by joining small units with one repeating bond: sugars are joined by **glycosidic links** to make carbohydrates, amino acids by **peptide bonds** to make proteins, and nucleotides by **phosphodiester links** to make nucleic acids. Each time a water molecule is lost; each time hydrolysis (adding water) takes it apart again. The shape of the finished chain decides what it does.</div>

## 1. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are **polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones**, or substances that give them on hydrolysis. The name comes from the old formula C<sub>x</sub>(H₂O)<sub>y</sub>, though that does not fit every one. They are classified by what they give on hydrolysis:

- **Monosaccharides** do not hydrolyse: glucose, fructose, galactose. An **aldose** has a CHO group, a **ketose** a C=O inside the chain; glucose is an aldohexose and fructose a ketohexose.
- **Oligosaccharides** give 2 to 10 monosaccharides: sucrose, maltose and lactose are the disaccharides.
- **Polysaccharides** give many: starch, cellulose, glycogen.

A sugar is **reducing** if it reduces Tollens’ or Fehling’s reagent. All monosaccharides are; disaccharides are only if they keep a free anomeric carbon. The letters **D and L** tell the arrangement at the last chiral carbon (D-glucose has its OH on the right at C5 in the Fischer drawing, like D-glyceraldehyde); almost all natural sugars are D.

### 1.1 Glucose: open chain and ring

Glucose is made from the hydrolysis of sucrose with dilute acid, or of starch with dilute H₂SO₄ under pressure at 393 K. Its structure was worked out from reactions:

| Reaction | Tells us |
|---|---|
| HI, heat gives n-hexane | six carbons in an unbranched chain |
| HCN adds, hydroxylamine gives an oxime | a carbonyl group is present |
| Bromine water gives gluconic acid | the carbonyl is an aldehyde (CHO) |
| Nitric acid gives saccharic acid (a dicarboxylic acid) | there is a primary alcohol (CH₂OH) at the other end |
| Acetic anhydride gives a pentaacetate | five OH groups |

But glucose does **not** give the Schiff test or the NaHSO₃ addition product, and its pentaacetate does not react with hydroxylamine. Something is hiding the aldehyde: the molecule is mainly a **ring**. The C5–OH adds to the C1 aldehyde to make a cyclic **hemiacetal**:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-bio-cyclic">
<div class="step"><b>The open chain.</b> C1 is the aldehyde carbon; C5 carries an OH.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The chain curls round</b> so the C5 hydroxyl comes close to C1.</div>
<div class="step"><b>C5–OH attacks the aldehyde carbon</b>: the ring (5 C + 1 O, a pyranose) closes.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Two ring forms.</b> The new OH on C1 points down (α) or up (β). They are anomers.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Mutarotation.</b> Either ring can open and close again: 36 % α, 64 % β, under 0.02 % open chain.</div>
</div>

The two forms have different optical rotations: α-D-glucose +111°, β-D-glucose +19.2°. A fresh solution of either slowly changes to the same equilibrium value, +52.5°. This change, **mutarotation**, is how the ring-opening was proved.

**Fructose** is a ketohexose with the C=O at C2; it closes to a five-membered furanose ring in the sugars it forms, and is the sweetest natural sugar. Fructose is laevorotatory (−92.4°). **Galactose** differs from glucose at C4 only.

### 1.2 Disaccharides and polysaccharides

Two monosaccharides join through their OH groups with the loss of water: a **glycosidic link**. Pick a sugar to see the units, the link and whether it is reducing, then hydrolyse it:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-bio-sugars"><figcaption>Choose a sugar and press the hydrolysis button. Sucrose splits into glucose and fructose, maltose into two glucose, lactose into galactose and glucose, and the polysaccharides into many glucose units.</figcaption></figure>

- **Sucrose** (table sugar from sugarcane) is glucose + fructose joined α-1,β-2. It is **non-reducing**, as both anomeric carbons are used. It is dextrorotatory (+66.5°); on hydrolysis it gives glucose (+52.5°) and fructose (−92.4°), whose mixture is **levorotatory (−20°)**, so the rotation “inverts” and the mixture is called **invert sugar**.
- **Maltose** is two glucose, α-1,4; **lactose** is galactose + glucose, β-1,4. Both are reducing, since they keep one free anomeric carbon.
- **Starch** (rice, wheat, potato) is a plant food store: about 20 % **amylose** (unbranched α-1,4 chain, water-soluble, gives the blue colour with iodine) and 80 % **amylopectin** (branched with α-1,6 links at each 20 to 25 units).
- **Cellulose** is the plant cell wall: glucose joined **β-1,4** in straight chains held side by side by hydrogen bonds. Cotton, paper and wood are cellulose. Humans lack the enzyme for the β link, so cellulose is fibre; cows digest it with the help of gut bacteria.
- **Glycogen** is the animal store (liver and muscle): like amylopectin but with more branches.

## 2. Proteins

Proteins are polymers of **α-amino acids**, compounds with an amino group and a carboxyl group on the same carbon, H₂N–CH(R)–COOH. About twenty occur in proteins. R decides the type: neutral (glycine, alanine, valine), acidic (aspartic and glutamic acid) or basic (lysine, arginine). **Essential** amino acids cannot be made in the body (valine, leucine, lysine...) and must come from food (dals, milk, eggs, soya); the others are non-essential.

### 2.1 Zwitterions and the isoelectric point

An amino acid has an acid and a base in the same molecule, so the acid group gives its H⁺ to the base group: it exists as a **zwitterion**, ⁺H₃N–CH(R)–COO⁻. This makes amino acids crystalline, high-melting and soluble in water. Change the pH and watch the charge change:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-bio-zwitter"><figcaption>Choose an amino acid and slide the pH. Low pH: positive (both groups protonated). Near the isoelectric point: a zwitterion with net charge zero. High pH: negative. Aspartic acid has a low pI, lysine a high one.</figcaption></figure>

At the **isoelectric point (pI)** the net charge is zero; the amino acid is then least soluble and does not move in an electric field. For a neutral amino acid pI is the average of its two pKa values.

### 2.2 The peptide bond

Two amino acids join when the –COOH of one reacts with the –NH₂ of the next, losing water to make an amide, **–CO–NH–**, the **peptide bond**. A chain of n amino acids has n − 1 peptide bonds, a free NH₂ end (**N-terminus**) and a free COOH end (**C-terminus**).

<div class="step"><b>Bring the ends together.</b> COOH of Gly meets NH₂ of Ala.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Water is lost.</b></div>
<div class="step"><b>The peptide bond</b> –CO–NH– links them; the bond is flat and cannot rotate freely.</div>
<div class="step"><b>A chain grows.</b> Four amino acids, three bonds, three waters lost.</div>

### 2.3 Four levels of structure

- **Primary:** the order of amino acids, held by peptide bonds. A single change (as in haemoglobin S, glutamic acid replaced by valine) can cause disease.
- **Secondary:** local patterns of the backbone held by hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H: the **α-helix** and the **β-pleated sheet**.
- **Tertiary:** the whole chain folds into a 3D shape, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, hydrophobic packing and **disulphide (S–S)** bridges between cysteines.
- **Quaternary:** several folded chains pack together (haemoglobin has four).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="@bio-protein-folding"><figcaption>A bead chain becomes a helix and a sheet, then a folded chain, then four subunits. Each step adds a new type of attraction.</figcaption></figure>

Proteins are **fibrous** (keratin in hair, collagen: long and insoluble) or **globular** (enzymes, haemoglobin, insulin: folded and soluble). **Denaturation** is the loss of secondary and tertiary structure caused by heat, acid or alkali: an egg white sets when boiled and does not “un-boil”. The primary structure, the peptide bonds, is unharmed.

## 3. Enzymes

Enzymes are biological **catalysts**, mostly proteins (a few RNA). They speed up reactions in living cells by a factor of a million or more at body temperature (about 310 K) and neutral pH, and each is **highly specific**: urease only splits urea; maltase only splits maltose. Their names usually end in **-ase**.

The reactant, the **substrate**, fits into a small pocket, the **active site**, held by weak attractions; it is converted to product and the enzyme is released unchanged. This is the **lock-and-key** model (and, a refinement, the **induced fit**: the site adjusts a little to the substrate). By holding the substrate in the right position, the enzyme **lowers the activation energy**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-bio-enzyme"><figcaption>Slide the substrate concentration and switch the inhibitor on. The rate levels off when every enzyme is busy. A competitive inhibitor looks like the substrate and blocks the site, so more substrate is needed to reach the same rate.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c12c-bio-mm"></div>

Each enzyme has an **optimum temperature and pH** (pepsin works at pH 2 in the stomach, trypsin at pH 8 in the intestine). Too hot or too acidic and it denatures. Inhibitors that bind the active site are the basis of many drugs. Everyday examples: amylase in saliva starts digesting starch, lactase splits lactose, and the enzymes in curd bacteria and yeast turn milk and dough.

## 4. Vitamins

Vitamins are organic compounds needed in tiny amounts that the body cannot make enough of. **Fat-soluble** vitamins (A, D, E, K) are stored in body fat; **water-soluble** ones (B group, C) are not stored (except B₁₂) and must be taken regularly.

## 5. Nucleic acids

Nucleic acids carry the instructions for building every protein. **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) is in the nucleus and stores the information; **RNA** (ribonucleic acid) copies and uses it. Both are polymers of **nucleotides**. A nucleotide has three parts: a **sugar** (2-deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), a **phosphate** group and a nitrogen **base**. A **nucleoside** is just the base plus the sugar.

The bases are purines (**adenine A, guanine G**) and pyrimidines (**cytosine C, thymine T** in DNA, **uracil U** in RNA). Nucleotides are joined by **phosphodiester links** (sugar–phosphate–sugar) into a long backbone, with the bases sticking out.

### 5.1 The double helix

Two DNA strands run in opposite directions and coil round each other (Watson and Crick, 1953, using X-ray photographs by Franklin and Wilkins). The bases meet in the middle and pair by hydrogen bonds: **A with T (two bonds) and G with C (three bonds)**. So the amount of A equals the amount of T, and G equals C (Chargaff's rules). ### 5.2 Biological functions

DNA does two jobs. **Replication:** the two strands separate and each acts as a template, so two identical DNA molecules form before a cell divides. **Protein synthesis:** DNA is **transcribed** into messenger RNA (mRNA) and the mRNA is **translated** at a ribosome into a protein. Every three bases (a **codon**) on the mRNA give one amino acid; tRNA molecules carry the amino acids. A change in a base (a mutation) may change a protein. Run the whole process:

<div class="keypoints">

- Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones; mono-, di- and polysaccharides. Reducing sugars have a free anomeric carbon; sucrose does not.
- Glucose: aldohexose; mostly a pyranose ring formed by the C5–OH adding to C1=O; α and β anomers differ at C1; mutarotation reaches +52.5°.
- Sucrose = glucose + fructose (invert sugar is levorotatory); maltose = 2 glucose (α-1,4); lactose = galactose + glucose (β-1,4). Starch: amylose + amylopectin (α); cellulose: β-1,4; glycogen: animal store.
- Amino acids are zwitterions; at the isoelectric point the net charge is zero. Peptide bond –CO–NH–; n amino acids give n − 1 bonds.
- Protein structure: primary (sequence), secondary (α-helix, β-sheet, hydrogen bonds), tertiary (folding, S–S bridges), quaternary (subunits). Denaturation keeps the primary structure.
- Enzymes: protein catalysts, specific, lock-and-key, lower the activation energy, work at optimum pH and temperature; competitive inhibitors block the active site.
- Vitamins: A, D, E, K fat-soluble; B and C water-soluble; each has a deficiency disease (A: night blindness, B₁: beri-beri, C: scurvy, D: rickets, B₁₂: pernicious anaemia).
- Nucleotide = base + sugar + phosphate. DNA: deoxyribose, A T G C, double helix, A–T two H bonds, G–C three. RNA: ribose, U, single strand (m, r, t). Replication and protein synthesis (transcription then translation).

</div>
