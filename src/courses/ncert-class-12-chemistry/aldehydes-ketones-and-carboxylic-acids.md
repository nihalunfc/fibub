**Chemistry · Class 12 · Chapter 8 · about 90 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids, explain their boiling points, predict the products of nucleophilic addition, oxidation, reduction, aldol and Cannizzaro reactions, tell them apart with laboratory tests, and explain why carboxylic acids are acidic.

<div class="bigidea">One group, C=O, does almost everything in this chapter. Oxygen pulls the electrons of the double bond towards itself, so the carbon is slightly positive (δ⁺) and the oxygen slightly negative (δ⁻). Nucleophiles attack the carbon. If the carbon also has an O–H (a carboxylic acid), the O–H comes off as H⁺ and the anion is stabilised by sharing its charge between two oxygens.</div>

## 1. The carbonyl group and naming

The **carbonyl group** is C=O. In an **aldehyde** the carbon is bonded to at least one H (R–CHO, at the end of a chain). In a **ketone** it is bonded to two carbons (R–CO–R′, inside a chain). In a **carboxylic acid** the carbon also has an OH (R–COOH). The carbonyl carbon is sp² with 120° angles and the group is flat.

**Naming.** Aldehydes: replace “e” of the alkane with **“al”** (propanal; the CHO carbon is carbon 1, so no number is needed). Ketones: replace “e” with **“one”** and number the C=O so it gets the lowest number (pentan-2-one). Acids: replace “e” with **“oic acid”** (butanoic acid). Common names to know: formaldehyde (methanal), acetaldehyde (ethanal), acetone (propanone), formic acid (methanoic), acetic acid (ethanoic), benzaldehyde, benzoic acid.

## 2. Making aldehydes and ketones

| Method | Gives |
|---|---|
| Oxidation of 1° alcohol with PCC, or Cu at 573 K (dehydrogenation) | aldehyde |
| Oxidation of 2° alcohol (K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺, or Cu 573 K) | ketone |
| Ozonolysis of alkenes (O₃, then Zn/H₂O) | aldehyde or ketone |
| Hydration of alkynes (Hg²⁺/H₂SO₄): ethyne gives ethanal; others give ketones | aldehyde or ketone |
| Rosenmund reduction: acid chloride + H₂ over Pd/BaSO₄ (poisoned) | aldehyde |
| Stephen reduction: nitrile + SnCl₂/HCl, then water | aldehyde |
| Etard reaction: toluene + CrO₂Cl₂, then water | benzaldehyde |
| Gattermann–Koch: benzene + CO + HCl, AlCl₃/CuCl | benzaldehyde |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation: benzene + RCOCl, AlCl₃ | aromatic ketone |
| Acid chloride + dialkylcadmium | ketone |

Do not try to learn the table by heart in one go. Group them: **oxidise an alcohol** (any), **reduce an acid derivative** (Rosenmund, Stephen), and **attach C=O to a ring** (Etard, Gattermann–Koch, Friedel–Crafts). Explore how the conversions connect on the reaction map:

## 3. Physical properties

Aldehydes and ketones are polar, so their molecules attract each other by **dipole–dipole forces**. They have no O–H, so they cannot hydrogen-bond to themselves. Their boiling points therefore sit between alkanes and alcohols. Carboxylic acids form **dimers** with two hydrogen bonds per pair, so they boil higher than alcohols of the same mass.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-carb-bp"><figcaption>Six compounds with almost the same molar mass (58 to 60 g/mol). Pick each one: the two molecules underneath show the force between them, and the bar shows the boiling point it produces.</figcaption></figure>

The carbonyl oxygen can accept hydrogen bonds from water, so the first members (methanal, ethanal, propanone) mix with water in all proportions. Solubility falls as the carbon chain gets longer. Benzaldehyde and benzoic acid are only slightly soluble. Many aldehydes have pleasant smells and are used in flavours and perfumes (vanillin, cinnamaldehyde in cinnamon).

## 4. Chemical reactions of aldehydes and ketones

### 4.1 Nucleophilic addition

A nucleophile (Nu⁻) attacks the δ⁺ carbon, the π electrons move onto oxygen to give an alkoxide, and then the oxygen picks up H⁺:

<div class="steps" data-fig="@chem-carbonyl-addition" data-title="Nucleophilic addition to C=O" data-ms="6000">
<div class="step"><b>The carbonyl carbon is δ⁺.</b></div>
<div class="step"><b>The nucleophile attacks it.</b></div>
<div class="step"><b>A tetrahedral alkoxide forms.</b></div>
<div class="step"><b>Protonation</b> gives the product.</div>
</div>

Important additions: **HCN** (a cyanohydrin; adds one carbon and an OH), **NaHSO₃** (a crystalline bisulphite compound, used to purify aldehydes), **alcohols with dry HCl** (acetals, which protect the carbonyl group), **NH₂–Z compounds** such as hydroxylamine (oximes), hydrazine (hydrazones), semicarbazide (semicarbazones) and **2,4-DNP** (orange-yellow solid, a test for any aldehyde or ketone), and **Grignard reagents** (alcohols).

**Reactivity order:** HCHO > RCHO > R₂C=O. Two reasons: alkyl groups push electrons towards the carbon (less δ⁺) and they crowd the carbon so the nucleophile cannot get in (steric hindrance). Aromatic aldehydes are less reactive than aliphatic ones because the ring donates electrons by resonance.

<div class="myth">

**“Ketones are more reactive than aldehydes because they have more carbon.”** The opposite is true: the two alkyl groups of a ketone both reduce the positive charge on carbon and block the approach of the nucleophile. That is also why aldehydes are easy to oxidise and ketones are not.

</div>

### 4.2 Oxidation and reduction

Aldehydes are oxidised easily (they have an H on the carbonyl carbon). Ketones need strong oxidants and break up. Reduction goes the other way. Move up and down the ladder:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-carb-ladder"><figcaption>Choose a direction and a starting compound. The arrow shows the next rung and the text gives the reagents: PCC and KMnO₄ going up; NaBH₄, LiAlH₄, Clemmensen and Wolff–Kishner going down.</figcaption></figure>

- **Reduction to alcohol:** H₂/Ni, NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄. Aldehyde gives 1° alcohol; ketone gives 2° alcohol.
- **Reduction to alkane:** **Clemmensen** (Zn–Hg, conc. HCl) or **Wolff–Kishner** (N₂H₄, then KOH in ethylene glycol, heat).
- **Oxidation of aldehydes:** Tollens, Fehling, KMnO₄ and others give the acid.

Laboratory tests turn these differences into colours. Choose a compound and a test:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-carb-tests"><figcaption>Pick a compound and a test. The tube shows what you would see: silver mirror (Tollens), red-brown precipitate (Fehling, aliphatic aldehydes only), orange precipitate (2,4-DNP, any aldehyde or ketone), yellow CHI₃ (iodoform, methyl ketones and CH₃CH(OH)– alcohols) and CO₂ bubbles (acids with NaHCO₃).</figcaption></figure>

### 4.3 Reactions that use the α hydrogens

Aldehydes and ketones with an α hydrogen react with **dilute base** by the **aldol reaction**. A base removes an α hydrogen to give an enolate, which attacks a second molecule's C=O. This forms a C–C bond, and heating then removes water:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-carb-aldol">
<div class="step"><b>Ethanal has α hydrogens</b>: the CH₃ next to C=O is slightly acidic.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Base removes an α hydrogen.</b> An enolate forms.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The enolate attacks a second ethanal.</b> A new C–C bond forms.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Protonation gives the aldol</b>, 3-hydroxybutanal.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Heat removes water.</b> But-2-enal forms: an aldol condensation.</div>
</div>

Aldehydes and ketones **without** an α hydrogen (methanal, benzaldehyde) cannot do this. With concentrated NaOH they do the **Cannizzaro reaction**, a disproportionation: half the molecules are oxidised to the carboxylate and half reduced to the alcohol.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-carb-cannizzaro">
<div class="step"><b>No α hydrogen</b>, so no enolate.</div>
<div class="step"><b>OH⁻ attacks the C=O.</b></div>
<div class="step"><b>A hydride jumps</b> to a second aldehyde.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Two products:</b> the acid salt and the alcohol.</div>
<div class="step"><b>One is oxidised, one is reduced</b>: disproportionation.</div>
</div>

Other reactions: **halogenation** of methyl ketones in base gives the haloform (iodoform) reaction; **aromatic aldehydes and ketones** undergo electrophilic substitution at the **meta** position (the C=O withdraws electrons from the ring). Uses: methanal as 40 % formalin (preserving specimens) and in Bakelite; ethanal for acetic acid; propanone as a solvent (nail-polish remover).

## 5. Carboxylic acids: structure, making them

The carboxyl group –COOH is the carbonyl group and the hydroxyl group together, and each changes the other: the OH is more acidic than in an alcohol, and the C=O is less reactive towards nucleophiles than in a ketone. The first members have sharp smells (methanoic acid is the sting of ants and nettles; ethanoic acid is vinegar, about 5 % in water).

**Preparation.** (1) Oxidation of primary alcohols or aldehydes (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇). (2) Oxidation of alkylbenzenes with hot KMnO₄ (toluene gives benzoic acid; the whole side chain becomes COOH). (3) **Hydrolysis of nitriles** and amides: RCN + 2 H₂O → RCOOH + NH₃ (acid or base). (4) **Grignard reagents** with CO₂ (dry ice), then acid, add one carbon. (5) Hydrolysis of acid derivatives (acid chlorides, anhydrides, esters).

## 6. Acidity of carboxylic acids

Carboxylic acids are weak acids (ethanoic acid pKa 4.76) but far stronger than alcohols (pKa 16) and phenols (10). They turn blue litmus red and release CO₂ from carbonates and hydrogencarbonates (phenols do not). The reason is resonance in the anion:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c12c-carb-carboxylate">
<div class="step"><b>The acid.</b> Different C=O and C–O bonds.</div>
<div class="step"><b>It loses H⁺.</b></div>
<div class="step"><b>The charge sits on one oxygen</b> in this structure.</div>
<div class="step"><b>The other structure.</b> The π bond and the charge swap places.</div>
<div class="step"><b>Equal bonds, shared charge.</b> The real ion is a mixture of both.</div>
</div>

**Effect of substituents.** Electron-withdrawing groups on the chain or ring (Cl, F, NO₂) pull electrons through the σ bonds (**−I effect**), stabilise the anion and **raise** acidity; electron-donating groups (alkyl, OCH₃) **lower** it. The effect falls rapidly with distance and rises with the number of withdrawing groups.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c12c-carb-acidity"><figcaption>Switch between the chlorinated ethanoic acids (slide the number of Cl atoms) and the para-substituted benzoic acids. Each Cl lowers the pKa by roughly 1.5 to 2 units; –NO₂ on the ring raises the strength, –CH₃ and –OCH₃ lower it.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c12c-carb-ph"></div>

<div class="myth">

**“Formic acid is a weaker acid than acetic acid because it is smaller.”** Methanoic acid (pKa 3.75) is stronger than ethanoic acid (4.76). The CH₃ of ethanoic acid pushes electrons towards the carboxylate and destabilises it, while methanoic acid has only an H.

</div>

## 7. Reactions of carboxylic acids

1. **With metals and bases** (O–H bond): salts, with H₂ from Na, Zn or Mg. **With NaHCO₃**: CO₂ effervescence, a test for –COOH.
2. **Esterification** (C–OH bond): acid + alcohol with a little concentrated H₂SO₄ is a reversible reaction, with water as the other product. Use an excess of one reactant or remove water to push it to the ester.

<!--fig:tool esterification-->

<div class="problem" data-problem="c12c-carb-ester"></div>

3. **Acid chlorides** with SOCl₂, PCl₅ or PCl₃ (the by-products of SOCl₂, SO₂ and HCl, are gases, so it gives a pure product). **Anhydrides** by heating with P₂O₅ or from an acid chloride. **Amides** from ammonium salts on heating.
4. **Reduction** with LiAlH₄ gives the primary alcohol (NaBH₄ will not do it).
5. **Decarboxylation:** sodium salt heated with soda lime (NaOH + CaO) loses CO₂ to give an alkane with one carbon less. Kolbe electrolysis of the sodium salt gives an alkane with the two R groups joined.
6. **Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ):** an acid with an α hydrogen with Br₂ and red phosphorus gives the α-bromo acid.
7. **Ring substitution:** –COOH directs the incoming group to **meta** (nitration of benzoic acid gives 3-nitrobenzoic acid) and is a deactivator, so benzoic acid does not undergo Friedel–Crafts reactions.

Infrared spectra show the C=O stretch strongly near 1700 cm⁻¹ (aldehyde 1725, ketone 1715, acid 1710, ester 1740), and an acid adds a very broad O–H band from 2500 to 3300 cm⁻¹:

Uses: ethanoic acid in vinegar and to make cellulose acetate and aspirin; methanoic acid in leather and rubber; benzoic acid and sodium benzoate as food preservatives; long-chain acids (stearic, palmitic) as soaps; adipic acid for nylon-6,6.

<div class="history">

- **1853** Stanislao Cannizzaro finds that benzaldehyde with strong base gives both an alcohol and an acid.
- **1872** Charles Wurtz and Alexander Borodin independently report the aldol reaction, the first controlled way to join two carbonyl compounds by a C–C bond.
- **1900** Victor Grignard's reagents make acids from CO₂ and alcohols from carbonyl compounds.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**Beyond the syllabus (extension, check your school's list).** Enolate chemistry beyond aldol: crossed aldol reactions with two different aldehydes (they give mixtures unless one has no α H) and the mechanism of the HVZ reaction through an acyl bromide enol are common in entrance exams but are not asked as full mechanisms in the board paper.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Aldehyde R–CHO, ketone R–CO–R′, acid R–COOH; C=O is sp², planar, polar (C δ⁺). Names: -al, -one, -oic acid.
- Made by oxidising alcohols, Rosenmund (acid chloride), Stephen (nitrile), Etard and Gattermann–Koch (benzaldehyde), Friedel–Crafts acylation (aromatic ketones).
- Boiling points: alkane < ether < aldehyde/ketone < alcohol < acid (dimers). First members dissolve in water.
- Nucleophilic addition: HCN, NaHSO₃, ROH (acetal), NH₂–Z (oximes, hydrazones, 2,4-DNP). Reactivity HCHO > RCHO > R₂CO.
- Aldehydes are oxidised (Tollens silver mirror; Fehling red ppt, aliphatic only); ketones are not. Methyl ketones give iodoform. Reduce with NaBH₄, LiAlH₄ (to alcohol), Clemmensen and Wolff–Kishner (to CH₂).
- Aldol needs an α H (dilute base, then heat gives the condensation); Cannizzaro needs no α H (conc. NaOH): acid salt + alcohol.
- Acids are acidic because the carboxylate shares charge over two O atoms; –I groups strengthen, alkyl groups weaken them; they release CO₂ from NaHCO₃.
- Acid reactions: esterification (reversible), acid chlorides (SOCl₂), LiAlH₄ to alcohol, decarboxylation (soda lime), HVZ (α-bromination), meta-direction in the ring.

</div>
