**Practice · Class 9 Maths · Chapter 8.** Read the lesson, [Predicting What Comes Next: Exploring Sequences and Progressions](/learn/courses/ncert-class-9-maths/sequences-and-progressions/), first. Try each question on paper before you open the answer.

<div class="bigidea">For any AP question, begin by writing down a and d. Then write the formula a + (n − 1)d with the numbers in it, and check by trying n = 1 (you must get a). Remember: n − 1 steps, not n.</div>

## How to set out answers

1. Write what you know: a = ..., d = ..., n = ...
2. Write the formula aₙ = a + (n − 1)d.
3. Substitute with brackets round negative numbers and solve.
4. Check: does the answer fit the first few terms?

## 1-mark questions

1. Write the next term: 6, 11, 16, 21, ...
<details><summary>Answer</summary>26 (add 5).</details>

2. Find the common difference of the AP 40, 34, 28, ...
<details><summary>Answer</summary>34 − 40 = −6.</details>

3. Write the formula for the nth term of an AP with first term a and common difference d.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>aₙ = a + (n − 1)d.</details>

4. How many dots are there in the 4th square pattern?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>4 × 4 = 16.</details>

## 2-mark and 3-mark questions

5. Is 3, 6, 12, 24, ... an AP? Give a reason. *(2 marks)*
<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. The differences are 3, 6, 12, which are not the same. (It is a geometric progression: each term is double the one before.)</details>

6. Find the 15th term of the AP 8, 13, 18, ... *(2 marks)*
<details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 8, d = 5. a₁₅ = 8 + 14 × 5 = 78.</details>

7. Which term of the AP 7, 11, 15, ... is 71? *(3 marks)*
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

7 + (n − 1) × 4 = 71, so (n − 1) × 4 = 64, n − 1 = 16 and n = 17. 71 is the 17th term.

</details>

8. Is 100 a term of the AP 3, 8, 13, ...? Explain. *(3 marks)*
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

3 + (n − 1) × 5 = 100 gives (n − 1) × 5 = 97, and n − 1 = 19.4. Since n is not a whole number, 100 is not a term of the AP.

</details>

9. A row of squares is made from matchsticks: 1 square uses 4 sticks, 2 squares use 7, 3 squares use 10. Write the rule and find how many sticks 25 squares need. *(3 marks)*
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Each extra square adds 3 sticks, so the counts form an AP with a = 4, d = 3. The nth term is 4 + (n − 1) × 3 = 3n + 1. For n = 25: 3 × 25 + 1 = 76 sticks.

</details>

Fresh numbers every time. Try until you get three right in a row.

## 5-mark questions

10. The 4th term of an AP is 14 and the 9th term is 34. Find the first term, the common difference and the 20th term. *(5 marks)*
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

From the 4th term to the 9th is 5 steps, so 5d = 34 − 14 = 20, and d = 4. Then a + 3 × 4 = 14 gives a = 2. The 20th term is 2 + 19 × 4 = 78. Check: the 9th term is 2 + 8 × 4 = 34. ✓

</details>

11. (a) How many dots are in the 12th triangular pattern? (b) Which triangular pattern has 66 dots? (c) Show that two consecutive triangular numbers add up to a square number, using the 5th and 6th. *(5 marks)*
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) T₁₂ = 12 × 13 ÷ 2 = 78. (b) n(n + 1) ÷ 2 = 66 gives n(n + 1) = 132 = 11 × 12, so n = 11. (c) T₅ = 15 and T₆ = 21. Their sum is 36 = 6².

</details>

## Case-based question

Vikram’s grandmother in Madurai puts coins into a hundi on festival days. On the first day she puts ₹10, and each day after that she puts ₹5 more than the day before. At the same time Vikram tries a game: he puts 1 rupee on day 1 and doubles the amount every day.

12. (a) Write the first five amounts for his grandmother and for Vikram. (b) How much does his grandmother put in on day 12? (c) How much does Vikram put in on day 12? (d) Who puts in more on day 12, and which sequence is an AP?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) Grandmother: 10, 15, 20, 25, 30. Vikram: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. (b) a = 10, d = 5: 10 + 11 × 5 = ₹65. (c) 2¹¹ = ₹2,048. (d) Vikram puts in far more on day 12 (₹2,048 against ₹65). The grandmother’s amounts form an AP (constant difference 5). Vikram’s amounts multiply by 2 each day: a geometric progression, not an AP.

</details>

## Assertion and reason

13. **Assertion (A):** The sequence 2, 5, 8, 11, ... has the nth term 3n − 1. **Reason (R):** The first term is 2 and the common difference is 3.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both are true and R explains A: a + (n − 1)d = 2 + 3(n − 1) = 3n − 1.</details>

14. **Assertion (A):** The sequence 1, 4, 9, 16, ... is an AP. **Reason (R):** Square numbers increase every time.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>A is false: the differences 3, 5, 7 are not constant. R is true (they do increase) but increasing is not the same as increasing by a fixed amount. So A is false and R is true.</details>

15. **Assertion (A):** The sum 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 equals 25. **Reason (R):** The sum of the first n odd numbers is n².
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both are true and R explains A: n = 5 and 5² = 25.</details>

<div class="keypoints">

- AP: each term is the previous plus d. aₙ = a + (n − 1)d. Constant differences mean an AP.
- To find a, d or n, set up the formula and solve. n must be a positive whole number.
- Triangular numbers n(n + 1) ÷ 2; square numbers n²; the sum of the first n odd numbers is n².
- Geometric: multiply by a common ratio r each time; it outgrows adding in the long run.

</div>
