**Module 6 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will find the hidden assumption in any argument and prove your answer with the negation test.

<div class="bigidea">An assumption is a fact the author needs but never says. Deny it, and the argument falls apart.</div>

## 1. What is being asked

Stems: "The argument assumes…", "depends on which assumption?", "relies on…". You are looking for the missing piece between the evidence and the conclusion (Lesson 6.1, the gap).

## 2. Predict the gap

<div class="steps" data-title="Worked example">
<div class="step"><i>"Fennick Foods switched to a cheaper brand of tyre and now spends far less on tyres. So the switch reduced its total cost of running trucks."</i></div>
<div class="step">Evidence is about <b>tyre spending</b>. Conclusion is about <b>total running cost</b>. The words changed: that is where the gap is.</div>
<div class="step">Prediction: the cheaper tyres do not raise other costs (repairs, fuel, breakdowns) by more than they save.</div>
<div class="step">The right answer says exactly that. A choice about "the factory the tyres come from" or "other companies switching" does nothing to the gap.</div>
</div>

**Tip:** look for a **new word** in the conclusion that never appears in the evidence (profit vs sales, total cost vs tyre cost, safer vs fewer crashes). The assumption links the old word to the new one.

## 3. The negation test

To check a choice, turn it into its opposite and ask: **does the argument still work?**

- "Some drivers will switch to the bus" → negated: "No drivers will switch." The bus removes no cars; the argument dies. **Necessary.**
- "Most drivers will switch" → negated: "Not most will switch (maybe only a few)." A few switching still reduces cars; the argument survives. **Not necessary.**

<figure class="fig" data-fig="negation-test"><figcaption>Pick a choice and press Negate. If the bridge from evidence to conclusion breaks, the choice is an assumption the argument needs.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**"The strongest-sounding choice is the best assumption."** The reverse is usually true. A necessary assumption is the <i>minimum</i> the argument needs. Words like "most", "all", "always" and "the main reason" are often too strong; "some", "at least one", "not all" are often just right.

</div>

## 4. Three classic assumptions

1. **No other cause:** "Tea drinkers have fewer colds, so tea prevents colds" assumes tea drinkers do not differ in another way that matters.
2. **The plan has no fatal side effect:** "Moving the help desk overseas will save money" assumes lost customers do not cancel the savings.
3. **The numbers mean what they seem to:** "Fee income rose because the fee doubled and visitors fell only 10%" assumes a similar share still pays.

## 5. Sufficient assumptions

Occasionally the stem says "the conclusion follows logically **if** which is assumed?". Now you need a choice that makes the conclusion certain, not merely possible. These are often "only" statements: "Only employees who finished the course got a certificate" makes "Priya has a certificate, so she finished" airtight. Strong wording is fine here.

<div class="keypoints">

- An assumption is unstated but required: it links the evidence to the conclusion.
- Find it by spotting the new idea in the conclusion.
- Negation test: deny the choice; if the argument breaks, it is necessary.
- Necessary assumptions are modest; "too strong" choices are a common trap.
- "Follows logically if" asks for a sufficient assumption, which can be strong.

</div>
