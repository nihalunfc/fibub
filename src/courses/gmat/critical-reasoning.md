**Module 6 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will break any short argument into its parts and know what a Critical Reasoning question is really asking.

<div class="bigidea">Every argument is evidence plus a conclusion, joined by an unstated assumption. Almost every Critical Reasoning question is about that hidden link.</div>

## 1. What a Critical Reasoning question looks like

A short passage (usually 40 to 100 words) makes an argument or gives some facts. A question asks you to strengthen, weaken, find an assumption, spot a flaw, draw a conclusion or explain a puzzle. There are five answer choices. You do not need any outside knowledge; you need to read precisely.

Expect about 9 to 11 Critical Reasoning questions in the Verbal section, so it is worth a third of your Verbal score.

## 2. Take the argument apart

<div class="steps" data-title="Worked example: find the parts">
<div class="step"><i>"Our new website went live in May. Online orders in June were the highest ever. Therefore the new website boosted sales."</i></div>
<div class="step"><b>Conclusion</b> (the claim the author wants you to accept): the new website boosted sales. Look for "therefore", "so", "thus", "clearly", "hence".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Evidence</b> (the facts given in support): the site went live in May; June orders were a record. Look for "because", "since", "given that", "for example".</div>
<div class="step"><b>Gap</b> (what must also be true): nothing else caused the June record, such as a holiday sale or a competitor closing. The author assumes the timing proves the cause.</div>
</div>

Write the conclusion in your own words before you read the choices. If you cannot find it, the question is probably an inference or paradox question (Lessons 6.5 and 6.6), where there is no conclusion.

## 3. The common gaps

The GMAT reuses a small number of reasoning patterns. Learn to spot them and most questions become recognition.

| Pattern | The gap | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cause from a link | No other cause, no reversed cause | "Tea drinkers get fewer colds, so tea prevents colds" |
| Plan will work | No side effect cancels the benefit | "Cheaper tyres will cut our costs" |
| Sample to group | The sample is typical | "80% of our website visitors agree, so most people do" |
| Numbers shift | The two numbers measure the same thing | "Sales rose, so profits rose" |
| Percent vs total | A rate change is not a count change | "Crime rate fell, so fewer crimes" |
| Analogy | The two cases are alike in the way that matters | "It worked in Paris, so it will work here" |

<div class="myth">

**"Pick the answer that is true in real life."** The passage sets the world. A choice can be true, even famous, and still do nothing to the argument. Judge each choice only by its effect on the link between evidence and conclusion.

</div>

## 4. Read the question stem first

The last sentence tells you the job. Read it **before** the passage so you know what to look for.

| Stem says | Type | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| "assumes", "depends on", "relies on" | Assumption | 6.2 |
| "strengthens", "supports", "weakens", "casts doubt" | Strengthen / weaken | 6.3 |
| "most useful to know", "evaluate" | Evaluate | 6.4 |
| "flawed because", "vulnerable to criticism" | Flaw | 6.4 |
| "must be true", "most strongly supported", "conclude" | Inference | 6.5 |
| "roles", "bold" | Boldface | 6.6 |
| "explains", "resolves the discrepancy" | Paradox | 6.6 |

## 5. The four-step method

<div class="steps" data-title="One method for every CR question">
<div class="step"><b>1. Read the stem.</b> Name the question type.</div>
<div class="step"><b>2. Read the passage and split it:</b> conclusion, evidence, and (for most types) the gap.</div>
<div class="step"><b>3. Predict.</b> Before looking at choices, say roughly what the answer will do: "it will show another cause of the June record."</div>
<div class="step"><b>4. Eliminate.</b> Go through all five choices and cross out those that are irrelevant, too strong, or do the opposite job. Pick from what is left.</div>
</div>

Aim for about **2 minutes** per question. If two choices remain, ask which one deals with the gap more directly.

## 6. For deeper practice

The [LSAT course](/learn/courses/lsat/) goes further into the same skills: [the parts of an argument](/learn/courses/lsat/argument-anatomy/), [conditional logic](/learn/courses/lsat/conditional-logic/) and [common flaws](/learn/courses/lsat/common-flaws/). GMAT questions use the same reasoning in a business setting.

<div class="keypoints">

- Every argument: evidence → conclusion, joined by an unstated assumption (the gap).
- Conclusion words: therefore, so, thus, hence. Evidence words: because, since, given that.
- Read the stem first and name the question type.
- Predict the answer before reading the choices, then eliminate.
- Judge choices by their effect on the argument, never by real-world truth.

</div>
