**Module 3 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will turn word problems into equations and solve one or two equations quickly, often without finding every unknown.

<div class="bigidea">An equation is a balance. Do the same thing to both sides and it stays true; keep going until the unknown stands alone.</div>

## 1. One equation, one unknown

<div class="steps" data-title="Worked example: 4(x − 3) + 2 = 2x + 8">
<div class="step">Clear brackets: 4x − 12 + 2 = 2x + 8, so 4x − 10 = 2x + 8.</div>
<div class="step">Collect x on one side: subtract 2x from both sides: 2x − 10 = 8.</div>
<div class="step">Collect numbers on the other: add 10: 2x = 18.</div>
<div class="step">Divide by 2: <b>x = 9</b>. Check: 4(6) + 2 = 26 and 2(9) + 8 = 26. ✓</div>
</div>

With fractions, multiply every term by the common denominator first: x/3 + x/4 = 14 → 4x + 3x = 168 → x = 24.

## 2. Words into equations

| Words | Maths |
|---|---|
| is, was, will be | = |
| 5 more than x | x + 5 |
| 3 less than x | x − 3 (order matters) |
| twice as many as y | 2y |
| x is 4 years older than y | x = y + 4 |
| the sum of x and y is 20 | x + y = 20 |

Name each unknown with a letter that reminds you what it is (c for children, a for adults), and write down what you are asked for before you start.

## 3. Two equations, two unknowns

<figure class="fig" data-fig="line-pair"><figcaption>Each linear equation is a line. The solution of the system is where the lines cross. Make the slopes equal to see what happens when there is no solution.</figcaption></figure>

**Elimination:** add or subtract the equations so one letter disappears.
3x + 2y = 16 and x − 2y = 0 → add: 4x = 16, x = 4, then y = 2.

**Substitution:** solve one equation for a letter and put it into the other.
y = 2x − 1 and 3x + y = 14 → 3x + 2x − 1 = 14 → x = 3, y = 5.

## 4. The shortcut: solve for what is asked

GMAT questions often ask for x + y, x − y or 2x + 3y, not x and y separately. Try **adding or subtracting the whole equations** first.

<div class="steps" data-title="Worked example: 3x + 5y = 41 and 5x + 3y = 31. Find x + y.">
<div class="step">Add them: 8x + 8y = 72.</div>
<div class="step">Divide by 8: <b>x + y = 9</b>. Done in two steps; finding x and y separately would take four.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Two unknowns always need two equations."** Two <i>different</i> equations give one solution, but you might not need it. And two equations that are really the same line (one is a multiple of the other) never pin down x and y at all. Data Sufficiency (Lesson 8.1) tests exactly this.

</div>

## 5. Lines on a graph

A line is y = mx + b: **m** is the slope (rise ÷ run) and **b** is where it crosses the y-axis. Through (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) the slope is (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁). Parallel lines have equal slopes.

## 6. Age problems

Set everyone's age **now** with letters, then add the same number of years to each person for the future (or subtract for the past).

<div class="problem" data-problem="li-g03"></div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Do the same to both sides; clear brackets and fractions first.
- Translate carefully: "3 less than x" is x − 3.
- Elimination (add/subtract equations) or substitution solves two equations.
- When asked for x + y or similar, try adding or subtracting the equations whole.
- Same slope and different intercept → no solution; same line → infinitely many.

</div>
