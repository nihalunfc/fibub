**Module 6 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will label every part of an argument and explain any apparent contradiction.

<div class="bigidea">Boldface: find the author's conclusion, then label each bold part for or against it. Paradox: find the new fact that lets both surprising facts be true.</div>

## 1. Boldface: map the argument

These passages often contain **two** views: someone else's prediction and the author's reply, which usually starts with "But", "However" or "Yet".

<div class="steps" data-title="Worked example: the sugar tax">
<div class="step">Economists predict a sugar tax will raise a lot of money. <b>They note people here drink more sugary drinks than almost anywhere.</b></div>
<div class="step">"But this prediction is likely to be wrong." ← the <b>author's conclusion</b>.</div>
<div class="step"><b>In a nearby country, many people switched to untaxed sugar-free drinks.</b> ← evidence <b>for the author</b>.</div>
<div class="step">So: bold 1 supports the view the author opposes; bold 2 supports the author's conclusion.</div>
</div>

Label each part with one of: **author's conclusion**, **evidence for it**, **opposing view**, **evidence for the opposing view**, or **background**. Then find the choice that matches your labels.

## 2. Paradox: explain, do not deny

Stems: "Which best explains the apparent discrepancy?", "most helps to resolve". Two facts seem to clash. The answer adds a third fact that makes both sensible.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="rate-total"><figcaption>A better on-time rate can come with more delayed passengers. Slide the number of flights and the on-time rate to see when the total number of delays rises.</figcaption></figure>

Common resolutions:

- **Rate vs total:** a better percentage of a much bigger number (more passengers) can still be more delays.
- **Different groups:** the two facts are about different people, places or times.
- **A balancing effect:** standing desks burn more calories, but standing workers eat more.
- **Another motive:** fewer local burglaries, yet fear from a nearby crime wave sells alarms.

<div class="myth">

**"The answer should show one of the facts is wrong."** Never. Both facts are given as true. A choice that disputes one of them cannot resolve the paradox.

</div>

**Try it:** Tap each sentence to label it as background, premise or conclusion, then press Check.

## 3. Practice

<div class="keypoints">

- Boldface: find the author's conclusion first (often after "But"), then label each bold part.
- Five labels: conclusion, evidence for it, opposing view, evidence for the opposing view, background.
- Paradox: add a fact that lets both facts be true; never deny either.
- Common resolutions: rate vs total, different groups, balancing effect, other motive.
