**Module 7 · about 50 minutes.** This lesson takes the vocabulary of the [JAM crystal structure lesson](/learn/courses/iit-jam-physics/crystal-structure/) (lattice, basis, Miller indices) and builds the one idea every later solid-state lesson runs on: the reciprocal lattice and its Brillouin zones. Diffraction, phonons, electron bands and Fermi surfaces are all drawn in this space.

<div class="bigidea">Anything that repeats with the lattice (an electron density, a potential, a vibration pattern) can be written as a sum of plane waves. Only certain wave vectors fit the repeat. Those wave vectors form a second lattice, the reciprocal lattice, and the cell around its origin, the first Brillouin zone, is the natural home of every wave in the crystal.</div>

## 1. Bravais lattices and primitive cells, quickly

A Bravais lattice is the set of points `$\vec R = n_1\vec a_1 + n_2\vec a_2 + n_3\vec a_3$` with integer `$n_i$`, and its defining property is that **every point has identical surroundings, in the same orientation**. There are 5 in 2D and 14 in 3D. Arrays that fail the orientation test are lattices with a basis: the honeycomb (graphene), hcp, diamond and the kagome net.

A **primitive cell** holds exactly one lattice point; its volume `$V_c = |\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)|$` is the same for every choice of primitive vectors. Centred lattices are described with a bigger conventional cell, so keep the two volumes apart:

| Lattice | Conventional cube holds | Primitive vectors (one choice) | `$V_c$` |
|---|---|---|---|
| sc | 1 point | `$a\hat x,\ a\hat y,\ a\hat z$` | `$a^3$` |
| bcc | 2 points | `$\tfrac a2(-1,1,1),\ \tfrac a2(1,-1,1),\ \tfrac a2(1,1,-1)$` | `$a^3/2$` |
| fcc | 4 points | `$\tfrac a2(0,1,1),\ \tfrac a2(1,0,1),\ \tfrac a2(1,1,0)$` | `$a^3/4$` |
| hexagonal | — | `$a\hat x,\ \tfrac a2(\hat x + \sqrt3\hat y),\ c\hat z$` | `$\tfrac{\sqrt3}2a^2c$` |

The symmetric primitive cell is the **Wigner-Seitz cell**: all points closer to one lattice point than to any other. It is built by drawing the perpendicular bisector planes to the neighbours and keeping the innermost region, so it has the full symmetry of the lattice. For sc it is a cube, for bcc a truncated octahedron, for fcc a rhombic dodecahedron.

## 2. The reciprocal lattice

Ask which plane waves `$e^{i\vec G\cdot\vec r}$` have the period of the lattice. We need `$e^{i\vec G\cdot(\vec r + \vec R)} = e^{i\vec G\cdot\vec r}$` for every `$\vec R$`, so

```math
e^{i\vec G\cdot\vec R} = 1 \quad\text{for all } \vec R \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \vec G\cdot\vec R = 2\pi\times\text{integer}
```

The solutions form a lattice of their own, generated by vectors `$\vec b_j$` with `$\vec a_i\cdot\vec b_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$`:

```math
\vec b_1 = 2\pi\,\frac{\vec a_2\times\vec a_3}{\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)},\quad
\vec b_2 = 2\pi\,\frac{\vec a_3\times\vec a_1}{\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)},\quad
\vec b_3 = 2\pi\,\frac{\vec a_1\times\vec a_2}{\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)}
```

Then `$\vec G = h\vec b_1 + k\vec b_2 + l\vec b_3$` gives `$\vec G\cdot\vec R = 2\pi(hn_1 + kn_2 + ln_3)$`, as required. A lattice-periodic function therefore has the Fourier series `$f(\vec r) = \sum_{\vec G} f_{\vec G}\,e^{i\vec G\cdot\vec r}$` with `$f_{\vec G} = \frac1{V_c}\int_{\text{cell}} f\,e^{-i\vec G\cdot\vec r}\,d^3r$`.

Three facts follow at once and are worth memorising:

1. **The reciprocal of the reciprocal is the direct lattice** (the definition is symmetric in `$\vec a$` and `$\vec b$`).
2. **The reciprocal lattice depends only on the Bravais lattice**, never on the basis. The basis decides how strongly each `$\vec G$` scatters (the structure factor, [lesson 7.2](/learn/courses/gate-physics/structure-factor/)).
3. **`$\vec G_{hkl}$` is normal to the planes `$(hkl)$` and `$|\vec G_{hkl}| = 2\pi/d_{hkl}$`**, for any lattice, when `$h, k, l$` have no common factor.

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>Why G is normal to (hkl).</b> The plane nearest the origin cuts the axes at a₁/h, a₂/k, a₃/l. Two vectors lying in it are a₁/h − a₂/k and a₂/k − a₃/l.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Dot them with G = h b₁ + k b₂ + l b₃.</b> G · (a₁/h − a₂/k) = 2π − 2π = 0, and the same for the other. So G is perpendicular to the plane.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>The spacing.</b> The distance from the origin to this plane is the projection of a₁/h on the unit normal: d = (G/|G|) · a₁/h = 2π/|G|.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Consequence.</b> Every family of lattice planes is one point of the reciprocal lattice (its shortest G in that direction). Multiples nG are the higher orders of Bragg reflection.</p></div>
</div>

**Worked example (non-cubic spacing).** A tetragonal crystal has `$a = 4.0$` Å and `$c = 6.0$` Å. Its reciprocal axes are orthogonal with lengths `$2\pi/a, 2\pi/a, 2\pi/c$`, so `$|\vec G_{101}|^2 = (2\pi)^2(1/a^2 + 1/c^2)$` and `$1/d^2 = 1/16 + 1/36 = 0.0903$` Å⁻², `$d_{101} = 3.33$` Å. For hexagonal axes the same method gives `$1/d^2 = \tfrac43(h^2 + hk + k^2)/a^2 + l^2/c^2$`; the angle between two plane normals is the angle between their `$\vec G$`s, never between the bare index triples (unless the crystal is cubic).

## 3. Reciprocal lattices you must know

Work `$\vec b_1 = 2\pi\,\vec a_2\times\vec a_3/V_c$` for the fcc vectors above: `$\vec a_2\times\vec a_3 = \tfrac{a^2}4(-1,1,1)$` and `$V_c = a^3/4$`, so `$\vec b_1 = \tfrac{2\pi}a(-1,1,1)$`. These are bcc primitive vectors.

| Direct lattice | Reciprocal lattice | Shortest `$\vec G$` | First Brillouin zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| sc, edge `$a$` | sc, edge `$2\pi/a$` | `$2\pi/a$` | cube |
| bcc, edge `$a$` | fcc, cube edge `$4\pi/a$` | `$2\sqrt2\pi/a$` (the {110} planes) | rhombic dodecahedron (12 faces) |
| fcc, edge `$a$` | bcc, cube edge `$4\pi/a$` | `$2\sqrt3\pi/a$` (the {111} planes) | truncated octahedron (8 hexagons + 6 squares) |
| 2D hexagonal, side `$a$` | hexagonal, side `$4\pi/(\sqrt3a)$`, turned `$30^\circ$` | `$4\pi/(\sqrt3a)$` | regular hexagon |
| 2D oblique, `$a_1, a_2, \gamma$` | oblique, angle `$180^\circ - \gamma$` | `$|\vec b_i| = 2\pi/(a_i\sin\gamma)$` | hexagon (in general) |

In fcc-indexing language: `$\tfrac{2\pi}a(h,k,l)$` is a reciprocal vector of **fcc** only when `$h, k, l$` are all even or all odd, and of **bcc** only when `$h + k + l$` is even. These are exactly the X-ray selection rules for the two cubic lattices: missing reflections are just points that are not in the true reciprocal lattice.

## 4. Brillouin zones

The **first Brillouin zone** is the Wigner-Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. Its faces are **Bragg planes**: the perpendicular bisectors of `$\vec G$`. They matter because a wave with wave vector `$\vec k$` is Bragg-reflected exactly when `$\vec k$` ends on such a plane. For elastic scattering `$|\vec k + \vec G| = |\vec k|$`, i.e.

```math
2\vec k\cdot\vec G + G^2 = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec k\cdot\hat G' = \tfrac12|\vec G'| \quad(\vec G' = -\vec G)
```

So electrons with `$\vec k$` on a zone face are reflected and band gaps open there; phonons on a zone face are standing waves with zero group velocity.

The **`$n$`th zone** is the set of points reached from the origin by crossing exactly `$n - 1$` Bragg planes. Higher zones are broken into pieces, but each can be moved by reciprocal vectors to fill the first zone exactly once, so **every zone has the same volume `$(2\pi)^3/V_c$`**. For the square lattice of side `$a$`, the second zone lies between the first square (side `$2\pi/a$`) and the square of side `$2\sqrt2\pi/a$` turned by 45°: area `$8\pi^2/a^2 - 4\pi^2/a^2 = 4\pi^2/a^2$`, as promised.

**High-symmetry points** have standard names. For the fcc lattice (zone points in units of `$2\pi/a$`):

| Point | Where | Coordinates | Distance from `$\Gamma$` |
|---|---|---|---|
| `$X$` | centre of a square face | `$(1, 0, 0)$` | `$2\pi/a$` |
| `$L$` | centre of a hexagonal face | `$(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$` | `$\sqrt3\pi/a$` (nearest face) |
| `$K$` | middle of an edge between two hexagons | `$(\tfrac34, \tfrac34, 0)$` | `$3\sqrt2\pi/(2a)$` |
| `$W$` | corner (square meets two hexagons) | `$(1, \tfrac12, 0)$` | `$\sqrt5\pi/a$` (farthest) |

For bcc: `$H = \tfrac{2\pi}a(1,0,0)$` (corner, farthest), `$N = \tfrac{2\pi}a(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$` (face centre, nearest, `$\sqrt2\pi/a$`), `$P = \tfrac{2\pi}a(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$`. For the 2D hexagonal lattice: `$M$` at `$2\pi/(\sqrt3a)$` and `$K$` at `$4\pi/(3a)$`, where graphene's Dirac cones sit. A point is inside the fcc zone when `$|k_x| + |k_y| + |k_z| \le \tfrac32$` and every `$|k_i| \le 1$` (units `$2\pi/a$`); on the surface when one of these is an equality.

**Worked example (the zone in numbers).** Copper is fcc with `$a = 3.61$` Å. `$V_c = a^3/4 = 11.76$` Å³, so `$V_{BZ} = 248.05/11.76 = 21.1$` Å⁻³. `$\Gamma X = 2\pi/a = 1.74$` Å⁻¹ and `$\Gamma L = \sqrt3\pi/a = 1.51$` Å⁻¹.

<div class="myth">

**"The Brillouin zone is the conventional cell of the reciprocal lattice, and higher zones are shells of growing radius."** Neither. The first zone is the *Wigner-Seitz* cell (it has the full symmetry, and its volume is the primitive one, `$(2\pi)^3/V_c$`, not `$(2\pi)^3/a^3$`). Higher zones are not spherical shells: a point at distance `$1.2\pi/a$` from `$\Gamma$` in a square lattice can be in the first zone (along a diagonal) or the second (along an axis). The rule is "how many Bragg planes have I crossed", never "how far am I".

</div>

## 5. Counting states and the Fermi sphere in the zone

With periodic boundary conditions on a crystal of `$N$` cells, the allowed `$\vec k$` form a fine grid with one point per `$(2\pi)^3/(NV_c)$`. The first zone therefore holds exactly **`$N$` wave vectors**: one per primitive cell. Each electron band holds `$2N$` electrons (spin), and each phonon branch has `$N$` modes. Because `$\vec k$` and `$\vec k + \vec G$` describe the same Bloch state or phonon, `$\hbar\vec k$` is a **crystal momentum**, conserved only up to `$\hbar\vec G$`.

**Worked example (does the Fermi sphere fit?).** A monovalent fcc metal has `$n = 4/a^3$` electrons, so `$k_F = (3\pi^2n)^{1/3} = (12\pi^2)^{1/3}/a = 4.91/a$`. The nearest zone faces (`$L$`) are at `$\sqrt3\pi/a = 5.44/a$`. Ratio 0.90: the sphere sits inside the zone, which is why Na, K, Cu, Ag and Au have nearly spherical Fermi surfaces. A divalent simple cubic metal has `$k_F = (6\pi^2)^{1/3}/a = 1.24\,\pi/a$`: its sphere pokes through the faces (into zone 2) while the corners of zone 1 stay empty, so it conducts although "two electrons per cell fill one band".

<div class="keypoints">

- `$\vec a_i\cdot\vec b_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$`; `$\vec G$` satisfies `$e^{i\vec G\cdot\vec R} = 1$`. The reciprocal lattice depends only on the Bravais lattice.
- `$\vec G_{hkl}\perp(hkl)$` and `$|\vec G_{hkl}| = 2\pi/d_{hkl}$` in every crystal; plane normals and angles come from `$\vec G$`, not from `$[hkl]$`.
- sc ↔ sc (`$2\pi/a$`); bcc ↔ fcc with cube edge `$4\pi/a$`; 2D hexagonal → hexagonal of side `$4\pi/(\sqrt3a)$`, turned 30°; 2D angle `$\gamma \to 180^\circ - \gamma$`.
- Zone volume `$(2\pi)^3/V_c$` with the *primitive* volume: sc `$8\pi^3/a^3$`, bcc `$16\pi^3/a^3$`, fcc `$32\pi^3/a^3$`. All zones have equal volume.
- fcc zone: `$X$` at `$2\pi/a$`, `$L$` at `$\sqrt3\pi/a$`, `$K$` at `$3\sqrt2\pi/2a$`, `$W$` at `$\sqrt5\pi/a$`. bcc zone: `$N$` at `$\sqrt2\pi/a$`, `$H$` at `$2\pi/a$`. Hexagonal 2D: `$K$` at `$4\pi/3a$`.
- The zone holds `$N$` wave vectors; `$\hbar\vec k$` is crystal momentum, conserved modulo `$\hbar\vec G$`.

</div>
