**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 3 · about 60 minutes.** By the end you will be able to compare metals with non-metals, use the reactivity series to predict reactions, explain how ionic compounds form and behave, describe how metals are extracted, and explain how corrosion is prevented.

<div class="bigidea">Metals tend to give electrons away and non-metals tend to take them. How easily a metal gives them up is its reactivity, and that single idea explains how it reacts with oxygen, water and acids, how it is dug out of the ground, and how it rusts.</div>

## 1. Physical properties: telling metals from non-metals

Take a copper wire, a piece of aluminium foil, a lump of sulphur and a stick of graphite. They differ in many ways. Typically **metals** are:

- **lustrous** (shiny) when freshly cut or polished,
- **malleable** (can be hammered into sheets; think of silver varakh on sweets),
- **ductile** (can be drawn into wires: copper cables),
- good **conductors** of heat (cooking pans) and electricity,
- **sonorous** (ring when struck: temple bells, school gongs),
- **hard** and with high melting points, and solid at room temperature.

**Non-metals** are usually dull, brittle (snap or crumble when hit), poor conductors, not sonorous, and many are gases or soft solids. Exceptions are the interesting part: **mercury** is a liquid metal, **sodium** and **potassium** are soft enough to cut with a knife, **graphite** conducts, **diamond** is the hardest known natural substance and has a very high melting point, and **iodine** is a shiny non-metal.

## 2. Chemical properties of metals

### 2.1 With oxygen

Most metals combine with oxygen to make **metal oxides**: metal + oxygen → metal oxide. For example, 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO (black), 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃ and 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (the dazzling white flame). Sodium and potassium are so reactive that they catch fire in air, so they are stored under **kerosene**. Aluminium and zinc form a thin, tough oxide layer that protects them from further corrosion. Gold and platinum do not react with oxygen at all, which is why they stay shiny.

Metal oxides are generally **basic**: they react with acids to give a salt and water (CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O). A few oxides, **aluminium oxide and zinc oxide**, react with both acids and bases. They are called **amphoteric** oxides. Non-metal oxides, such as CO₂ and SO₂, are acidic and react with bases.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-metal-oxides"><figcaption>Choose an oxide and see whether it dissolves (reacts) in acid, in base, or in both. Basic oxides react with acid only, acidic oxides with base only, amphoteric ones with both, and CO is neutral.</figcaption></figure>

### 2.2 With water and with dilute acids

The more reactive a metal, the faster it releases hydrogen. Sodium reacts violently with cold water, calcium reacts less violently, magnesium reacts only with hot water, while aluminium, zinc and iron react with **steam** and copper does not react at all. With dilute hydrochloric acid, magnesium bubbles fiercely, zinc and aluminium less so, iron slowly and copper not at all.

- 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ (cold water)
- Mg + 2H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂ (hot water)
- 3Fe + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ (steam)
- Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

Change the reagent and watch seven metals race:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-metal-react"><figcaption>Seven test tubes, one reagent at a time. The number of hydrogen bubbles shows how vigorous the reaction is. The order always comes out the same: Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Cu.</figcaption></figure>

### 2.3 Displacement: the reactivity series

When a metal is put into a solution of the salt of another metal, the more reactive one **displaces** the less reactive one. Putting the results together gives the **reactivity series** (activity series), a list from the most to the least reactive metals:

**K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au**

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-metal-displace"><figcaption>Choose a metal strip and a salt solution. If the strip is higher in the series than the metal in the solution, a coat of the lower metal forms on the strip and the colour of the solution changes. Otherwise nothing happens.</figcaption></figure>

## 3. How do metals and non-metals react? Ionic bonds

Noble gases are unreactive because their outer shell is **full**: 2 electrons for helium, 8 (an **octet**) for the rest. Other atoms react to reach the same stable arrangement. A **metal** atom has few outer electrons, so it is easiest to **lose** them and become a **positive ion (cation)**. A **non-metal** atom has nearly full shells, so it is easiest to **gain** electrons and become a **negative ion (anion)**.

Sodium (2, 8, 1) gives its one outer electron to chlorine (2, 8, 7), making Na⁺ (2, 8) and Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8). The opposite charges attract strongly: an **ionic bond**. The compound is an **ionic compound**. Watch the electrons move:

### 3.1 Properties of ionic compounds

- **Hard crystals, but brittle**, because a blow slides a layer of ions so like charges face each other and repel.
- **High melting and boiling points** (NaCl melts at 801 °C), because a lot of energy is needed to break the strong attraction between ions.
- **Soluble in water, usually**, but not in kerosene or petrol.
- **Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved**, not as solids: current in an ionic compound needs free-moving ions.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-metal-lattice"><figcaption>Switch the state to see the bulb light only when the ions are free to move. In the solid, push the top layer sideways: like charges meet, repel and the crystal cracks. A metal would just bend.</figcaption></figure>

## 4. Occurrence and extraction of metals

Elements or compounds found naturally in the earth are **minerals**; a mineral from which a metal can be obtained profitably is an **ore**. Ores are mixed with impurities (soil, sand, rock) called **gangue**. Gold and platinum occur free (native). Most metals occur as oxides, sulphides or carbonates, and have to be extracted. Extraction has three stages: **enrichment** of the ore (removing gangue), **extracting the metal**, and **refining** it.

How you extract the metal depends on where it is in the reactivity series:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-metal-extract">
<div class="step">The series splits into three zones: very reactive metals at the top, medium ones in the middle, and unreactive ones at the bottom.</div>
<div class="step">Least reactive (Hg, Cu, Ag, Au): heating the ore, or its sulphide in air, is enough: 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂. The oxide is unstable.</div>
<div class="step">Middle (Zn, Fe, Pb): ores are sulphides or carbonates. Convert to the oxide by roasting or calcination, then reduce with carbon: ZnO + C → Zn + CO.</div>
<div class="step">Most reactive (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al): carbon is not strong enough. Electrolyse the molten compound: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na at the cathode.</div>
<div class="step">The metals so made are impure. Refine them, for example by electrolytic refining of copper.</div>
</div>

**Roasting** is heating a sulphide ore strongly in excess air: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂. **Calcination** is heating a carbonate ore strongly in limited air: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂. Both give the oxide.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-metal-zinc">
<div class="step">Concentrate the ore: ground ore is floated in water with oil, the sulphide floats in the froth and the gangue sinks.</div>
<div class="step">Roast zinc sulphide in air: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂.</div>
<div class="step">Calcine a carbonate ore such as calamine: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂.</div>
<div class="step">Reduce the oxide with coke: ZnO + C → Zn + CO. The oxide loses oxygen (is reduced) and carbon gains it (is oxidised).</div>
<div class="step">For metals like iron, aluminium can do the reducing: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe + heat. This thermite reaction gives molten iron to weld railway tracks and broken machine parts.</div>
</div>

### 4.1 Refining

Metals from reduction are not pure. The most common method is **electrolytic refining**. For copper, the impure metal is the **anode**, a thin strip of pure copper is the **cathode**, and the electrolyte is an acidified copper sulphate solution. At the anode copper dissolves: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻. At the cathode the same amount of copper is deposited: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. Soluble impurities stay in solution and insoluble ones, often with silver and gold, fall as **anode mud**.

## 5. Corrosion

When metals are attacked slowly by air, moisture or chemicals they **corrode**. Iron rusts (reddish-brown), silver tarnishes (black), copper turns green with a layer of basic copper carbonate, and aluminium forms a protective grey oxide. Rust is hydrated iron oxide. It needs both **air and water**, and iron beneath it keeps rusting, so rust can eat a bridge or a ship away. See the experiment in action:

[Rusting lab](/learn/rusting-lab/)

### 5.1 Prevention

- **Painting, oiling or greasing**: keeps air and water from the metal.
- **Galvanisation**: coating iron with a layer of **zinc**. Even if scratched, zinc is more reactive than iron and corrodes first, protecting the iron (sacrificial protection). Used on buckets, pipes, roofs and bridges.
- **Tin plating, chrome plating, anodising and alloying**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-metal-protect"><figcaption>Try each coating, whole or scratched, and slide the days. Paint and tin protect only while whole: tin is less reactive than iron, so it makes rust <i>worse</i> at a scratch. Zinc keeps protecting even when scratched.</figcaption></figure>

### 5.2 Alloys

An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with other metals or non-metals. Alloys are made by melting the metals together and cooling the mixture. Pure metals are often too soft or rust too easily, but alloys are harder, stronger or more resistant:

- **Steel**: iron with a little carbon (0.5 to 1.5%): hard and strong. **Stainless steel**: iron, nickel and chromium, does not rust.
- **Brass**: copper and zinc (about 30%); used for taps and musical instruments. **Bronze**: copper and tin; statues, medals.
- **Solder**: lead and tin; its low melting point suits joining wires. **Gold jewellery**: 24 carat gold is pure, but too soft, so 22 carat contains copper or silver.

An alloy is harder because atoms of different sizes jam the layers so that they cannot slip over each other as easily:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-metal-alloy"><figcaption>Increase the push. In the pure metal the layers slip at about a quarter of the force. In the alloy the odd-sized atoms (orange) lock the layers, and it takes much more force.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="keypoints">

- Metals: lustrous, malleable, ductile, conduct heat and electricity, sonorous. Non-metals: brittle, dull, poor conductors (graphite and diamond are exceptions of a kind).
- Reactivity series: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from solution.
- Metal oxides are basic (Al₂O₃ and ZnO are amphoteric); non-metal oxides are acidic.
- Ionic bond: metal gives electrons, non-metal takes them, oppositely charged ions attract. Ionic compounds are brittle, have high melting points and conduct only when molten or dissolved.
- Extraction depends on reactivity: heat (low), reduce with carbon after roasting or calcining (middle), electrolysis of the molten compound (high). Refine by electrolysis.
- Rusting needs air and water. Prevent it by painting, galvanising (zinc), plating or alloying.
- Alloys are mixtures that are harder, stronger or more resistant than pure metals.

</div>
