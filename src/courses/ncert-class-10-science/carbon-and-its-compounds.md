**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 4 · about 60 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain why carbon forms so many compounds, draw and name simple carbon compounds, predict how they react, and explain how soaps and detergents clean.

<div class="bigidea">Carbon has four electrons in its outer shell and shares them rather than giving or taking. It can share with other carbon atoms, in chains, branches and rings, and with H, O, Cl and N. That one habit makes millions of compounds, from petrol and sugar to plastics, medicines and the soap in your bathroom.</div>

## 1. Bonding in carbon: sharing, not giving

Sodium gives away one electron and chlorine takes it: an ionic bond. Carbon has **4 outer electrons**. To get a full shell of 8 it would have to gain 4 (a −4 ion, very unstable) or lose 4 (a +4 ion, which needs a huge amount of energy). So carbon **shares** electrons with other atoms. A shared pair holds two atoms together: a **covalent bond**.

Think of two students sharing one textbook before the exam. Neither owns it alone, and both are held together by it.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-carb-bond">
<div class="step">Carbon: 4 outer electrons. Too many to lose or gain, so it shares.</div>
<div class="step">Hydrogen, H₂: each H gives one electron, and the pair is shared. Both atoms now count 2 electrons, a full shell. A single bond, H–H.</div>
<div class="step">Oxygen, O₂: each O has 6 and needs 2 more, so two pairs are shared. A double bond, O=O.</div>
<div class="step">Nitrogen, N₂: each N has 5 and needs 3 more, so three pairs are shared. A triple bond, N≡N: very strong, so nitrogen in the air barely reacts.</div>
<div class="step">Methane, CH₄: carbon shares one pair with each of four hydrogens. Carbon counts 8, each H counts 2.</div>
</div>

**Properties of covalent compounds.** The molecules attract each other only weakly, and there are no free ions or electrons. So covalent compounds usually have **low melting and boiling points** (many are gases or liquids) and **do not conduct electricity**. Ionic compounds are the opposite: strong forces between ions, high melting points, and conduction when molten or dissolved.

## 2. The versatile nature of carbon

Two properties make carbon special.

1. **Catenation**: carbon atoms join to other carbon atoms to make long chains, branches and rings. The C–C bond is strong, so these chains are stable. (Silicon tries, but its chains are weak and quickly break in water.)
2. **Tetravalency**: with a valency of 4, one carbon can bond with four other atoms, which gives many ways to build a molecule.

### 2.1 Chains, branches and rings

Compounds of carbon and hydrogen only are **hydrocarbons**. If all the bonds between the carbons are single, the hydrocarbon is **saturated** (an **alkane**). If there is a double bond (**alkene**) or a triple bond (**alkyne**), it is **unsaturated**. Every carbon always ends up with four bonds, and the rest of its bonds go to hydrogen.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-carb-shapes"><figcaption>Pick a structure. Each carbon shows how many hydrogens it carries (4 minus the bonds it already uses). Pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane all have the formula C₅H₁₂ but are three different molecules with different boiling points: <b>isomers</b>. Switch to the ring and the double and triple bonds to see the hydrogens drop.</figcaption></figure>

Compounds with the same molecular formula but a different arrangement of atoms are called **structural isomers**. Butane (C₄H₁₀) has two, pentane (C₅H₁₂) has three, and decane (C₁₀H₂₂) has 75.

<div class="myth">

**“A double bond is two bonds, so carbon has five bonds.”** Carbon always has four bonds in total. A double bond counts as two of the four. That is why C₂H₄ has only 4 hydrogens: each carbon uses 2 bonds on the other carbon and 2 on hydrogens.

</div>

### 2.2 Functional groups and homologous series

A hydrocarbon chain can carry other atoms in place of hydrogen. Such an atom or group, which decides how the compound reacts, is a **functional group**:

| Group | Name | Ending or prefix | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| –Cl, –Br | halo | chloro-, bromo- (prefix) | chloropropane |
| –OH | alcohol | -ol | ethanol |
| –CHO | aldehyde | -al | ethanal |
| >C=O | ketone | -one | propanone |
| –COOH | carboxylic acid | -oic acid | ethanoic acid |

A series of compounds with the same functional group and the same general formula, each member differing from the next by a **–CH₂–** unit (14 u), is a **homologous series**. Methane, ethane, propane, butane... is the series of alkanes. Members of a series have similar chemical properties and steadily changing physical properties.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-carb-homolog"><figcaption>Slide the number of carbons and switch the series. The formula follows CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (alkane), CₙH₂ₙ (alkene) or CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ (alkyne). The boiling point climbs steadily: the first four alkanes are gases, then liquids, and the long ones are waxy solids.</figcaption></figure>

## 3. Naming carbon compounds

The name has three parts: a **root** for the number of carbons, then a **suffix** for single, double or triple bonds, and the group.

1. Count the carbons in the longest chain: 1 meth-, 2 eth-, 3 prop-, 4 but-, 5 pent-, 6 hex-.
2. Add **-ane** for single bonds, **-ene** for a double bond, **-yne** for a triple bond.
3. If there is a functional group: for a **halogen**, put a prefix (chloro-, bromo-) before the name. For others, drop the final **e** of the name and add the ending (-ol, -al, -one, -oic acid). Drop the e only before an ending that starts with a vowel: so propane + ol = propanol, but propane + nitrile would keep the e.

## 4. Chemical properties of carbon compounds

Four types of reaction cover almost everything.

### 4.1 Combustion

Carbon compounds burn in oxygen to give carbon dioxide, water, heat and light:

```math
\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat and light}
```

This is why they are good fuels (LPG, petrol, wood, cooking gas). **Saturated** hydrocarbons burn with a clean blue flame because they burn completely. **Unsaturated** ones burn with a yellow sooty flame because the carbon in them is not completely burnt. A LPG stove with a yellow flame and black soot on the vessel means the air holes are blocked. Coal and petroleum also burn with a sooty flame when the air supply is poor.

### 4.2 Oxidation

Oxidising agents such as **alkaline potassium permanganate** or **acidified potassium dichromate** add oxygen to a compound without burning it. Ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid. The purple colour of the permanganate fades as it gives its oxygen.

### 4.3 Addition

An unsaturated compound adds atoms across its double or triple bond. With hydrogen and a **nickel catalyst**, ethene becomes ethane. Vegetable oils (unsaturated) are turned into vanaspati ghee (saturated fat) this way, which is called hydrogenation.

### 4.4 Substitution

In a saturated hydrocarbon one atom is replaced by another. In sunlight, methane and chlorine swap an H for a Cl:

```math
\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{sunlight}} \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}
```

## 5. Some important carbon compounds: ethanol and ethanoic acid

### 5.1 Ethanol (C₂H₅OH)

A colourless liquid, soluble in water in all proportions, boiling at 78 °C. It is neutral to litmus. It is in alcoholic drinks and is a solvent in medicines, tinctures, cough syrups and perfumes. **Fuel use:** India mixes ethanol from sugarcane into petrol (E20 petrol). Reactions:

- **With sodium:** 2CH₃CH₂OH + 2Na → 2CH₃CH₂ONa + H₂↑ (a slow fizz of hydrogen).
- **Dehydration:** heated at 443 K with excess concentrated H₂SO₄, it loses water and becomes ethene. The acid is a **dehydrating agent**.
- **Oxidation** to ethanoic acid, as above.

**Effects of alcohol.** Ethanol slows the nervous system, so reflexes become slow and judgement poor; this is why drink-driving causes accidents. Over time it harms the liver. **Methanol** is similar but far more toxic: it is oxidised in the liver to methanal, which attaches to the proteins in cells and clots them. Even a little can cause blindness or death. To prevent misuse, **industrial alcohol** is made undrinkable by adding a poison (methanol) and a blue dye. This is **denatured alcohol** or methylated spirit.

### 5.2 Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH)

Commonly called acetic acid. A 5% to 8% solution in water is **vinegar**, used as a preservative in pickles. Pure ethanoic acid freezes at 16.6 °C, so in cold weather it solidifies as glassy crystals, and is called **glacial** acetic acid. It is a **weak acid**: it only partly ionises. Compare an equally concentrated HCl solution, which is almost fully ionised.

- **With a carbonate or hydrogencarbonate:** CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑ (fizzing; the gas turns lime water milky).
- **With a base:** CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O (neutralisation).
- **With an alcohol (esterification):** CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄ as catalyst). The product, an **ester**, has a sweet fruity smell. Esters are used in perfumes and flavourings. Heating an ester with NaOH gives back the alcohol and the sodium salt of the acid (**saponification**, the reaction used to make soap).

## 6. Soaps and detergents

### 6.1 How soap cleans

Soap is the sodium (or potassium) salt of a long-chain carboxylic acid, e.g. sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COONa. It has two ends: an **ionic head** that loves water (hydrophilic) and a long **hydrocarbon tail** that loves oil and grease (hydrophobic, also called lipophilic). In water, the tails bury themselves in the grease and the heads stay outside in the water. Rubbing breaks the grease into tiny droplets, each wrapped in soap: a **micelle**. All the heads are negatively charged, so the micelles repel each other and do not rejoin. They are rinsed away with the water.

<div class="step">Grease on the cloth does not mix with water, so plain water does not clean it.</div>
<div class="step">Soap molecules spread in the water, each with a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail.</div>
<div class="step">The tails pull into the grease; the heads face the water.</div>
<div class="step">Rubbing breaks the grease into small droplets, each surrounded by soap: micelles.</div>
<div class="step">The charged heads make micelles repel one another, so they stay separate and are rinsed away.</div>

### 6.2 Hard water and detergents

Hard water contains calcium and magnesium salts. Their **Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions** react with soap to form an insoluble, sticky **scum** (calcium stearate). The soap is used up making scum before any lather forms, and the scum sticks to clothes and makes them dull. A **detergent** is usually the sodium salt of a sulphonic acid (or an ammonium or chloride salt). Its calcium salts are **soluble**, so no scum forms, and it works well in hard water. Detergents are used in shampoos and washing powders. Some old detergents do not break down by bacteria (non-biodegradable) and pollute rivers; modern ones are biodegradable.

<div class="keypoints">

- Carbon shares electrons (covalent bonds) because it has 4 outer electrons; covalent compounds have low melting points and do not conduct electricity.
- Catenation and tetravalency give chains, branches and rings; same formula, different structure means isomers.
- Alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (saturated); alkenes CₙH₂ₙ and alkynes CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ (unsaturated). Homologous series differ by CH₂ (14 u).
- Names: root + ane/ene/yne; halogen as a prefix; ending -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid after dropping the e.
- Reactions: combustion (clean blue flame for saturated, sooty for unsaturated), oxidation (alcohol to acid), addition (H₂, Ni), substitution (Cl₂, sunlight).
- Ethanol: reacts with Na, dehydrates to ethene, oxidises to ethanoic acid. Ethanoic acid: weak acid, reacts with carbonates, bases and alcohols (ester, sweet smell).
- Soap: ionic head and hydrocarbon tail; micelles trap grease. Hard water makes scum with soap but not with detergent.

</div>
