**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 2 · about 55 minutes.** By the end you will be able to test for acids and bases, write the reactions of acids and bases, use and understand the pH scale, and explain how common salts such as baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder and plaster of Paris are made and used.

<div class="bigidea">An acid is something that gives hydrogen ions (H⁺) in water; a base gives hydroxide ions (OH⁻). When they meet, H⁺ and OH⁻ join to make water, and what is left is a salt. The pH number tells you how many H⁺ ions are around: every step of 1 on the scale is ten times more or fewer.</div>

## 1. Acids and bases: how do we tell?

Lemon juice, vinegar, tamarind and curd taste **sour**: they contain acids. Soap, baking soda and the milk of magnesia taken for acidity feel **slippery** and taste **bitter**: they are bases. Never taste or touch unknown chemicals in the lab. We use **indicators**, substances that change colour in an acid or a base.

- **Litmus** (from a lichen): blue litmus turns **red** in acid; red litmus turns **blue** in base.
- **Phenolphthalein**: colourless in acid, **pink** in base.
- **Methyl orange**: **red** in acid, **yellow** in base.
- **Turmeric** (haldi): yellow in acid and neutral, **red-brown** in base. That is why a haldi stain on a white shirt turns red when soap is rubbed on it, and yellow again when you rinse it with water and a lemon.
- **Olfactory indicators**: onion, vanilla and clove oil lose their smell in a base but keep it in an acid.

## 2. What do acids and bases do?

### 2.1 Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen

Dilute acid poured on zinc fizzes, and the gas can be burnt with a squeaky pop:

```math
\text{Zn} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\uparrow
```

### 2.2 Acid + metal carbonate or hydrogencarbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

```math
\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2
```

The CO₂ turns lime water milky, because it makes insoluble white calcium carbonate. Pass **more** CO₂ and the milkiness disappears (soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate forms). Marble statues and limestone buildings are attacked by acid rain for the same reason.

Step through both tests, as you would in a lab:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-acid-tests">
<div class="step">Put zinc granules in a flask and add dilute HCl. A delivery tube leads to a dish of soap solution.</div>
<div class="step">Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. The gas bubbles up and fills soap bubbles.</div>
<div class="step">Bring a burning splinter near a bubble: it burns with a pop. That is the test for hydrogen.</div>
<div class="step">Now use sodium carbonate and dilute HCl. This time lead the gas through clear lime water.</div>
<div class="step">Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. The lime water turns milky because of insoluble calcium carbonate.</div>
<div class="step">Keep bubbling: CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ → Ca(HCO₃)₂, which dissolves, so the milky colour clears.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Lime water tests for hydrogen.”** No: lime water tests for <b>carbon dioxide</b> only. Hydrogen gas is tested by the pop test with a flame. Do not mix the two up.

</div>

### 2.3 Acid + base → salt + water (neutralisation)

```math
\text{NaOH} + \text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
```

Acid and base cancel each other's properties. A **base** that dissolves in water is an **alkali** (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂). Many metal oxides are basic: CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O (the black powder dissolves into a blue-green solution). Non-metal oxides are acidic: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O, the same reaction as the milky lime water.

### 2.4 What do all acids have in common? Ions!

Acids contain hydrogen, but so do glucose and alcohol, and they are not acids. The difference: an acid gives **hydrogen ions (H⁺)** when dissolved in **water**. A base gives **hydroxide ions (OH⁻)**. Dry HCl gas does not turn dry blue litmus red; once it meets water, H⁺ is released and the paper reddens. Pick a substance below to see what happens at the particle level. A strong acid splits completely; a weak acid like vinegar's ethanoic acid only a little:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-acid-ions"><figcaption>The bulb lights as the ions carry current: brighter for more ions. HCl splits fully, ethanoic acid hardly at all, and glucose does not split at all. That is the difference between a strong acid, a weak acid and a non-acid.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="keypoints">

- Acids: sour; blue litmus → red; give H⁺ in water. Bases: bitter, slippery; red litmus → blue; give OH⁻ in water.
- Acid + metal → salt + H₂; acid + carbonate → salt + H₂O + CO₂; acid + base → salt + H₂O.

</div>

## 3. Neutralisation in action

The reaction is really just H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Watch it happen drop by drop. Add NaOH to a flask of acid: the pH hardly moves at first, because there is so much acid to use up, then it shoots up across 7 as the last of the acid goes. That sudden jump is the **end point** that chemists look for.

## 4. How strong is an acid or base? The pH scale

The **pH** scale runs from 0 to 14. The “p” stands for the German word for power, and pH is defined by pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the concentration of hydrogen ions in mol/L. You do not need to calculate with logs at this level; just remember:

- pH below 7: **acidic** (the lower, the stronger)
- pH = 7: **neutral** (pure water)
- pH above 7: **basic** (the higher, the stronger)

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>[H⁺]</dt><dd>concentration of hydrogen ions: moles of H⁺ in a litre of solution</dd>
<dt>pH</dt><dd>−log₁₀[H⁺]; a small number for a crowded, strong acid and a large number for a base</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Each step of 1 in pH is a factor of 10 in H⁺. pH 3 has 10 times the H⁺ of pH 4 and 100 times that of pH 5. The scale squeezes a huge range of concentrations into 0 to 14.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

**Universal indicator** is a mixture of indicators that gives a different colour at every pH: red for strong acid, orange, yellow, **green for neutral**, blue and violet for strong base. pH paper uses the same idea.

### 4.1 Dilution

To dilute an acid or a base, add water. Adding water does **not** remove the ions; the same H⁺ ions are spread through more water. The concentration falls, so the pH moves toward 7, but never beyond. Always add the acid to water, slowly, stirring, and never water to concentrated acid. Mixing is highly **exothermic**, and the splash from the hot, spitting liquid can burn you.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-acid-dilution"><figcaption>Slide the dilution. The same 30 ions sit in a bigger and bigger beaker. For an acid starting at pH 1, a 10× dilution gives pH 2, 100× gives 3, and so on, but the pH gets stuck just below 7 because water itself has a little H⁺.</figcaption></figure>

### 4.2 pH in everyday life

- **Our body:** our body works in a narrow pH range, about 7.0 to 7.8 (blood is 7.4). Plants and animals are sensitive to pH. Acid rain has pH below 5.6 and can kill fish in lakes and rivers.
- **Stomach:** it makes hydrochloric acid (pH about 1.5) to help digest food. Too much causes indigestion; an **antacid** (a mild base such as milk of magnesia) neutralises the extra.
- **Teeth:** bacteria in the mouth break down sugars and make acid. When the pH drops below about 5.5, tooth enamel (calcium phosphate, the hardest thing in the body) begins to dissolve. Toothpastes are basic and neutralise the acid. Brush after meals.
- **Soil:** crops have a preferred pH; farmers add lime (a base) to acidic soil.
- **Insect stings:** a bee sting contains methanoic (formic) acid; rub on baking soda, a mild base. Nettle leaves also have it, and dock leaves, found growing beside nettles, are traditionally rubbed on the sting.

## 5. More about salts

When an acid and a base neutralise, the product other than water is a **salt**. Common salt, NaCl, is just one of many. Salts with the same positive or negative ion belong to a **family**: NaCl, KCl and Na₂SO₄ all have the Na⁺ or Cl⁻ ion in the family of sodium or chloride salts.

### 5.1 The pH of salts

- Strong acid + strong base (HCl + NaOH): neutral salt, pH about 7.
- Strong acid + weak base (HCl + NH₄OH): **acidic** salt, pH below 7.
- Weak acid + strong base (CH₃COOH + NaOH): **basic** salt, pH above 7.

### 5.2 Common salt and its family: the chlor-alkali process

Sea water and underground deposits are the source of **common salt** in India, from the salt pans of Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. It is not only for cooking. When electricity is passed through a **brine** (concentrated salt solution), it splits:

```math
2\text{NaCl(aq)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}
```

The name “chlor-alkali” comes from chlorine and alkali (NaOH). **Chlorine** forms at the anode, **hydrogen** at the cathode, and **sodium hydroxide** stays in the solution. Each is valuable: H₂ for fuel and margarine, Cl₂ for water treatment and PVC, NaOH for soap, paper and artificial fibres.

<!--fig:tool chlor-alkali-->

Follow the whole family from brine:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-acid-family">
<div class="step">Start with brine, a solution of ordinary salt.</div>
<div class="step">Electricity splits it into NaOH, Cl₂ and H₂ (chlor-alkali).</div>
<div class="step">Cl₂ + dry slaked lime Ca(OH)₂ → CaOCl₂, bleaching powder. It bleaches cotton and linen, disinfects drinking water, and is an oxidising agent in industry.</div>
<div class="step">Brine, ammonia and CO₂ give baking soda, NaHCO₃: a mild base used in antacids and in cooking.</div>
<div class="step">Heating baking soda gives soda ash, Na₂CO₃; recrystallised with water it gives washing soda, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, used to soften hard water.</div>
<div class="step">One raw material gives NaOH, Cl₂, H₂, bleaching powder, baking soda and washing soda.</div>
</div>

**Baking soda** (NaHCO₃) has a double life. It neutralises acidity in antacids. In baking powder (baking soda plus a mild solid acid like tartaric acid) it gives bubbles of CO₂ when mixed with water, which make idli, dhokla and cakes soft and fluffy. When heated alone: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.

**Washing soda** (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) is used in glass, soap and paper industries and to remove permanent hardness of water.

### 5.3 Water of crystallisation and plaster of Paris

Some salts hold water inside their crystals, called **water of crystallisation**. Blue copper sulphate crystals are CuSO₄·5H₂O: five water molecules per formula unit. Heat them and the water is driven off, leaving white powder; add water and the blue returns. Washing soda, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, and gypsum, CaSO₄·2H₂O, are other examples.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c10s-acid-crystal"></div>

**Plaster of Paris** (POP) is made by heating gypsum carefully at 373 K (100 °C). It is a fine white powder that, when mixed with water, sets into a hard mass of gypsum again. It is used by doctors for plaster casts on broken bones, for toys and statues, for the false ceilings of homes, and to make surfaces smooth.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-acid-pop">
<div class="step">Gypsum, CaSO₄·2H₂O: two water molecules for each CaSO₄.</div>
<div class="step">Heat gently at 373 K (100 °C): three-quarters of the water leaves as steam.</div>
<div class="step">What is left is CaSO₄·½H₂O: plaster of Paris. Keep it dry.</div>
<div class="step">Add water and stir: a smooth paste you can pour in a mould.</div>
<div class="step">It takes the water back and sets into hard gypsum.</div>
<div class="step">Do not overheat: above about 200 °C all the water goes and the powder will not set.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Salts are all neutral.”** Only salts of a strong acid with a strong base are neutral. Sodium carbonate solution is basic (it feels soapy), and ammonium chloride solution is acidic.

</div>

<li><b>1810</b> Humphry Davy shows that hydrochloric acid has no oxygen, only hydrogen and chlorine, so acids are not “oxygen compounds”.</li>
<li><b>1884</b> Svante Arrhenius proposes that acids give H⁺ ions and bases OH⁻ ions in water.</li>

<div class="keypoints">

- Indicators (litmus, phenolphthalein, methyl orange, turmeric) show acid or base by colour.
- Acid + metal → salt + H₂ (pop test). Acid + carbonate → salt + H₂O + CO₂ (lime water milky). Acid + base → salt + H₂O (neutralisation).
- Acids give H⁺ and bases give OH⁻ in water; strong acids split fully, weak ones partly.
- pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 basic; each unit is a factor of 10. Dilution moves pH toward 7, never past it.
- pH matters in the stomach, teeth (enamel dissolves below 5.5), soil and rain. Add acid to water, not water to acid.
- Salt pH: strong + strong gives neutral; strong acid + weak base gives acidic; weak acid + strong base gives basic.
- Brine gives NaOH, Cl₂ and H₂ (chlor-alkali). Bleaching powder: Cl₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaOCl₂. Baking soda NaHCO₃; washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O; gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O → POP CaSO₄·½H₂O.

</div>
