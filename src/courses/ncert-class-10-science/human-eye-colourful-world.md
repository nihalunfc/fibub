**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 10 · about 55 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how the eye makes an image and focuses on near and far objects, work out the lens that corrects short and long sight, and explain the colours of the rainbow, the twinkling of stars, early sunrise, the blue sky and red sunsets.

<div class="bigidea">The eye is a living camera with a lens that changes shape, and the sky is a giant glass prism and screen. Every colourful sight in this chapter comes from the same two ideas from the last chapter: light bends when it changes medium (refraction, dispersion) and small particles throw light sideways (scattering).</div>

## 1. The human eye

The eye is a nearly round ball about 2.3 cm across. It works like a camera: a lens in the front throws a **real, inverted image** on a light-sensitive screen at the back.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-anatomy"><figcaption>Pick each part to light it up and read its job. Move the brightness slider to see the iris make the pupil smaller in bright light and wider in dim light. The orange dots are light travelling in through the cornea, the pupil and the lens to the retina.</figcaption></figure>

- **Cornea:** the clear front surface. Light is refracted most here (about two thirds of the bending).
- **Iris and pupil:** the iris is a ring of muscle; the pupil is the hole in its middle. Going from a bright street into a dark cinema hall, the pupil widens so more light gets in.
- **Eye lens:** a flexible convex lens. It fine-tunes the focus so the image is sharp on the retina.
- **Ciliary muscles:** hold the lens and change its curvature.
- **Retina:** the screen. It has about 120 million **rod cells** (sensitive to dim light, no colour) and about 6 million **cone cells** (bright light and colour). They send electrical signals along the **optic nerve** to the brain.
- **Aqueous and vitreous humour:** clear fluids that give the eye its shape and let light through.

Walk through what happens to the light from an object, one step at a time:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-eye-image">
<div class="step">Light spreads out from every point of an object, and some of it enters your eye.</div>
<div class="step">The cornea bends the rays strongly towards each other. Most of the focusing power of the eye is here.</div>
<div class="step">The pupil lets in a controlled amount of light. The lens refines the focus, a little more for near objects, a little less for far ones.</div>
<div class="step">The rays meet again on the retina and form a real, inverted, smaller image of the object.</div>
<div class="step">The retina turns the picture into signals. The optic nerve carries them to the brain, which turns the image the right way up. We never notice that the image on the retina is upside down.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“The lens does most of the focusing.”** The cornea does most of the bending. The lens is what lets the eye *change* its focus, which is why people who have lens surgery can still see a world in focus once a lens replacement is fitted.

</div>

## 2. Power of accommodation

The retina is always the same distance behind the lens (about 2.4 cm in this model). To make a sharp image on it, the **focal length of the lens must change** with the distance of the object. This ability is the **power of accommodation**.

- Looking at a **distant** object: the ciliary muscles **relax**, the lens becomes **thinner**, its focal length is **longer**.
- Looking at a **near** object: the muscles **contract**, the lens becomes **thicker**, its focal length is **shorter**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-accommodation"><figcaption>Slide the object closer. The lens thickens and the power needed rises from about 41.7 D (far) towards 45.7 D (25 cm). Closer than about 25 cm the lens cannot thicken any more: the picture goes behind the retina and the image is blurred.</figcaption></figure>

There is a limit. The **near point**, the closest distance at which the eye sees clearly without strain, is about **25 cm** for a young adult (the least distance of distinct vision). The **far point** of a normal eye is **infinity**. Normal vision therefore covers 25 cm to infinity.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c10s-eye-accom"></div>

Two small facts: you can see the same thing with **both eyes** for a wider field of view (about 150° with one eye, about 180° with two) and for a sense of depth. And your eye holds an image for about 1/16 of a second (persistence of vision), which is why a film made of still pictures looks like movement.

## 3. Defects of vision and their correction

When the eye cannot adjust properly, the image does not fall on the retina and the world looks blurred. There are three common defects.

**Myopia (near-sightedness or short-sight).** You see near objects clearly but distant ones look blurred. The image of a far object forms **in front of the retina**. The cause: the eyeball is too long, or the lens is too curved (focal length too short). The far point is nearer than infinity. It is corrected by a **concave lens** of suitable power, which spreads the rays a little before they enter the eye.

**Hypermetropia (far-sightedness or long-sight).** You see distant objects well but nearby ones are blurred. The image of a near object forms **behind the retina**. The cause: the eyeball is too short, or the lens is too flat. The near point is farther than 25 cm. It is corrected by a **convex lens**, which converges the rays a little before they enter the eye.

**Presbyopia.** With age the ciliary muscles weaken and the lens stiffens, so the power of accommodation falls. Both the near point and the far point may be affected. It is corrected with **bifocal lenses**: concave on top for distant vision, convex below for reading.

Try each case. Pick an eye, choose what it is looking at, then switch the spectacles on. (For hypermetropia and presbyopia, choose “A book at 25 cm” to see the defect.)

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-defects"><figcaption>The eye is modelled as one lens at a fixed distance from the retina. The orange spot on the retina is blur: the focus is in front of it (myopia, far object) or behind it (hypermetropia and presbyopia, near object). The right spectacle lens, added to the eye’s own power, brings the focus back to the retina. Thin lenses in contact add their powers.</figcaption></figure>

To find the lens, ask: “where does the image have to be?” The lens must make the object look as if it is at a distance the eye can handle.

```math
\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} \qquad P = \frac{1}{f}\ (\text{metres})
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>Myopia</dt><dd>u = −∞ (a far object); v = −(far point). So f = −(far point), a concave lens</dd>
<dt>Hypermetropia</dt><dd>u = −25 cm (the book); v = −(near point). The image is virtual, at the near point, so f is positive: a convex lens</dd>
<dt>P</dt><dd>power in dioptres; negative for concave and positive for convex lenses</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>The spectacle lens does not cure the eye: it moves the apparent position of the object to a place where the eye can already focus on it.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

**Worked example.** A girl can see clearly only up to 80 cm. For distant objects, u = −∞ and v = −80 cm, so f = −80 cm = −0.8 m and P = 1/(−0.8) = **−1.25 D**, a concave lens.

Most modern spectacle lenses are made of glass or plastic. Some people also use **contact lenses**, and in some cases surgery on the cornea (laser surgery) is used to correct the focus. Regular eye check-ups, good light for reading, and **eye donation** (the cornea of a person who has died can restore the sight of someone with a damaged cornea) are all part of looking after sight.

## 4. Refraction of light through a prism

You met refraction through a glass slab in the last chapter. A **triangular glass prism** has two slanting faces that are not parallel, so the ray does not come out parallel to the way it went in: it is **deviated** towards the thick base of the prism.

- The angle between the two refracting faces is the **angle of the prism (A)**.
- The ray bends **towards the normal** when it enters the glass (first face) and **away from the normal** when it leaves (second face). Because the faces slope towards each other, both bends turn the ray the same way.
- The angle between the incident ray extended forward and the emergent ray is the **angle of deviation (δ)**:

```math
\delta = i + e - A
```

where i is the angle of incidence and e the angle of emergence.

## 5. Dispersion of white light by a glass prism

When a narrow beam of sunlight goes through a prism, it comes out as a band of colours on a screen: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red (**VIBGYOR**). The band is called the **spectrum**, and the splitting of white light into its colours is **dispersion**.

Why does it happen? Each colour travels at a slightly different speed in glass, so each has a slightly different refractive index. **Violet is slowed the most**, so it bends the most; **red is slowed the least**, so it bends the least. (In vacuum all colours travel at the same speed, so there is no dispersion without a medium.)

### Newton’s experiments

Isaac Newton used a prism to split sunlight in 1666. Did the prism *add* the colours? He put a second, identical prism **upside down** in the path of the spectrum. The colours came together again as white light. So white light is a mixture of the colours, and the prism only separates them.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-newton"><figcaption>First, “Two prisms”: the inverted second prism bends each colour back by the same amount that the first prism spread it, so they overlap again as white light. Then “Newton’s disc”: a card with the seven colours looks white when it spins fast, because the eye cannot separate colours that arrive faster than about 16 per second.</figcaption></figure>

### The rainbow

A rainbow is dispersion by **millions of tiny raindrops**, each acting like a small prism. It appears when the **Sun is behind you** and it is raining in front of you, often after a shower with the Sun out. Follow the light through one drop:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-eye-raindrop">
<div class="step">Sunlight meets a spherical raindrop. The path of the ray is traced for one particular height; this is the one that sends the most light back towards the observer.</div>
<div class="step">The light is refracted as it enters the drop, and splits into colours: violet bends more than red.</div>
<div class="step">It reaches the back of the drop and is reflected inside it (internal reflection), travelling back towards the front.</div>
<div class="step">It is refracted again as it leaves the drop, spreading the colours further. Red leaves at about 42° from the line back to the Sun, violet at about 40°.</div>
<div class="step">You get only one colour from each drop. Drops higher in the sky send you red, drops lower send you violet; all the drops at the same angle form an arc. The result is a bow with red on the outside and violet on the inside.</div>
</div>

Where and when can you see one? The bow is part of a circle around the point exactly opposite the Sun (where the shadow of your head falls). Move the Sun and see.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-rainbow"><figcaption>The top of the bow is at 42° minus the Sun’s height. In the early morning and late afternoon the Sun is low and you see a big arch. At midday in the tropics the Sun is too high for any rainbow. Switch on the secondary bow: light reflected twice inside the drops gives a fainter outer bow with the colours reversed.</figcaption></figure>

You can make your own rainbow on a sunny day by standing with your back to the Sun and spraying a fine mist from a hose or a garden sprinkler.

## 6. Atmospheric refraction

The atmosphere is not a uniform glass slab: its density decreases with height, so its refractive index changes smoothly. A ray of light from space therefore bends **a little at every layer**, always towards the normal (downwards), as it enters denser and denser air. This is **atmospheric refraction**, and it has surprising effects.

**Twinkling of stars.** Starlight reaches us after crossing air that is never still: warm and cool pockets keep drifting and mixing, so the refractive index at each height keeps changing a little. The path of the light bends a bit differently from moment to moment. So the star seems to move slightly, and the amount of light reaching the eye changes: the star **twinkles**.

**Why planets do not twinkle.** A star is so far away that it is a **point source** of light: all its light follows one wobbling path. A planet is much closer, and is like a tiny **disc**, that is, a collection of many point sources. Their paths wobble in different ways, so the light that goes down at one moment is made up by the light that goes up from another. On the whole the brightness stays steady.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-twinkle"><figcaption>One wobbling path for a star: its apparent position and brightness jump about. For a planet, four paths (standing for the many points of its disc) wobble independently and the average stays still. Turn the turbulence down to calm air and even a star stops twinkling, as it does for astronauts above the atmosphere.</figcaption></figure>

**Advance sunrise and delayed sunset.** Light from the Sun when it is just below the horizon bends down along its path through the air and reaches you, so you see the Sun when it is still about 0.57° below the horizon. The Sun moves 15° per hour, so this lifts the sunrise (and delays the sunset) by about **2 minutes**. The Sun’s disc also looks flattened near the horizon, because the lower edge is lifted more than the upper edge.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-sunrise"><figcaption>Slide the time. With air, you can see the Sun even when its true position is below the horizon (the dashed circle). Switch the air off and the Sun appears only when it has really risen.</figcaption></figure>

## 7. Scattering of light

When light meets particles of dust, smoke, or even air molecules, some of it is thrown off in all directions. This is **scattering of light**. How much each colour is scattered depends on the size of the particles compared with the wavelength of the light.

- **Very small particles** (air molecules, much smaller than the wavelength): scatter **blue light much more than red**. The strength goes as 1/λ⁴, so blue light (about 450 nm) is scattered about four times more than red light (about 650 nm).
- **Larger particles** (dust, water droplets in mist and cloud): scatter **all colours roughly equally**, so the scattered light is **white**.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c10s-eye-scatter"></div>

### The Tyndall effect

When a beam of light passes through a colloid, such as a sunbeam through mist, smoke or through a dusty room, or the light from a torch in water with a few drops of milk, the beam becomes **visible from the side**. This is the **Tyndall effect**: the particles scatter some light towards your eye.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-tyndall"><figcaption>White light is a mix of blue, green and red photons (dots). Slide the particles from tiny to large. For tiny particles the scattered light is blue and the beam that gets through becomes reddish (see the swatch at the right). For large particles all colours scatter alike and the sideways light is white.</figcaption></figure>

A forest on a misty morning shows it nicely: sunlight coming through the gaps between the trees makes bright visible rays because mist droplets scatter the light.

### Why is the sky blue?

Sunlight is white, a mixture of all colours. As it enters the atmosphere, the air molecules scatter the short wavelengths (blue) far more than the long ones (red). The scattered blue light reaches your eyes from every part of the sky, so the sky looks blue. **If the Earth had no atmosphere, the sky would be dark**, as it is for an astronaut or on the Moon, and the Sun would shine out of black space.

### Why does the Sun look red at sunrise and sunset?

At noon the Sun is overhead and its light goes through the thinnest layer of air. At sunrise and sunset it is near the horizon, and the light travels through **a much thicker layer of air** (many times the thickness) to reach you. On the way, most of the blue and green is scattered out of the beam, and what reaches your eyes is mostly orange and red.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-eye-sky"><figcaption>Slide the Sun down from noon to the horizon. The beam’s path through the air gets longer; the beam changes colour as blue is scattered sideways (blue arrows) and removed. The numbers show how much of each colour is left. The colour chip shows what the Sun looks like.</figcaption></figure>

**Other examples.** **Clouds look white** because the water droplets are large and scatter all colours equally. **Danger signals and traffic lights use red** because red is the least scattered colour and can be seen from far away through fog and smoke. **The sea looks blue** partly because it reflects the sky and partly because water absorbs red light and scatters blue. **The Sun looks orange after a dust storm or a forest fire**, because the many small particles scatter the blue away.

<div class="keypoints">

- The eye: cornea (most of the bending), iris and pupil (amount of light), lens (fine focus), ciliary muscles (change the lens), retina (rods and cones), optic nerve. The image on the retina is real and inverted.
- Accommodation: the lens gets thicker (shorter f) for near objects and thinner for far ones. Normal near point 25 cm, far point infinity.
- Myopia: far objects blur, image in front of the retina; concave lens (P negative). Hypermetropia: near objects blur, image behind the retina; convex lens (P positive). Presbyopia: weak accommodation with age; bifocal lenses.
- Prism: deviation δ = i + e − A. Dispersion: violet bends most, red least; VIBGYOR. A second inverted prism recombines the colours into white light.
- Rainbow: dispersion plus internal reflection in raindrops; the Sun is behind the observer; red outside, violet inside.
- Atmospheric refraction: stars twinkle (point source, changing paths), planets do not (extended source); the Sun is seen about 2 minutes before it rises and after it sets.
- Scattering: blue is scattered most by small particles (∝ 1/λ⁴), making the sky blue and the low Sun red; the Tyndall effect makes a beam visible; large particles scatter all colours, so clouds are white.
