**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 1 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you will be able to write and balance a chemical equation, name the five kinds of reaction, spot oxidation and reduction, and explain why iron rusts and food turns rancid.

<div class="bigidea">In a chemical reaction the atoms stay exactly the same; only their partners change. Old bonds break, new bonds form, and a new substance appears. A balanced equation is simply a bookkeeping record that no atom was lost or made on the way.</div>

## 1. What is a chemical reaction?

When milk turns into curd, when iron gets a coat of rust, when a Diwali sparkler burns, new substances with new properties are made. These are **chemical changes**. Melting ice or cutting a mango does not make a new substance: those are physical changes.

You can usually tell a reaction has happened by one or more of these signs:

- a **change in state** (a solid turns into a gas),
- a **change in colour**,
- **evolution of a gas** (bubbles),
- a **change in temperature** (the beaker gets hot or cold),
- formation of a **precipitate** (an insoluble solid that appears in a clear liquid),
- **light**, as when magnesium ribbon burns.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-react-signs"><figcaption>Switch between the four signs. Each one on its own is only a clue; a gas or a colour can also come from a physical change. The sure test is whether a <b>new substance</b> has been made.</figcaption></figure>

Safety note from the lab: sand the magnesium ribbon first to remove its dull oxide layer, hold it with tongs, and never look straight at the flame.

## 2. Writing a chemical equation

Chemists write what happens in a short form. First a **word equation**, with an arrow meaning “gives” or “forms”:

> magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

The left side holds the **reactants** (what you start with) and the right side the **products** (what you get). Now replace the names with formulas. This is the **skeleton equation**:

```math
\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{MgO}
```

Notice that oxygen is written O₂, not O: gases such as hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine exist as molecules of two atoms. Count the atoms: 2 oxygen atoms on the left, only 1 on the right. Atoms cannot disappear, so this is **not balanced**.

### Why balance? The law of conservation of mass

Lavoisier showed (in the 1700s, by weighing everything in sealed vessels) that the total mass of the products is the same as the total mass of the reactants. In a reaction **atoms are neither created nor destroyed**, so each element must have the same number of atoms on both sides.

### Balancing step by step

Here is the method for hydrogen burning in oxygen to give water. Step through it and watch the atom counts.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-react-balance">
<div class="step">Write the skeleton using the correct formulas: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. Count atoms on each side. Oxygen: 2 on the left, 1 on the right.</div>
<div class="step">To get 2 oxygen on the right, put a <b>2 in front of H₂O</b>. Now oxygen is 2 = 2, but hydrogen on the right has jumped to 4.</div>
<div class="step">To get 4 hydrogen on the left, put a <b>2 in front of H₂</b>. Now H is 4 = 4 and O is 2 = 2.</div>
<div class="step">Count one more time and check the masses: 2 × 2 + 32 = 36 u on the left and 2 × 18 = 36 u on the right. The equation is balanced.</div>
<div class="step">The golden rule: change only the coefficients (the big numbers in front). Writing H₂O₂ to fix the oxygen would give hydrogen peroxide, a different substance, and a false equation.</div>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“I can balance by changing the little numbers.”** The subscripts belong to the substance: H₂O is water, H₂O₂ is a bleach-like liquid. Only the **coefficients** in front say how many molecules there are, so only those may change.

</div>

A harder one, step by step: iron heated in steam makes iron oxide and hydrogen. Start with the formula that has the most atoms, fix one element at a time, and recount after every change.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-react-balance2">
<div class="step">Skeleton: Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂. Fe is 1 against 3 and O is 1 against 4. Begin with Fe₃O₄, the biggest formula.</div>
<div class="step">Make Fe equal: write 3 Fe on the left.</div>
<div class="step">Make O equal: Fe₃O₄ has 4 oxygen, so write 4 H₂O. This puts 8 hydrogen on the left.</div>
<div class="step">Make H equal: write 4 H₂ on the right. Now Fe 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 4 = 4.</div>
<div class="step">Every atom matches, and we never changed a subscript. The same atoms are on both sides, so mass is conserved.</div>
</div>

You can also add **state symbols**: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. Conditions such as heat, light, a catalyst or pressure are written above or below the arrow. So the full equation is 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g).

## 3. Types of chemical reactions

Thousands of reactions fall into just a few patterns. Letters stand for atoms or groups. Watch the pieces move and the bonds break and form:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-react-types"><figcaption>Choose a type and drag the progress bar, or press play. In <b>combination</b> two things join into one. In <b>decomposition</b> one splits into two. In <b>displacement</b> A pushes B out and takes its partner C. In <b>double displacement</b> two compounds swap partners.</figcaption></figure>

### 3.1 Combination reactions

Two or more substances join to form a **single product**.

- Burning coal: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)
- Making water: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
- Slaking quicklime: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heat

The last one is how whitewash is made: quicklime added to water hisses and heats the beaker so much that it is too hot to touch. Calcium hydroxide, the product, is the whitewash used on walls. After a few days it reacts with CO₂ in the air to form a thin shiny layer of calcium carbonate: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. That thin marble-like layer is what gives freshly whitewashed walls their shine.

### Exothermic and endothermic reactions

Reactions that **give out heat** are **exothermic**: burning of natural gas, respiration (your cells burn glucose to give you energy), and the rotting of vegetable matter into compost, which is why a compost heap is warm. Reactions that **take in heat** are **endothermic**: heating limestone, and photosynthesis (which takes in light energy). Move the slider to see the energy picture and the thermometer.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-react-energy"><figcaption>In an exothermic reaction the products have <i>less</i> energy than the reactants, and the extra leaves as heat, so the surroundings warm up. In an endothermic one the products have more, so energy is taken in and the surroundings cool.</figcaption></figure>

### 3.2 Decomposition reactions

One substance breaks down into two or more simpler substances: the opposite of combination. It needs energy, which can be **heat, light or electricity**.

- **Heat (thermal decomposition):** CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). This makes quicklime for cement.
- **Heat:** green crystals of iron(II) sulphate lose water, then break into brown Fe₂O₃ and the gases SO₂ and SO₃, which smell like burning sulphur: 2FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g).
- **Light (photolysis):** 2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g). White silver chloride turns grey in sunlight. Old black-and-white photographic film worked this way.
- **Electricity (electrolysis):** 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-react-decompose"><figcaption>Choose the energy source. With electricity, hydrogen collects at the negative electrode and oxygen at the positive; the hydrogen volume is always <b>twice</b> the oxygen volume, because water has two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen.</figcaption></figure>

Decomposition by heat is endothermic. Even so, these reactions are very useful: they are how we get lime, metals from their ores and gases from compounds.

### 3.3 Displacement reactions

A more reactive element **displaces** a less reactive one from its compound. Put an iron nail in blue copper sulphate solution and in about half an hour the nail is coated brown and the blue colour fades to pale green:

```math
\text{Fe(s)} + \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{Cu(s)}
```

Iron is more reactive than copper, so it takes the sulphate and pushes copper out. Other examples: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu, and Pb + CuCl₂ → PbCl₂ + Cu. Try both directions, and watch the ions swap:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-react-displace"><figcaption>Choose iron in copper sulphate, then copper in iron sulphate. Only one of these happens. Copper is <i>less</i> reactive than iron, so it cannot push iron out.</figcaption></figure>

### 3.4 Double displacement reactions

Here two compounds **swap their ions**. When you mix sodium sulphate and barium chloride solutions, a thick white solid appears:

```math
\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{BaCl}_2\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{BaSO}_4\text{(s)}\!\downarrow + 2\text{NaCl(aq)}
```

An insoluble solid that settles out is a **precipitate**, so this is also called a **precipitation reaction**. Mixing lead nitrate with potassium iodide gives a beautiful yellow precipitate of lead iodide (the “golden rain” demo). A neutralisation, where an acid meets a base, is also a double displacement: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O. You will meet it in the next chapter.

## 4. Oxidation and reduction

Heat a copper powder in air. The shiny brown powder turns black: copper has combined with oxygen to make copper(II) oxide, 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO. Copper has been **oxidised** because it **gained oxygen**.

Now pass hydrogen over the hot black powder: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. The black powder turns brown again. CuO has lost oxygen: it has been **reduced**. The hydrogen has gained oxygen, so it has been **oxidised**.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-react-redox"><figcaption>Switch between the two reactions. In both, watch the red O atoms. The atom that <b>gains</b> an oxygen is oxidised; the one that <b>loses</b> an oxygen is reduced. In the second reaction the oxygen moves from copper to hydrogen.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>Oxidation</dt><dd>gain of oxygen (or loss of hydrogen) by a substance</dd>
<dt>Reduction</dt><dd>loss of oxygen (or gain of hydrogen) by a substance</dd>
<dt>Oxidising agent</dt><dd>the substance that gives oxygen to another (here CuO, which gets reduced)</dd>
<dt>Reducing agent</dt><dd>the substance that takes oxygen away (here H₂, which gets oxidised)</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Oxygen is simply being handed from one substance to another. One substance cannot lose oxygen unless another gains it, so oxidation and reduction always happen together: a <b>redox</b> reaction.</dd>
</dl>
</div>

<div class="myth">

**“Oxidation and reduction are separate reactions.”** They are two halves of one reaction. Whenever something is oxidised, something else is reduced at the same time.

</div>

## 5. Oxidation in everyday life

### 5.1 Corrosion

When a metal is attacked slowly by air, moisture or acids in its surroundings, it is **corroded**. The most familiar example is the **rusting of iron**: a reddish-brown flaky coat that forms on a bicycle left in the monsoon. The black coating on silver and the green coating on copper vessels are also corrosion. Corrosion costs a country crores of rupees every year in bridges, railings, ships and pipes.

Iron rusts only when it meets **both oxygen and water**. A nail in dry air stays clean, and so does one in boiled water with oil on top (no air). Salt, as in sea water, speeds it up, so coastal bridges and ships rust fast. Ways to prevent it: **paint**, **oil or grease**, a **zinc coat (galvanising)**, or making **alloys** such as stainless steel. You will learn why zinc is so good in the chapter on metals.

### 5.2 Rancidity

When food containing fats and oils (ghee, chips, oily sweets) is kept for a long time, it is slowly oxidised, giving it a bad smell and taste. This is **rancidity**. Ways to slow it: add **antioxidants**, store in **airtight containers**, keep **in the fridge**, and flush the pack with **nitrogen**, as potato-chip manufacturers do.

<div class="keypoints">

- A chemical reaction makes new substances; reactants are on the left, products on the right of the arrow.
- Atoms are never created or destroyed, so a balanced equation has the same number of each atom on both sides; change coefficients, never subscripts.
- State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq). Conditions go above the arrow.
- Five patterns: combination, decomposition (heat, light, electricity), displacement (more reactive pushes out less reactive), double displacement (partners swap, often a precipitate), and redox.
- Exothermic reactions give out heat (burning, respiration, quicklime + water); endothermic ones take it in (heating limestone, photosynthesis).
- Oxidation is gain of oxygen (or loss of hydrogen); reduction is loss of oxygen. They always occur together.
- Rusting needs oxygen and water; rancidity is oxidation of fats and oils. Paint, galvanising, alloys, airtight packs, antioxidants and nitrogen slow them down.

</div>
