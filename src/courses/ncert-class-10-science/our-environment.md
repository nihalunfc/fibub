**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 13 · about 50 minutes.** By the end you will be able to name the parts of an ecosystem, draw food chains and webs, use the 10 per cent law, explain biological magnification, describe how the ozone layer is damaged, and say how we can manage our garbage.

<div class="bigidea">Every living thing is part of an ecosystem. Energy enters from the Sun, flows one way along food chains and is mostly lost as heat at each step (only about 10 per cent is passed on), while materials are recycled by decomposers. What we release into the environment, such as pesticides, CFCs or plastic, can travel through the system and harm it, and so we must manage our waste with care.</div>

## 1. What is an ecosystem?

An **ecosystem** is all the living things of a place (plants, animals, microbes) together with the non-living things (soil, water, air, sunlight), and the way they interact. A pond, a forest, a paddy field, a garden and an aquarium are ecosystems. Forests, ponds and lakes are **natural** ecosystems; gardens and crop fields, made by people, are **artificial**.

### 1.1 Components of an ecosystem

- **Biotic (living) components**: **producers** (green plants and algae that make food by photosynthesis), **consumers** (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores and parasites that eat other organisms), and **decomposers** (bacteria and fungi that break down dead matter).
- **Abiotic (non-living) components**: sunlight, temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and water.

## 2. Food chains and food webs

A **food chain** is a sequence of organisms in which each one is eaten by the next: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. Arrows point from the food to the eater, in the direction of the flow of energy. Each step is a **trophic level**:

- 1st: **producers** (grass, algae).
- 2nd: **herbivores** (primary consumers).
- 3rd: **carnivores that eat herbivores** (secondary consumers).
- 4th: **carnivores that eat carnivores** (tertiary consumers, top carnivores).

An animal that eats both plants and animals, such as a crow or a human, can be at more than one level.

In real life most animals eat more than one thing and are eaten by more than one thing, so food chains are linked into a **food web**. Remove one species and see how the web reacts:

## 3. Flow of energy: the 10 per cent law

The Sun is the source of energy. Producers capture a small part (about 1 per cent) of the sunlight falling on them and store it as chemical energy in food. When a herbivore eats a plant, only about **10 per cent** of that energy is stored in the herbivore's body as new tissue. The other 90 per cent is used for life processes (moving, breathing, keeping warm) or is lost as **heat**, and some parts are not digested. The same happens at each further step, and this is the **10 per cent law**.

```math
E_n = E_1 \times (0.1)^{\,n-1}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>E₁</dt><dd>energy fixed by the producers (first trophic level), in joules</dd>
<dt>Eₙ</dt><dd>energy reaching the nth trophic level</dd>
<dt>0.1</dt><dd>the fraction (10 per cent) passed on at each step; real values range from about 5 to 20 per cent</dd>
<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Energy is not recycled: it flows one way, from the Sun, through the organisms, and out as heat. Very little is left after 4 or 5 steps, so food chains are short and there are few top carnivores (tigers are far fewer than deer).</dd>
</dl>

A bonus for us: if we eat plant foods (grains, vegetables) we are at the second level and get more of the original energy than if we eat meat from a herbivore (third level). That is one reason why a hectare of land can feed more vegetarians than meat-eaters.

<div class="myth">

**“Energy goes round in a cycle like water.”** Water and minerals are recycled, but energy is not. Each step loses most of it as heat, and the heat cannot be used again by the organisms. The Sun must keep supplying new energy.

</div>

### 3.1 Biological magnification

Farmers spray pesticides, such as DDT (now banned for most uses), on their fields. Some wash into ponds and rivers. A chemical that is **not biodegradable** (not broken down by the body) is taken in with food and **stays** in the body. A small fish eats many algae, so it collects the chemical from all of them; a big fish eats many small fish, and so on. At each step the concentration **multiplies**. This is **biological magnification**: it is greatest in the top carnivores, and humans, who often eat at the top of the food chain, get the largest dose. Crop plants take pesticides from the soil, so even grain and vegetables, milk and meat can carry some.

<div class="keypoints">

- Ecosystem = living (producers, consumers, decomposers) + non-living parts. Decomposers recycle materials.
- Food chain: producers → herbivores → carnivores. Many chains make a food web, which is more stable.
- Energy flows one way. Only about 10% passes to the next trophic level (10 per cent law), so chains are short.
- Biological magnification: non-biodegradable chemicals (like DDT) get more concentrated at each step; the top carnivores have the most.

## 4. How do our activities affect the environment?

### 4.1 The ozone layer and its depletion

**Ozone (O₃)** is a molecule made of three oxygen atoms. Ordinary oxygen, which we breathe, is O₂. High in the **stratosphere** (about 15 to 35 km above the ground) there is a thin layer rich in ozone, which absorbs most of the Sun's harmful **ultraviolet (UV) radiation**. Without it, strong UV would cause skin cancer and cataracts in humans, and harm crops and the tiny plants of the oceans.

Ozone is made in the stratosphere when UV splits O₂:

```math
\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} \text{O} + \text{O}, \qquad \text{O} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{O}_3
```

In the 1980s, scientists saw that the layer over Antarctica was getting very thin each spring (the “ozone hole”, found in 1985 by the British Antarctic Survey). The cause: **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)**, chemicals used in refrigerators, air conditioners, fire extinguishers, aerosol sprays and foam. CFCs are very stable and drift up to the stratosphere. There, UV breaks them and frees **chlorine atoms**, and each chlorine atom destroys ozone in a repeating cycle:

```math
\text{Cl} + \text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{ClO} + \text{O}_2, \qquad \text{ClO} + \text{O} \rightarrow \text{Cl} + \text{O}_2
```

Because chlorine comes back each time, one atom can destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules.

<div class="myth">

**“The ozone hole is the same as global warming.”** They are different problems. The ozone hole is a thinning of the ozone layer by CFCs and lets more UV reach us. Global warming is caused by greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide trapping heat. (Both are made worse by human activity, and some CFC substitutes do affect warming, but the two problems are not the same.)

</div>

### 4.2 Managing the garbage we produce

Everything we throw away is **garbage** (waste): kitchen peels, paper, plastic, glass, metal, old batteries and phones. A city like Delhi or Mumbai makes thousands of tonnes of it every day. It is helpful to split it into two kinds:

- **Biodegradable** waste: can be broken down by bacteria and fungi (vegetable peels, paper, cotton, leaves, wood).
- **Non-biodegradable** waste: cannot be broken down by living things, so it lasts a very long time (plastic, glass, metals, many chemicals, synthetic cloth).

Non-biodegradable waste causes harm: **plastic** blocks drains, chokes animals such as cows, turtles and birds that swallow it, and breaks into tiny pieces (microplastics). **What can we do?**

1. **Segregate** at source: keep wet (kitchen) and dry (paper, plastic, metal) waste in separate bins. This is already a rule in many Indian cities.
2. **Reduce, reuse, recycle**: use a cloth bag instead of plastic ones, refill bottles, and give metal, paper and glass for recycling. Take electronic waste (e-waste) and batteries to authorised collection points.
3. **Compost** the biodegradable waste at home or in a community pit, so it turns into manure. Some places use earthworms (vermicompost).
4. **Dispose safely**: waste that cannot be used goes to a sanitary landfill; burning plastic makes toxic fumes and must be avoided.

<div class="problem" data-problem="c10s-env-compost"></div>

<div class="myth">

**“If it is biodegradable, we can throw it anywhere.”** Biodegradable wastes also cause harm if they are dumped in large heaps: they rot, smell, breed flies and mosquitoes, and make methane. They should be composted in a proper way. And even a biodegradable plastic-like item needs the right conditions to rot.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Ozone (O₃) in the stratosphere absorbs UV. CFCs release chlorine, which destroys ozone in a repeating cycle. The Montreal Protocol phases CFCs out.
- Biodegradable waste is broken down by microbes; non-biodegradable waste (plastic, glass, metal) lasts for decades or longer.
- Segregate, reduce, reuse, recycle, compost, and dispose of the rest safely.
- Pesticides, metals and plastics can enter food chains and magnify.

</div>
