**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 7 · about 65 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain why variation matters, describe every mode of asexual reproduction, follow pollination to fertilisation in a flower, describe human reproduction and a pregnancy, and explain how birth control and reproductive health keep people safe.

<div class="bigidea">Reproduction makes new organisms of the same kind. Some organisms use only one parent and make copies (asexual); others combine cells from two parents (sexual). Copying DNA is never perfect, and sexual reproduction mixes the DNA of two parents, so the offspring are not exact copies. That variation is what lets a species survive when the world changes.</div>

## 1. Why copies are never exact: DNA and variation

Every living cell carries instructions for building a body: its **DNA**. When a cell divides, it first makes a copy of its DNA. Enzymes do the copying with great care: each **A** base pairs only with **T**, and each **C** only with **G**. Still, once in a while a wrong base slips in. So the copy is *almost* identical, and sometimes slightly different. This small difference is **variation**.

Step through the copying of one DNA molecule:

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-repr-dna">
<div class="step">One DNA molecule: two strands, held together by pairs of bases. A always faces T and C always faces G.</div>
<div class="step">The two strands unzip. Each strand is now a template.</div>
<div class="step">New bases line up on each template, following the pairing rule, and are joined into a new strand.</div>
<div class="step">Two molecules are made, each with one old strand and one new strand. Normally both are exact copies.</div>
<div class="step">Occasionally a wrong base is put in. The bases no longer match the original.</div>
<div class="step">That copy carries a change. If the change alters a protein, a trait may be different: this is variation.</div>
</div>

Why does a species need variation? Imagine a pond with identical algae. A very hot summer arrives. If all of them need cool water, **all die**. If a few are slightly more heat-tolerant because of a copying error long ago, those few survive and multiply. Variation does not help the individual much, but it helps the **species** survive changes in the environment (heat, a new disease, less food). Try it yourself:

With copying errors at 0% the whole population dies. With some errors, a few darker (more heat-proof) individuals are there to survive.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="myth">

**“Variation is only for the bold: nature plans it.”** Copying errors are random. Variation is not made on purpose to help; it simply happens, and whichever variants suit the new conditions survive. Nothing is “decided” by the species.

## 2. Modes of reproduction in single organisms (asexual)

In **asexual reproduction** one parent makes new individuals. No gametes, no partner. The young are **clones**: they carry the same DNA (apart from rare copying errors). It is quick and needs only one organism, so it is common in simple organisms and in many plants.

### 2.1 Fission

**Binary fission**: the cell copies its DNA, the nucleus divides, and the cell pinches into two equal parts. Amoeba and bacteria do this. **Multiple fission**: the nucleus divides many times first, and the cytoplasm then splits around each nucleus, giving many cells at once. Plasmodium (the malaria parasite) does this in a protective cyst.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-repr-fission"><figcaption>Choose binary or multiple fission and play or slide the progress. Watch the nucleus divide first and the cytoplasm follow.</figcaption></figure>

Fission is fast because every new cell divides again. A bacterium that divides every 20 minutes becomes 2, 4, 8, 16... a doubling:

```math
N = 2^{k}, \qquad k = \frac{\text{time}}{\text{time for one division}}
```

<div class="meaning">
<dl>
<dt>N</dt><dd>number of cells after k divisions, starting from a single cell</dd>

<dt>Physical meaning</dt><dd>Each division doubles the count, so the number grows by repeated multiplication, not repeated addition. After 10 divisions there are already 1,024 cells; after 20, over a million.</dd>
</dl>

### 2.2 Fragmentation

Some simple multicellular organisms simply break into pieces, and each piece grows into a full individual. **Spirogyra**, a green filament found in ponds, does this: a filament of cells breaks into fragments when conditions are right.

### 2.3 Regeneration

Some organisms can regrow the missing parts of their bodies. **Planaria**, a flatworm, can be cut into pieces and each piece regrows into a complete worm. It happens because cells near the cut divide quickly and then **specialise** (change into the cells needed, such as nerve or skin). Hydra also regenerates. Regeneration is *not* the same as the way every organism reproduces: a lizard regrows a tail, but the tail does not become a new lizard. In Planaria, though, one worm becomes many.

### 2.4 Budding

In **budding**, a small bump called a bud grows out of the parent body, gets a copy of the nucleus, grows and then breaks off as a new individual. Yeast (a single cell) and Hydra both bud. The yeast in a bit of warm, sugary dough buds fast; that is why dough rises.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-repr-bud"><figcaption>Choose yeast or Hydra and play the growth. The bud starts small, grows into a miniature of its parent, and separates.</figcaption></figure>

### 2.5 Spore formation

Many fungi, such as **Rhizopus** (bread mould), make **spores**: tiny round cells inside a sporangium. Spores are light, spread through the air, and have a tough coat that protects them in dry or hot conditions. Whenever a spore lands on damp, warm food, it germinates into a new mould. Next time bread goes mouldy in the monsoon, you are seeing spore formation.

<div class="step">Inside each sporangium, spores form in huge numbers.</div>
<div class="step">The sporangium bursts. The light spores drift in the air.</div>
<div class="step">A spore lands on damp bread. Its tough coat kept it safe through dry weather.</div>
<div class="step">In warmth and moisture, the spore germinates into a new hypha.</div>
<div class="step">It spreads into a new colony and makes new sporangia.</div>

### 2.6 Vegetative propagation

In **vegetative propagation**, a new plant grows from a part of the old one (root, stem or leaf), not from a seed. It is natural in some plants: potato (eyes on the tuber), Bryophyllum (buds in the leaf notches), ginger (stem pieces), grass (runners). Farmers also use it on purpose, by **cutting** (rose, sugarcane, banana pieces), **layering** (jasmine: a branch bent to the ground and covered with soil, still attached to the plant until it roots) and **grafting** (a shoot of a good variety is joined to a rooted plant).

Why do farmers use it? Seedless plants (banana, seedless oranges, roses) cannot grow from seeds. It is **faster** than a seed (a cutting can flower earlier), and every new plant is an **exact clone**, so the farmer knows what fruit it will bear. A grafted Alphonso mango tree will give Alphonso mangoes, not a surprise.

- DNA copying is very accurate but not perfect. Small errors give variation, which helps a species survive changes.
- Asexual reproduction: one parent, clones. Methods: binary and multiple fission, fragmentation, regeneration, budding, spore formation, vegetative propagation.
- Cells after k fissions: N = 2ᵏ.
- Vegetative propagation gives uniform plants quickly, and works for seedless plants.

## 3. Sexual reproduction

In **sexual reproduction** two **gametes**, one male and one female, fuse in **fertilisation** to form a **zygote**. The zygote divides and develops into the new organism. Because the two parents contribute different DNA and gametes are all slightly different, the young are never exact copies of either parent: much more variation than in asexual reproduction. It is slower and costs energy, but the variation is a great advantage for a species in a changing world.

Gametes carry **half** the number of chromosomes of a body cell. A human body cell has 46 chromosomes, an egg or sperm has 23, and their fusion makes a zygote with 46 again. If gametes had the full number, the number would double in every generation.

## 4. Sexual reproduction in flowering plants

### 4.1 The parts of a flower

A flower is the reproductive organ of a plant. Its parts, from outside to inside:

- **Sepals** (green): protect the bud.
- **Petals** (coloured, scented): attract insects.
- **Stamens** (male): each has an **anther** (makes pollen grains, which contain the male gametes) held up by a **filament**.
- **Carpel** (female; also called the pistil): **stigma** (sticky top, catches pollen), **style** (tube) and **ovary** (swollen base holding the **ovules**; each ovule has an egg cell).

A flower with both stamens and carpels (hibiscus, mustard) is **bisexual**. A flower with only one kind (papaya, watermelon) is **unisexual**.

### 4.2 Pollination

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. If the stigma belongs to the same flower (or another flower of the same plant), it is **self-pollination**; if it is a flower on a different plant, **cross-pollination**. The pollen is carried by **wind** (rice, maize), **insects** (bees on mustard, butterflies), **birds** or **water**. Flowers attract insects with colour, smell and nectar, so the animals carry pollen by accident while collecting food. The animals also help our farms: no bees on a mango tree, fewer mangoes.

### 4.3 Fertilisation and what comes after

Pollen lands on the stigma and, helped by sugars there, grows a long **pollen tube** down the style to the ovary. The male gamete moves down the tube and fuses with the egg cell in the ovule. This is **fertilisation**, and the product is the zygote.

Then (the flower has done its job):

1. The **zygote** divides many times and becomes the **embryo** (a tiny plant).
2. The **ovule** becomes the **seed**, a hard coat around the embryo and its stored food.
3. The **ovary** becomes the **fruit** (a mango, a pea pod, a tomato).
4. Petals, sepals and stamens wither and fall.

<div class="myth">

**“Pollination and fertilisation are the same.”** Pollination is the *landing* of pollen on the stigma. Fertilisation happens later, inside the ovule, when the gametes fuse. Pollination can happen without fertilisation (if the tube never reaches the ovule).

</div>

### 4.4 Germination

When the seed gets **water, air and suitable warmth**, it **germinates**. The embryo wakes up and grows: the **radicle** (first root) goes down, then the **plumule** (first shoot) comes up. The seed's stored food (in the cotyledons) feeds the seedling until its leaves can do photosynthesis. Take away water, air or warmth, and the seed stays dormant.

## 5. Reproduction in human beings

Humans reproduce sexually. At **puberty** (about 10 to 14 years) the body changes under the influence of hormones, and the reproductive organs mature: a boy begins to make sperm, a girl begins to release eggs. These are normal changes. This section describes the system as a biology topic, at school level.

### 5.1 The male reproductive system

- **Testes** (two, in the **scrotum**): make **sperm** and the hormone **testosterone**. The scrotum holds them outside the body because sperm need a temperature slightly lower than the body's.
- **Vas deferens**: tube that carries sperm up and joins the urethra.
- **Seminal vesicle and prostate**: add a fluid that nourishes sperm. Sperm plus this fluid is **semen**.
- **Urethra and penis**: the urethra carries semen (and, at other times, urine) out through the penis.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-repr-male"><figcaption>Choose a part to highlight it and read its job. The red dots show sperm moving along the vas deferens.</figcaption></figure>

### 5.2 The female reproductive system

- **Ovaries** (two): make **eggs** and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone. From puberty one egg is released about once a month.
- **Oviducts** (fallopian tubes): catch the egg; fertilisation usually happens here.
- **Uterus** (womb): a muscular organ where the baby grows. Its inner wall (lining) is thick and rich in blood vessels.
- **Cervix and vagina**: the neck of the uterus and the passage leading to the outside (the birth canal).

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-repr-female"><figcaption>Choose a part to highlight it. The dot follows an egg from the ovary into the oviduct.</figcaption></figure>

### 5.3 The monthly cycle

About every 28 days (it varies, especially in the first years), an egg ripens in an ovary and is released: **ovulation**, around day 14. At the same time the uterus builds up a thick, blood-rich lining to receive a fertilised egg. If no sperm fertilises the egg within about a day, the egg dies and the lining is not needed: it breaks down and leaves the body through the vagina over 3 to 5 days. This is **menstruation** (the period). It is a normal, healthy process and not something unclean.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-repr-cycle"><figcaption>Slide the day. The graph shows the thickness of the uterus lining. Switch to “Fertilisation” to see what changes: the lining stays and there is no period.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c10s-repr-ovulation"></div>

<div class="myth">

**“Periods are impure and a girl should stay away from school or the kitchen.”** Menstruation is the body shedding an unneeded lining. There is nothing impure about it. Good hygiene (clean cloth or sanitary pad, changed regularly) and rest if there is pain are all that is needed.

</div>

### 5.4 Fertilisation, pregnancy and birth

If a sperm reaches the egg in the oviduct, the two gametes fuse: **fertilisation**. The zygote divides as it moves to the uterus: 2, 4, 8, 16 cells. The ball of cells sinks into the thick lining: **implantation**. Now a **pregnancy** has begun and the monthly period stops.

The embryo gets food and oxygen from the mother through the **placenta**, a disc in the wall of the uterus, rich in blood vessels with finger-like **villi**. Food and oxygen pass from the mother's blood to the embryo's, and carbon dioxide and wastes pass back. The two blood supplies stay separate. The placenta joins the baby by the **umbilical cord**. From about the eighth week the embryo is called a **foetus**. Pregnancy lasts about **nine months** (40 weeks), after which the muscles of the uterus push the baby out: **birth**.

## 6. Reproductive health

**Reproductive health** means being physically, mentally and socially well in matters of reproduction, with the knowledge to look after oneself and the freedom to make good choices.

### 6.1 Birth control

Having a child is a big responsibility. A family may wish to **space** the births or to stop at a number it can look after. This is a **choice**, to be made by the couple together, on a doctor's advice. Methods:

- **Barrier methods**: a **condom** or a **diaphragm** stops the sperm from reaching the egg. A condom also reduces the spread of infections.
- **Chemical (hormonal) methods**: oral **pills** change hormone balance so no egg is released. Doctors must advise; there can be side effects.
- **Copper-T (IUD)**: a small device placed in the uterus by a doctor, so a fertilised egg cannot implant.
- **Surgical methods**: **vasectomy** (cut and tie the vas deferens in the man) and **tubectomy** (the oviducts in the woman). Very effective and meant to be permanent.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-repr-block"><figcaption>Choose a method to see exactly where it stops the path from sperm to a pregnancy, and whether it also protects against infections.</figcaption></figure>

### 6.2 Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

Some infections spread through sexual contact: **gonorrhoea, syphilis, warts and HIV-AIDS**. They can be prevented by using a condom. Birth control pills, copper-T and surgery do **not** protect against them. A doctor can treat many STIs if they are found early.

### 6.3 Girls and boys, equal worth

India has had a falling **sex ratio** (girls per 1,000 boys) in some regions because of the cruel practice of aborting female foetuses. This is a crime under the law. A girl and a boy are of equal worth, and a baby's sex does not depend on the mother (see the next chapter, Heredity). Medical termination of pregnancy (abortion) is legal in India only for specific health reasons and with trained doctors, and tests to find the sex of the baby are banned.
