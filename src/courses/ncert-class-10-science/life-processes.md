**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 5 · about 70 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how living things get and use energy, how the human body digests food, breathes, moves blood and removes waste, and how plants transport water and food.

<div class="bigidea">Living things are always running repairs and building. That needs raw materials and energy, so every organism must take in food (nutrition), get energy out of it (respiration), move materials around (transport) and throw out waste (excretion). The processes that keep a living thing alive are called life processes.</div>

## 1. What is life, and what keeps it going?

Even when you sleep, your heart beats and your cells keep working. Molecules are being broken down and built up inside every cell all the time. A moving cricket ball loses energy and stops; a living body must **keep supplying** energy. It takes **food** for raw material and energy, uses **oxygen** to release the energy, moves things by a **transport system**, and removes **waste**. These four life processes are the topic of this chapter.

## 2. Nutrition

**Autotrophic nutrition.** Green plants make their own food from CO₂ and water, using sunlight. **Heterotrophic nutrition**: animals and fungi depend on others for food. The three types are: **saprotrophs** (bread mould, mushrooms) feed on dead matter; **parasites** (Cuscuta or amarbel, tapeworm) feed on a living host; and **holozoic** nutrition, where the food is taken in and digested inside the body (humans, cow, Amoeba). Amoeba extends false feet (pseudopodia) to wrap round a food particle and digest it inside a food vacuole.

### 2.1 Photosynthesis

The carbohydrate (glucose) is made in the leaf from CO₂ and water, in the presence of chlorophyll and sunlight:

```math
6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{chlorophyll, sunlight}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2
```

The process has these parts: (i) light is absorbed by **chlorophyll** in the **chloroplasts**; (ii) light energy is changed to chemical energy and **water is split** into hydrogen and oxygen; (iii) **CO₂ is reduced** to carbohydrate. The glucose is used right away for energy or stored as **starch**.

### 2.2 Stomata

The leaf takes in CO₂ and gives out O₂ and water vapour through tiny pores called **stomata**, on the underside of the leaf. Each pore is guarded by two bean-shaped **guard cells**. When water flows into the guard cells they swell and curve apart so the pore **opens**; when they lose water the pore **shuts**. The plant closes them when it is hot and dry to save water, and at night when there is no photosynthesis. Stomata also let out a great deal of water vapour, which is transpiration.

### 2.3 Nutrition in human beings

Digestion breaks big, insoluble food molecules into small soluble ones that can be absorbed. The **alimentary canal** is a long tube from mouth to anus, with glands (salivary glands, liver, pancreas) pouring in juices. **Enzymes** are the biological catalysts that do the breaking.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-life-gut">
<div class="step">Mouth: teeth chew, saliva wets the food, and salivary amylase starts to turn starch into sugar.</div>
<div class="step">Oesophagus: waves of muscle contraction (peristalsis) push the food down.</div>
<div class="step">Stomach: hydrochloric acid and pepsin start digesting proteins. Mucus protects the wall from the acid.</div>
<div class="step">Small intestine: bile (from the liver) emulsifies fats; pancreatic juice (trypsin, lipase, amylase) digests proteins, fats and starch.</div>
<div class="step">Small intestine: intestinal juice completes digestion. Villi, finger-like folds full of blood vessels, absorb glucose, amino acids and fatty acids.</div>
<div class="step">Large intestine: absorbs water and salts from the undigested food.</div>
<div class="step">The rest leaves the body through the anus (egestion).</div>
</div>

Key facts: the **small intestine** is about 6 to 7 metres long, so the food has time to be digested and absorbed, and it is the longest part in plant eaters (cow) because they digest cellulose. **Bile** is alkaline and makes the food from the stomach alkaline too, so pancreatic enzymes can work. Tooth decay is caused by acid-making bacteria on food left stuck on the teeth.

## 3. Respiration

All the energy for life comes from food, released by **respiration**. The first step happens in the cytoplasm of cells: the 6-carbon **glucose** is split into two 3-carbon **pyruvate** molecules. What happens to pyruvate next depends on the availability of oxygen:

- **Aerobic respiration** (with oxygen, in the **mitochondria**): pyruvate is broken down completely to CO₂ and water, releasing a lot of energy (about 30 ATP per glucose).
- **Anaerobic respiration** (without oxygen): in yeast, the product is ethanol and CO₂ (fermentation); in muscle cells, **lactic acid**. Only about 2 ATP per glucose.

The energy is stored in the cell as **ATP**, the energy currency. Breaking the end phosphate of ATP gives about 30.5 kJ per mole, enough to drive the contraction of a muscle, the building of proteins or the conduction of a nerve impulse. Wherever energy is needed, the cell uses ATP.

### 3.1 Breathing and gas exchange in humans

Air enters through the nostrils (where hair and mucus filter dust), goes down the **windpipe** (trachea, kept open by rings of cartilage) and into the **bronchi**, which divide into smaller tubes ending in about 300 million tiny air sacs, the **alveoli**. The alveoli have thin walls with a dense mesh of capillaries, a huge surface area, and that is where oxygen enters the blood and CO₂ leaves. Oxygen is carried by **haemoglobin** in the red blood cells; CO₂ is mostly carried dissolved in the blood plasma.

Breathing is controlled by muscles. To **breathe in**, the diaphragm flattens and the rib muscles lift the ribs, so the chest cavity gets bigger; the pressure falls and air rushes in. To **breathe out**, the reverse happens.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-life-breath"><figcaption>Change the breathing rate and how deep each breath is. The diaphragm flattens as the lungs fill. Air moved per minute is the rate times the volume of one breath, and it rises sharply in exercise.</figcaption></figure>

Aquatic animals like fish breathe the small amount of oxygen dissolved in water, so they breathe much faster than land animals; their gills have a large area of thin filaments rich in blood vessels.

## 4. Transportation

### 4.1 In human beings

Blood is a fluid that carries food, oxygen, waste and hormones. It has **plasma** (a liquid that carries food, CO₂ and waste), **red blood cells** (haemoglobin, oxygen), **white blood cells** (fight germs) and **platelets** (clot the blood at a cut). The heart pumps it round the body through **blood vessels**: arteries (thick, elastic walls, carry blood away from the heart under high pressure), veins (thinner, with valves, carry blood back to the heart) and capillaries (one cell thick; exchange takes place here).

The heart is a muscular organ with **four chambers**: two upper atria that receive blood, two lower ventricles that pump it out. The left and right sides are kept apart so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix. This is how it works, in **double circulation**:

1. Oxygen-poor blood from the body enters the **right atrium**, then the **right ventricle**, which pumps it to the **lungs**.
2. In the lungs it loses CO₂ and picks up oxygen, then returns to the **left atrium** and flows into the **left ventricle**.
3. The left ventricle, with its thick muscular wall, pumps this oxygen-rich blood round the whole body.

Every drop of blood therefore passes through the heart twice on each round. Birds and mammals, which are warm-blooded, need a lot of energy and so need this separation to keep supplying oxygen.

**Blood pressure** is the force of the blood against the vessel walls. It is highest when the ventricles contract (systolic, about 120 mm Hg) and lowest when they relax (diastolic, about 80 mm Hg) in a healthy adult. High blood pressure (hypertension) strains the arteries and can cause heart attack or stroke. **Platelets** stop bleeding by clotting. **Lymph** is a colourless fluid that leaks from capillaries into the tissues; it carries digested fat from the intestine and drains back into the blood, helping to fight infection.

<div class="myth">

**“Arteries carry oxygenated blood and veins carry deoxygenated blood.”** Not always. Arteries carry blood <i>away from</i> the heart, and veins carry it <i>to</i> the heart. The pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood from the heart to the lungs, and the pulmonary vein brings oxygen-rich blood back.

</div>

### 4.2 In plants

**Xylem** carries water and minerals from the roots to the leaves. It is made of dead, hollow vessels and tracheids, forming continuous pipes from root to leaf. Root hairs absorb water by osmosis, creating a pressure that pushes it up a little, but the main driving force in tall plants is **transpiration pull**: as water evaporates from the leaves through the stomata, it pulls up the continuous water column behind it, like a drinking straw. The rate rises with sun, wind and dry air.

**Phloem** carries the food (sugars) made in the leaves to all parts, including roots, fruits and growing buds. This is **translocation**. It uses energy from ATP to load sugar into the tubes, which raises the pressure there so the sap flows to where it is lower. It happens in both directions. The tubes are living.

## 5. Excretion

**Excretion** is the removal of harmful metabolic waste. In humans, the **kidneys** do most of it, as part of the urinary system (two kidneys, two ureters, a bladder, a urethra). The waste is mainly **urea** (from the breakdown of proteins in the liver) and **uric acid**.

Each kidney has about a million filtering units called **nephrons**. Each has a ball of capillaries, the **glomerulus**, wrapped in a cup (the Bowman’s capsule), and a long tubule. Blood is filtered in the capsule; the useful things are **reabsorbed** by the tubule, and what is left is urine.

<div class="steps" data-fig="c10-life-nephron">
<div class="step">Blood enters the kidney by the renal artery. It carries urea.</div>
<div class="step">Filtration: about 180 litres of fluid a day is pushed out of the glomerulus into the capsule.</div>
<div class="step">Selective reabsorption: glucose, amino acids, most salts and water return to the blood.</div>
<div class="step">The collecting duct adjusts water: more if you are short of water, less if you have drunk a lot.</div>
<div class="step">Urine passes down the ureter to the bladder, and leaves through the urethra.</div>
</div>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c10s-life-kidney"></div>

**Artificial kidney (haemodialysis).** If the kidneys fail, a machine can clean the blood: it passes through tubes with a thin membrane, surrounded by dialysis fluid with the same glucose and salts as blood but no urea. Urea diffuses out; blood is returned to the patient. A person needs about 3 to 4 sessions of a few hours every week.

**Excretion in plants.** Plants release extra oxygen and CO₂ through the stomata; extra water by transpiration; waste in the leaves and bark that fall off; and some wastes are stored as resins and gums in old xylem, or as latex.

<div class="keypoints">

- Life processes: nutrition, respiration, transport and excretion. Autotrophs make food; heterotrophs eat.
- Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, in chloroplasts with light; stomata let gases in and out and are opened by guard cells swelling.
- Digestion: amylase (mouth), pepsin and HCl (stomach), bile, trypsin, lipase (small intestine); villi absorb; large intestine absorbs water.
- Respiration: glucose → pyruvate (cytoplasm); aerobic (mitochondria, about 30 ATP); anaerobic (ethanol + CO₂ in yeast, lactic acid in muscle, about 2 ATP).
- Breathing: diaphragm flattens and ribs lift to breathe in; alveoli exchange gases; haemoglobin carries O₂.
- Double circulation: right side to lungs, left side to body; thick left ventricle; arteries away from the heart, veins to it.
- Plants: xylem carries water up (transpiration pull); phloem carries sugars both ways (translocation, uses ATP).
- Kidney: nephron filters 180 L a day, reabsorbs most; urea leaves in about 1.5 L of urine.

</div>
