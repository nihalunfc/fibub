**Science · Class 10 · Chapter 8 · about 55 minutes.** By the end you will be able to explain how variation builds up, work out the results of Mendel's crosses with a Punnett square, tell dominant from recessive traits, explain how a gene produces a trait, and show how the sex of a child is determined.

<div class="bigidea">Children look like their parents because they get copies of the parents' genes: half from each. A gene can have different versions (alleles); a dominant allele shows even in a single copy, while a recessive allele shows only when both copies are recessive. Which allele a gamete carries is a matter of chance, so we can predict the ratios but not the individual child.</div>

## 1. Accumulation of variation during reproduction

In the previous chapter you saw that DNA copying is accurate but not perfect: sometimes a small change slips in. Now follow the changes **down the generations**. A cell copies its DNA and divides in two. Each of the two copies may carry a small new change. When those two cells divide again, their children may carry a further change, on top of the ones before. So the changes **accumulate**: the farther down a line of descent, the more differences from the ancestor.

Most descendants are very close copies, a few have several changes. It is rarely the case that two lines of descent are quite alike. Slide the generations and the chance of a change:

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-her-variation"><figcaption>The bars show how many descendants carry 0, 1, 2... DNA changes after g generations of two offspring each. Raise the generations and the bars spread to the right: variation accumulates. Raise the chance of a change and the exact copies (green) become rarer.</figcaption></figure>

In sexual reproduction the variation is much greater. Every child gets a **mixture** of the DNA of two parents, and the mixing is different in each child. Even so, the changes that count for inheritance are those in the DNA of the **gametes**. A change in a skin cell dies with the person; a change in an egg or sperm may be passed on.

<div class="myth">

**“Variation is acquired in life and handed on.”** If a body-builder gets big muscles, or a person loses a finger, the children are not born with muscles or a missing finger. Only changes to the DNA in the gametes are inherited. Traits we gain in our lifetime (an **acquired** trait) are not.

</div>

## 2. Heredity: how traits pass from parents to children

**Heredity** is the passing of characteristics from parents to offspring. The characteristics we can see or measure (height, flower colour, seed shape) are called **traits**. Some traits are **inherited** (eye colour, attached or free ear lobes), others are acquired. In every species the offspring show **similarities** to their parents, but also **differences**. Gregor Johann Mendel, a monk and school teacher in what is now the Czech Republic, was the first to work out the rules for this, by growing pea plants in the garden of his monastery from 1856 for about eight years.

## 3. Mendel's contributions: the rules of inheritance

Why peas? They grow fast, have several clear, either-or traits (tall or short, round or wrinkled seed, purple or white flower), and he could control pollination by hand. He also began with **pure** plants: those that always give the same trait when self-pollinated for generations.

### 3.1 One trait at a time: the monohybrid cross

Mendel crossed a pure tall plant with a pure short plant. All the offspring (called the **F1 generation**, the first filial) were **tall**. None were medium. The short trait seemed to have vanished.

He then let the F1 plants self-pollinate. In the **F2 generation** short plants came back, in a ratio of about **3 tall : 1 short**. So the short trait had been present all along, hidden in the F1, and appeared again in the next generation.

His explanation (in modern words):

- Each trait is controlled by a pair of **factors** (we now call them **alleles** of a gene), one inherited from each parent.
- The **dominant** allele (T, tall) shows its trait even when only one copy is present. The **recessive** allele (t, short) shows only when both copies are recessive.
- A gamete carries only **one** allele of each pair, chosen at random. At fertilisation the alleles are paired again.

Some words to keep straight:

- **Genotype**: the alleles a plant has (TT, Tt or tt). **Phenotype**: the trait you see (tall or short).
- **Homozygous**: two alleles the same (TT or tt, “pure”). **Heterozygous**: two different alleles (Tt, “hybrid”).
- A **Punnett square** is a grid that shows all the ways in which the gametes can combine.

<div class="myth">

**“Dominant means common, or better.”** Dominant only means that the allele hides the other when both are present. A dominant allele can be rare in a population, and a dominant trait can even be harmful. It is about how the alleles behave in an individual, not about being stronger.

### 3.2 Two traits at once: the dihybrid cross

Mendel then followed **two traits together**: seed shape (round R, wrinkled r) and seed colour (yellow Y, green y). He crossed a pure plant with round yellow seeds (RRYY) with a pure plant with wrinkled green seeds (rryy). All the F1 seeds were round and yellow (RrYy): both dominant traits show.

When the F1 plants self-pollinated, the F2 seeds came in **four** types, in the ratio **9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green**. Two of these (round green and wrinkled yellow) are **new combinations** that neither original parent had. This tells us that shape and colour are inherited **independently** of each other. A gamete from RrYy can get R or r, and Y or y, in any pairing: RY, Ry, rY, ry, equally often. Sixteen boxes in the square.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="c10-her-dihybrid"><figcaption>Slide the number of filled boxes from 0 to 16. Round seeds are circles, wrinkled seeds are stars; yellow and green are shaded. Count the four types: 9, 3, 3 and 1.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="problem" data-problem="c10s-her-dihybrid"></div>

Mendel's two big ideas: (1) **Law of segregation**: the two alleles of a pair separate when gametes form, so each gamete carries only one. (2) **Law of independent assortment**: the alleles of different traits are sorted into gametes independently. (This last one is true for genes on different chromosomes, which is the case for his peas.)

You can see where the independence comes from in the chromosomes. They come in **pairs**, one from each parent, and a gamete gets one chromosome of every pair at random:

## 4. How do these traits get expressed?

A trait is not “drawn” in the DNA. A **gene** is a stretch of DNA with the instructions to make one **protein**, and the protein (an enzyme or a hormone, for example) does the work that leads to the trait. Take plant height. The pea height gene makes an enzyme that helps the plant to produce a **growth hormone**. A working copy of the gene (T) gives plenty of enzyme and so plenty of hormone, and the plant grows tall. The recessive allele (t) makes less (or a weaker) enzyme: less hormone and a shorter plant. In Tt there is one working copy, which is **enough**, so the plant is tall. That is why T is dominant: one good copy gives enough protein.

<div class="keypoints">

- Variation accumulates as DNA is copied again and again; only changes in gametes are inherited. Acquired traits are not.
- Monohybrid cross TT × tt: F1 all Tt (tall), F2 3 tall : 1 short (genotypes 1 : 2 : 1).
- Dihybrid cross RrYy × RrYy: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1; the traits are inherited independently.
- A gene makes a protein; the protein leads to the trait. One working copy (dominant) is enough.
- Genotype is the allele pair; phenotype is the trait you see.

## 5. Sex determination

Humans have **23 pairs** of chromosomes (46 in all). 22 pairs are the same in men and women. The 23rd pair is the **sex chromosomes**: **women have two X chromosomes (XX)** and **men have one X and one Y (XY)**.

Every **egg** has one X (a woman has only X to give). A **sperm** carries either X or Y, in equal numbers. At fertilisation:

- X sperm + X egg → **XX**: a **girl**.
- Y sperm + X egg → **XY**: a **boy**.

So the chance of a boy or a girl is **1/2 each time**, and the sex of the child depends on the **father's** sperm, not the mother. A woman cannot be blamed for having daughters, and nobody can choose the sex of a baby. The birth of one child has no effect on the next: a family with three girls still has a 1/2 chance of a boy at the fourth birth.

Compare with other species:

- In some **reptiles** (turtles, crocodiles) the sex depends on the **temperature** at which the eggs are incubated, not on chromosomes. 
- In some animals (snails) an individual can change sex during its life.
- In **birds** (beyond the syllabus), the female has the two different sex chromosomes, so it is the mother's egg that decides.

<div class="myth">

**“Having a girl is the mother's fault.”** Every egg carries X, and it is the father's sperm (X or Y) that decides. Blaming a woman for a daughter is not only unkind: it is also wrong biology.

<div class="keypoints">

- Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes; the 23rd pair decides sex: XX female, XY male.
- All eggs carry X; sperm carry X or Y with equal probability. The father's sperm decides the baby's sex; each birth has a 1/2 chance of a girl or a boy.
- Mendel's laws: segregation (one allele per gamete) and independent assortment.
- Ratios are probabilities: they appear with large numbers, not necessarily in a single family.

</div>
