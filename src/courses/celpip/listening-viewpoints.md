**Module 2 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will know how a Viewpoints talk is organised, how to map the different sides and their reasons, and how to find the speaker’s own position. You will practise on four original talks with 24 questions.

<div class="bigidea">Part 6 presents an issue, then one side with its reasons, then the other side with its reasons, and often a middle or personal view. Map it like a debate: issue, for, against, middle, next.</div>

## 1. What Part 6 looks like

Part 6, **Listening to Viewpoints**, is the last listening part. You hear one longer recording, usually about two to three minutes, in which a speaker (a radio host, a reporter or a presenter) discusses an issue and presents **more than one opinion** about it. Then you answer **6 questions** with four options each.

- One main speaker, sometimes reporting what other people think.
- Heard **once**; questions after.
- Topics are public issues: a four-day school week, working from home, a ban on noisy machines, tipping.

Because it comes last, Part 6 also tests your stamina. Many people are tired by now. Plan a deep breath before it starts.

## 2. What it really tests

Part 6 tests **listening to arguments**. You need to:

- Identify the **issue** (what is being debated).
- Separate the **sides** and the **reasons** each side gives.
- Know **who** holds each view (supporters, parents, a business owner, researchers).
- Find the **speaker’s own position**, if there is one.
- Recognise **concessions** ("I’ll admit that...", "that may be true, but...").
- Understand the **purpose** of a sentence or an example.

**Everyday analogy.** Think of two neighbours arguing about a new dog park, while a third neighbour listens and then says what she thinks. If you only remember "dogs" and "park", you cannot explain the argument. You need the shape: who wants it, why; who is against it, why; and what the third neighbour suggested. Part 6 is always that shape.

## 3. The method: the debate map

<div class="steps">

<div class="step">

**Before the audio: draw the map.** Write `Issue:` at the top, then two columns `+ (for)` and `− (against)`, and a line at the bottom for `Middle / speaker:` and `Next:`.

</div>
<div class="step">

**First sentences: catch the issue.** The talk almost always starts by stating what is being debated: `4-day school week, rural`.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Each side: note who and why.** `+ supporters: save $ buses/heat; easier to hire teachers; US: turnover ↓`. `− parents: Fri childcare $; researchers: long day tiring for kids`.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Catch the turn to the speaker’s own view.** Signals: *in my view, personally, I believe, the way I see it, I think*. Write it on the bottom line. If the speaker reports a middle position from someone else, note whose it is.

</div>
<div class="step">

**End: catch what happens next.** Many talks end with a meeting, a vote, a study or an open question. Note it.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Situation line: *Listen to a talk about school uniforms.*

> Should public schools in Canada require uniforms? Supporters say uniforms reduce bullying about clothes and save families money, because children are not asking for expensive brands. Some principals also say students focus better when they look ready for work. Opponents disagree. They point out that a full set of uniforms can cost several hundred dollars at the start of the year, which is hard for families with several children. Others argue that clothing is a way for teenagers to express who they are. I can see both sides. I would not make uniforms compulsory, but I like the idea of a simple dress code, such as no logos, which keeps the focus on learning without the cost. Our local school board will survey parents this spring.

Map:
`Issue: compulsory uniforms in public schools`
`+ supporters: less bullying, save $ (no brands); principals: focus`
`− opponents: start-up cost hundreds, many kids; self-expression`
`Speaker: not compulsory; simple dress code (no logos)`
`Next: board surveys parents in spring`

*Which argument do opponents make?* A. Uniforms cost a lot at the start of the year. B. Uniforms reduce bullying. C. Students focus better. D. Families save money on brands. **A.** B, C and D are all real arguments in the talk, but from the *other* side. This is the biggest trap in Part 6.

*What is the speaker’s view?* A. Uniforms should be compulsory. B. There should be no rules about clothes. C. A simple dress code is better than uniforms. D. Parents should decide alone. **C.** B is too extreme: the speaker wants *some* rules.

*Why does the speaker mention "no logos"?* To give an example of a simple dress code. Part 6 often asks about the **purpose** of an example.

## 5. Question types and traps

| Question | Trap |
|---|---|
| What is the talk mainly about? | One side’s argument instead of the whole issue |
| Which reason do supporters give? | A reason from the opposing side |
| What is the business owner’s main objection? | The small point she concedes before *but* |
| What is the speaker’s opinion? | An extreme version (always, never, ban everything) |
| Why does the speaker mention X? | Treating an example as a new argument |
| What will happen next? | Something already done |

**Concession is the key move.** Speakers often agree with part of the other side before disagreeing: "Electric models are fine for homeowners, *but*...", "The speaker was funny, *I’ll give him that*. *But*...". The concession is true, but it is not the speaker’s main view. The main view comes after the turn.

<div class="strategy">

**Watch for extreme words in options.** The speaker’s view in Part 6 is usually balanced or qualified: *not a fixed rule, for high school but not elementary, at restaurants but not at counters*. Options with *always, never, all, ban completely, everyone must* are usually wrong for a qualified speaker. Cross them out first.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The last opinion I heard is the speaker’s opinion."** Not always. A speaker may end by reporting someone else ("A school trustee I spoke to takes a middle position"), or may end with what happens next. The speaker’s own view is marked by first-person words: *I think, in my view, personally*. If there is no "I", the speaker may simply be reporting, and the question may ask whose view it is.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- **Stating the issue:** *the question of, the debate over, is considering, has divided*
- **Supporting side:** *supporters say, those in favour argue, the main benefit is*
- **Opposing side:** *critics, opponents point out, not everyone is happy, many are not convinced*
- **Conceding:** *that may be true, but; I’ll admit; it’s fair to say; I can see that*
- **Speaker’s view:** *in my view, personally, the way I see it, I believe, I think*
- **Middle positions:** *both sides are right, it depends, a middle position, instead of banning*

</div>

## 6. Practice: four viewpoints talks

Draw the debate map on paper for each talk. Listen once, fill in both sides and the speaker’s view, then answer all six questions.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 6: **one talk with different opinions, 6 questions after it**, heard once; it comes last, so save energy for it.
- Map it like a debate: **issue, for (+ reasons), against (− reasons), speaker or middle view, next**.
- The biggest trap is a real argument from the **wrong side**.
- Concessions come before *but*; the main view comes after.
- The speaker’s view is marked by *I think, in my view, personally*, and is usually qualified, not extreme.

</div>
