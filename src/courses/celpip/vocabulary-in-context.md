**Module 3 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will be able to work out the meaning of most unknown words in a CELPIP reading text using five kinds of context clues and a few common word parts, and you will know when an unknown word does not matter at all.

<div class="bigidea">You do not need to know every word: the sentence around a new word almost always tells you enough to answer the question.</div>

## 1. Why this matters in CELPIP

Every CELPIP reading part includes words that some test takers have not seen before, especially in Part 3 (Reading for Information) and Part 4 (Reading for Viewpoints). There is no dictionary in the test, and the timer keeps running. Research on second-language reading (Nation 2013) suggests that readers need to know about 95 to 98 percent of the words in a text to read it comfortably. That still leaves one or two unknown words in every short paragraph. Strong readers are not the ones who know every word; they are the ones who deal with new words quickly.

You also meet these words inside the questions and options. A question might ask what a person thinks, and the right option uses a word you need to understand, such as *reluctant* or *sceptical*. So the skill helps you twice: in the text and in the answers.

**An everyday analogy.** Think of a jigsaw puzzle with one piece missing. You can still see the picture. You know the missing piece is part of the sky because blue pieces surround it. An unknown word is the missing piece, and the words around it show you its colour.


Tap a phrase from the question, then the passage phrase that means the same.

## 2. Step one: does the word matter?

Before you try to work out a word, ask: **does the question need it?** If a question asks *when* the ferry leaves, and the unknown word is in a sentence about the weather, skip it. Many words you do not know are decoration: descriptions, examples, adjectives that add colour but not facts.

Spend effort on a word only when:

- it is in the sentence that answers the question,
- it appears in one of the options you are choosing between, or
- the question asks about it directly.

## 3. The five kinds of context clues

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Definition or restatement.** The writer explains the word, often after *that is*, *in other words*, *which means*, a comma or a dash. *Items cannot be refunded, that is, you cannot get your money back.*

</div>
<div class="step">

**Contrast.** A word such as *but*, *unlike*, *however*, *rather than* or *instead of* tells you the new word means the opposite of something nearby. *Unlike her talkative brother, Noor is reserved.* Reserved = not talkative.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Example.** A list or an example shows what the word means. *The trail is treacherous: ice covers the rocks and several hikers have fallen.* Ice and falls point to dangerous.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Cause and result.** The sentence shows what led to the word, or what it led to. *Tickets were scarce, and many fans left without seats.* If fans left without seats, there were few tickets.

</div>
<div class="step">

**General sense and tone.** The whole situation suggests a positive or negative meaning. *The new bus route is a boon for seniors.* The rest of the sentence is good news, so boon is something helpful.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Word parts that help

English builds many words from smaller parts. You do not need a long list; a few common ones solve a lot of words.

| Part | Usually means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| un-, in-, im-, dis-, non- | not | unclear, inefficient, impossible, dislike, non-refundable |
| re- | again or back | reopen, refund, rebuild |
| pre- | before | preview, prepay |
| over- / under- | too much / too little | overcharged, underpaid |
| -less | without | careless, endless |
| -able, -ible | can be done | affordable, accessible |
| -ful | full of | helpful, stressful |
| -ize, -ise | to make | prioritize, modernize |

**A warning.** Word parts give you a first guess, not a final answer. *Inflammable* actually means "burns easily", and *in-* in *indoor* means inside. Always check your guess in the sentence.

## 5. A method for any unknown word

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Decide if it matters.** If the question does not depend on it, move on.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Find the word's job.** Is it a thing (noun), an action (verb) or a description (adjective or adverb)? Look at the words before it: *a* or *the* suggests a noun, *to* or a subject suggests a verb, *very* or *is* suggests a description.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Look for a clue.** Read the sentence before and after. Which of the five clues is there: definition, contrast, example, cause and result, or general sense?

</div>
<div class="step">

**Guess, then test.** Put a simple word in its place and read the sentence again. If it makes sense and fits the whole paragraph, use it and keep going.

</div>
</div>

## 6. A worked example

> The council's response to the flooding was widely criticized as *tardy*: residents waited four days for sandbags, by which time many basements were already full of water.

**Does it matter?** If a question asks *Why were residents unhappy?*, yes.
**Job:** *was ... tardy* describes the response: an adjective.
**Clue:** after the colon comes an example: waiting four days, by which time it was too late. That is cause and result.
**Guess:** *slow* or *late*. Test: "The council's response was widely criticized as slow." It makes sense.

So the answer to *Why were residents unhappy?* is something like *Help arrived too late*, even though the word *late* is not in the text. This is how Part 3 and Part 4 build their paraphrases.

## 7. Traps with words you think you know

Some of the hardest words are ones that look familiar but are used in a different way:

- **Same word, different meaning.** *Reserved* can mean booked (*a reserved table*) or quiet (*a reserved person*). *Heated* can mean warm or angry. Let the sentence decide.
- **Look-alike words.** *Complimentary* (free, or praising) and *complementary* (going well together). *Affect* (to change something) and *effect* (a result).
- **Strength differences.** *Concerned* is weaker than *alarmed*; *dislikes* is weaker than *opposes*. In Part 4, the strength of a word can decide the answer.

<div class="phrases">

- **Definition signals:** *that is*, *in other words*, *which means*, *known as*, *or* (*a yurt, or round tent*)
- **Contrast signals:** *but*, *however*, *unlike*, *rather than*, *instead of*, *although*, *while*
- **Example signals:** *for example*, *such as*, *including*, *like*, a colon (:)
- **Cause and result signals:** *because*, *since*, *so*, *as a result*, *therefore*, *that is why*

</div>

<div class="strategy">

**Replace, do not translate.** When you meet a new word, do not try to find the exact word in your first language. Put a simple English word in its place (*good*, *bad*, *slow*, *a lot*, *a problem*) and keep reading. A rough meaning in English is faster and is almost always enough for the question.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The only way to get better at CELPIP reading is to memorize long word lists."** Word lists help a little, but most of the words in a real test will not be on your list. Guessing from context is a skill you can practise, and it works on every word you will ever meet. The best plan is both: read widely every day, and practise the five clues on the words you do not know.

</div>

## 8. Build your vocabulary between tests

- **Read real Canadian texts daily:** city websites, community newsletters, local news. Each day, pick three new words, guess them from context, then check in a dictionary. You will be right more often than you expect.
- **Keep a word notebook with the sentence**, not just the word. A word is easier to remember in the sentence where you found it.
- **Learn word families together:** *decide, decision, decisive*; *rely, reliable, unreliable*. One root gives you several words.
