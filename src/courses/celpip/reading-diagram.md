**Module 3 · about 20 minutes.** By the end you will know how Reading Part 2 is built, how to read a diagram so that you do not miss the small print, and how to match each gap in the email to the right row, column or note, including the quick sums the part sometimes needs.

<div class="bigidea">In Part 2 the email gives you the person's needs and the diagram gives you the options: each answer is where a need meets a row.</div>

## 1. What Part 2 looks like

Reading Part 2, **Reading to Apply a Diagram**, has **8 questions**. You see two things:

- **A diagram**: a schedule, a price list, a menu, a floor plan, a map, a flyer or a comparison of products or services. It usually has rows and columns, a few icons or short notes, and some small print at the bottom.
- **An email** about the diagram, written by someone who is using it to make a choice. The email has gaps. For each gap you choose from a drop-down list.

After the email there are usually one or two more multiple-choice questions about the diagram or the situation. The part has its own timer; the test screen shows the exact time.

## 2. What it really tests

This part tests something you do every week in Canada: read a timetable, compare prices, choose a plan, find a room on a map. The skill is **combining two sources**. Neither the diagram nor the email alone gives the answer. The email says *what the person needs* (a child's age, a budget, a day, a time limit). The diagram says *what is available*. You find the one option that satisfies all the needs.

**An everyday analogy.** It is like ordering for a friend at a restaurant. Your friend says, "Nothing spicy, no nuts, under fifteen dollars." You look at the menu, cross out the spicy dishes, cross out anything with nuts, and check the prices. The menu is the diagram; your friend's wishes are the email.

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Look at the diagram for 30 seconds.** Read the title, the column headings and the notes under the table. Do not read every cell. Know what kind of information is where: names on the left, times or prices across.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Read the small print on purpose.** Stars, footnotes, *except*, *only*, *extra*, *included*, *waived*, *discount*. Make a quick mental note: "weekend costs more", "7 p.m. is Fridays only".

</div>
<div class="step">

**Read the email one sentence at a time.** For each sentence before a gap, pick out the need: an age, a budget, a day, a size, a reason. Ask: which column does this need point to?

</div>
<div class="step">

**Cross out rows.** Use each need to remove options, as if you were striking lines in the table. Usually one row survives. If two survive, read the email again for the extra need you missed.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Do the sum if there is one, then check the sentence.** Add, subtract or take a percentage. Then put your choice into the gap and read the full sentence to be sure the grammar and meaning fit.

</div>
</div>

## 4. A worked example

Diagram (original):

| Room | Seats | Projector | Price per hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maple | 8 | No | $20 |
| Birch | 12 | Yes | $35 |
| Cedar | 20 | Yes | $50 |

*Rooms booked for 4 hours or more get 1 hour free.*

Email: *We are 10 people, we need to show slides and our budget is small, so I booked the ____ room. We need it for four hours, so the total is ____.*

**Gap 1.** Needs: 10 people, slides, small budget. Maple has only 8 seats and no projector, so cross it out. Birch has 12 seats and a projector. Cedar also has enough seats and a projector, but it costs $15 more an hour, and the budget is small. Only one row meets all three needs: **Birch**.

**Gap 2.** Four hours at $35 is $140. But the small print says 4 hours or more gets 1 hour free, so they pay for 3 hours: **$105**. Many test takers choose $140 because they stop reading at the table. The note is the whole question.

## 5. Question types and traps

| Gap or question type | What you do | Typical trap |
|---|---|---|
| Choose the option (*so I booked the ____*) | Match every need to a row | A row that fits one need but not another |
| A detail from the row (*it starts at ____*) | Read across the chosen row | Reading the row above or below |
| A calculation (*the total is ____*) | Add, subtract, find a percentage or time gap | The discount itself instead of the price after it |
| A reason (*because it is too ____*) | Compare the person with a limit in the diagram | The opposite (old and young, early and late) |
| A note-based fact (*but that one only runs on ____*) | Use the footnote | Forgetting the star or the extra fee |

**The changed-plan trap** works here too. Writers often say *I wanted X, but...* and then choose something else. The gap is about the final choice.

**The two-direction trap** appears with schedules. A ferry or bus table has times in both directions. Always check which column is *leaves* and which is *arrives*, and from which place.

<div class="phrases">

- **Needs:** *we are ___ people*, *it has to be under*, *I can only go on*, *I need it to have*
- **Limits:** *too old / too young*, *not before*, *no later than*, *at least*, *at most*
- **Money:** *included*, *extra*, *waived* (you do not pay), *off* (a discount), *per day / per km / each*
- **Small print:** *only*, *except*, *excluding*, *subject to*, *while supplies last*, a star (*)

</div>

<div class="strategy">

**Treat the email's needs like a checklist.** In your head, list the needs for each gap: "8 years old, drawing, no extra cost". Then go row by row and tick or cross. Never choose a row because it matches the first need you notice. Correct rows match all of them.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Part 2 is about maths, and I am bad at maths."** The sums in Part 2 are small: add two prices, take 10 percent, count the hours between two times. You never need a calculator. Most errors come from missing a note or reading the wrong row, not from arithmetic. Read the small print first and the maths becomes easy.

</div>

## 6. Quick maths you may need

- **Percentages off:** 10% of a number is that number divided by 10. 20% is double that. $80 with 25% off: a quarter of 80 is 20, so $60.
- **Time gaps:** count to the next full hour, then add the rest. 10:15 to 3:30 is 45 minutes to 11:00, then 4 hours 30 minutes: 5 hours 15 minutes.
- **Per unit costs:** 30 km at $0.49 is about 30 times 50 cents, which is about $15.
- **Round when the options are far apart.** If the options are $15, $35 and $60, an estimate is enough.

## 7. Practise

Each round shows one diagram and its email: a children's program list, a truck rental price list, a ferry schedule and a gym membership table. On a phone, scroll the table sideways if it is wide.

If you get a question wrong, go back to the diagram and find the exact cell or note that decides it. Say out loud which need you missed. That habit fixes Part 2 faster than any other.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 2 has 8 questions: a diagram plus an email with gaps, and one or two more questions.
- The email gives the needs; the diagram gives the options; the answer meets every need.
- Read the notes and stars under the diagram before the email: many questions come from them.
- Check direction and row in schedules, and watch for the changed plan after *but*.
- The maths is small: percentages, totals and time gaps; estimate when the options are far apart.

</div>
