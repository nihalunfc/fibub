**Module 3 · about 20 minutes.** By the end you will know exactly what Reading Part 1 shows on the screen, how to read a personal or work email for the details that the questions ask about, and how to complete the reply without falling for the usual traps.

<div class="bigidea">Part 1 is a conversation on paper: understand what the first writer wants and decides, and the reply almost fills itself.</div>

## 1. What Part 1 looks like

Reading Part 1, **Reading Correspondence**, has **11 questions**. On the left of the screen you see a message: a letter or email between friends, neighbours, family or coworkers. It is usually four or five paragraphs long. On the right you see questions about it with four choices each.

After those questions, you see a **reply** to the message, written by the person who received it. The reply has gaps. For each gap you choose a word or phrase from a drop-down list. The reply only makes sense if you understood the first message, so the gaps are a second test of the same reading.

The part has its own timer (the test screen shows the exact time). You can move up and down between questions until the timer ends, so you can answer in any order.

## 2. What it really tests

Part 1 tests the reading you do in daily life: understanding why someone is writing, what has happened to them, what they plan to do, what they ask you to do, and how they feel. The topics are ordinary: a move, a new job, a broken car, a family event, a change of plans at work.

The questions focus on four things:

- **Purpose:** why the person is writing.
- **Facts and details:** dates, places, prices, reasons.
- **Decisions and plans:** what the writer finally chooses, often after changing their mind.
- **Feelings and attitude:** happy, worried, disappointed, relieved, and whether that feeling changes.

**An everyday analogy.** Imagine a friend leaves you a long voice message, and then you must call back. You would not repeat every word; you would answer the main points: "Sorry about your job, great news about the course, and yes, my brother wants the car." The reply in Part 1 is that call back. If you know the main points, you know what the reply needs.

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Skim the whole message first (about one minute).** Find who is writing to whom, their relationship (friend, boss, neighbour) and the reason for writing. Look for *I'm writing to*, *I have news*, *I wanted to ask*.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Note the turning points.** Each paragraph usually has one topic. Inside it, watch for *but*, *however*, *instead*, *actually*, *at first*. What comes after these words is usually the true situation and the source of a question.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Answer the message questions in order.** The questions usually follow the order of the text. Read the question, scan for its paragraph, read the whole sentence and choose the option that paraphrases it.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Read the whole reply before filling any gap.** The reply also follows the order of the first message. Each gap answers one point. Ask: which point is this sentence about?

</div>
<div class="step">

**Fill each gap with meaning first, grammar second.** Find the fact in the first message, then check that your choice fits the sentence (a verb after *to*, a noun after *the*). Read the sentence once more with your choice in place.

</div>
</div>

## 4. A worked example

Here is a short original message and reply.

> Hi Leah, guess what? I finally passed my driving test! I failed the first time in June because I was too nervous on the highway, but this time the examiner said I was calm and careful. I was planning to buy a used car right away, but my uncle says he will give me his old minivan in the spring when he retires. So for now I will keep taking the bus. Hugs, Nadia

**Question:** *How will Nadia get around this winter?*
A) In her uncle's minivan
B) In a used car she is buying
C) By bus
D) Her uncle will drive her

**Thinking:** the question is about *now, this winter*. Scan for the plan. "I was planning to buy a used car right away, **but**..." so B is the early plan. The minivan comes "in the spring", so A is the wrong time. "**So for now** I will keep taking the bus." The answer is **C**. Option D is invented: nobody says the uncle will drive her.

Now the reply:

> Hi Nadia, congratulations! I know how nervous you were on the ____ last time.

Choices: *highway* / *bus* / *test centre* / *minivan*. She failed "because I was too nervous on the highway". The answer is **highway**. Every option is a word from her message; only one fits the fact.

## 5. Question types and traps

| You see | What it asks | Typical trap |
|---|---|---|
| *Why is X writing?* | The main purpose | The polite opening line, or a small extra at the end |
| *What happened to X?* | A past event | An event that happened to someone else in the message |
| *What will X probably do?* | The final decision | The first plan, before *but* or *instead* |
| *How does X feel?* | Feeling now | The feeling *at first*, which has changed |
| *What does X ask the reader to do?* | A request | Something the writer will do, not the reader |
| A gap in the reply | One point of the message | A word from the message used about the wrong fact |

**The changing-feeling trap** deserves a special note. Writers in Part 1 often describe a journey: *At first I was upset, but now I'm excited.* Questions with *now* or *currently* want the second feeling.

**The who-does-what trap** is also common. Messages mention several people: a sister, a boss, a cousin. Before choosing, ask: is this option about the right person?

<div class="phrases">

- **Purpose:** *I'm writing to let you know*, *I have some news*, *I wanted to ask you a favour*
- **Change of plan:** *I was going to... but*, *instead*, *on second thought*, *in the end*, *so for now*
- **Preference:** *I'd rather*, *I'd prefer to*, *it makes more sense to*
- **Request:** *Could you*, *Would you mind*, *I was wondering if you could*
- **Feeling:** *I was thrilled / relieved / disappointed / worried*, *at first... but now*

</div>

<div class="strategy">

**Make a three-word map of each paragraph.** As you skim, give each paragraph a tiny label in your head: "bakery closing", "college money", "selling car". When a question or a gap comes, you know which paragraph to jump to. This saves more time than reading faster.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The reply is a new text, so I should read it like a new passage."** The reply is not new. It answers the first message point by point, in the same order. Treat it as a checklist of the first message's main points, and each gap becomes a detail you have already found.

</div>

## 6. Practise

Each round shows one message and reply with nine questions: five about the message and four gaps. Work through the method, then read the explanation for every answer, including the ones you got right.

After each round, check which trap caught you. If you often choose the early plan, practise circling (in your head) every *but* and *instead* while you skim.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 1 has 11 questions: questions about a message, then gaps in a reply.
- Skim first for who, to whom and why; then label each paragraph with a few words.
- Watch the turning words (*but*, *instead*, *at first*, *so for now*): the answer is usually after them.
- The reply follows the first message point by point; each gap matches one fact.
- Check the right person, the right time and the right feeling before you choose.

</div>
