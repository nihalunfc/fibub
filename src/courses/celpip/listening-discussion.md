**Module 2 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will know how Part 5 works, how to keep three speakers apart, and how to follow agreement and disagreement in a group. You will practise on three original discussions with 24 questions.

<div class="bigidea">Part 5 is a meeting with three people. The questions ask who suggested what, who agreed, who objected and why, and what the group decided, so keep one column of notes per person.</div>

## 1. What Part 5 looks like

Part 5, **Listening to a Discussion**, is one of the two longest listening parts. You see and hear a short scene with **three people** discussing something: coworkers planning an event, neighbours solving a building problem, friends planning a trip. It is often presented as a **short video**, so you can see who is speaking. Then you answer **8 questions**, each with four options.

- About two to three minutes of discussion.
- Heard (and watched) **once**; questions after.
- 8 questions, tied with Part 1 for the most in the section.

In this course the practice plays voices only, without video. That is harder than the test, which makes it good training: if you can keep three voices apart by ear, the video in the test will feel easy.

## 2. What it really tests

Part 5 tests whether you can follow a **group conversation** where ideas build on each other. You need to know:

- **Who** made each suggestion.
- **Who agrees** and **who objects**, and **why**.
- How the discussion **develops**: an idea is changed, combined or replaced.
- What the group **decides**, and **who will do** each task.
- Sometimes the **attitude** of one speaker (optimistic, unsure, persuaded).

**Everyday analogy.** Think of three friends choosing a restaurant. Ali wants pizza, Bea says pizza is too heavy, Carl suggests sushi, Ali complains it is expensive, Bea finds a sushi deal, and Ali agrees. If someone later asks "Who was against pizza?" or "What changed Ali’s mind?", you need to remember the people, not just the food. That is Part 5.

## 3. The method for Part 5: the three-column grid

<div class="steps">

<div class="step">

**Draw three columns before the audio.** Put each speaker’s initial at the top as soon as you hear their names (or see their faces). If you miss a name, use "M1", "W", "M2".

</div>
<div class="step">

**Every idea goes under its speaker.** `F: weekday daytime?` `G: meeting room, $ on food`. Mark the first speaker to suggest something with a star.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Mark reactions with symbols.** Under the person reacting: `✗ meeting room (feels like work)` or `✓ bowling (kids can come)`. Draw a quick arrow from a reaction to the idea it answers.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Mark changes of mind.** When someone moves from ✗ to ✓, write what changed them: `V: ✗ $12k → ✓ free install`.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Box the decision and tasks.** At the end, box the plan and write each person’s job under their column: `L: send survey`, `F: check dates`.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Situation line: *Listen to three students planning a group presentation.*

> **Jae:** Our presentation is on Thursday. Should we split the topic into three parts and each do one?
> **Ruth:** That’s fastest, but last time we did that, the parts didn’t connect at all. The teacher took marks off.
> **Felipe:** What if we split it, but meet on Tuesday to practise the whole thing together?
> **Jae:** Tuesday I work until six.
> **Felipe:** Then Tuesday at seven, at the library. Online won’t work; my internet is terrible.
> **Ruth:** Seven is fine with me. And I’ll make the slides so they all look the same.
> **Jae:** Great. I’ll book a library room.

Grid:

| J | R | F |
|---|---|---|
| ★ split in 3 | ✗ split alone (no connection, lost marks) | ★ split + practise Tue |
| ✗ Tue (work till 6) | ✓ 7 | Tue 7 library; ✗ online (internet) |
| task: book room | task: slides same look | |

*Why does Ruth object to Jae’s first idea?* Because last time the parts did not connect and they lost marks.
*Who suggests practising together?* **Felipe.** A trap option would be "Ruth", who raised the problem but did not propose the fix.
*Why will they not meet online?* Felipe’s internet is poor.
*What will Jae do?* Book a library room. A trap would be "make the slides", which is Ruth’s job.

## 5. Question types and traps in Part 5

| Question | Trap |
|---|---|
| Who suggested X? | The person who first raised the problem, or who agreed first |
| Why does A object to B’s idea? | A reason given by the third person |
| What changed C’s mind? | Something said earlier, before the change |
| What does the group decide? | An idea that was discussed but replaced |
| What will B do? | A task that belongs to another speaker |
| How does A feel about the forecast / plan? | A feeling that fits the topic but not the speaker |

**Listen for reference words.** In groups, people say "I like *that*", "*Your* idea", "*Here*?" or "*Free*? Then I have no objection." These short words connect a reaction to an earlier idea. When you hear one, ask yourself: *which* idea is that?

<div class="strategy">

**The objection-and-condition rule.** In Part 5, people rarely say a simple yes. They agree **with a condition**: "Okay, as long as we still cook over a fire one night", "Then I have no objection, as long as the door gets fixed too". These conditions are a favourite question. Whenever you hear *as long as* or *only if*, write the condition under that speaker’s column.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The video will tell me the answers."** The video shows people and the setting, which helps you know who is talking. It does not show the reasons, objections or decisions, which is what the questions ask. Glance at the video to match faces to voices in the first few seconds, then keep your eyes mostly on your notes.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- **Suggesting:** *what if, how about, we could, why don’t we, my idea is*
- **Agreeing:** *I like that, good point, I’m in, I’m sold, agreed, deal, fair point*
- **Objecting:** *honestly, I’m not sure, the only issue is, that’s a lot, before we...*
- **Agreeing with a condition:** *as long as, only if, okay, but, then I have no objection*
- **Assigning tasks:** *can you, I’ll, you’re good at, would you mind*

</div>

## 6. Practice: three discussions

Draw a three-column grid on paper for each discussion. Listen once, then answer all eight questions. The voice names in the player help you match each line to a person.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 5: **three people, often a short video, 8 questions after it**, heard once.
- Use a **three-column grid**: each idea and reaction goes under the person who said it.
- Track suggestions, objections with reasons, changes of mind and the final plan.
- Agreement often comes **with a condition** ("as long as..."): note it.
- Tasks at the end belong to specific people; do not mix them up.

</div>
