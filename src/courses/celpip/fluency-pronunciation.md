**Module 5 · about 25 minutes.** By the end you will know the six habits that make speech easy to follow, how to check each one with your own recordings, and you will have practised read-aloud tasks and 38 drill items.

<div class="bigidea">You do not need a perfect accent; you need to be easy to follow. Stress the right syllables and words, link and group your words, pause in sensible places, and keep going when you slip.</div>

## 1. What Listenability means

In all eight speaking tasks, one of the four rating areas is **Listenability**. It covers:

- **rhythm, pronunciation and intonation**;
- **pauses, interjections and self-correction** (how you hesitate and fix mistakes);
- **grammar and sentence structure**;
- **variety of sentence types**.

Notice what is *not* on that list: a Canadian accent. Many high scorers have a clear accent from their first language. What they have is **rhythm** that English listeners expect, so their meaning reaches the listener without effort.

**Everyday analogy.** Think of a GPS voice. You understand it instantly not because of its accent but because it stresses the important word ("turn **LEFT** in **two hundred** metres") and pauses between instructions. That is Listenability.

This lesson covers six habits. Work on **one habit a week** while you practise the speaking tasks in this module.

## 2. Habit 1: word stress

In every English word with two or more syllables, **one syllable is stronger**: longer, louder and a little higher. If you stress the wrong syllable, listeners may not recognise the word even if every sound is correct. *a-PART-ment*, not *A-part-ment*; *de-VEL-op*, not *DE-vel-op*.

Useful patterns (they have exceptions, so check a dictionary when unsure):

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| Most two-syllable nouns and adjectives: first syllable | **TA**-ble, **HAP**-py, **WIN**-dow |
| Most two-syllable verbs: second syllable | re-**LAX**, de-**CIDE**, be-**GIN** |
| Same word, noun or verb | a **PRE**-sent / to pre-**SENT**; a **RE**-cord / to re-**CORD** |
| Words ending in *-tion, -sion, -ic, -ical*: syllable just before | in-for-**MA**-tion, de-**CI**-sion, e-co-**NOM**-ic |
| Words ending in *-ity, -ify*: two before | com-**MU**-ni-ty, i-**DEN**-ti-fy |

**How to check yourself.** Most free online dictionaries show stress with a mark (ˈ) before the stressed syllable and play the word aloud. Make a list of the words you use often in answers (*neighbourhood, recommend, comfortable, environment*) and check each one once.

## 3. Habit 2: sentence stress and rhythm

In a sentence, **content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, negatives like *can’t*) are stressed. **Function words** (*a, the, to, of, and, can, is, for*) are short and weak. That is what gives English its "da-DUM-da-DUM" rhythm.

> I **think** we should **take** the **bus** to the **game**.

The weak words often shrink: *to* becomes "tuh", *and* becomes "n", *can* becomes "kn". If you give every word the same weight, you sound slow and robotic, and the important words get lost.

**Contrastive stress.** To show a contrast or correction, put extra stress on the new word: *"It’s not the **red** one, it’s the **blue** one."* This is very useful in Task 5 (comparing) and Task 8 (describing).

## 4. Habit 3: linking

Fluent speakers join words together. Three common links:

- **Consonant + vowel:** the consonant moves to the next word. *turn off* sounds like "tur-noff"; *an apple* like "a-napple".
- **Same consonant:** say it once, a bit longer. *bus stop* sounds like "bu-stop"; *big game* like "bi-game".
- **Vowel + vowel:** a small *w* or *y* sound joins them. *go out* sounds like "go-wout"; *I agree* like "I-yagree".

You do not need to master all of these. Even linking the consonant + vowel pairs makes your speech noticeably smoother.

## 5. Habit 4: pausing in thought groups

A **thought group** is a short chunk of words that belong together, usually three to seven words. Pause **between** groups, never **inside** one.

> When I moved to Halifax / I didn’t know anyone, / so I joined / a community soccer team.

Pausing at the slashes sounds natural. Pausing in the middle of "a community soccer team" ("a com... munity...") makes the listener lose the meaning. Pauses at commas, before *and, but, because, so, which*, and after time phrases (*last summer, / I...*) are natural places.

**Intonation tip.** Your voice usually **falls** at the end of a statement and a *wh-* question, and **rises** on a yes/no question and on items in a list before the last one (*bikes ↗, canoes ↗, and a dock ↘*).

## 6. Habit 5: fillers and thinking time

Everyone hesitates. The problem is not hesitating; it is **long silences** and **constant "um... uh..."**. Replace them with short phrases that buy time and sound natural:

- *Well, let me think...*
- *The way I see it...*
- *What I mean is...*
- *Let me give you an example...*
- *How can I put this...*

Use them occasionally, not in every sentence. Two or three in a 60-second answer is plenty.

## 7. Habit 6: self-correction

When you make a mistake, fix it **fast and move on**:

> *"Last year he go... he went to Vancouver for work."*

A quick repair shows the rater that you know the correct form. Do **not** stop, apologise or restart the whole answer, and do not correct tiny slips that do not change the meaning; that breaks your rhythm more than the mistake did.

## 8. The self-recording method

You can be your own accent coach with a phone and ten minutes a day.

<div class="steps">

<div class="step">

**1. Record one answer.** Use any speaking task in this module and its timer. Tick "Record myself"; the recording stays on this device.

</div>
<div class="step">

**2. Listen once for rhythm only.** Do the important words stand out? Do the small words shrink? Mark one sentence that sounded flat.

</div>
<div class="step">

**3. Listen once for pauses.** Where did you stop? Inside a thought group or between groups? Count your "um"s.

</div>
<div class="step">

**4. Fix one thing and re-record.** Choose a single habit, such as stressing content words or pausing at commas, and record the same answer again. Compare the two.

</div>
<div class="step">

**5. Shadow a model.** Read a model answer from Lessons 5.6 to 5.9 aloud at the same time as you imagine a fluent speaker, copying the stress and pauses. Then record yourself reading it alone.

</div>
</div>

## 9. Read-aloud practice

Before each recording, mark the stressed words and put slashes between thought groups. Then read the passage at a natural speed. The timer gives 30 seconds to mark the text and 60 seconds to read.

**A marked version of read-aloud 1** (stressed words in bold, thought groups with slashes):

When I **first** ar**rived** in **Win**nipeg, / I was sur**prised** / by how **friend**ly people were. / My **neigh**bour, / an **old**er man named **Ger**ald, / came **o**ver on my **sec**ond **day** / with a **loaf** of **home**made **bread**. / He **showed** me / where the **near**est **gro**cery store was, / ex**plained** the re**cy**cling **sched**ule, / and **e**ven **lent** me a **snow** shovel / when the **first storm** came in No**vem**ber. / Be**cause** of him, / the **ci**ty felt like **home** / much **fast**er than I ex**pect**ed.

</div>

**Why this helps your score.** Clear word and sentence stress make your *pronunciation and rhythm* easy to follow; pausing between thought groups improves your *pauses*; and reading model sentences aloud trains you to produce a *variety of sentence types* automatically when you speak freely.

<div class="strategy">

**Weekly self-check (listen to one recording).**

- [ ] My long words had the stress on the right syllable (I checked any I was unsure of).
- [ ] Content words stood out; small words were short and quick.
- [ ] I linked at least some consonant + vowel pairs (*turn off, an apple*).
- [ ] I paused between thought groups, not in the middle of them.
- [ ] My voice fell at the end of statements and rose on yes/no questions.
- [ ] I used thinking phrases instead of long silences, and fewer than five "um"s.
- [ ] I fixed slips quickly without stopping or apologising.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Speaking faster sounds more fluent."** Speed is not fluency. Racing through an answer squeezes out the pauses and stress that help the listener, and it usually increases mistakes. A steady pace with clear thought groups sounds far more fluent than a fast, flat stream of words.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- **Thinking time:** *Well, let me think... The way I see it... What I mean is... How can I put this...*
- **Self-correction:** *I mean...; sorry, I mean...; or rather...; let me rephrase that...*
- **Moving on:** *Anyway...; So, as I was saying...; The main point is...*

<div class="keypoints">

- Listenability rewards being **easy to follow**, not having a Canadian accent.
- **Word stress:** one strong syllable per word; check the words you use often.
- **Sentence stress:** stress content words, shrink function words; use contrastive stress to compare.
- **Link** words and **pause between thought groups**, never inside one.
- Replace long silences with short thinking phrases, and fix slips **quickly** without stopping.
- Record, listen for one habit, fix it and re-record: ten minutes a day is enough.

</div>
