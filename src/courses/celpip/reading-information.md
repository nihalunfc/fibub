**Module 3 · about 20 minutes.** By the end you will know how Reading Part 3 works, how to give each paragraph a quick label, how to spot a paraphrase, and how to decide with confidence when the answer is E, not given.

<div class="bigidea">Part 3 is a matching game: each statement is a paraphrase of one paragraph's idea, so your job is to know what each paragraph is about and to recognise the same idea in new words.</div>

## 1. What Part 3 looks like

Reading Part 3, **Reading for Information**, has **9 questions**. You read a factual text, such as a news feature, a guide or a short report, divided into four paragraphs labelled **A, B, C and D**. Next to it is a list of statements. For each statement you choose from a drop-down list:

- **A, B, C or D**: the paragraph that contains this information, or
- **E**: the information is **not given** in any of the paragraphs.

The text is longer than in Parts 1 and 2, and the language is a little more formal. Topics are about Canadian life: community projects, parks, health, technology, local history, the environment. The part has its own timer; the test screen shows the exact time.

## 2. What it really tests

Part 3 tests two skills at the same time:

- **Understanding the main idea of each paragraph.** Each paragraph has a job: the background, the benefits, the problems, the advice, the result.
- **Recognising paraphrase.** The statements almost never copy the text. *Paid for mostly by a provincial grant* becomes *Most of the money came from the government*. *Cell service is weak* becomes *Phones may not work*.

**An everyday analogy.** Picture a grocery store with four aisles: fruit, bread, dairy and cleaning products. Someone asks where to find "something to spread on toast". You do not need to read every label; you know butter is in dairy. A statement is the shopping request, and each paragraph is an aisle with a clear sign. Once the signs are clear in your head, matching is fast. E is the item the store does not sell at all.

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Label each paragraph (2 to 3 minutes).** Read each paragraph fairly quickly and give it a label of two or three words: *A: rules differ*, *B: benefits*, *C: problems for wild bees*, *D: how to help*. This is the most important step.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Read one statement and find its key idea.** Ignore small words. Ask: what is this statement really about? A benefit, a rule, a number, a warning, a result?

</div>
<div class="step">

**Go to the paragraph whose label matches.** Then scan inside it for the exact sentence. Look for synonyms, not identical words: *booked* for *reserved*, *young people* for *teenagers*.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Check that every part of the statement is supported.** A statement is only a match if the paragraph says all of it. If the paragraph says "some", and the statement says "all", or if one part is missing, keep looking.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Decide on E only after checking.** If no paragraph supports the full statement, or the text says the opposite, choose E. Do not choose E just because you could not find it quickly.

</div>
</div>

## 4. A worked example

Here are two short original paragraphs:

> **A.** The town pool, built in 1978, closed last spring after inspectors found cracks in its walls. Repairs would cost more than building a new pool.
>
> **B.** The council has approved a new aquatic centre beside the high school. It will include a lap pool, a shallow pool for children and a hot tub. Construction begins in May and should take two years.

Statement 1: *Fixing the old facility was judged too expensive.*
Key idea: cost of repairs. **Paragraph A**: "Repairs would cost more than building a new pool." Fixing = repairs; facility = pool. Match.

Statement 2: *The new centre will have something for young families.*
Key idea: features of the new centre. **Paragraph B**: "a shallow pool for children". Young families = children. Match.

Statement 3: *The new centre will charge lower fees than the old pool.*
Key idea: prices. A talks about repair costs, not fees. B lists features and dates, not prices. Nothing supports it: **E**.

Notice how statement 3 is tempting: it is about the new centre (paragraph B's topic) and uses a money idea (paragraph A's topic). Mixing two paragraphs is the favourite way to build an E statement.

## 5. Types of statements and traps

| Statement looks like | What is going on | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Same idea, new words | A true paraphrase | Match it to the paragraph |
| Copies a word from one paragraph, but the idea is different | Word-match trap | Check the meaning, not the word |
| Mixes a topic from one paragraph with a detail from another | Classic E statement | Look for a single sentence that supports all of it |
| Says the opposite of the text | E (the text does not give it as a fact) | Choose E |
| Sounds reasonable, general knowledge | Possibly E | Only the text counts, not what you know |

**The general-knowledge trap** is worth a warning. A statement like *Bees are important for farming* sounds true, and you may know it is true. But if no paragraph says it, the answer is E. Part 3 tests reading, not knowledge of the world.

**The same-paragraph-twice question.** Paragraphs can be the answer more than once. In a set of nine statements, one paragraph might be used three times and another once. Do not try to balance the letters.

<div class="phrases">

- **Common paraphrase pairs:** *reserve / book*, *purchase / buy*, *assist / help*, *reduce / cut*, *increase / grow, climb*, *require / need, must*, *permit / allow*, *the province / the government*
- **Words that limit a claim:** *some*, *most*, *a few*, *several*, *mostly*, *only*, *usually*
- **Paragraph jobs:** background, benefits, problems or criticism, solutions or advice, results, the future

</div>

<div class="strategy">

**Write your labels on the whiteboard or paper you get.** If your test centre gives you something to write on, jot down the four labels as A, B, C, D with two words each. Glancing at four short labels is much faster than rereading four paragraphs nine times.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"There is usually only one E, so if I already chose E once, the next one must be a paragraph."** There is no fixed number of E answers. Some sets have none, some have two or more. Judge each statement on its own evidence.

</div>

## 6. Practise

Each round shows one text with four paragraphs and nine statements. Type a single letter: A, B, C, D, or E for not given. Before you start, label the paragraphs.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="drill" data-set="rp3" data-n="9"><figcaption>Reading Part 3 practice: rooftop bees, winter camping and a library makerspace. Type one letter per answer.</figcaption></figure>

When you miss a question, ask which kind of mistake it was: did you match a word instead of an idea, miss a limiting word such as *most*, or choose a paragraph because the general topic fitted? Keep a tally; it shows you what to train next.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 3 has 9 questions: match each statement to paragraph A, B, C or D, or choose E (not given).
- Label each paragraph with two or three words first; it makes every later question faster.
- Statements paraphrase the text; look for the same idea in new words, not the same words.
- Every part of the statement must be supported; mixed paragraphs and opposites mean E.
- Only the text counts, not general knowledge, and there is no fixed number of E answers.

</div>
