**Module 3 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will have one reading method that works in all four CELPIP reading parts, you will know how to share out your time, and you will have practised skimming, scanning and checking on six short Canadian texts.

<div class="bigidea">Good test readers do not read harder; they read in the right order: big idea first, then the question, then the exact place, then a careful check.</div>

## 1. What CELPIP Reading looks like

Reading is the second section of the test, right after Listening. It has four parts and 38 scored questions:

| Part | Name | Questions | What you read |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reading Correspondence | 11 | A letter or email, then a reply with gaps to complete |
| 2 | Reading to Apply a Diagram | 8 | A diagram (schedule, price list, map, flyer) and an email about it |
| 3 | Reading for Information | 9 | A text in paragraphs A to D; you decide where each statement comes from |
| 4 | Reading for Viewpoints | 10 | An article with opinions, then a reader's comment with gaps |

The whole section takes **43 to 56 minutes**. The range exists because some versions include a few unscored trial questions, and you cannot tell which ones they are. Each part has its own timer in the corner of the screen, and you can move between questions inside a part until its time ends. All answers are chosen from a list (multiple choice or a drop-down menu), and **wrong answers cost nothing**. Never leave a question empty.

## 2. What the section really tests

CELPIP Reading is not a vocabulary contest. It tests whether you can do what adults in Canada do every day: understand an email from a friend or a landlord, use a bus schedule, find facts in a news article and follow an argument. Researchers who study second-language reading, such as Grabe (2009), describe two kinds of skill working together:

- **Fast skills**: recognising common words instantly, reading in chunks, guessing what comes next.
- **Careful skills**: noticing small words that change meaning (*not*, *only*, *except*, *instead*), linking a pronoun to the person it means, and seeing who holds which opinion.

The method below uses the fast skills to find the right place and the careful skills to choose the right answer.

**An everyday analogy.** Think of looking for a friend's house in a new neighbourhood. You do not read every house number on every street. First you check the map for the right street (skim). Then you drive along that street watching only the numbers (scan). When you see the right number, you slow down and check the name on the mailbox (check). Reading in CELPIP works the same way.

## 3. The method: skim, question, scan, check

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Skim (30 to 60 seconds).** Read the title, the first sentence of each paragraph and the last line. Ask: what kind of text is this, who wrote it, to whom, and why? Do not stop at unknown words.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Read the question, not the options.** Underline (in your head) the key word: *when*, *why*, *who*, *what does she ask*. Decide what kind of answer you need: a time, a reason, a person, an action.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Scan for the place.** Move your eyes quickly looking for the key word or a word with the same meaning. Names, numbers and capital letters are easy to spot. Stop when you find the right sentence.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Check the whole sentence, then the options.** Read the sentence before and after. Now look at the options and remove the ones that are wrong, too wide or about a different person or time. Choose the one that says the same idea in different words.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Decide and move on.** If two options still look possible, choose the better one and go to the next question. Come back only if time is left.

</div>
</div>

## 4. A worked example

Read this short text:

> Hi Aisha, thanks for the invitation to your housewarming. I would love to come, but my parents land at the airport that evening, so I can only stop by for an hour around four. Should I bring anything? I make a good samosa! Ramon

Question: *What time will Ramon probably arrive?*
A) In the evening
B) Around four
C) After his parents land
D) He will not come

**Skim:** a reply to an invitation. Ramon is coming, but with a problem.
**Question:** *what time* means I am looking for a time.
**Scan:** two time words appear: "that evening" and "around four".
**Check:** "that evening" is when his parents land, not when he arrives. "I can only stop by for an hour around four." The answer is **B**.

Notice the trap. Option A uses a real word from the text ("evening"), but it belongs to a different event. Option C mixes two facts into something the text never says. Option D is the opposite of "I would love to come". This pattern repeats across all four parts.

## 5. Common question types and traps

| Question type | Example | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | *Why is she writing?* | Phrases such as *I am writing to*, *I wanted to ask*; usually not the first polite line |
| Detail | *How much does it cost?* | Numbers, names, dates; read the whole phrase around them |
| Inference | *What will he probably do?* | The final decision, often after *so*, *in the end*, *I think I will* |
| True / not true | *Which is true?* | Words that make an option too wide: *always*, *every*, *all*, *never* |
| Gap in a reply | *I'm glad you ___.* | Grammar fit plus meaning that matches the first message |

The four classic traps:

1. **Same word, different meaning or event.** The option repeats a word from the text but links it to the wrong person, time or thing.
2. **The early plan that changes.** The writer first considers one idea, then says *but*, *instead* or *actually* and chooses another.
3. **True but not asked.** The option is a correct fact from the text, but it does not answer this question.
4. **Too wide.** The text says *most* or *on weekdays*; the option says *all* or *every day*.

<div class="phrases">

- **Purpose:** *I am writing to / about*, *The reason I am contacting you*, *I was wondering if*
- **Change:** *however*, *but*, *instead*, *actually*, *on second thought*, *in the end*
- **Reason:** *because*, *since*, *as*, *due to*, *that is why*
- **Limit:** *only*, *except*, *unless*, *not until*, *at least*
- **Example (often not the answer):** *for example*, *such as*, *like*

</div>

## 6. Managing your time

Each part has its own clock, so you cannot borrow minutes from one part to use in another. A simple plan for any part:

- **First 10 to 15 percent of the time:** skim the text.
- **Most of the time:** answer in order, one question at a time, using the method.
- **Last minute:** make sure every question has an answer, even a guess.

If a question takes more than about a minute and a half, choose your best guess and move on. One hard question is worth the same as one easy question.

<div class="strategy">

**Answer what the question asks, not what you remember.** After skimming, you will "know" the text, and it is tempting to answer from memory. Always scan back and find the sentence. Most wrong answers come from memory, not from weak English.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I must understand every word before I answer."** You do not. Even strong readers meet unknown words in CELPIP. Most questions can be answered from the sentences around them, and lesson 3.6 shows how to work out a word from context. Stopping to puzzle over one word costs time that you need for the questions.

</div>

## 7. How to practise outside the test

- Read one short real Canadian text every day: a city notice, a store flyer, a community newsletter, a local news story. Give yourself 60 seconds to say its purpose out loud, then find three details.
- When you get a drill question wrong, write down **which trap** caught you (same word, early plan, true but not asked, too wide). After a week you will see your own pattern.
- Read on a screen, not only on paper. The real test is on a computer, and scrolling changes how you find things.

<div class="keypoints">

- Reading has 4 parts and 38 scored questions in about 43 to 56 minutes; each part has its own timer.
- Use one method everywhere: skim, read the question, scan for the place, check the whole sentence.
- Right answers usually say the same idea in new words; copied words are often a trap.
- Watch for four traps: same word with a different meaning, the early plan, true but not asked, too wide.
- Wrong answers cost nothing: answer every question, and move on after about a minute and a half.
