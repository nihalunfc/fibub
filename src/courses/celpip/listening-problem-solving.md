**Module 2 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will know how Part 1 is built, how to keep track of a problem as it is solved, and you will have answered 24 questions on three original conversations.

<div class="bigidea">Part 1 is a story with a plot: a problem appears, ideas are tried and rejected, and a plan is chosen. If you track the plot (problem, ideas, reasons, final plan), the questions answer themselves.</div>

## 1. What Part 1 looks like

Part 1, **Listening to Problem Solving**, is the first and longest listening part. You hear **one conversation**, usually between two people who know each other: roommates, coworkers, family members, a customer and a staff member. Together they face a problem and look for a solution.

- The conversation is split into **three sections**. After each section the recording stops and you answer **two or three questions**. Then the next section plays.
- There are **8 questions** in total, all multiple choice with four options.
- The conversation plays **once**. You cannot go back to an earlier section.
- The test screen shows the exact time; the questions in each section have a short time limit, so answer and move on.

Before the audio, the screen usually shows a picture of the situation and a line such as "Listen to a conversation between two roommates." Use it to predict.

## 2. What it really tests

Part 1 checks whether you can follow a conversation as it **develops**: what the problem is, what each person suggests, why an idea is accepted or rejected, and what they decide in the end. Typical questions:

- What is the problem? Why is it a problem for one of them?
- What does the man suggest? Why does the woman reject it?
- What is true about the solution (cost, time, place)?
- What will they probably do next?

**Everyday analogy.** Part 1 is like watching two friends try to fix a flat tire. First you learn the problem (the tire is flat and they have a wedding in an hour). Then they try things (the spare is also flat, the garage is closed), and finally they find a fix (a neighbour lends them a pump). If someone asks you later what happened, you remember the story, not every sentence. That story is what Part 1 asks about.

## 3. The method for Part 1

<div class="steps">

<div class="step">

**Before section 1: draw the plot frame.** Write three short lines on your paper: `P:` (problem), `Ideas:` and `Plan:`. Add the two speakers’ initials.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Section 1: catch the problem and why it matters.** The first section almost always sets up the problem and the reason it is urgent or difficult ("the order is Saturday", "my exams are in April"). Note both.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Section 2: list ideas with ✓ or ✗ and the reason.** Each new idea gets a short note and the reason it fails or works: `church ✗ booked`, `pizza oven ✗ too hot`, `Pierre’s regular oven ?`.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Section 3: catch the final plan and who does what.** Listen for decision words (*okay, let’s, I’ll, then we*) and for jobs: who will call, book, pay or wait.

</div>
<div class="step">

**After each section: answer from the frame.** Answer the questions at once, while the section is fresh. Then clear your mind for the next section; earlier answers cannot be changed.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Situation line: *Listen to two coworkers organising a training day.* This is an original mini version with one section and two questions.

> **Nora:** The trainer just cancelled. She’s sick, and the training is tomorrow morning.
> **Ali:** Can we push it to next week?
> **Nora:** Not really. The new safety rules start on Monday, and everyone has to be trained before then.
> **Ali:** Then what about the online version? It’s the same course, just on video.
> **Nora:** That works for the office staff. But the warehouse team has to practise with the equipment, so they need someone in person.
> **Ali:** Okay. Office staff do it online tomorrow, and I’ll ask our supervisor, who is certified, to run the warehouse session on Friday.

Notes: `P: trainer sick, training tmrw. Must be before Mon (rules)`. `Ideas: next wk ✗ (Mon) / online ✓ office, ✗ warehouse (equipment)`. `Plan: office online tmrw; supervisor → warehouse Fri`.

**Question 1.** *Why can they not move the training to next week?*
A. The trainer is busy next week. B. New rules start on Monday. C. The warehouse is closed. D. The office staff are on holiday.
Thinking: my note says "✗ (Mon)" next to "next wk". Nora said the new safety rules start Monday. **B.** Option A uses "trainer", but the trainer is sick, not busy next week.

**Question 2.** *What will happen to the warehouse team?*
A. They will do the course online tomorrow. B. They will be trained by their supervisor on Friday. C. They will skip the training. D. They will wait for the first trainer.
Thinking: online was ✗ for the warehouse. The plan line says supervisor, Friday. **B.** Option A is true for the office staff, which is a classic "true but about someone else" trap.

## 5. Traps in Part 1

| Trap | What it looks like | Defence |
|---|---|---|
| The first idea | An option describes the first suggestion, which is later rejected | Only trust ideas with ✓ in your notes |
| Right idea, wrong person | An option is true for one group or speaker but the question asks about another | Note who each idea is for |
| Total versus share | "$300 more" in total, but the question asks about each person | Write the maths in your notes: `300 ÷ 2 = 150 each` |
| The back-up plan | "If she says no, we’ll have dinner instead" | The back-up is not the plan unless the condition happens |
| Order of jobs | Someone says "Don’t call until..." | Note conditions and waits: `call ← after Pierre yes` |

<div class="strategy">

**Answer the "why" before it is asked.** Every time a speaker rejects an idea, say the reason silently to yourself in three words ("booked for fundraiser", "too hot", "five hour drive"). Part 1 almost always asks at least one "why" question, and you will already have the answer.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"Part 1 is easy because it is first."** Part 1 has the most questions of any listening part (8 of 38), and it is long, so attention drops by section 3. Many test takers lose marks at the end, on the final plan. Treat section 3 as the most important section, not the least.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- **Stating the problem:** *we have a problem, the thing is, the trouble is, it just died, I can’t...*
- **Suggesting:** *what about, how about, maybe we could, why don’t we, what if*
- **Rejecting:** *I thought of that, but; that won’t work because; the only issue is; too (hot, late, far)*
- **Agreeing:** *fair enough, good point, that could work, I’m in, deal, then let’s*
- **Assigning jobs:** *I’ll call, can you, you’re seeing her, so you ask*

</div>

## 6. Practice: three Part 1 conversations

Each conversation has three sections, just like the test. Each section is one group of questions: listen once, then answer before moving on. Keep your plot frame on paper.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="drill" data-set="lp1" data-n="8"><figcaption>Three original Problem Solving conversations, 24 questions. Each round gives about one conversation’s worth of sections; New questions gives the next.</figcaption></figure>

**Check yourself.** After each round, look at which questions you missed. If they were in section 3, practise keeping your focus to the end: sit up, take a breath before section 3, and write the plan line first.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 1: **one conversation, three sections, 8 questions**, questions after each section, heard once.
- Track the plot: **problem → ideas (✓/✗ + reason) → final plan → who does what**.
- The first idea is often rejected; trust only ideas the speakers accept.
- Watch for totals versus shares, back-up plans, and ideas that are true for a different person.
- Section 3 holds the final plan: keep your focus until the very end.

</div>
