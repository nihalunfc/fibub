**Module 2 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will know how Part 3 recordings are organised, how to note facts with their conditions, and how to avoid number traps. You will practise on four original recordings with 24 questions.

<div class="bigidea">Part 3 is someone explaining how something works. Each fact usually comes with a condition or exception, and the questions test those conditions, so note rules as "rule; except when...".</div>

## 1. What Part 3 looks like

Part 3, **Listening for Information**, plays one recording of about two minutes, then asks **6 questions**. The recording is usually one of these:

- A **conversation** where one person asks questions and the other explains (a newcomer at a library, a caller to a recycling depot, a new employee and a supervisor).
- A **short talk** by one speaker giving information (a tour guide, an instructor, a recorded message).

The recording plays **once**, and the questions come after it. There are four options per question.

## 2. What it really tests

Part 3 tests whether you can pick out and hold **factual information**: what is allowed, what it costs, how long something takes, what you need to bring, what happens if you break a rule, and what order things happen in. It is the most "list-like" listening part.

The challenge is not hard vocabulary. It is **quantity**: many facts come quickly, and several of them sound similar (three weeks, six weeks, fifty items). The facts also come with **conditions**: free *if* you live in the city, paid breaks *only on* an eight hour shift.

**Everyday analogy.** Think of a new coworker showing you around on your first day: "Coffee is free, but the good machine is upstairs. Lockers are first come, first served, except the ones with red tags, which are for night staff." You cannot remember every word, but if you remember each rule with its exception, you will not get into trouble. Part 3 tests the same skill.

## 3. The method for Part 3

<div class="steps">

<div class="step">

**Predict the topics.** From the situation line ("a newcomer asking about a library card"), guess the likely topics: who can get it, what you need, how many items, how long, fines, extra services. Write them down the left side of your paper, one per line.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Fill in facts beside each topic.** As you hear each fact, write it on its line: `need: photo ID + address (digital ok)`. If a topic comes up you did not predict, add a new line.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Mark conditions with "if" and exceptions with "except".** `fees: none; >6 wk = replacement cost`. `breaks: >5 h = 30 min unpaid; 8 h = +2×15 paid`.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Keep numbers attached to their nouns.** Never write a lone number. Write `50 items`, `3 wk each`, `6 wk lost`. Lone numbers become traps.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Answer by checking the condition.** When a question gives a specific case ("What happens if an item is two weeks late?"), test it against your rule: two weeks is less than six, so no charge.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Situation line: *Listen to an instructor explaining the rules of a community pottery class.*

> **Instructor:** Welcome, everyone. Classes run for eight weeks, every Monday from seven to nine. Clay and glazes are included in your fee, but if you want to take home more than three pieces, there’s a five dollar firing charge for each extra piece. Please wear old clothes; aprons are on the hooks by the door. If you miss a class, you can make it up on Wednesday evenings, but only twice in the session. And one important rule: never put a piece in the kiln yourself. Leave it on the green shelf, and I’ll fire everything on Fridays.

Notes, by topic:
`when: 8 wk, Mon 7-9`
`cost: clay+glaze incl.; >3 pieces = $5 each extra`
`clothes: old; aprons by door`
`missed class: make up Wed, max 2`
`kiln: never yourself → green shelf; fired Fri`

*A student wants to take home five pieces. How much extra will she pay?* Five minus three free pieces is two extra, at five dollars each: **ten dollars**. A trap option would be "$25" (five pieces at five dollars) or "$5".

*What should students do with finished pieces?* A. Put them in the kiln. B. Leave them on the green shelf. C. Take them home on Friday. D. Give them to the instructor on Wednesday.
**Answer: B.** A is exactly what the instructor said *never* to do. C uses "Friday", which is when the instructor fires them, not when students take them home.

## 5. Question types and traps

| Question | Example | Trap to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Requirement | What does he need to bring? | An item mentioned as "not needed" |
| Number | How long can items be borrowed? | A different number from the same talk |
| Condition | What happens if...? | Applying the rule without its exception |
| Combination | Which days is it closed? | Answering with only one of two facts |
| Recommendation | What does the guide suggest? | Something allowed but not suggested |
| Speaker’s attitude | How is the explanation? | Choosing a feeling with no evidence |

<div class="strategy">

**Write the left column before the audio starts.** The few seconds before a Part 3 recording are your best moment. Five predicted topic words on the left of your page give your notes a shape, and you will notice at once when a fact belongs to a topic you have not filled yet.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"I should write down every number I hear."** Writing every number without its meaning makes it worse: you end up with "3, 50, 6, 7" and no idea which is which. Write fewer numbers, always with their noun. If you must choose, catch the number linked to a condition or exception; that is the one most likely to be asked.

</div>

<div class="phrases">

- **Conditions:** *if, as long as, only if, provided that, once, until*
- **Exceptions:** *except, but, unless, apart from, the only thing is, one important rule*
- **Requirements:** *you need, you’ll have to, make sure, bring, don’t forget*
- **Permissions:** *you’re welcome to, you can, feel free to, it’s fine to*
- **Prohibitions:** *please don’t, never, you can’t, not allowed, we don’t take*
- **Order:** *first, then, once you have, after that, we’ll finish with*

</div>

## 6. Practice: four information recordings

Before each recording, write four or five predicted topics on paper. Listen once, fill in the facts, then answer all six questions.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="drill" data-set="lp3" data-n="6"><figcaption>Four original Listening for Information recordings, 24 questions. Each round is one recording; New questions moves to the next.</figcaption></figure>

**After checking.** For each mistake, ask: did I miss the fact, or did I miss its condition? Most Part 3 errors are missed conditions. Replay and listen for the word *but*, *except* or *if* that you missed.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 3: **one explanation (conversation or talk), 6 questions after it**, heard once.
- Predict the topics and write them down the left side before the audio starts.
- Note every rule with its condition or exception: `rule; except...`.
- Keep numbers attached to their nouns: `3 wk each`, never just `3`.
- For "what if" questions, test the case against the rule and its exception.

</div>
