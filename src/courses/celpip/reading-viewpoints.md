**Module 3 · about 22 minutes.** By the end you will know how Reading Part 4 is built, how to keep track of several people's opinions in one article, how to tell a strong view from a partial one, and how to complete the reader's comment that follows.

<div class="bigidea">Part 4 is about people, not facts: for every opinion in the article, know who holds it, how strongly, and why.</div>

## 1. What Part 4 looks like

Reading Part 4, **Reading for Viewpoints**, has **10 questions**. It is the longest and most demanding reading part. You read an article, usually five or six paragraphs, about a question that people disagree on: a city plan, a new rule at work or school, a change in how people live. The article quotes several people with different views, and the writer usually gives their own view near the end.

Then come two kinds of questions:

- **Multiple-choice questions about the article**: what a person thinks, why, what the writer believes, how the article is organised.
- **A reader's comment with gaps**: someone has written a short response to the article, agreeing with some people and disagreeing with others. You choose a word or phrase for each gap.

The part has its own timer; the test screen shows the exact time. Because the text is long, timing matters more here than anywhere else.

## 2. What it really tests

Part 4 tests whether you can follow an argument. That means more than understanding sentences. You need to:

- **Attribute** each opinion to the right person.
- **Measure** how strong it is: fully for, fully against, or somewhere in between.
- **Find the reason** behind it.
- **Separate** facts (*the trial is for one summer*) from opinions (*a street full of people is good for everyone*).

**An everyday analogy.** Think of a family dinner where everyone discusses whether to get a dog. Your mother loves the idea. Your father is not against it but wants to wait until summer. Your brother is allergic. Later, your aunt asks, "So what does your father think?" You would not say "he is against it". You would say "he agrees, but later". Part 4 asks you to report a discussion that accurately.

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Skim for the question and the people (about 2 minutes).** Read the title and first paragraph to find the debate. Then go through the article and note each name and their role: *Joshi, councillor*, *Delisle, hardware store*, *Nguyen, cyclists*, *writer*.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Give each person a sign and a reason.** In your head, mark each one: plus (for), minus (against) or plus-minus (partly). Add a two-word reason: *Joshi + more shoppers*, *Delisle +/- heavy items*.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Find the writer's view.** Look near the end for *in my view*, *this writer believes*, *the real question is*, *the writer's view?*. The writer's view often balances the others.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Answer the article questions.** For each, go to the person's paragraph and read the whole quote plus the sentence after it. Watch for *but*, *although*, *however*, *still*, which mark a partial view.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Complete the comment using your map.** The comment names people (*I agree with Dr. Mackie*, *I understand Mr. Delisle*). Go straight to that person's sign and reason, then choose the option that paraphrases it.

</div>
</div>

## 4. A worked example

An original short article:

> The town of Birchwood plans to replace its outdoor rink with a covered one. Recreation director Sandra Bell says a roof would let families skate even in rainy winters. "We lost almost four weeks of skating last year," she says. Local resident Marc Lavoie likes the idea but questions the price: "A roof is nice, but the $2 million could fix every playground in town first." Hockey coach Ama Owusu is fully in favour, saying her teams travel an hour to practise when the ice melts. In this writer's opinion, the town should ask residents to vote.

Question: *What is Marc Lavoie's position?*
A) He is against the covered rink
B) He thinks the money might be better used elsewhere
C) He wants more playgrounds instead of skating
D) He fully supports the plan

**Thinking.** Lavoie "likes the idea **but** questions the price". So his sign is plus-minus. A is too strong (he likes the idea). D ignores the "but". C goes beyond the text: he says the money could fix playgrounds *first*, not *instead of skating*. **B** captures his view.

Comment gap: *I agree with Coach Owusu, because driving an hour to ____ is not fair to young players.* Owusu's reason: her teams "travel an hour to practise". The answer is **practise** (the verb; Canadian spelling uses *practice* for the noun).

## 5. Question types and traps

| Question | What to find | Typical trap |
|---|---|---|
| *What does X think?* | X's sign and its strength | Too strong (*completely against*) when X is partly against |
| *Why does X think this?* | The reason in X's paragraph | Another person's reason |
| *What does the writer believe?* | The writer's own words near the end | A quoted opinion the writer only reports |
| *Who would agree that...?* | Match the idea to a person | A person who mentions the topic but takes the other side |
| Comment gaps | The view of the person the commenter names | The commenter's own new idea, or the right idea with the wrong direction (*up* for *down*) |

**The reporter trap.** Articles quote many people, and the writer does not agree with all of them. A sentence like "Supporters say the rules are already making a difference" reports someone else's view. Only phrases such as *this writer believes* show the writer's own.

**The strength trap.** Options often change *some* to *all*, *worries* to *opposes*, *prefers* to *demands*. Read for the exact strength.

<div class="phrases">

- **For:** *is enthusiastic*, *strongly supports*, *welcomes*, *is in favour of*, *sees it as a chance*
- **Against:** *opposes*, *objects to*, *is sceptical*, *warns that*, *argues against*
- **Partly:** *has mixed feelings*, *is not against... but*, *likes the idea, although*, *agrees in principle*, *with one concern*
- **Writer's view:** *in this writer's opinion*, *I believe*, *the real issue is*, *it seems clear that*
- **Reporting others:** *supporters say*, *critics argue*, *according to*, *she points out*

</div>

<div class="strategy">

**Spend the first two minutes on the people map, not on the questions.** It feels slow, but with ten questions about the same handful of people, a clear map pays back many times. Most Part 4 errors come from mixing up who said what, not from difficult words.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"The writer's view is always the same as the first person quoted."** Articles often open with the person who proposed an idea, and the writer then weighs the other sides. Many writers take a middle position, such as "a trial is the right way" or "schools should do both". Always find the writer's own words before answering.

</div>

## 6. Managing a long text

- If time is short, read the first and last paragraphs carefully and the middle paragraphs only for names and signs.
- Answer the comment gaps even if you are behind: they often need just one person's view, which your map gives you.
- Never leave a gap empty. Choose the option that matches the named person's sign.

## 7. Practise

Each round shows one article with nine questions: five about the article and four comment gaps. Build your people map before you look at the questions.

After each round, check: did you lose marks on strength (too strong or too weak), on attribution (wrong person) or on the writer's view? Each needs a different fix, and your answers show which one is yours.

<div class="keypoints">

- Part 4 has 10 questions: questions about an opinion article, then gaps in a reader's comment.
- Map every person: name, role, sign (for, against, partly) and a two-word reason.
- Find the writer's own view near the end; quotations show other people's views.
- Watch strength words (*some*, *all*, *worries*, *opposes*) and partial views after *but* or *although*.
- Comment gaps follow the person the commenter names: go to that person in your map.

</div>
