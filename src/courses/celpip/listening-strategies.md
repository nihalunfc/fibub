**Module 2 · about 18 minutes.** By the end you will have one method that works for all six listening parts, you will know which words to listen for, and you will have practised the method on 24 short original recordings.

<div class="bigidea">You hear each recording once, so listen with a plan: predict what kind of thing will happen, note only what changes, and choose the option that matches the meaning, not the words.</div>

## 1. What CELPIP Listening looks like

The Listening section comes first in CELPIP-General and takes **46 to 55 minutes**. It has six parts and 38 scored questions, sometimes with a few unscored trial questions mixed in.

| Part | Name | Questions | You hear |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem Solving | 8 | Two people solving a problem, in three sections |
| 2 | Daily Life Conversation | 5 | An everyday conversation |
| 3 | Listening for Information | 6 | Someone explaining or asking for information |
| 4 | News Item | 5 | A short local news report |
| 5 | Discussion | 8 | Three people discussing something, often as a short video |
| 6 | Viewpoints | 6 | A talk that presents different opinions |

Three facts shape everything in this module:

- **Each recording plays once.** There is no replay button.
- **The questions come after the recording.** You cannot read them first, so you do not know exactly what will be asked.
- **Wrong answers cost nothing.** Never leave a question blank.

Before each recording, the screen shows a short line such as "Listen to a conversation between two neighbours." That line is your only preview, and it is more useful than most people think.

## 2. What the section really tests

CELPIP does not test whether you can catch every word. It tests whether you can follow **what people mean**: the problem, the plan, the decision, the feeling, the reason. Listening researchers such as John Field describe two jobs your brain does at once: *decoding* (turning sounds into words) and *meaning building* (connecting the words to the situation and to what you already know). Learners often spend all their energy on decoding and run out of attention for meaning. The method below protects your attention for meaning.

**Everyday analogy.** Think of watching a hockey game on TV. You do not follow every skater. You watch the puck, and you notice the moments that change the score. In a CELPIP recording, the "puck" is the plan or problem, and the moments that change the score are changes of mind, reasons and decisions.

## 3. The method: predict, note, decide

<div class="steps">
<div class="step">

**Predict (5 seconds, before the audio).** Read the line on the screen. Ask: who are these people, where are they, and what usually happens in this situation? A tenant calling a manager usually means a problem and a repair time. A news item usually means an event, a cause, people affected and what happens next.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Listen for the shape.** Most CELPIP recordings follow a shape: a situation, a problem or question, one or more ideas, a change of mind, and a final decision. Keep asking yourself "What is the problem? What have they decided so far?"

</div>
<div class="step">

**Note only what changes.** Write 3 to 6 words at a time: names, numbers, days, and arrows for changes. "Fri → Wed", "Thai ✗ pizza ✓", "Lena: ask Sam". Never write full sentences.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Decide by meaning.** When the questions appear, answer from your notes and memory. Pick the option that says the same idea as the speaker, usually in different words. Be suspicious of an option that copies the speaker’s exact words.

</div>
<div class="step">

**Move on.** If you are unsure, remove the options that are clearly wrong, choose the best one left, and go to the next question. Never let one hard question steal attention from the next recording.

</div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

The screen says: *Listen to a student booking a study room.* You predict: a room, a time, maybe a problem with the time, a choice.

> **Student:** Hi, can I book a study room for four people on Wednesday at three?
> **Clerk:** The rooms for four are all taken at three. I have one at five, or a bigger room for eight people at three.
> **Student:** Five is too late; two of us have a class. We’ll take the bigger room.
> **Clerk:** Sure. It’s yours from three until five.

Your notes might be: `Wed 3, room 4 ✗ → 5 ✗ (class) → big room (8) 3-5 ✓`.

Question: *Which room will the students use?*
A. A room for four people at five
B. A larger room in the afternoon
C. A room for four people at three
D. No room; they will study at home

Thinking: C uses the exact words "four people at three", but my notes show a cross next to it. A also repeats words from the recording, "four" and "five", but the student says five is too late. B says "larger" and "in the afternoon", which are different words for "bigger room" and "from three until five". My last arrow points there. **Answer: B.** Notice that the right answer paraphrased the recording, and both traps copied it.

## 5. The words that matter most

Some short words carry the whole answer. Train your ear to jump when you hear them.

<div class="phrases">

- **Change of plan:** *actually, on second thought, I was going to... but, instead, I’d rather, how about, let’s not*
- **Contrast (the real point comes next):** *but, though, however, the thing is, honestly, I’ll give you that, but*
- **Reason:** *because, since, so, that’s why, the reason is*
- **Decision:** *okay, let’s, I’ll, fine, deal, put me down for, I’ll take*
- **Condition:** *if, unless, as long as, only if, provided that*
- **Feeling:** *I’m worried, I can’t stand, I don’t mind, I’m sold, not my idea of fun*

</div>

**Everyday analogy.** These words are like turn signals on a car. You do not need to see the whole road; when the signal blinks, you know the car is about to change direction.

## 6. Question types you will meet in every part

- **Main idea:** "What is the conversation mainly about?" Answer from the overall situation, not one detail.
- **Detail:** times, prices, places, names. Watch for several numbers where only one is final.
- **Reason:** "Why does she...?" Listen after *because, since, so*.
- **Decision or next action:** "What will he probably do next?" Usually the last plan in the recording.
- **Feeling or attitude:** "How does she feel about...?" Listen to tone and to what comes after *but*.
- **Inference:** "What can you infer...?" The answer is not said directly but is clearly supported.

<div class="strategy">

**The notes grid.** Before each recording, draw two quick columns on your note paper: left for **person A**, right for **person B** (or three columns for Part 5). Put each idea under the person who said it. Most "who said what" and "who changed their mind" questions become easy.

</div>

<div class="myth">

**"If I understand every word, I will get every question."** Not true. Many people catch every word and still choose the trap, because the trap uses those exact words. And many people miss a few words but still get the answer, because they followed the plan and the decision. Listen for meaning first.

</div>

## 7. Practice: predict, note, decide

Each clip below tests one habit: catching a change of plan, listening past "but", noticing which number is final, or following who decided what. Press play, take notes, then answer. Do not replay before answering: in the test you hear it once. After you check, replay to hear the key words.

**How to use your score.** If you missed questions marked as "change of plan" traps, slow down at words like *actually* and *instead*. If you missed number questions, write every number you hear and cross out the ones that change.

<div class="keypoints">

- Every recording plays **once** and the questions come **after** it, so read the situation line and predict.
- Use one method for every part: **predict, note what changes, decide by meaning**.
- Notes are short: names, numbers, days and arrows. Never full sentences.
- Words like *actually, but, instead, as long as* signal the answer; the **final** plan is usually what is asked.
- An option that copies the speaker’s exact words is often a trap; the right one usually paraphrases.
- Wrong answers cost nothing: always choose something and move on.

</div>
