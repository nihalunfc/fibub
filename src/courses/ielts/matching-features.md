**Module 4 · about 14 minutes.** Read first: [Which paragraph contains the information?](/learn/courses/ielts/matching-information/). By the end you will match ideas to the people (or places, or dates) who hold them, quickly and without mixing up two people who say similar things.

<div class="bigidea">Work from the names, not from the statements. Names are easy to find; find every one, read what is said beside it, and only then match.</div>

## 1. What it looks like

You get a list of **features**, usually people (researchers, writers, companies), sometimes places, periods or products, labelled A, B, C... and a list of statements. You match each statement to a feature.

> Match each statement with the correct researcher, A to E. You may use any letter more than once.
> 1. Colour words appear in a fixed order as languages develop.
> 2. Children learn colour words later than other everyday words.
>
> A Dr Osei · B Professor Lin · C Marta Ruiz · D Dr Haddad · E Professor Kerr

Some features may not be used at all; some may be used twice. The statements are **not** in text order.

## 2. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>1. Scan for every name</b> in the list and circle each one in the passage. Note that a person may be mentioned by full name the first time and only by surname ("Lin") or a pronoun ("she", "the linguist") later.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>2. For each name, read the sentence before and the sentences after.</b> Write a two- or three-word note in the margin: "Osei: order fixed", "Lin: kids late".</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>3. Now read the statements</b> and match them to your notes, not to the passage. Paraphrases will be heavy: "a fixed order" may be "a predictable sequence".</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>4. Check the near-misses.</b> Where two people seem to fit, go back and read both places word by word.</p></div>
</div>


Tap a phrase from the question, then the passage phrase that means the same.

## 3. Worked example

A short text:

> **A** Many linguists once believed that the way a language divides up colour was arbitrary. **Professor Lin** was among the first to challenge this, arguing that colour terms enter a language in a largely predictable sequence: words for dark and light first, then red, then others.
>
> **B** Not everyone agreed. **Dr Osei** found several languages that did not fit Lin's order, and suggested that the pattern reflects the colours that matter most in a community's daily life. **Marta Ruiz**, who studied children in three countries, added a further point: children use colour words correctly much later than words for shapes or animals, even when they can clearly see the difference.

Statements and answers:

1. **Colour terms are added to a language in a set order.** Lin: "enter a language in a largely predictable sequence". **Lin**.
2. **The way people live may influence which colours get names.** Osei: "reflects the colours that matter most in a community's daily life". **Osei**.
3. **Young learners master colour vocabulary relatively late.** Ruiz: "use colour words correctly much later". **Ruiz**.

Notice the trap in 1: "many linguists once believed" is also about colour terms, but it is the *opposite* view and it is not a person in the list.

## 4. The traps

- **The twins trap.** Two people discuss the same topic, one agreeing and one disagreeing. The statement matches the topic of both but the view of only one. Always check the view, not just the topic.
- **Reported views.** "Lin's critics argue..." is not Lin's view. "Osei, following Lin, accepts that..." can be both.
- **Pronouns.** "She later showed..." may be the answer for the person named two sentences earlier.
- **Dates and periods.** If the features are dates ("A before 1900, B 1900-1950, C after 1950"), find every year in the text and work out which band each event falls into. Watch for "a decade later" and "by the end of the century".

<div class="myth">

**"Each person can only be used once."** Read the instructions. If they say "You may use any letter more than once", one researcher can easily have two statements, and another may have none.

</div>

## 5. Practise

<div class="keypoints">

- Find and mark every name first; people may reappear with a surname or a pronoun.
- Write a two- or three-word note next to each name, then match statements to your notes.
- Statements are not in text order. A feature may be used twice or not at all.
- Two people on the same topic: match the view, not the topic.
- "X's critics say" is not X's view.
