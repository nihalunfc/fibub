**Module 7 · about 16 minutes.** By the end you will write body paragraphs that develop one idea fully, which is what moves an essay from band 6 to band 7 and above.

<div class="bigidea">One paragraph, one idea, fully developed: say it (point), say why or how (explain), show it (example), and connect it to the question (link).</div>

## 1. The PEEL pattern

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>Point.</b> A topic sentence with the main idea: "The main reason to ban cars from city centres is the effect on public health."</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Explain.</b> Two or three sentences on why or how: traffic pollution, its effect on breathing, who suffers most.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Example.</b> A specific case: a city, a group, a personal experience. "When one European capital closed its main square to traffic, local air quality improved noticeably within a year."</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Link.</b> One sentence tying the paragraph back to your position: "For this reason, a ban would directly improve residents' lives."</p></div>
</div>

## 2. Before and after

**Band 5-6 style (listing):**

> Banning cars is good. Pollution is bad for health. Also, there are many accidents. Cars are noisy too. Furthermore, public transport is better. So cars should be banned.

Five ideas, none explained. The reader learns nothing new.

**Band 7-8 style (developing):**

> The most compelling reason to ban cars from city centres is the benefit to public health. Exhaust fumes from heavy traffic contain fine particles that damage the lungs, and those most at risk are children and older people, who often have no choice about where they live or walk. Removing traffic from the busiest streets reduces this exposure directly, rather than relying on gradual improvements in engine technology. For instance, after one large city closed several central streets to private vehicles, residents reported cleaner air and many more people began to walk and cycle there. A ban would therefore improve the daily health of thousands of people at relatively little cost.

One idea, explained (what, who, why this solution), shown (an example), and linked.

## 3. Good examples

- **Specific but general knowledge:** "many hospitals", "in countries such as Japan", "a friend of mine who...".
- **Hypothetical is fine:** "For example, a student who chooses a subject she loves is more likely to finish her degree."
- **Avoid invented numbers:** "73% of people..." looks made up. Say "a large number of", "most".

## 4. Explaining more: question your own sentence

After each sentence, ask "So what?" or "Why?" and write the answer as the next sentence. That habit alone turns a list into an argument.

| Sentence | So what? / Why? |
|---|---|
| Online learning is flexible. | Students can study around their jobs and families. |
| | This means people who could never attend a campus can now earn a degree. |

<div class="myth">

**"More ideas means a higher task response score."** Development counts more than quantity. Two ideas, each explained and supported, beat six ideas in a list.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- One paragraph, one main idea, stated in the topic sentence.
- Explain why or how in two or three sentences; ask yourself "So what?".
- Add a specific, believable example; no invented statistics.
- Finish with a sentence linking back to your position.
- Develop two ideas well rather than listing many.
