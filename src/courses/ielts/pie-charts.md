**Module 5 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will describe one or two pie charts with the language of proportion, and avoid the most common logic mistake.

<div class="bigidea">A pie shows shares of a whole, not amounts. Talk about proportions ("a quarter of", "the largest share"), and when there are two pies, compare how the shares changed.</div>

## 1. Language of proportion

Move the slider: one figure, several ways to say it.

<figure class="fig" data-fig="fraction-words"><figcaption>Choose a percentage and see the fraction words and size words that describe it.</figcaption></figure>

| Exact | In words |
|---|---|
| 50% | half |
| 25% / 75% | a quarter / three quarters |
| 33% / 67% | a third / two thirds |
| 20% / 10% | a fifth / one in ten |
| 52% | just over half, the majority |
| 48% | just under half, nearly half |
| 3% | a tiny fraction, a very small minority |

Verbs that link a category to its share: **account for, make up, represent, constitute, comprise** (careful: "comprise" takes the whole as subject: *the budget comprised five items*).

## 2. One pie

Rank the slices. The overview names the biggest (or two biggest) and perhaps the smallest. Details: the large slices in one paragraph, the small ones in the other.

## 3. Two or more pies

Usually the pies show two years or two groups. Now the main features are **changes in share**:

- Which slice grew most? Which shrank most?
- Did the ranking change (did a new slice become the largest)?
- Which stayed about the same?

Language: *the share of X rose from 12% to 20%*, *X's share doubled*, *X overtook Y as the largest category*.

## 4. Worked example

*The pie charts show how a typical household in one country spent its budget in 1980 and 2020.*

| Category | 1980 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|
| Housing | 22% | 34% |
| Food | 30% | 15% |
| Transport | 14% | 16% |
| Leisure | 10% | 18% |
| Clothing | 12% | 6% |
| Other | 12% | 11% |

<div class="meaning">

**Model answer (about 175 words)**

The two pie charts illustrate the proportion of a typical household budget spent on six categories in one country in 1980 and 2020.

Overall, housing replaced food as the largest expense, and the share spent on leisure rose considerably, while the shares of food and clothing both fell by half.

In 1980, food accounted for almost a third of household spending (30%), more than any other category, followed by housing at just over a fifth (22%). By 2020 the position had reversed: housing made up just over a third of the budget (34%), whereas the share of food had halved to 15%.

Of the smaller categories, leisure grew most, from one in ten to almost a fifth of spending (18%). Clothing, by contrast, fell from 12% to only 6%. Transport and other items changed very little, each staying at between 11% and 16% in both years.

</div>

## 5. The logic trap

<div class="myth">

**"Food fell from 30% to 15%, so families spent less money on food."** Not necessarily. A pie shows shares of a total, and the total may have grown. Write "the proportion spent on food halved", not "spending on food halved", unless the chart gives amounts.

</div>

## 6. Practise

<div class="qbank" data-bank="ielts" data-topics="writing-task1" data-mode="practice" data-n="3" data-sub="pie"></div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Pies show shares of a whole: use proportion language and verbs like account for and make up.
- Vary: give the exact figure once, then fractions and size words.
- One pie: rank the slices. Two pies: compare the changes in share and in ranking.
- A smaller share is not necessarily a smaller amount.
- Group large slices and small slices into separate paragraphs.

</div>
