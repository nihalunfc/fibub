**Module 4 · about 15 minutes.** Read first: [Skimming and scanning](/learn/courses/ielts/skim-and-scan/) and [Matching headings](/learn/courses/ielts/matching-headings/). By the end you will find a single detail hidden somewhere in seven paragraphs without reading the passage three times.

<div class="bigidea">Matching information is a scavenger hunt for one <i>kind</i> of sentence. The first noun in each question ("a reason", "an example", "a prediction") tells you what kind of sentence to look for.</div>

## 1. What it looks like

The passage has lettered paragraphs, A to G. You get statements like these:

> Which paragraph contains the following information? You may use any letter more than once.
> 1. a reference to a decision made by a local government
> 2. an explanation of why one plan failed
> 3. a comparison between two types of material

You write a letter for each. It looks like matching headings, but it is different in two big ways:

| | Matching headings | Matching information |
|---|---|---|
| What you match | the **main idea** of a whole paragraph | **one detail**, often a single sentence |
| Letters used | each paragraph once | a letter can be used twice, some not at all |
| Order | answers in no order | answers in no order |

So here the detail **is** the answer. The thing that was a trap in headings is now the target.

## 2. Read the question noun first

Every statement starts with a noun phrase that names a type of information. Learn to translate it into "what the sentence will look like":

| The question says | Look for a sentence that... | Signal words in the text |
|---|---|---|
| a reason, an explanation | says why | because, since, due to, as a result, the cause |
| an example | names one specific case | for example, such as, in one city, one company |
| a comparison | puts two things side by side | more than, unlike, whereas, similar to, while |
| a prediction | talks about the future | will, is likely to, by 2050, expect |
| a criticism, an objection | disagrees with something | however, critics, but, a weakness, fails to |
| a description of a method | says how something is done | first, then, by + -ing, the process |
| a reference to / a mention of | just names the thing, briefly | could be half a sentence |
| a definition | says what a term means | is known as, means, refers to, is called |
| a suggestion, a recommendation | says what should happen | should, could, it would be better to, advise |
| a number, a figure, a date | contains a number | digits, years, "twice", "a third" |

"A reference to" is the hardest, because the information may be just a few words inside a sentence about something else.


Tap a phrase from the question, then the passage phrase that means the same.

## 3. The method

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>1. Leave it until later.</b> If a passage has other question types, do them first. By then you know roughly what each paragraph contains.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>2. Underline the noun and the topic</b> in each statement: "an <u>explanation</u> of why one <u>plan failed</u>". The noun is the kind of sentence; the topic is what it is about.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>3. Start with the easiest statements</b>: ones with names, numbers or unusual words you can scan for. Write the letter, even if you are not sure yet.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>4. For the rest, go paragraph by paragraph</b>, not question by question. Read paragraph A once and ask: "Does any remaining statement live here?" Then B, and so on. This way you read the passage once, not seven times.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>5. Check the noun.</b> If a paragraph talks about the topic but has no reason in it, it cannot be the answer to "a reason". Topic and type must both match.</p></div>
</div>

## 4. Worked example

Use the passage "Bees on the rooftops" from the earlier lessons (it is in the practice room). Three statements:

1. **a reference to a recommendation from conservation groups.** Topic: conservation groups. Scan for "conservation": paragraph E, last sentence, "some conservation groups to advise against adding new hives". Advise = recommend. **E**.
2. **an example of a measure taken by a city.** Type: one specific case. Paragraph G: "One has created a map...". **G**. Paragraph F also has measures, but by companies, not a city: the topic does not match.
3. **an explanation of why bees seemed to do well in cities.** Type: reason. Paragraph B: "Urban gardens, parks and street trees flower across a long season, and many cities had restricted the use of garden pesticides." Those are the reasons cities were a refuge. **B**.

Notice that in 2, paragraph F was a tempting wrong answer: the right type (measures) but the wrong topic (companies). That is the most common trap.

## 5. The traps

<div class="myth">

**"I found the word, so that's the paragraph."** The same word often appears in two or three paragraphs. Find the paragraph where the word sits inside the right **type** of sentence. "Pesticides" appears in B (as a reason bees died) and nowhere as a recommendation.

</div>

- **Word matching without meaning.** The question says "a decline", the paragraph says "a fall" or "fewer". Expect synonyms, as in [Spotting the paraphrase](/learn/courses/ielts/spot-the-paraphrase/).
- **Using each letter once.** If the instructions say "You may use any letter more than once", two answers can share a letter. Do not change a good answer just because the letter is "used".
- **Too much time.** This type has no order to help you. Set a limit of about a minute per statement; guess and move on, then come back.

## 6. Practise

<div class="qbank" data-bank="ielts" data-topics="reading-academic-2,reading-general" data-mode="practice" data-n="12" data-facets="qtype=matchinfo"></div>

<div class="keypoints">

- You match one detail, not a main idea. A letter can be used more than once.
- The noun at the start ("a reason", "an example", "a prediction") tells you the kind of sentence to find.
- Do this type after the others on the same passage; you will know where things are.
- Work paragraph by paragraph, asking which statements live in each.
- Both the topic and the type of information must match.

</div>
