**Module 1 · about 14 minutes.** By the end you will know exactly what happens in each paper, how many questions to expect, and how long you have for each, so nothing on test day surprises you.

<div class="bigidea">Most lost marks come from time, not English. Know the clock of each paper so well that you never wonder "how long have I got?"</div>

<figure class="fig" data-fig="test-clock"><figcaption>The written papers come in this order, one after the other. Speaking is a separate appointment.</figcaption></figure>

## 1. Listening: 30 minutes, 4 parts, 40 questions

| Part | What you hear | Example situation | Questions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Conversation between two people, everyday social life | Booking a room for a party, joining a gym | 10 |
| 2 | One person talking, everyday social life | A guide describing a park, a radio notice about a festival | 10 |
| 3 | Up to four people, study or training | Two students and a tutor discussing a project | 10 |
| 4 | One speaker, academic talk | A short lecture on the history of glass or bird migration | 10 |

The parts get harder as you go. Part 1 often asks you to write names, numbers and dates you hear spelled out; Part 4 is a lecture with no break in the middle.

**What happens in the room:** before each part you get some seconds to read the questions; use them to predict the answers (Lesson 2.2). Every recording plays **once**. On paper you write on the question paper while listening, then you get **10 minutes** at the end to copy answers onto the answer sheet. On computer you type as you go and get **2 minutes** at the end to check.

**Question types you can meet:** form, note, table, flow-chart and summary completion; multiple choice; matching; labelling a map, plan or diagram; sentence completion; short answers. Each has its own lesson in Module 2.

## 2. Reading: 60 minutes, 3 sections, 40 questions

**Academic:** three long passages (each about 700 to 1,000 words), taken from books, journals, magazines and newspapers and written for a non-specialist reader. They get harder from Passage 1 to Passage 3. At least one gives a detailed argument.

**General Training:** three sections that move from everyday to more demanding:

- Section 1: two or three short texts about everyday life, such as notices, adverts and timetables.
- Section 2: two texts about work, such as a job description, a contract, staff training or a workplace policy.
- Section 3: one longer, more complex text on a general topic.

**There is no extra time.** You write answers straight onto the answer sheet (or type them) within the 60 minutes. That is the trap: many people do Passage 1 slowly and run out of time on Passage 3. Lesson 3.4 gives you a minute-by-minute plan.

**Question types:** True / False / Not Given, Yes / No / Not Given, matching headings, matching information to paragraphs, matching features (such as people to ideas), matching sentence endings, sentence, summary, note, table and flow-chart completion, diagram labels, multiple choice and short answers.

## 3. Writing: 60 minutes, 2 tasks

| | Task 1 | Task 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Academic | Describe and compare the main features of a graph, table, chart, diagram, process or map | An essay giving an opinion, discussing views, or a problem and solutions |
| General Training | A letter: asking for information, complaining, explaining a situation, inviting a friend | Same kinds of essay, often on everyday topics |
| Minimum words | 150 | 250 |
| Suggested time | 20 minutes | 40 minutes |
| Weight | about one third | about two thirds |

Answers that are under the word count lose marks, and memorised answers that do not fit the question score very low. You can write in pencil (paper) or type (computer, with a word counter on screen).

## 4. Speaking: 11 to 14 minutes, 3 parts

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>Part 1, 4 to 5 minutes.</b> The examiner checks your ID, then asks simple questions about you: your home, work or studies, and two or three everyday topics such as food, weather or weekends.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Part 2, 3 to 4 minutes.</b> You get a task card with a topic and three or four points to cover. You have <b>1 minute</b> to make notes, then speak for <b>1 to 2 minutes</b>. The examiner may ask one short question at the end.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Part 3, 4 to 5 minutes.</b> A discussion of wider questions linked to the Part 2 topic. Here the examiner wants reasons, comparisons and examples, not short answers.</p></div>
</div>

The whole conversation is recorded. The examiner marks you on four things: fluency and coherence, vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation (Module 8 explains each).

## 5. The whole test in numbers

| Paper | Time | Questions or tasks | Same for Academic and GT? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listening | 30 min (+10 paper / +2 computer) | 40 | Yes |
| Reading | 60 min | 40 | No |
| Writing | 60 min | 2 tasks | No |
| Speaking | 11 to 14 min | 3 parts | Yes |

The total test time is about 2 hours 45 minutes.

<div class="myth">

**"Spelling doesn't matter in Listening and Reading."** It does. A correct answer spelled wrongly is marked wrong. British and American spellings are both accepted, but "acommodation" is not. Lesson 2.3 and Lesson 10.4 cover the words people misspell most.

</div>

<div class="keypoints">

- Listening: 4 parts, 40 questions, about 30 minutes; each recording is played once.
- Reading: 3 sections, 40 questions, 60 minutes with **no extra transfer time**.
- Writing: Task 1 (150+ words, about 20 min) and Task 2 (250+ words, about 40 min, worth twice as much).
- Speaking: 3 parts, 11 to 14 minutes, a recorded conversation with an examiner.
- Spelling counts in Listening and Reading answers.

</div>
