<div class="bigidea">Part 1 is mostly names, numbers and dates. They are "free marks" only if you have trained your ear for the sounds that are easy to confuse.</div>

## 1. The letters that cause trouble

English letter names do not match many other languages. Say each group aloud and notice the vowel sound they share:

| Sound | Letters | Often confused |
|---|---|---|
| "ay" (as in day) | A, H, J, K | A and E; H and 8 |
| "ee" (as in see) | B, C, D, E, G, P, T, V, Z (British "zed", American "zee") | B and V; D and T; G and J |
| "e" (as in bed) | F, L, M, N, S, X, Z (British) | M and N; F and S |
| "eye" | I, Y | I and E; Y and "why" |
| "oh" | O | O and zero |
| "you" | Q, U, W ("double-you") | U and "you" |
| "ah" | R | R and "are" |

People spelling on the phone often say **"double"** for two same letters ("double L" = LL) and may add a word to help: "B for bravo", "M as in mother".

## 2. Numbers

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>-teen or -ty?</b> 13 to 19 have stress at the end: thir-TEEN, four-TEEN. 30 to 90 have stress at the start: THIR-ty, FOR-ty. In fast speech the final "n" can be hard to hear, so listen for the <b>stress</b>, not the ending.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Big numbers.</b> British English puts "and" before the tens: "two thousand, four hundred <i>and</i> sixty" = 2,460. "One point five million" = 1.5 million (or 1,500,000).</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Phone numbers.</b> Read digit by digit, in groups: "oh one two three, double four, five six" = 0123 445 6. "Oh" is zero. "Double" repeats the digit, and "treble" or "triple" makes three.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>Prices.</b> "Nine ninety-nine" = £9.99. "Twelve fifty" = £12.50. "A hundred and twenty" = 120. Write the currency only if the sign is not already printed next to the gap.</p></div>
</div>

## 3. Dates and times

**Dates** can be said "the fifth of May" or "May the fifth" (British), or "May fifth" (American). Write **5 May** or **May 5**: both are accepted. Avoid 5/5, which is ambiguous.

**Times**: "quarter past seven" = 7.15; "half past" = .30; "quarter to eight" = 7.45; "twenty to six" = 5.40. In the 24-hour clock, "eighteen hundred" = 18.00. If "am" or "pm" is already printed in the question, don't add it.

## 4. The traps

1. **Corrections**: "It's on the 14th. No, sorry, the 15th." Always wait for the final answer.
2. **Two numbers close together**: "Normally it's £35, but this week it's £29." The question tells you which one you need.
3. **Spelled and then repeated**: the speaker spells a name, the listener reads it back with a mistake, and the speaker corrects it. Write the corrected version.
4. **Units in the gap**: if the form says "£___", write only the number. Writing "£29 pounds" or "29 pounds" can break the word limit.

<div class="myth">

**"Capitals matter."** For names you may write capitals or small letters: "okafor", "Okafor" and "OKAFOR" are all accepted. But the spelling must be exactly right, letter by letter.

</div>
