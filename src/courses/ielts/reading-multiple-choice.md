**Module 4 · about 15 minutes.** By the end you will answer multiple-choice questions by proving the wrong options wrong, and you will handle the two hardest kinds: "choose TWO" and the writer's purpose.

<div class="bigidea">In a well-made multiple-choice question, every wrong option is wrong for a reason you can point to in the text. Find those reasons and the right answer is what is left.</div>

## 1. Three kinds

| Kind | Example | Where the answer is |
|---|---|---|
| Detail | "What did the 2019 study find?" | one or two sentences |
| Choose TWO or THREE | "Which TWO problems does the writer mention?" | spread over a paragraph or more |
| Global | "What is the writer's main purpose?" "Which is the best title?" | the whole passage |

Detail questions follow the order of the passage. Global questions usually come last.

## 2. The method for detail questions

<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><p><b>1. Read the stem only</b> (the question, not the options). Underline its key words and find the place in the text.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>2. Read the place</b> and say the answer in your own words <i>before</i> you look at the options.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>3. Now read A to D</b> and match your words to one option.</p></div>
<div class="step"><p><b>4. Prove the others wrong.</b> For each other option, find the reason: contradicted, not mentioned, or about something else. If you cannot find a reason, reread.</p></div>
</div>

## 3. Why wrong options are wrong

Writers of tests build distractors in a few standard ways. Learn the families:

| Distractor | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Word match | uses a word from the text with a different meaning | text: "the bank of the river"; option: "a bank loan" |
| Too strong | turns "some", "may", "often" into "all", "will", "always" | "proved" for "suggested" |
| Wrong subject | true of something else in the passage | true of wild bees, not honey bees |
| Not said | sounds sensible, but the text never says it | real-world knowledge |
| Opposite | reverses the relation | "led to" for "was caused by" |
| Half right | first half matches, second half does not | "rose and then fell" when it only rose |

## 4. Worked example

Text: *"A study of one European capital found that, as hive numbers rose over a decade, the amount of nectar and pollen available per colony fell... The authors could not show directly that wild bees were harmed, but they warned that the city might be close to its limit."*

What did the study conclude?

- A that wild bees in the city had declined sharply
- B that the city may have nearly as many hives as it can support
- C that honey bees should be removed from the capital
- D that each hive was producing more honey than before

My words first: "maybe too many hives soon". **B** matches ("close to its limit"). Now prove the rest wrong: A is **too strong** ("could not show directly that wild bees were harmed"); C is **not said**; D is the **opposite** (the honey fell).

## 5. Choose TWO

The options cover a whole section. Treat each option as a True/False question: find it in the text and decide. Exactly two will be TRUE (stated); the others will be FALSE or NOT GIVEN. Write one letter per answer box; the order does not matter.

## 6. The writer's purpose

Read the first and last paragraphs again and ask: "Why did the writer write this? To explain, to argue, to compare, to describe, to warn?" Then reject:

- options that match **only one paragraph**;
- options with a **stronger opinion** than the writer shows ("to argue that X should be banned" when the writer only raises doubts);
- options that are **true but too narrow** ("to describe the history of rooftop hives" when history is one paragraph).

<div class="myth">

**"The option that uses words from the text is the right one."** Often it is the reverse. Correct options usually paraphrase; options that copy words are a classic word-match trap.

</div>

## 7. Practise

<div class="keypoints">

- Read the stem, find the place, answer in your own words, then read the options.
- Prove each wrong option wrong: word match, too strong, wrong subject, not said, opposite, half right.
- Choose TWO: test each option like True/False; one mark per correct letter.
- Purpose questions cover the whole passage; reject options that fit one paragraph or are too strong.
- Copied words are a warning sign, not a guarantee.
