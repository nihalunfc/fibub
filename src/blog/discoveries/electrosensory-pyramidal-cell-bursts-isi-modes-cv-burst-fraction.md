Weakly electric fish sense their surroundings through an electric field they generate themselves, and the first brain area that reads that field, the electrosensory lateral line lobe, is full of **pyramidal cells that fire in bursts**: two or three spikes packed close together, then a pause. A 2025 paper in *PLOS Computational Biology* recorded 32 of these cells in live fish and asked whether two very different models, one built from ion channels and one a stripped-down three-variable model, can reproduce what the cells do. The models are judged on six numbers measured from the spike trains, and the [[spike-train-analyser]] computes all six in a browser. To go the other way and build spikes from conductances, use the [[hodgkin-huxley-simulator]]; for imaging instead of electrodes, the [[calcium-imaging-dff]] tool; and for the mains hum that spoils an electrode recording, the [[notch-filter-psd]].

## The paper in brief

The authors recorded pyramidal cells in the hindbrain of *Apteronotus leptorhynchus* with no stimulus applied, so what they measured is the cells' own baseline activity: firing rates **between 5 and 60 Hz** across 32 cells from 8 fish. In vivo those cells burst in twos and threes, not the 5 to 8 spike bursts seen in slices, and in a representative cell the intervals inside a burst cluster around **12 ms** while the gaps between bursts cluster near **100 ms**.

They then fitted two models per cell. The first is a biophysical model with the usual sodium and potassium currents plus the dendritic feedback thought to cause the bursting. The second is a modified Hindmarsh-Rose model, which has no ion channels at all, only three abstract variables. Both were scored by correlating six measured statistics across the 32 cells: mean firing rate, burst fraction, mean interval, median interval, the SD of the intervals and the **CV**, the SD divided by the mean. The biophysical model scored 0.99, 0.88, 0.95, 0.92, 0.83 and 0.98; the simpler model was close behind. Their conclusion is that you do not need the channels to get the spike statistics right, which matters because the simple model runs hundreds of times faster.

## Checked with the tools

Two different things happen in this section, and it is worth keeping them apart. Step 1 is **arithmetic on the paper's own stated numbers**. Steps 2 to 4 use **simulated spike trains we built** from the paper's burst description, to ask how much each of its six statistics can actually tell you. Nothing here is the paper's recorded data, which is not in the text.

**Step 1: is the burst description consistent with the reported rates?** If a cell fires *n* spikes per burst with 12 ms between them and 100 ms between bursts, each burst cycle lasts (n − 1) × 12 + 100 ms and delivers *n* spikes. That gives:

| Spikes per burst | Firing rate implied |
|---|---|
| 2 | 17.9 Hz |
| 2.5 | 21.2 Hz |
| 3 | 24.2 Hz |

All three sit comfortably inside the 5 to 60 Hz the paper reports, in the lower middle of it. The two interval modes and the rate range are telling the same story, which is a small but real consistency check: three stated numbers, one of which is implied by the other two.

**Step 2: a burster and a Poisson cell, side by side.** We built 40 trials of 5 s with 2 or 3 spikes per burst, gamma-distributed intervals whose modes sit at the paper's 12 ms and 100 ms, and a 3 ms dead time, and a Poisson train matched to the same mean rate. Then we built a third train: the same bursts, but with the gaps between bursts drawn from an exponential distribution instead, so the burst clock itself is random. Read with the tool:

| Statistic | Bursting (regular clock) | Poisson, same rate | Bursting (Poisson clock) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean firing rate | 18.1 Hz | 18.0 Hz | 17.6 Hz |
| Mean interval | 54.0 ms | 55.3 ms | 55.6 ms |
| Median interval | **15.5 ms** | **36.9 ms** | **14.6 ms** |
| SD of interval | 59.4 ms | 56.6 ms | 93.8 ms |
| CV of interval | **1.10** | **1.02** | **1.69** |
| Fano factor of counts | **0.28** | 0.73 | **1.63** |
| Intervals under 30 ms | 60.7 % | 42.9 % | 69.6 % |

The lesson is blunt. **Mean rate, mean interval and the SD of the interval barely move** between a burster and a Poisson cell at the same rate: the first three rows are nearly identical. The **median interval** separates them at a glance (15 ms against 37 ms), because more than half of a burster's intervals are inside bursts. The **CV** separates them only weakly, 1.10 against 1.02, because a regular burst clock pulls the CV down just as bursting pushes it up. And the **Fano factor**, which counts spikes per trial rather than intervals, measures the burst *clock*, not the bursting: 0.28 for a regular clock and 1.63 for a random one, with the identical burst structure inside.

So a model that matches mean rate, mean interval and SD of interval has matched almost nothing; a model that matches the median interval and the burst fraction has matched something real. The paper's six statistics are not six independent tests, and its two weakest correlations (SD of interval, 0.83, and burst fraction, 0.88) are on the two statistics that carry the most information about bursting.

**Step 3: the interval histogram is where bursting shows.** Figure 1 puts the burster's and the Poisson cell's interval histograms on one axis. The burster has two humps, one at the paper's 12 ms and one near 110 ms; the Poisson cell has one, falling away smoothly from zero. No summary statistic shows this as plainly as the shape does, which is why the paper compares interval distributions with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test as well as correlating the six numbers.

**Step 5: how CV and rate work together.** Figure 3 varies the gap between bursts from 30 to 450 ms for 1, 2, 3 and 4 spikes per burst. Every burst size covers the same range of firing rates, so **the rate alone tells you nothing about burst size**, but at a given rate the CV rises steadily with it: near 22 Hz it is about 0.41 for single spikes, 0.88 for twos, 1.17 for threes and 1.39 for fours. Read the other way, a cell with a CV of 1.1 at 20 Hz is firing in twos or threes, which is what the paper reports in vivo.

## How we made the graphs

Every number and all three charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/electrosensory-pyramidal-cell-bursts-isi-modes-cv-burst-fraction.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `summarise`, `isis`, `histogram` and `autocorrelogram` from `src/lib/sci/spikes.ts`, the maths behind the [[spike-train-analyser]].

From the paper: 2 to 3 spikes per burst, an intra-burst interval mode of 12 ms, an inter-burst mode of about 100 ms, 5 s records and the 5 to 60 Hz rate range. **Our construction**: gamma intervals of shape 16 inside bursts and shape 6 between them (a gamma with shape *k* and mean *m* has its mode at *m*(*k* − 1)/*k*, so means of 12.8 and 120 ms put the modes at 12 and 100 ms), a 3 ms dead time, 40 trials, the exponential-gap variant and the Poisson comparison. **The trains are simulated. They are not the paper's recordings**, which are not published as numbers in the text.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/spike-train-analyser.mjs` on every change to fibub, checked against an independent NumPy 2.5 calculation:

- On the tool's own 20-trial example, the mean rate must be 12.7 spikes/s, the Fano factor 0.9714048901782014 and the pooled CV 1.6287401178350271, matching `np.diff` and `np.var`.
- The 40-bin PSTH, the 50-bin autocorrelogram and the 50-bin interval histogram must match `np.histogram` bin for bin, including NumPy's rule that the last bin includes its right edge.
- A long simulated Poisson train must come back with a CV within 0.03 of 1 and a Fano factor within 0.25 of 1, which is the sanity check behind every comparison in this article.
- New with this article: the 33 spike times of our bursting trial (below) must give a rate of 16.5 spikes/s, a mean interval of 56.34 ms and a pooled CV of 1.1154, and a 10 ms interval histogram whose second bin holds 16 of its 32 intervals.

4. The result shows *Trains* **1**, *Spikes in window* **33**, *Mean firing rate* **16.50 spikes/s**, *CV of ISIs (pooled)* **1.12** and *ISIs under 2 ms* **0 of 32**. *Fano factor (counts)* says **needs 2+ trains**, which is the point of Step 2: the Fano factor is a between-trial measure.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report the median interval, not just the mean**, when cells burst: it is the one single number that separates a burster from a Poisson cell at the same rate.
- **Say what your Fano factor is measuring.** Over trials it reads the burst clock's regularity; a bursting cell can have a Fano factor well below 1 if its bursts are evenly spaced.
- **Check a model fit the hard way**: match the interval histogram and the autocorrelogram, not only a list of summary numbers that mostly repeat each other.
- **Set an ISI threshold for burst detection** by looking at where the histogram's valley sits, instead of inheriting a threshold from another paper.
- **Flag sorting errors** with the refractory-period count before any of the statistics are believed.

## The fine print

- The spike trains here are **simulated** from the paper's stated burst description; the paper's recordings are presented in figures, not as numbers.
- The paper reports its interval modes for one representative cell. Across 32 cells they will vary, and our two gamma shapes are a guess at that spread.
- Our comparison asks how well each statistic separates burst structures. It says nothing about whether the paper's models are good, which its own fits answer.
- A real cell's bursts are not independent of each other, and real recordings include slow rate drift. Both change the CV and the Fano factor.
- Burst fraction depends on the threshold used to define a burst, so two papers' burst fractions are comparable only if their thresholds are.
