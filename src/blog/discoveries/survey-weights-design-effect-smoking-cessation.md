When a survey says "66.9 % of people who tried to quit smoking failed", two different pieces of arithmetic stand behind it. One is **weighting**: the people interviewed are not a miniature of the city, so each answer counts for more or fewer residents. The other is the **design effect**, how much less precise a clustered, weighted sample is than a simple random one of the same size. A 2026 survey of 7,183 residents in Chengdu, published in *Frontiers in Public Health*, reports both: a weighted failure rate of 66.9 % with a design effect of 2.95 and an effective sample size of 312. This article rebuilds those numbers with the [[survey-weighting]] tool, and splits the design effect into the part the weights cause and the part the clustering causes. For what it means for planning, see [[sample-size]]; for the cross-tabulations that follow, [[crosstab-chi-square]].

## The paper in brief

The team sampled Chengdu in four stages: all 23 districts and counties, three townships in each chosen with probability proportional to size, two communities in each township, 55 households in each community, and one resident aged 15 or over per household picked with a Kish table. That gave 7,183 usable interviews. Weights were built as the sampling weight times a non-response adjustment times a post-stratification factor on age and sex against the 2020 census, and they add to **18,047,490**, Chengdu's resident population aged 15 and over.

Among the 923 people who had ever tried to quit, **580 failed and 343 succeeded**. The weighted failure rate is **66.9 %** with a design-based standard error of 2.66 %, a **design effect of 2.95** and an **effective sample size of 312**. The paper's substantive findings are about who fails: younger smokers, heavier smokers, those without a firm intention to quit and those exposed to other people's smoke.

## Checked with the tools

All of this is a **recomputation from figures stated in the paper**. We did not get the data; we rebuilt a weight for every one of the 923 ever-quitters from the published margins, which is exactly what a reader with a photocopy of the tables can do.

**Step 1: the design effect, from the paper's own two numbers.** A simple random sample of 923 people would estimate a 66.9 % rate with a standard error of √(0.669 × 0.331 / 923) = **1.549 %**. The paper's design-based standard error is 2.66 %. The design effect is the ratio of variances, (2.66 / 1.549)² = **2.949**, and the effective sample size is 923 / 2.949 = **313**. The paper reports 2.95 and 312. So those two numbers are internally consistent, and the 95 % interval around the headline is **61.7 % to 72.1 %**, which the paper does not print.

**Step 2: rebuild the weights.** The paper gives, for each age group, how many ever-quitters were interviewed and what share of the weight they carry. Dividing one by the other gives the weight of each cell relative to an average respondent:

| Age group | Share of the 923 interviewed | Share of the weight | Weight of a respondent, failed / succeeded |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15-35 | 13.1 % | 29.7 % | 2.19 / 2.67 |
| 36-55 | 31.9 % | 32.8 % | 1.00 / 1.12 |
| over 55 | 55.0 % | 37.5 % | 0.66 / 0.70 |

A young ever-quitter counts for about **three times** as much as an older one. That is the whole reason the weighted failure rate (66.9 %) sits four points above the raw one (580 / 923 = **62.84 %**): young smokers are under-represented in a household survey and more likely to relapse. Feeding these six cell weights into the tool returns a weighted failure rate of **66.90 %**, as it must, and shows where the shift comes from.

**Step 3: post-stratify from scratch with the tool.** Starting from equal weights and raking the 923 respondents to the age margins alone (29.71 %, 32.83 %, 37.46 %), the tool's own post-stratification gives weights of **2.2665, 1.0306 and 0.6806** times the average, adding to exactly 18,047,490, and a weighted failure rate of **68.90 %**. That is two points above the published 66.9 %, and the gap is informative: age alone would push the estimate further than the paper's weights do, because the real weights also carry the sampling and non-response factors, which differ *within* each age group (notice how the "succeeded" column of the table above is slightly heavier than the "failed" one at every age).

**Step 4: split the design effect.** Kish's design effect from unequal weights is n Σw² / (Σw)². On the six-cell weights it is **1.272**, an effective sample size of 726. The published design effect is 2.949. Dividing one by the other leaves **2.32** for everything the weights do not explain, which is mostly clustering: the survey interviewed about 923 / 138 = **6.7 ever-quitters per sampled community**. With deff = 1 + (m − 1)ρ, that implies an intra-cluster correlation of about **ρ = 0.23** for quit failure within a community, a plausible value for a behaviour that travels in neighbourhoods and social circles.

So of the 611 respondents' worth of precision lost (923 down to 312), roughly a quarter is the price of the weights and three quarters the price of sampling whole communities. Figure 2 puts that in the units a reader cares about: the margin of error on 66.9 % is ±3.0 points for a simple random sample of 923, ±3.4 with the weights, and ±5.2 as published.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/survey-weights-design-effect-smoking-cessation.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `rake`, `kish`, `weightedPct` and `parseMargin` from `src/lib/sci/surveyw.ts`, the maths behind the [[survey-weighting]] tool.

The counts, weighted shares, standard error, design effect, effective sample size, weight total and sample structure are **stated in the paper**. The per-respondent weights are **our reconstruction** from those margins: they reproduce the published age shares and failure rate by construction, but they cannot show how weights vary *inside* a cell, so our 1.272 is a **lower bound** on the weighting design effect. The intra-cluster correlation is **our inference** from the two design effects and the average cluster size.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/survey-weighting.mjs`, which pins this maths against an independent Python implementation:

- Raking a 40-respondent example on three variables must give the same weights as a numpy iterative-proportional-fitting routine and as the raking calibration equations of Deville and Särndal solved with `scipy.optimize.root` (smallest weight 0.48163736, largest 2.17694145), with a Kish design effect of 1.2576859940 and an effective sample size of 31.80444101.
- Post-stratification on one variable must be exact in a single pass, and every margin must be hit to within 10⁻¹³.
- Raking to a population total of 5,000,000 with design weights must still reproduce the target total exactly and the same design effect.
- New with this article: raking 923 respondents whose age shares are 13.11 %, 31.85 % and 55.04 % to margins of 29.71 %, 32.83 % and 37.46 % must give weights 2.2665, 1.0306 and 0.6806 times the average, a Kish design effect of 1.2667, and a weighted failure rate of 68.90 %; and the six-cell reconstruction must give a design effect of 1.2717 and a weighted rate of 66.90 %.

## Check it yourself

The paper's 923 rows are not something you can type, so here is the same mechanism on a 20-person stand-in (**our own made-up rows**, with roughly the paper's age mix). On the [[survey-weighting]] tool:

1. In *Respondents*, paste this table (the first line is the header):
   `ID, Age, Outcome` then, one per line, `R1, 15-35, failed`; `R2, 15-35, failed`; `R3, 15-35, quit`; `R4, 36-55, failed`; `R5, 36-55, failed`; `R6, 36-55, failed`; `R7, 36-55, failed`; `R8, 36-55, quit`; `R9, 36-55, quit`; `R10, >55, failed`; `R11, >55, failed`; `R12, >55, failed`; `R13, >55, failed`; `R14, >55, failed`; `R15, >55, quit`; `R16, >55, quit`; `R17, >55, quit`; `R18, >55, quit`; `R19, >55, quit`; `R20, >55, quit`.
2. Leave **Design weight column** on *None (all equal)*, set **Method** to *Post-stratification (first variable)* and **Weights add up to** to *Sample size*.
3. Set **Variable 1** to *Age* and type the census-implied margins in its *Population figures* box, one per line: `15-35, 29.71`, `36-55, 32.83`, `>55, 37.46`.
4. The weights come out as **1.9807** for the youngest, **1.0943** for the middle and **0.6811** for the oldest group, with a **Design effect (Kish)** of **1.203** and an **Effective sample size** of **16.63** out of 20.
5. The *Weighted percentages* table lists every column: the *Outcome* row for "failed" reads **55.0 %** in the *Sample* column and **58.7 %** in the *Weighted* column, the same upward shift the real survey shows (our exact figure is 58.72 %).
6. Switch **Weights add up to** to *Population size* and enter a population to see the weights scale to whole residents while the percentages and the design effect stay put.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recover a design effect from a published standard error** in one line, and get the confidence interval a paper leaves out.
- **Rebuild the weights of someone else's survey** from its published margins, to see which subgroup is doing the work behind a weighted estimate.
- **Separate weighting from clustering** before blaming your weights: in this survey the weights cost a quarter of the precision and the clustering three quarters.
- **Plan the next round.** An intra-cluster correlation of about 0.23 and 6.7 interviews per community means more communities and fewer households each; feed that into [[sample-size]].
- **Check a weighted subgroup is not resting on a handful of people** by reading the effective sample size rather than the raw count.

## The fine print

- Our per-respondent weights are a **reconstruction** from published margins, not the survey's own weight file. They match the published age shares and failure rate by construction, so the 1.272 design effect is a floor, not an estimate of the real weight variation.
- Kish's formula treats the weights as fixed and ignores clustering; the paper's 2.95 is a full design-based figure with 21 degrees of freedom. The split in Step 4 is an approximation that assumes the two effects multiply.
- The intra-cluster correlation uses an average cluster size; real clusters vary, which usually inflates the design effect a little further.
- We checked the arithmetic of a published estimate, not the survey's fieldwork, its definition of a quit attempt or its regression findings.
- Self-reported smoking and quit attempts are what they are: a weighting tool cannot repair a misremembered answer.
