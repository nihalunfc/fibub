A small plasma thruster is a cheap place to test a big idea: wrap a coil around the discharge, and the magnetic field should keep the fast electrons inside, so each watt of input makes more ions. A 2025 paper in the *Iraqi Journal of Physics* did exactly that with a laboratory argon engine, and its Langmuir probe found a denser, cooler plasma once the coil was on. The question a reader is left with is which particles a field of 9 or 25 mT can really hold, and that is a question of lengths and times rather than of opinion. The [[plasma-parameters]] calculator answers it from the paper's own density and temperature. For the measurement behind those numbers see the [[langmuir-probe]] tool, for the drift motions the field allows see [[particle-drifts]], and for the breakdown that lights the discharge in the first place see the [[paschen-curve]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ran a cylindrical argon plasma engine in a vacuum chamber at **0.2 mbar**, with 5 kV on the electrodes and 25 to 100 W of power, and put a coil around it that produced **9 mT and 25 mT**. The engine body is about 5 cm long, 5.5 cm wide and 0.7 cm thick. A Langmuir probe gave an electron density of about **2 to 6 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³** and an electron temperature of about **2 to 4 eV** with the field on.

Their reading is that the field keeps energetic electrons near the axis, so ionisation there goes up: density rises while the electron temperature falls, and the engine needs less energy to make the same plasma. Density up and temperature down together is the signature of better confinement rather than of harder heating, so the claim hangs together. What the abstract does not say is *how* magnetised that plasma is, and whether the ions feel the field at all.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated density, temperature and field**, not new measurements. Two numbers are **our assumptions**: the ion temperature, taken as 0.03 eV (room-temperature argon ions, usual for a cold low-pressure discharge, and not measured by a simple probe), and singly charged Ar⁺ ions. The plasma size is the paper's own 5 cm.

**Step 1: it is a textbook ideal plasma, and the probe is allowed.** At the middle of the measured range (nₑ 4 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³, Tₑ 3 eV) the tool gives a **Debye length of 2.04 µm**, about 1/25,000 of the engine, with **1,414 electrons inside a Debye sphere**. Both conditions for the usual plasma formulas, and for Langmuir-probe theory, are comfortably met. The electron plasma frequency is **56.8 GHz**, so microwaves below that are reflected: a 2.45 GHz interferometer would not get through this plasma at all.

**Step 2: the field grips the electrons easily.** An electron's thermal gyroradius is **0.165 mm at 25 mT** and 0.53 mm at 9 mT in the thin, hot corner of the range. Against an engine 50 mm long and 7 mm thick, that is a tight leash, and it is the mechanism the paper describes. Argon ions are a different story: their gyroradius is **4.46 mm at 25 mT** and **12.4 mm at 9 mT**, the latter larger than the engine's thickness. The field sorts the species: electrons are tied to field lines, ions are not.

**Step 3: how many turns before a collision?** A gyroradius smaller than the chamber is not enough; a particle must complete turns between collisions. The tool's Hall parameter ωτ answers that. For electrons it is **21.9 at 25 mT** and 3.1 at the dense, cool end at 9 mT, so electrons are magnetised throughout the paper's range; the crossing point ωceτe = 1 sits at only about **1.1 mT** (2.9 mT at the dense end). For argon ions ωciτi is about **1 × 10⁻⁴**: ion-ion collisions at 0.03 eV and 4 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³ are so frequent that it would take roughly **220 T**, four thousand times the coil's field, to magnetise them. Nothing a small laboratory coil does touches the ions directly.

**Step 4: the field is strong enough to matter, but not overwhelming.** Plasma beta, the ratio of particle pressure to magnetic pressure, is **0.078 at 25 mT** and **0.60 at 9 mT** (for the dense, cool end of the range). At 9 mT the plasma pressure is comparable to the field's, so the discharge can push the field around; at 25 mT the field is clearly in charge. That is a quantitative reason to expect the bigger field to work better, and it comes straight from the paper's own numbers.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/argon-plasma-engine-magnetic-field-debye-gyroradius.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `plasma()` from `src/lib/sci/phplasma.ts`, the maths behind the [[plasma-parameters]] calculator, which returns the Debye length, Debye number, plasma and cyclotron frequencies, thermal speeds, gyroradii, Braginskii collision times, mean free paths, Hall parameters, beta and Spitzer resistivity in one call. The field that just magnetises each species is found by bisection on the tool's own Hall parameter.

Densities (2 to 6 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³), electron temperatures (2 to 4 eV), the fields (0, 9, 25 mT), the pressure and the engine sizes come from the paper. The ion temperature of 0.03 eV, the choice of Ar⁺ with Z = 1, and reading the plasma size as the stated 5 cm length are **our assumptions**.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/plasma-parameters.mjs`, which pins this maths against the NRL Plasma Formulary's shortcut formulas and an independent SciPy script:

- A fusion-like plasma (10²⁰ m⁻³, 10 keV, 5 T, deuterium) must give a Debye length of 7.43 × 10⁻⁵ m and an electron plasma frequency of 89.8 GHz, the formulary's 7.43 × 10² √(Tₑ)/√n cm and 8.98 × 10³ √n Hz.
- The same plasma's Braginskii electron collision time must be 2.013 × 10⁻⁴ s and its beta 0.0322, both as the formulary gives them.
- New with this article: an argon discharge at 4 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³, Tₑ 3 eV, Tᵢ 0.03 eV and 25 mT must give a Debye length of 2.035 µm, electron and ion gyroradii of 0.1652 mm and 4.461 mm, an electron Hall parameter of 21.87 and an ion Hall parameter of 1.15 × 10⁻⁴.

4. *Ion* **Ar⁺**, which fills *Ion mass (u)* **40** and *Ion charge Z* **1**. *Plasma size L* **0.05**. Leave *Coulomb logarithm* blank.
5. The headline reads **Debye length 2.04 µm, plasma frequency 56.8 GHz, β = 0.0781**, and the pills say *Ideal plasma*, *Quasi-neutral*, *Collisional* and *Magnetised electrons*.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Decide how big a coil has to be** before building it: sweep the field until the electron Hall parameter passes 1 for your density and temperature, as we did here (about 1 mT for this discharge).
- **Sanity-check probe data**: if the Debye length is not far below the probe size, or N_D is near 1, the standard Langmuir-probe analysis in the [[langmuir-probe]] tool does not apply.
- **Choose a diagnostic frequency**: the electron plasma frequency says whether a microwave interferometer can pass through at all, and the cyclotron frequency says where to look for emission.
- **Separate the species in a model**: the Hall parameters tell you at a glance whether you need magnetised ions in your simulation, or only magnetised electrons with collisional ions.
- **Teach the regimes**: one page shows why a 25 mT coil changes electron transport and leaves the ions to collisions, and the [[particle-drifts]] simulator then shows what those magnetised electrons actually do.

## The fine print

- The paper's numbers are ranges from an abstract (2 to 6 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³, 2 to 4 eV); we compute at the ends and the middle, and no single case is "the" plasma.
- The ion temperature is our assumption. A hotter ion population would lengthen the ion collision time and raise ωciτi, but by nothing like the four orders of magnitude the ions would need.
- The collision times are the classical Coulomb ones. In a 0.2 mbar discharge, electron-neutral collisions are also important and would lower the electron Hall parameter; our value is therefore an upper bound.
- We could not read the journal's article page from here, so we worked from the abstract's values and the Crossref record, which gives the CC BY 4.0 licence and shows no correction.
- Classical, non-relativistic formulas for study and planning, not a design authority for hardware.
