Ask a chatbot for an essay and you get prose that uses more different words and is harder to read than what a student writes in half an hour. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Education* put numbers on both halves of that sentence: 50 GPT-4 Turbo texts against 50 timed in-class essays by non-native English speakers, compared on word variety, sentence structure and two readability formulas. The numbers are short and bare, which makes them a good test of what such measures actually mean, and the [[lexical-diversity-readability]] tool computes every one of them in the browser. For the corpus work either side of that step, the [[corpus-concordancer]] shows you the words in context and the [[abstract-word-counter]] keeps a draft inside a word limit.

## The paper in brief

**Lexical diversity** asks how much a text repeats itself. The oldest measure, the **type-token ratio** (TTR), divides the number of different words by the number of words. It has a known flaw: a longer text has more chances to repeat, so TTR falls with length even when the writing does not change. **MTLD** avoids that by asking how many words you can read before the running ratio drops below 0.72, and reporting the average length of such a stretch, in words. **vocd-D** and its exact cousin **HD-D** work out, from the word frequencies alone, the ratio a random sample of a fixed size would be expected to show.

The paper reports, for ChatGPT against students: TTR **0.69** against **0.61**, MTLD **118** against **66.56**, vocd-D **105** against **69.73**. On structure, ChatGPT's mean **T-unit** (a main clause with whatever hangs off it) is **15.91** words against the students' 23.61, but it packs **0.75** dependent clauses into each T-unit against 0.57. On readability, ChatGPT lands at Flesch-Kincaid grade **16.61** and Gunning fog **20.10**, the students at **12.12** and **14.18**. No test statistics, p-values or word counts are given.

## Checked with the tools

Those numbers are summary statistics with no spread and no sample sizes attached, so they cannot be re-tested. What they can be is **interrogated**: every one of the six measures is a fixed formula, so we can ask what combinations of writing produce them, and whether the set hangs together. That is pure recomputation, and all of it runs on the maths inside the [[lexical-diversity-readability]] tool.

**Step 1: the length trap, and why this paper avoids it.** We built model texts by drawing words from one fixed vocabulary whose frequencies follow a Zipf law, so the "writer" never changes, and measured them at lengths from 60 to 1000 words. TTR slides from **0.700** to **0.433**; MATTR over a 50-word window goes from 0.747 to 0.789 and HD-D from 0.739 to 0.804, both essentially flat (Figure 1). So TTR is a measure of length as much as of vocabulary. The paper matched its ChatGPT word counts to the student average, which is exactly the right precaution, and its TTR comparison is therefore fair, even though the absolute value means little on its own.

**Step 2: a 0.08 gap in TTR and a 51-word gap in MTLD are the same finding.** At first sight the paper's TTR gap looks small (13 % relative) while its MTLD gap looks huge (77 %). They are not in conflict: MTLD is a stretched scale. On our 350-word model texts, a TTR of 0.61 goes with an MTLD of **114.7** and a TTR of 0.69 with **218.6** (Figure 2), so a TTR ratio of 1.13 becomes an MTLD ratio of 1.91. The paper's own MTLD ratio, 1.77, is slightly *less* than that, meaning its two numbers are mutually consistent rather than one of them being overstated.

**Step 3: an anchor in real prose.** Random word draws are an upper bound on how varied real writing looks, because real text repeats its function words close together. For a fair reference we wrote a plain 359-word expository passage for this article and ran it through the tool: **201 different words, TTR 0.560, root TTR 10.61, MATTR 0.802, MTLD 83.47, HD-D 0.838, Yule's K 127.4**. Our passage therefore sits near the paper's student group (TTR 0.61, MTLD 66.56) and well below its ChatGPT group. A TTR of **0.69** in a text of a few hundred words is high for ordinary expository English, so it is worth knowing which words the paper counted: lemmas rather than word forms, or content words only, would lift it.

**Step 4: what the readability grades demand.** The Flesch-Kincaid grade is 0.39 × words per sentence + 11.8 × syllables per word − 15.59, and the Gunning fog index is 0.4 × (words per sentence + the percentage of complex words). Both were reported for both groups, so each group gives two equations in three unknowns, and we can draw the whole set of writing styles that fit. The fog index pins the sum: for ChatGPT, words per sentence plus the complex-word percentage must equal **50.25**; for the students, **35.45**. Figure 3 then shows the syllables per word each grade needs.

The result is pointed. If ChatGPT's sentences were as short as its T-units (15.91 words), a grade of 16.61 would need **2.203 syllables per word** and the fog index **34.3 %** complex words: no English does that. At 25 words per sentence it needs 1.903 syllables and 25.3 %; only at about **30 words per sentence** do the figures become ordinary hard prose (**1.737** syllables per word, **20.3 %** complex words). So ChatGPT's sentences must have held roughly **two T-units each**, which fits its reported 0.75 dependent clauses per T-unit. For the students, their own T-unit length of 23.61 words gives **1.568 syllables per word** and **11.8 %** complex words straight away: plain, ordinary writing. For comparison, our own deliberately plain passage measures 1.409 syllables per word and 6.4 % complex words, at grade 9.28.

## How we made the graphs

The script is `scripts/blog-figures/chatgpt-vs-student-essays-lexical-diversity-mtld-readability-recheck.mjs`. It calls three functions from `src/lib/sci/lexdiv.ts`, the engine behind the [[lexical-diversity-readability]] tool: `tokens` for word splitting, `diversity` for TTR, root TTR, MATTR, MTLD, HD-D and Yule's K, and `readability` for the Flesch, Flesch-Kincaid, fog, SMOG, Coleman-Liau and ARI scores plus the raw counts behind them.

The six reported values, the two T-unit lengths and the clause density come from the paper's text. The Zipf model texts (3000-word vocabulary, exponents 0.6 to 1.4, one fixed random seed) are **our model**, not the paper's data, and they are a model of word choice with no grammar. The two English passages are **our own writing**, included so that every number can be reproduced exactly. The feasible-set calculation in step 4 uses only the two published formulas and the paper's own reported grades.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/lexical-diversity-readability.mjs` on every change. It pins the tool against an independent Python script (plain window slicing for MATTR and MTLD, `scipy.stats.hypergeom` for HD-D, `Counter` for Yule's K, the readability formulas applied to the counted words, sentences and syllables):

- on one fixed example text, all 23 outputs must agree with Python: the counts, TTR, root TTR, log TTR, MATTR, MTLD, HD-D, Yule's K and the six readability scores;
- with the settings changed, MATTR over a 20-word window must be 0.919784, MTLD at a threshold of 0.8 must be 74.511364, and HD-D with a 30-word sample 0.884345;
- hand-checkable edge cases must hold: Yule's K of "a b a b" is exactly 2500, MTLD of a text with no repeats is infinite, and HD-D of one word repeated 50 times is 1/42;
- the syllable rules, the sentence splitter and the fog index's "complex word" rule must give the right answer on a list of known words ("interested" counts, "agreeing" and "well-organised" do not).
- New with this article: the 111-word passage below must give 111 words, 78 types, TTR 0.702703, MATTR 0.815806, MTLD 79.265107, HD-D 0.816503, Flesch 69.922365 and Flesch-Kincaid grade 8.102477.

## Check it yourself

On the [[lexical-diversity-readability]] page:

1. Clear the box *Paste English text* and paste this 111-word passage (written for this article):

   > A measure of lexical diversity puts a number on repetition. The oldest one divides the different words by all the words, which punishes a long text for its length alone. A moving window of fifty words avoids that, and so does a measure that asks how far you can read before the ratio drops. A third kind works out what a random sample of a fixed size would show. All of them agree on big differences and quarrel over small ones, so a study that reports a wide gap on one scale and a narrow gap on another is not catching a contradiction. It is reading one difference off two rulers.

2. Leave *MATTR window (words)* at **50**, *MTLD threshold* at **0.72** and *HD-D sample (words)* at **42**, and leave *Match case* unticked.
3. Under *Lexical diversity* you should see **111 words, 78 different ones**, TTR **0.703**, MATTR **0.816**, MTLD **79.27**, HD-D **0.817** and Yule's K **163.95**. Note the TTR: a short text scores high for no good reason, which is step 1 in miniature.
4. Under *Readability*: Flesch reading ease **69.92** ("plain English"), Flesch-Kincaid grade **8.10**, Gunning fog **10.28**, over **6** sentences. Both are far below the paper's two groups.
5. Now tick *Match case* and watch the type count rise: "The" and "the" become different words. This is the kind of choice that moves a published TTR by several points.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report a length-robust measure next to TTR.** Paste your texts, read MATTR and HD-D beside the raw ratio, and say in your methods which one your claim rests on.
- **Reverse-engineer a published readability score.** Two formulas and a little algebra tell you what sentence length and word length the authors' texts must have had, which is often not reported.
- **Check your own writing against a target.** Paste a draft, read the Flesch band, and shorten sentences until the grade drops; the tool shows the counts, so you can see which half of the formula is driving it.
- **Settle a disagreement between two measures.** If MTLD and TTR point different ways, the length or the threshold is usually the reason: change *MTLD threshold* and *MATTR window* and see.
- **Teach the measures.** The moving-window chart makes it obvious why a single ratio over a whole text is a blunt instrument.

## The fine print

- The paper reports group means only: no standard deviations, no sample statistics and no tests. Nothing here can tell you whether its differences would survive one.
- It does not state the essay lengths, the tokenisation, or whether words were lemmatised. All three move TTR and MTLD.
- Our MTLD-against-TTR curve comes from model texts with no grammar. Real prose repeats function words locally, so it lies below that curve; our own passage (TTR 0.560, MTLD 83.47) shows by how much.
- Step 4 assumes the paper's grades were computed with the standard Flesch-Kincaid and Gunning fog formulas. Software differs on how it counts syllables, sentences and complex words, and the tool says so on its own page.
- The paper's vocd-D is estimated by curve fitting; the tool's HD-D is the exact calculation the same idea approximates, so the two are close but not identical.
