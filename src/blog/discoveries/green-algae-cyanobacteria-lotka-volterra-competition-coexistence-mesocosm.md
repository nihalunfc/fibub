Add fertiliser to a lake and theory says trouble should follow: the "paradox of enrichment" predicts wild boom-and-bust swings, and the stronger competitor, often bloom-forming cyanobacteria, should push the green algae out. A 2025 study in *FACETS* built a chain of linked tanks, fed nutrients in at the top and let water flow downstream, and found neither. Instead, green algae and cyanobacteria settled at a steady, shared level. The authors summed up that standoff with the classic **Lotka-Volterra competition model**, two equations for two species that each slow their own growth and each other's. This article puts the paper's growth rates, carrying capacities and competition coefficients into the [[lotka-volterra-simulator]] and checks the equilibrium it reports. For the step before, see one species alone in [[population-growth-models]], and for the water itself, the [[water-quality-index]].

## The paper in brief

The team ran a **meta-ecosystem**, a set of habitats joined by flows, of three linked mesocosms (an upstream tank, a middle hub and a terminal tank) stocked with *Daphnia* water fleas, phytoplankton and nutrients. About 15.8 mg of phosphorus and 50 mg of nitrogen went into each tank every week as liquid fertiliser, and the water ran one way, downstream. Phosphorus, nitrogen and dead organic matter built up in the end tanks, where dissolved oxygen was lowest.

The surprise was the plankton. The authors estimated, within the tanks, a maximum growth rate of **0.071** for green algae and **0.19** for cyanobacteria, carrying capacities of **9.8 million** and **23.8 million cells per litre**, and competition coefficients of **1.87** and **0.094**. The two groups reached a **stable equilibrium of about 6.2 million green algae and 9.2 million cyanobacteria cells per litre**, with green algae held to roughly a quarter of their carrying capacity, and this held whatever the tank's place in the chain. They credit phosphorus limits, competition, the low appeal of cyanobacteria to *Daphnia*, and the green algae they added every two weeks.

The paper's text leaves two labels open. It does not say which carrying capacity belongs to which group, and its Results and Discussion give the two competition coefficients opposite names. The model itself can settle that.

## Checked with the tools

In the competition model, species 1 (green algae, N₁) and species 2 (cyanobacteria, N₂) each grow logistically, and each is slowed by the other:

dN₁/dt = r₁N₁(K₁ − N₁ − α₁₂N₂)/K₁ and dN₂/dt = r₂N₂(K₂ − N₂ − α₂₁N₁)/K₂.

Here r is the maximum growth rate, K the carrying capacity (the most cells the tank holds of that group alone), and α₁₂ how much one cyanobacterium cell counts against the green algae, as a share of one green algal cell. We worked in millions of cells per litre and read the growth rates as **per day (our assumption; the paper does not give the unit)**. The time unit changes how fast the curves move, not where they end.

**Step 1: which reading matches the paper?** We tried all four ways of pairing the stated numbers with the two groups, using the tool's maths for the equilibria and the textbook outcome:

| Reading | Green algae K₁ | Cyanobacteria K₂ | α₁₂ (cyano on green) | α₂₁ (green on cyano) | Tool's outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 23.8 | 9.8 | 1.87 | 0.094 | Coexistence at 6.64 green, 9.18 cyano |
| B | 23.8 | 9.8 | 0.094 | 1.87 | Green algae win, cyanobacteria die out |
| C | 9.8 | 23.8 | 1.87 | 0.094 | Cyanobacteria win, green algae die out |
| D | 9.8 | 23.8 | 0.094 | 1.87 | Coexistence at 9.18 green, 6.64 cyano |

Only **reading A** gives what the paper describes: coexistence with more cyanobacteria than green algae. Reading D also coexists, but the wrong way round. So the numbers say the green algae have the larger carrying capacity (23.8 million cells per litre), and the cyanobacteria press hard on the green algae (α₁₂ = 1.87) while barely feeling them in return (α₂₁ = 0.094), which is how the Results section labels them.

**Step 2: the equilibrium.** Reading A puts the coexistence point at **6.64 million green algae** and **9.18 million cyanobacteria cells per litre**. The paper reports about 6.2 and 9.2. Cyanobacteria match to within half a percent; green algae come out about 7 % higher, a gap well inside what rounding of 1.87 and the two capacities can produce. The green algae sit at **0.279** of their carrying capacity, the paper's "about a quarter". The point is a **stable node**: both eigenvalues of the Jacobian, the model's local rates of return, are negative (−0.016 and −0.182 per day), so any nudge dies away without cycles. That is the "locally stable equilibrium" the paper describes, and no boom and bust.

**Step 3: how slowly it settles.** From one million cells of each, the cyanobacteria shoot up within weeks, overshoot to 9.51 and then ease back, while the green algae creep up: 3.23 million cells per litre by day 50, 4.52 by day 100 and 5.49 by day 150. Both are within 5 % of the equilibrium only after about **236 days**. The slow eigenvalue, −0.016 per day, means a disturbance shrinks by a factor of e (about 2.7) only every 63 days. If the time unit really is days, a single season is barely long enough to see the end state, which may be one reason the regular green algae top-ups mattered.

**Step 4: how much room there is.** Coexistence needs α₁₂ below K₁/K₂ = 23.8 ÷ 9.8 = **2.43** (and α₂₁ below K₂/K₁ = 0.41). Figure 2 holds everything else fixed and slides α₁₂. The paper's 1.87 sits about a quarter below the line where green algae vanish. The authors call the coexistence "impressive" given the difference in carrying capacities; the model shows why it works: the green algae's much larger capacity is what lets them survive such a lopsided fight.

## How we made the graphs

Every number and both charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/green-algae-cyanobacteria-lotka-volterra-competition-coexistence-mesocosm.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `compete` (the two equations), `rk4` (fourth-order Runge-Kutta with 0.1-day steps), `competeEquilibria` (the equilibria and their eigenvalues) and `competeOutcome` (the textbook outcome from the isocline intercepts) from `src/lib/sci/lotkav.ts`, the maths behind the [[lotka-volterra-simulator]].

The growth rates, carrying capacities, competition coefficients and the equilibrium come from the paper's text. Reading the growth rates as per day, the starting numbers of one million cells each and the four ways of pairing the labels are **our assumptions**. The curves are a **simulation of the model**, not the mesocosm counts, which the paper shows only in figures.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/lotka-volterra-simulator.mjs`, which pins the simulator against SciPy and NumPy:

- A competition run (r 0.8 and 0.6, K 500 and 400, α 0.6 and 0.7) must reach (445.6464, 90.14425) at t = 40, as SciPy's `solve_ivp` does at a tolerance of 10⁻¹².
- Its coexistence point must be (448.276, 86.207), a stable node with eigenvalues −0.77735 and −0.06920, as `numpy.linalg.eigvals` gives.
- New with this article: the algae numbers (reading A) must give coexistence at (6.6414, 9.1757) with a slow eigenvalue of −0.01599 and reach (6.616, 9.178) after 400 days, and swapping the two coefficients must make species 1 win.

## Check it yourself

On the [[lotka-volterra-simulator]]:

1. Press **Competition**.
2. Type *Growth r₁* **0.071**, *Growth r₂* **0.19**, *Capacity K₁* **23.8**, *Capacity K₂* **9.8**, *Effect of 2 on 1, α₁₂* **1.87** and *Effect of 1 on 2, α₂₁* **0.094**. Species 1 is green algae, species 2 cyanobacteria, in millions of cells per litre.
3. Set *Starting N₁* and *Starting N₂* to **1**, *Run for (time)* to **400** and *Time step* to **0.1**.
4. The result reads about **species 1 6.616, species 2 9.178**, and *Outcome* says **Coexistence at (6.641, 9.176)**, "Stable coexistence: each species limits itself more than it limits the other." The *Equilibria* table lists the coexistence point as a **stable node**.
5. Swap the two coefficients (α₁₂ **0.094**, α₂₁ **1.87**): the outcome changes to "Species 1 wins and species 2 dies out."
6. Put them back and raise α₁₂ to **2.5**, above 23.8 ÷ 9.8: now species 2 wins.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Settle ambiguous labels.** When a paper's coefficients or capacities could be read two ways, run each reading in the [[lotka-volterra-simulator]] and keep the one that gives the reported equilibrium.
- **Ask how robust coexistence is.** Slide one α and watch for the point where the outcome flips, as in Figure 2, before planning a nutrient or grazing treatment.
- **Plan how long to run an experiment.** The slow eigenvalue tells you how many days a mesocosm needs to come close to its end state.
- **Start from one species.** Fit r and K for each alga alone with the logistic model in [[population-growth-models]], then bring in the competition terms.
- **Teach the paradox of enrichment.** Use the *Predator-prey* mode with a prey limit K to show how raising K turns damped swings into lasting cycles, and compare it with the calm outcome here.

## The fine print

- The paper does not label its carrying capacities or give the time unit of r, and its coefficient labels differ between sections. Our reading A is the one of four that matches its reported equilibrium; it is an inference, not a statement in the paper.
- The model ignores *Daphnia* grazing, nutrient flow between tanks and the green algae top-ups, all of which the authors say helped hold the balance.
- The 7 % gap in green algae could come from rounding in the stated coefficients or from fitting the equilibrium separately in the paper; we cannot tell from the text.
- The time series in Figure 1 is a simulation, not the measured cell counts.
