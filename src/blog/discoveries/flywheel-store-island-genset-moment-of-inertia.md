A diesel generating set running on its own, with no grid behind it, has one real weakness: it cannot change its power quickly. Switch on a big motor and the engine's speed dips for a second or two before the governor catches up, and the voltage and frequency dip with it. A 2025 paper in *E3S Web of Conferences* sizes a **kinetic energy store**, a steel flywheel geared to the engine, to lend the set that missing second: a 800 kg rotor spinning at 6000 rpm, allowed to slow by 148 rpm, hands over 0.043 kWh while the engine takes up the load. Everything in that sentence can be checked from the rotor's own dimensions, which is what the [[moment-of-inertia]] builder is for. For the rest of the chain, the 4:1 step-up sits in the [[gear-train-calculator]] and the comparison with batteries and other stores in [[energy-stores]].

## The paper in brief

The authors study a 150 kW generating set supplying an island (off-grid) load of about 130 kW, in which the worst single switching event changes the demand by **52 kW**, and the engine needs about **3 seconds** to respond. Their answer is a flywheel on the generator shaft through a **4:1 gearbox**: the engine turns at 1500 rpm, the rotor at 6000 rpm.

The rotor is plain steel (density 7900 kg/m³), 0.4 m across and 0.8 m long, with a rotating mass of **800 kg** and a stated moment of inertia of **16 kg·m²**. The control rule is a speed window: the rotor may wander by **±148 rpm** around 6000, which (through the gearbox) is the ±37 rpm the engine is allowed at 1500 rpm. Inside that window the store gives up or takes back **0.043 kWh**. The authors also check that the rotor is safe: the centrifugal stress comes out at **31 MPa** against an allowable **75 MPa**, so the design is strength-limited nowhere near its speed.

What makes this paper worth re-deriving is that every one of those numbers follows from two formulas and the rotor's shape. There is no hidden model, so a reader can check the whole design in a browser.

## Checked with the tools

We built the rotor in the [[moment-of-inertia]] builder's maths as **one solid cylinder on its own axle**: mass 800 kg, radius 0.2 m, length 0.8 m, axis *along* the part. That gives

- **I = 16.0000 kg·m²**, exactly the paper's 16 kg·m², since ½ × 800 × 0.2² = 16;
- a **radius of gyration of 0.1414 m** (that is 0.2/√2), which is *not* the 0.1 m "radius to the centre of gravity" the paper uses for its stress sum: two different averages of the same rotor, a point worth keeping straight;
- a mass that the geometry agrees with: 7900 × π × 0.2² × 0.8 = **794.2 kg**, which the paper rounds to 800 kg.

One small trap: the paper's text writes the inertia as *m r²*, the thin-ring formula, which for this rotor would give **32 kg·m²**. Its own result of 16 kg·m² is the solid-disc value ½mr², so the formula in the text is a typo, not the number used. The builder shows both in one line, because the shape you pick changes the answer by a factor of two.

**The 0.043 kWh.** Spin energy is E = ½Iω². At 6000 rpm, ω = 628.32 rad/s, so the rotor holds **3.158 MJ (0.8773 kWh)** and an angular momentum of 10,053 kg·m²/s. Letting it slow from 6000 to 5852 rpm releases

E(6000) − E(5852) = **153.9 kJ = 0.0427 kWh**,

which rounds to the paper's **0.043 kWh**. The window is one-sided: a swing of ±148 rpm about 6000 would be worth twice as much (0.0866 kWh), so the paper's figure is clearly the energy on the way down, which is the direction that matters when the load jumps.

**Does it actually cover the load step?** 52 kW for 3 s is **156 kJ**. The 148 rpm window gives 153.9 kJ, which is **51.3 kW held for 3 s**: about 1.4 % short. Asking the builder's maths the question the other way round, the droop needed for the full 156 kJ is **150.1 rpm**, or ±37.5 rpm at the engine — exactly the figure the paper quotes elsewhere for the engine's allowed speed band. So the design is consistent to the last rpm; the 148 is a rounded version of the same rule.

**Why the gearbox earns its keep.** Stored energy goes as the *square* of the speed, so a fixed speed window is worth much more at a high speed. The same rotor with the same 148 rpm window, but turning at the engine's own 1500 rpm, would release only **37.0 kJ**, a factor of **4.16** less — the step-up ratio itself, near enough, because the window is a fixed number of rpm rather than a percentage. Figure 2 shows that curve.

**A different rotor shape, free of charge.** Put the same 800 kg of steel in a rim instead of a solid disc (a thick tube with an inner radius of 0.15 m and an outer radius of 0.2 m) and the builder gives **I = 25.0 kg·m²** with a radius of gyration of 0.1768 m. The same 148 rpm window then releases **240.4 kJ (0.0668 kWh)**, 56 % more, with no extra mass and no higher tip speed. That is the classic flywheel trade: move the metal outwards.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/flywheel-store-island-genset-moment-of-inertia.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `composite` and `centralI` from `src/lib/sci/phinertia.ts` — the maths behind the [[moment-of-inertia]] builder, which applies each shape's own-centre formula and the parallel-axis theorem I = I_centre + md² and adds the parts up. The spin energy, the speed windows and the droop inversion are three lines of arithmetic on top of the inertia the library returns.

Inputs from the paper: density 7900 kg/m³, outer radius 0.2 m, width 0.8 m, mass 800 kg, 6000 rpm, 148 rpm, the 4:1 ratio, the 52 kW step and the 3 s response. **Our assumptions**, clearly marked in the script: that the rotor is a single uniform cylinder with its shaft ignored, and that the rim variant has an inner radius of 0.15 m. The paper gives no shaft or hub mass, so a real build would have slightly more inertia than 16 kg·m², not less.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/moment-of-inertia.mjs`, which pins the builder's shape formulas against volume integrals of a uniform density worked out in SymPy, and two composite bodies against exact rational sums:

- a rod-and-ball pendulum (0.5 kg rod 1 m long about one end, plus a 1 kg ball of radius 5 cm) must give exactly 7621/6000 = 1.2702 kg·m² about the pivot, a centre of mass at 0.86667 m and 0.1435 kg·m² about it;
- a 2 kg plate 40 × 30 cm with a 0.2 kg disc removed 10 cm off centre must give 0.0394167 kg·m² and a centre of mass at −0.011111 m;
- a 3 kg point mass at (0.3, 0.4) m must give 0.75 kg·m², the parallel-axis theorem on its own.
- **New with this article**: the paper's rotor as a solid disc must give exactly 16 kg·m² with a radius of gyration of 0.1 × √2 m, the thin-ring reading of the same numbers must give 32 kg·m², and the rim variant 25 kg·m², so the factor-of-two trap stays pinned.

## Check it yourself

On the [[moment-of-inertia]] page:

1. In *Start from*, pick **Flywheel on a shaft (made up)**, then set *Lengths in* to **metres (m), masses in kg**.
2. Type **6000** into *Spin speed, optional (rpm)*.
3. Delete the extra parts with **Remove** until one is left, and set that part to *Shape* **Solid cylinder or disc**, *Mass* **800**, *radius R* **0.2**, *length h* **0.8**, *Axis* **along the part**, *Centre x* **0**, *Centre y* **0**.
4. The headline reads **I = 16 kg·m² about the axis**, with *Radius of gyration* **0.1414 m** and, under *At 6000 rpm*, **3.158 × 10⁶ J of spin energy** (ω = 628.3 rad/s, angular momentum 10053 kg·m²/s).
5. Change *Spin speed* to **5852** and the energy becomes **3.004 × 10⁶ J**. The difference, 1.54 × 10⁵ J, is the 0.043 kWh the paper's store delivers.
6. Now change *Shape* to **Thick tube** with *inner radius R₁* **0.15**, *outer radius R₂* **0.2**, *length h* **0.8** and the same 800 kg: **I = 25 kg·m²**, and the 6000 rpm energy rises to **4.935 × 10⁶ J**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Size a flywheel store** for a generator, a press, a lift or a shear: build the rotor from discs, tubes and a shaft, read I, then work the usable energy from ½I(ω₁² − ω₂²) for the speed droop your control system allows.
- **Sanity-check a published rotor** in a minute, as we did here: the stated inertia, the stated mass and the stated dimensions must agree, or one of the three is a typo.
- **Compare shapes before buying metal**: the same mass in a rim beats a solid disc by half as much energy again, which the builder shows directly as I and the radius of gyration.
- **Teach the square law**: the same rpm window at 6000 rpm is worth four times what it is at 1500 rpm, which is why these stores sit behind a step-up gearbox; pair it with the [[gear-train-calculator]].
- **Set up the dynamics**, once I is known, in the [[lagrangian-helper]], and compare the store against batteries and capacitors with [[energy-stores]].

## The fine print

- The builder assumes **uniform, rigid parts**. A real rotor has a hub, a shaft, keyways and a bolted rim, which change I by a few per cent.
- Our rotor mass (794 kg from the dimensions) and the paper's (800 kg) differ by 0.7 %; we kept the paper's 800 kg for the inertia so the comparison is like for like.
- The 0.043 kWh is **mechanical** energy at the rotor. A real store loses some of it in the gearbox, the generator and the bearings, so the power reaching the load is lower; the paper's own strength and loss checks sit outside what this tool does.
- The stress check (31 MPa against 75 MPa allowable) is the paper's; the builder does not do strength, only inertia.
- We looked only at the energy balance of one load step. Whether the *frequency* stays inside its limits is a control question the paper treats separately.
