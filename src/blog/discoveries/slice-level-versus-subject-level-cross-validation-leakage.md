Medical imaging datasets are usually many pictures of few people. Split those pictures at random and the same person's brain appears in both the training and the test set, so a model can score nearly perfectly by recognising the person rather than the disease. A 2026 paper in *Applied Sciences* measures that gap on one dataset: **99.99 %** accuracy when 86,437 MRI slices from 347 subjects are split at the slice level, and **80.8 %** when the same model and the same pipeline are split by subject. This article rebuilds both splits with the [[train-test-splitter]], shows that the leak is not a matter of luck but of arithmetic, and reproduces the two accuracies with a classifier that does nothing but memorise. Then take the honest split onward with the [[confusion-matrix]] and check your table for duplicates first with the [[csv-profiler]].

## The paper in brief

The authors use the OASIS cross-sectional MRI collection as repackaged on Kaggle: **347 subjects, 86,437 axial slices**, about 250 slices per subject, labelled non-demented against demented in a **78:22** split, with only **two subjects** in the moderate-dementia class. They train an attention-based multi-scale network two ways. With **subject-level** stratified 10-fold cross-validation, where every slice of one subject stays in one fold, accuracy is **0.808** (AUC 0.873, sensitivity 0.620), rising to **0.824** when slice predictions are aggregated per subject. With **image-level** 10-fold cross-validation, the identical pipeline reports **0.9999** accuracy and an AUC of 1.0000.

The paper's point is that the second number is an artefact, and that published accuracies above 99 % on this dataset should be read as leakage. Our interest is that both numbers can be checked without a GPU.

## Checked with the tools

We rebuilt a cohort with the paper's shape (**our simulation**): 347 subjects, 86,437 slices, 249 or 250 slices each, and 76 demented subjects, which puts 21.9 % of slices in the demented class, close to the paper's 22 %. Then we used the tool's own split functions.

**Step 1: count the leak.** A record-wise (slice-level) 10-fold split of 86,437 rows gives folds of 8,644 and 8,643 slices. In **every one of the ten folds, all 347 subjects** have slices in both the training part and the test part. Not most of them: all of them, in all folds. The grouped (subject-level) split gives 34 or 35 subjects and **8,470 to 8,719 slices per fold**, matching the paper's "about 35 subjects and 8,000 to 10,000 images", with **zero** subjects split across folds.

**Step 2: the leak is arithmetic, not bad luck.** The chance that all of a subject's slices happen to land in the same fold of a ten-fold random split is 10 × (1/10)ⁿ for n slices: 10 % at two slices, 1 % at three, one in a thousand at four, one in a hundred thousand at six (Figure 1). At 249 slices it is about 10⁻²⁴⁸. No seed, no shuffle and no amount of repetition can rescue a record-wise split when each subject brings hundreds of rows.

**Step 3: reproduce both accuracies with a memoriser.** We built the dumbest possible classifier: for a test slice, look for other slices of the same subject in the training part; if there are any, copy their label, and otherwise guess the majority class. It has no pixels, no network and no training.

| Split | Our memoriser | The paper's network |
|---|---|---|
| Record-wise (slice-level) 10-fold | **1.0000** | 0.9999 |
| Grouped (subject-level) 10-fold | **0.7810** | 0.808 |

That is the whole story in two lines. The 0.9999 reported for image-level validation is what you get from identity, not from radiology. And the subject-level 0.808 is only **2.7 percentage points** above what a majority-class guess achieves on this imbalance, which is why the paper's own sensitivity of 0.620 matters more than its accuracy.

**Step 4: the price of the honest split.** Grouped splits move whole people, so the class mix of the test set bounces around. Over 200 grouped 80/20 splits of our cohort, the demented share of the test slices ranged from **10.0 % to 34.3 %** (95 % of them between 12.9 % and 31.4 %), while a stratified slice-level split held it at 21.9 % every time (Figure 2). With two moderate-dementia subjects and ten folds, at most **two of the ten folds** can contain one, which is a concrete reason to merge classes as the paper does rather than report per-class scores for a class with two people in it.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/slice-level-versus-subject-level-cross-validation-leakage.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `kfold`, `groupKfold`, `groupSplit`, `stratifiedSplit` and `balance` from `src/lib/sci/split.ts`, the maths behind the [[train-test-splitter]].

The cohort's shape (347 subjects, 86,437 slices, about 250 each, 22 % demented, two moderate-dementia subjects) and both accuracies come **from the paper**. Which subject has which label, and the even 249-or-250 slices per subject, are **our assumption**: we did not download the images, and no MRI data is used here. The memoriser is **our own** two-line classifier, not the paper's network.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/train-test-splitter.mjs`, which pins the split counts against scikit-learn 1.9:

- `train_test_split` sizes: 41 rows with 20 % test must give 32 + 9, and 137 rows with 15 % must give 116 + 21, as scikit-learn's `ceil` rule does.
- Stratified test counts per class must match `train_test_split(stratify=y)` over 60 random states (for a 40-row example with 24, 7 and 9 in the classes and 20 % test: 5, 1 and 2).
- `StratifiedKFold` per-fold class counts, `KFold` and `GroupKFold` fold numbers for every row, and the number of test groups from `GroupShuffleSplit`, must all match scikit-learn on a 200-row set with 23 groups.
- New with this article: a 347-subject, 86,437-slice cohort must give ten record-wise folds in which all 347 subjects appear on both sides, and ten grouped folds of 34 to 35 subjects and 8,470 to 8,719 slices with no subject split.

## Check it yourself

A 12-row stand-in shows the whole effect (**our own made-up rows**: four patients, three slices each). On the [[train-test-splitter]]:

1. In *Your data*, paste this table (first line is the header):
   `patient, slice, label` then `P1, s1, demented`; `P1, s2, demented`; `P1, s3, demented`; `P2, s1, demented`; `P2, s2, demented`; `P2, s3, demented`; `P3, s1, healthy`; `P3, s2, healthy`; `P3, s3, healthy`; `P4, s1, healthy`; `P4, s2, healthy`; `P4, s3, healthy`.
2. Set **Method** to *k-fold: a fold number for every row*, **Folds made** to *Shuffled*, **Number of folds k** to **4** and leave **Seed** at **42**. P1's three slices come out in folds **1, 3 and 1**: the same patient is in two folds, which is the leak.
3. Change **Folds made** to *Grouped* and set **Group column** to *patient*. Now each patient owns one fold: P4 in fold 1, P3 in fold 2, P2 in fold 3, P1 in fold 4.
4. Switch **Method** to *Grouped (whole groups stay together)* with **Test (%)** **25** and **Validation (%)** **0**. One whole patient goes to the test set: with seed 42 it is **P1**, so the test set is 3 rows and the training set 9.
5. Look at *Class balance*: the test set holds **3 demented and 0 healthy** rows. That is the cost of a grouped split on a small dataset, and the reason to check the balance table rather than trusting the percentage you typed.
6. Set **Class column** to *label* and try a few seeds: the test set's class mix jumps between all-demented and all-healthy, because there are only four patients to deal out.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published accuracy** before believing it: if the paper says "images were split 80/20" and each subject has many images, the number is about identity, not disease.
- **Make the split you meant to make**, with whole patients, farms, schools or sessions on one side, and download the parts as CSV.
- **Report the spread, not one seed.** Run several grouped splits and quote the range of the class mix and of the score.
- **Spot a class too small to validate**: with two subjects in a class, most folds have none, so per-class metrics are meaningless.
- **Pair the honest split with honest metrics** in the [[confusion-matrix]], where sensitivity on the minority class usually tells the real story, and plan the next cohort's size with [[sample-size]].

## The fine print

- No MRI data was downloaded. Our cohort has the paper's **shape** only, with equal slices per subject and an even spread of labels, both **our assumption**; the real dataset's per-subject counts vary, which changes the fold sizes a little but not the leakage.
- Our memoriser is a thought experiment made real, not a replication of the paper's network. It shows the ceiling that leakage creates and the floor that imbalance creates; the paper's model sits at the floor plus 2.7 points.
- The paper reports two slightly different subject-level accuracies (0.808 and 0.805) in different tables, and 0.824 after aggregating slices to subjects. We used 0.808, the figure in its abstract.
- Leakage has other routes besides subject identity: repeated scans of one person, data augmentation applied before the split, and feature scaling fitted on the whole table. Splitting by group fixes only the first.
- Accuracy on a 78:22 problem is a weak measure whatever the split. Read the sensitivity and the AUC.
