**Uric acid** in blood and urine is a marker for gout and kidney problems, and cheap disposable sensors that measure it at the bedside are a busy research area. A 2026 paper in *Applied Sciences* compares two off-the-shelf **screen-printed carbon electrodes**, small printed cards with a working, counter and reference electrode, and asks which one measures uric acid better. Part of that comparison is the **electroactive area**: how much of the electrode actually takes part in electron transfer. This article rebuilds the paper's areas with the [[cyclic-voltammetry]] analyser's Randles–Sevcik maths and shows exactly how the reported numbers fit together. For the next steps, fit uric acid calibration lines with the [[calibration-curve]] tool and work out detection limits with [[method-validation|method validation statistics]].

## The paper in brief

The authors tested two commercial carbon electrodes, here called DRP-110 and DRP-150, each with a 4 mm working disc (a drawn area of about 0.126 cm²). First they ran **cyclic voltammetry (CV)**, sweeping the voltage up and back, in 5 mM ferri/ferrocyanide, a standard test couple that swaps one electron quickly. They varied the scan rate from 10 to 100 mV/s and used the **Randles–Sevcik equation** to turn the size of the current into an electroactive area: about **1.87 cm²** for DRP-110 and **1.77 cm²** for DRP-150, roughly 15 and 14 times the drawn area.

Then they measured uric acid by differential pulse voltammetry in buffer at pH 7. Both electrodes show two oxidation peaks. DRP-150 had the steeper calibration lines and the lower limits of detection (0.055 µM at the first peak, 2.23 µM at the second), while DRP-110 gave a quieter background. Both responded to ascorbic acid (vitamin C) too, so selectivity remains a weak point, and recoveries from spiked artificial urine ranged from 68 % to 98 %.

## Checked with the tools

These are **recomputations** from the values stated in the paper's text, with the paper's own inputs: n = 1, C = 5 mM, D = 6.70 × 10⁻⁶ cm²/s, T = 298 K.

**Step 1: what does the drawn electrode give?** The Randles–Sevcik equation says the peak current of a fast, diffusion-controlled couple grows with the area and with the square root of the scan rate v: ip = k·n^1.5·A·√D·C·√v, where k = 2.687 × 10⁵ at 298 K. For a flat 4 mm disc (0.1257 cm²) in this solution the tool predicts **43.7 µA at 10 mV/s, 97.7 µA at 50 mV/s and 138.2 µA at 100 mV/s**.

**Step 2: what do the reported values mean?** The paper lists anodic and cathodic values of 6728.8 and 6292.2 µA for DRP-110. Read as peak currents at 50 mV/s, their mean (6510.5 µA) would need an electrode of 8.37 cm², which does not match the paper's own 1.87 cm². Read instead as the **slopes of peak current against √v**, in µA per (V/s)^½, the same mean gives an area of **1.872 cm²** for DRP-110 and **1.773 cm²** for DRP-150, with roughness factors (electroactive ÷ drawn area) of **14.9 and 14.1**. Those match the paper to every digit it gives. So the reported values behave as Randles–Sevcik slopes, and the paper's arithmetic is internally consistent.

| Quantity (DRP-110 / DRP-150) | Paper says | Tool gives |
|---|---|---|
| Electroactive area | about 1.87 / 1.77 cm² | 1.872 / 1.773 cm² (mean of the two values as √v slopes) |
| Roughness factor | 14.9 / 14.1 | 14.9 / 14.1 |
| Same values read as currents at 50 mV/s | not stated | 8.37 / 7.93 cm² |
| Peak current at 50 mV/s, drawn 4 mm disc | not stated | 97.7 µA |

**Step 3: what would such an electrode look like?** We simulated ideal voltammograms (a reversible one-electron couple; the shape is **our assumption**, not the paper's data) for both areas with the tool's digital simulation. The drawn disc peaks at 97.3 µA with the textbook peak separation of 58 mV; an electrode of 1.87 cm² in the same solution would peak near 1.45 mA (Figure 1).

**What the 15-fold area means.** Printed carbon inks are rough and porous, so an electroactive area above the drawn area is expected. But a factor of 15 is large, and in porous layers part of the current can come from solution trapped inside the pores, which reacts like a thin layer rather than by diffusion from the bulk, and inflates the apparent area (Streeter and co-workers, 2008). The near-0.5 log–log slopes argue that bulk diffusion dominates here. Either way, the area found this way is a useful number to compare the two cards, measured under the same conditions, rather than a literal surface area.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/screen-printed-carbon-electrodes-uric-acid-randles-sevcik-electroactive-area.mjs` imports `randlesSevcik`, `rsConstant`, `simulateCV`, `analyseCV`, `scanRateStudy` and `reversibleDeltaEp` from `src/lib/sci/cv.ts`, the maths behind the Cyclic voltammetry analyser. It computes every number above and draws Figure 1 (two simulations at 50 mV/s) and Figure 2 (Randles–Sevcik lines over the paper's ten scan rates, with simulated peaks).

Inputs from the paper: the 4 mm disc, 5 mM, D = 6.70 × 10⁻⁶ cm²/s, n = 1, 298 K, the scan rates and the four reported values. Our assumptions: a perfectly reversible couple with only one form present at the start, a formal potential placed at 0.20 V for drawing, and semi-infinite planar diffusion.

## How we test the maths

The analyser's maths is checked in `tests/sci.mjs` and `tests/sci-tools/cyclic-voltammetry.mjs`, run on every change:

- The Randles–Sevcik constant is 2.69 × 10⁵ at 25 °C, and a 3 mm disc in 1 mM solution with D = 10⁻⁵ cm²/s gives 18.99 µA at 100 mV/s.
- The finite-difference simulation of a reversible couple reproduces the Randles–Sevcik peak current within 1 %, a peak separation of 58 mV and a Nicholson peak ratio of 1.
- A scan-rate study of exact Randles–Sevcik currents returns the diffusion coefficient it was built from and a log–log slope of exactly 0.5.
- **New with this article:** a slope of 6510.5 µA per (V/s)^½ at 5 mM and D = 6.7 × 10⁻⁶ cm²/s gives 1.872 cm², and a 4 mm disc peaks at 97.7 µA at 50 mV/s.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[cyclic-voltammetry|cyclic voltammetry analyser]] and go to the **Randles–Sevcik equation** card.
2. Type **Peak current (µA)** 6510.5, **Concentration (mmol/L)** 5, **Diffusion coefficient (cm²/s)** 6.7e-6, **Scan rate (mV/s)** 1000 (that is, 1 V/s, which turns a slope into a current), and **Temperature (°C)** 25. Empty the **Electrode area (cm²)** box.
3. The tool shows **Electrode area 1.873 cm² (a disc 15.4 mm across)**: the paper's 1.87 cm².
4. Now type 0.126 in **Electrode area**, 50 in **Scan rate**, and empty **Peak current**. The tool shows **Peak current 97.96 µA**: what the drawn disc alone would give.
5. In the **Scan-rate study** card, paste your own scan rates and peak currents to get the √v line, the log–log slope and D.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Checking an electroactive area before you report it.** The Randles–Sevcik card turns a peak current or a √v slope into an area in one step, and says how big a disc that would be.
- **Comparing electrodes fairly.** Normalise currents by the electroactive area from the analyser before comparing sensitivities between electrode types.
- **Telling diffusion from adsorption.** The scan-rate study gives the log–log slope: near 0.5 for diffusion, near 1 for adsorbed species.
- **Calibration and detection limits.** Fit the peak current against uric acid concentration with the [[calibration-curve]] tool, and get the limit of detection and quantitation with [[method-validation]].
- **Teaching.** The simulated voltammogram shows students what a reversible 58 mV peak separation looks like before they meet real, slower electrodes.

## The fine print

- The paper calls the four values peak currents; our reading of them as √v slopes rests on the fact that this reading, and only this one, reproduces the paper's areas exactly. The slopes themselves are not stated separately.
- Ferri/ferrocyanide on carbon is often only quasi-reversible, which makes peaks a little smaller than the reversible Randles–Sevcik value; areas from it are then slight underestimates.
- Our voltammograms are simulations of an ideal electrode. They are not the paper's measured curves.
- This is research on sensors, not a medical test. Uric acid results for a person come from a clinical laboratory; ask a health professional about yours.
