A **relative gravimeter** is a very fine spring balance. It measures how much gravity changes from place to place, to a few millionths of a percent, but its readings are in its own units and must be turned into milligals (mGal, one thousandth of a centimetre per second squared) with a **meter factor**. To find that factor, surveyors carry the meter along a **calibration line**: a set of stations where gravity has been measured with absolute instruments, ideally with a big gravity difference between the ends that can be travelled quickly. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Earth Science* describes such a line on Jiugong Mountain in central China, with a difference of **326.9 mGal**, the largest of any short line in the country. This article uses the maths of the [[gravity-corrections]] tool to see what the line's quoted uncertainty means for a meter factor, and why the authors measured the gravity gradient at every absolute station. To combine error sources, try the [[uncertainty-calculator]]; to look at the ground noise the authors also checked, the [[seismogram-viewer]].

## The paper in brief

The authors set up **four absolute stations** and **eight relative stations** from the foot to the top of the mountain, with a round trip of about two and a half hours. Two absolute gravimeters, an FG5X and an A10 (both drop a mirror in a vacuum and time its fall with a laser), measured at the absolute stations after their lasers and clocks were checked against reference standards. The two instruments agreed within 7.05 µGal (1 µGal = 0.001 mGal) at every station, a normalised error of 0.58, within their uncertainties.

They also measured the **vertical gravity gradient** at each absolute station, because an absolute gravimeter measures at a height of about a metre and the value must be moved to the station mark. The measured gradients ranged from 2.37 to 3.89 µGal per centimetre, against a textbook average of about 3.09. Relative gravimeters then tied all twelve stations together.

The headline result: a gravity difference of **326.9338 mGal** from end to end, known to **6.4 µGal**, so that a relative gravimeter can be calibrated to better than **2 parts in 100,000** (2.0 × 10⁻⁵). The earlier Lushan line spans about 229 mGal.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: what 6.4 µGal means for a meter factor.** A meter factor scales every reading, so its error grows with the size of the gravity difference. Across 326.9338 mGal, a meter-factor error of 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ makes **6.54 µGal**, matching the 6.4 µGal the authors give for the line. Turned round, 6.4 µGal over the line is **1.96 × 10⁻⁵**: the paper's "better than 2.0 × 10⁻⁵". The same 6.4 µGal over the 229 mGal Lushan line would allow only 2.79 × 10⁻⁵, which is why a larger difference makes a better line.

**Step 2: a simulated calibration run.** To see it with the tool's maths we **simulated** a relative gravimeter (our numbers, not the paper's) that reads 3 parts in 10,000 low and drifts upward by 0.012 mGal per hour, carried foot, top, foot, top, foot in five hours. With a meter factor of 1, the tool's drift correction gives a difference of **326.836 mGal**, 98 µGal short. Setting the factor to 326.9338 ÷ 326.836 = **1.0003** brings it to **326.934 mGal**. Figure 2 shows how the error grows with the factor: about **3.3 µGal for every part per 100,000**, so a factor good to 2 parts per 100,000 keeps the line within its 6.4 µGal.

**Step 3: why the gradients matter.** The [[gravity-corrections]] tool, like most survey software, uses the normal **free-air gradient** of 0.3086 mGal per metre (3.086 µGal/cm). Over 1.2 m, a typical instrument height (**our assumption**), that predicts a fall of **370.3 µGal**. With the gradients the authors measured, the true fall is **283.9 µGal** at JZ04 and **466.8 µGal** at JZ12 (Figure 1). Using the normal gradient would move the absolute values by **86 µGal** at JZ04 and **−96 µGal** at JZ12, more than ten times the line's whole uncertainty. On a mountain the gradient varies with the terrain, so it has to be measured.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/jiugong-mountain-gravimetric-calibration-line-326-mgal-vertical-gradient.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `freeAir` (the normal free-air gradient), `parseHours` and `reduce` (drift correction, meter factor and observed gravity) from `src/lib/sci/gravity.ts`, the maths behind the [[gravity-corrections]] tool.

The line difference, its uncertainty, the scale-factor figure, the Lushan difference and the four measured gradients come from the paper's text. The 1.2 m height, the simulated gravimeter (3 × 10⁻⁴ low, 0.012 mGal/h drift, five readings), the base value of 979,500 mGal and the latitude of 29.5° are **our assumptions**. The loop is **simulated**, not the paper's data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/gravity-corrections.mjs`, which checks the tool against an independent NumPy reduction:

- Drift, observed gravity and free-air and Bouguer anomalies of a made-up survey must match NumPy to 10⁻⁶ mGal, and the survey's built-in anomaly must come back to within its 0.001 rounding.
- GRS80 normal gravity must be 978,032.67715 mGal at the equator and 983,218.63685 mGal at the pole; the Bouguer slab must be 0.04193 mGal per metre for 1000 kg/m³ and the free-air correction 0.3086 mGal/m.
- New with this article: the simulated calibration loop must give 326.836 mGal with a meter factor of 1, 326.934 mGal with 1.0003 and a drift of 0.012 mGal/h, and the normal fall over 1.2 m must be 370.32 µGal.

## Check it yourself

On the [[gravity-corrections]] tool:

1. Type **FOOT** for *Base station name*, **979500** for *Gravity at the base (mGal)* and **1** for *Meter factor (mGal per reading unit)*.
2. Clear the data box and paste our simulated loop, one station per line with tabs or commas (station, time, reading, latitude, elevation): **FOOT, 08:00, 1000.000, 29.5, 0**; **TOP, 09:15, 673.179, 29.5, 0**; **FOOT, 10:30, 1000.030, 29.5, 0**; **TOP, 11:45, 673.209, 29.5, 0**; **FOOT, 13:00, 1000.060, 29.5, 0**.
3. *Drift* reads **+0.012 mGal/h**, and the table's observed gravity at TOP is **979173.164**: a difference of 326.836 mGal.
4. Change *Meter factor* to **1.0003**. TOP now reads **979173.066**: a difference of 326.934 mGal, the line's value.
5. The free-air correction in the table is 0.3086 mGal per metre of elevation; change the elevation of the first TOP line to **1.2** and its free-air correction in the table reads **0.370** mGal, the 370 µGal of Step 3.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Calibrate a relative gravimeter**: reduce your loop over a calibration line with the [[gravity-corrections]] tool and adjust the meter factor until the difference matches the published one.
- **Budget a microgravity survey**: multiply the expected gravity range by the meter factor's uncertainty, then add drift and reading errors in the [[uncertainty-calculator]].
- **Decide when to measure gradients**: compare the normal-gradient correction with a measured one at a site with steep terrain or buildings nearby.
- **Check a site for noise** before long readings by looking at a seismometer record in the [[seismogram-viewer]].
- **Teach gravity reduction**: show how drift, meter factor and instrument height each shift a result by tens of microgals.

## The fine print

- We checked the arithmetic of the line's uncertainty and the size of the gradient effect; we did not re-adjust the paper's network or its absolute measurements.
- The paper gives no station elevations or coordinates in the text, so we used none; the simulated loop has made-up readings and a made-up base value.
- The 1.2 m instrument height is our assumption; the paper measured gradients between 72 and 120 cm.
- A real calibration fits the meter factor to all stations at once, often with a slight non-linearity; our single-factor example is the simplest case.
