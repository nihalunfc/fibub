Drop a ball on a table and it bounces. Drop an ultracold neutron on a very flat mirror and, for a neutron, "bouncing" only works at certain heights: the neutron's energy in Earth's gravity comes in steps, just like the levels of an atom. The **qBounce** experiment prepares neutrons in these gravitational states, then shakes the mirror at the right frequency to flip a neutron from one state to another, and uses the resonance to test gravity at micrometre distances. Everything in the paper hangs on one idea: the neutron sits in a *linear* potential, and a linear potential has levels given by the zeros of the Airy function. That is exactly the "bouncing ball" setting of the [[schrodinger-solver]], so we rebuilt the paper's scales with it, using exact constants from the [[physical-constants]] page and converting between energy and frequency with the [[unit-converter]].

## The paper in brief

This is a conference review of the **Gravity Resonance Spectroscopy** method. A neutron above a horizontal mirror feels the potential V = m g z. Writing the Schrödinger equation in a natural length z₀ = (ħ²/2m²g)^⅓ and energy E₀ = m g z₀ makes the problem parameter-free: the allowed energies are E₀ times the successive zeros of the Airy function. The paper gives z₀ ≈ 5.87 µm, a time scale t₀ = ħ/E₀ of about 1 ms, and then discusses the experiments: a state selector that is a slit of about 29.3 ± 0.9 µm between a mirror and an absorber, a vibrating mirror that drives transitions with a precision of 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV (about 0.4 % total), initial populations of roughly 70 % in the lowest state and 30 % in the second, and what the spectroscopy could say about hypothetical extra forces. The text does not print the energies or resonance frequencies themselves, so these are what we computed.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from the paper's stated formulas** with CODATA constants, plus one extension (the ceiling) that the paper only sketches.

**Step 1: the scales.** With the neutron's mass, ħ and g, the length scale is z₀ = **5.8686 µm**, the paper's 5.87. The energy scale is E₀ = **0.60165 peV** (a picoelectronvolt is 10⁻¹² eV), and the time scale t₀ = **1.094 ms**, the paper's "about 1 ms".

**Step 2: the levels.** Put the neutron mass, a force F = m g = 1.0252 × 10⁻¹⁶ eV/nm and the mirror at x = 0 into the solver's "Linear (bouncing ball)" potential. The exact Airy levels it lists are, for the first three states, **1.4067 peV, 2.4595 peV and 3.3214 peV**. The finite-difference solver on an 80 µm window with 2000 points agrees with the exact levels to 6 × 10⁻⁶ (relative). A neutron in the lowest state would rise classically only to the height where its energy runs out, E/(m g): **13.7 µm, 24.0 µm and 32.4 µm** for states 1, 2 and 3.

That explains the paper's state selector. A slit of 29.3 µm is higher than the turning points of states 1 and 2, but lower than that of state 3, so the absorber lets the lowest two through and cuts the third: just the 70 % / 30 % mixture of states 1 and 2 that the paper reports (the solver cannot compute the populations themselves, but their order of magnitude fits).

**Step 3: the resonance frequencies.** A mirror vibrating at frequency ν drives the transition between two states when h ν equals their energy difference. The levels above give:

| Transition | Energy gap | Resonance frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 1 → 2 | 1.0528 peV | **254.56 Hz** |
| 1 → 3 | 1.9147 peV | **462.98 Hz** |
| 2 → 3 | 0.8619 peV | **208.41 Hz** |
| 2 → 4 | 1.6237 peV | **392.61 Hz** |
| 1 → 4 | 2.6765 peV | **647.17 Hz** |

These are idealised frequencies, for a mirror with nothing above it. The paper's quoted sensitivity of 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV corresponds to a frequency of only **0.48 Hz**, about 0.2 % of the 254.56 Hz line and 0.1 % of the 462.98 Hz line, which is why the method can bound tiny extra forces.

**Step 4: our extension, a ceiling.** The paper's illustrative second boundary is a ceiling about 30 µm above the mirror. Solving the same problem with a hard wall at 29.3 µm (the slit height) raises the levels: state 1 by **0.04 %**, state 2 by **1.8 %**, state 3 by **11.8 %** and state 4 by **31 %**. With the paper's 30 µm the shifts are 0.03 %, 1.4 % and 10.1 % for the first three. That moves the 1 → 3 resonance from 462.98 Hz to **557.7 Hz** at 29.3 µm and **543.9 Hz** at 30 µm; the slit's ±0.9 µm uncertainty alone moves it between **540 and 578 Hz**. It also shifts the lowest state by only 0.025 to 0.067 % over that range, which is why the experiment treats the absorber as a clean "cut" rather than a detuning of the ground state.

## How we made the graphs

The script is `scripts/blog-figures/gravity-resonance-spectroscopy-qbounce-neutron-airy-levels.mjs`. It calls `linearLevels`, `airyZeros`, `solve` and `energies` from `src/lib/sci/phschro.ts`, the maths behind the [[schrodinger-solver]]. The paper provides the formulas for z₀, E₀ and the Airy-zero levels, and the slit height and its error. The neutron mass, ħ, g and h are standard values and **our input**; the frequencies, the classical heights and the ceiling shifts are **our own calculations** with the tool's solver. Nothing is fitted or simulated.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/schrodinger-solver.mjs`, which pins the solver against SciPy and closed forms:

- the Airy-function zeros against `scipy.special.ai_zeros` (2.33810741, 4.08794944, 5.52055983, ...);
- the harmonic-oscillator, box, finite-well and Pöschl–Teller levels against closed forms and SciPy's tridiagonal eigenvalue solver;
- the finite-difference levels against the exact levels with an error that falls as the grid is refined.
- New with this article: the neutron's gravitational levels must be 1.40672, 2.45952 and 3.32144 peV with a length scale of 5.8686 µm, and a hard ceiling at 29.3 µm must raise the lowest three levels by 0.04 %, 1.84 % and 11.8 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[schrodinger-solver]] page:

1. Set *Units* to **Electron (nm and eV)**, *Mass* to **1838.683661** (the neutron in electron masses) and *Potential* to **Linear (bouncing ball)**.
2. Type *Slope F (force)* **1.0252e-16** (the neutron's weight in eV per nm), *Left wall x₀* **0**, *Right wall x₁* **80000** (80 µm), and *Levels to find* **3**.
3. The page reads *E₀* about **1.40671e-12 eV**, with the exact Airy value **1.40672e-12** next to it in the table. The next two levels are 2.4595e-12 and 3.3214e-12 eV: 1.4067, 2.4595 and 3.3214 peV.
4. Change *Right wall x₁* to **29300**: the levels become 1.4073, 2.5048 and 3.7139 peV, the ceiling's raised values.
5. Divide a gap by Planck's constant (4.1357 peV per kHz, or use the unit converter) for the resonance frequency: (2.4595 − 1.4067) peV gives 254.6 Hz.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a resonance frequency before an experiment:** find the 1 → 3 or 2 → 4 frequency for any gravity or force value in seconds (Schrödinger solver).
- **Test how a boundary matters:** move an absorber and see which states are cut and which are shifted (Schrödinger solver).
- **Teach quantum bouncing:** let students see the Airy-function states, then the same problem in the classical ball picture (Schrödinger solver, unit converter).
- **Convert between energy and frequency** quickly, with exact constants (physical constants, unit converter).
- **Explore a modified potential**, such as an extra short-range force, by typing its formula in the tool and watching how the levels move (Schrödinger solver).

## The fine print

- The paper is a short conference review, not a data paper. The paper's own text prints no level energies or frequencies, so there is nothing for our numbers to disagree with except the scales z₀ ≈ 5.87 µm and t₀ ≈ 1 ms, which match.
- Our frequencies are for a mirror with nothing above it. The real experiment has absorbers, roughness and a finite transition probability, so measured resonances sit elsewhere. The paper also quotes a mirror drive of 243.5 Hz for one 2011 dataset; the extracted text does not say which transition that targeted, so we left it out of the comparison.
- The ceiling is an infinitely hard wall, a simplification of an absorber that really removes neutrons gradually.
- The solver treats the problem as one-dimensional and nonrelativistic, which is exactly right for a neutron at metres per second.
