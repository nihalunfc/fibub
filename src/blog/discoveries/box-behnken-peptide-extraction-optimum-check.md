Thousands of papers a year end with a sentence like "the optimum conditions were pH 7.1, 49 °C and 5,870 U/g". That number comes from a **response surface design**: a small set of planned runs, a quadratic model fitted to them, and the top of that fitted surface. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Nutrition* uses one to optimise an enzymatic extraction of peptides from the mushroom *Leucopaxillus giganteus*. Because the paper prints its fitted model, anyone can rebuild the design and check whether the reported optimum really is the top of it. This article does that with the [[experiment-design]] tool, which builds the same Box-Behnken and Plackett-Burman designs and does the canonical analysis. For the fitting side, see the [[regression-workbench]]; to plan how many runs you need, [[sample-size]].

## The paper in brief

The authors first **screened** four factors, pH, temperature, enzyme addition and time, in a 12-run Plackett-Burman design. Three mattered; time did not, and they fixed it. They then ran a three-factor **Box-Behnken design** with five centre runs over pH 6.5 to 7.5, 45 to 55 °C and 4,000 to 8,000 U/g, and fitted a full quadratic model in coded units:

Y = 28.91 + 0.063A − 0.19B − 0.041C − 0.62AB + 0.017AC + 0.53BC − 0.34A² − 1.33B² − 0.46C²

where A is pH, B is temperature and C is enzyme addition, each on a −1 to +1 coded scale. The reported optimum is **49 °C, 5,870 U/g and pH 7.1**, with a predicted peptide extraction rate of about 29 % and a validated **28.93 %**. The model is reported as highly significant (F = 1158.77) with no lack of fit (p = 0.80) and a residual standard deviation of **0.035**, a coefficient of variation of 0.12 %.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the design and the model equation the paper prints**. We do not use its run-by-run data table: instead the tool builds the design, we evaluate the paper's own equation at those runs, and the tool fits the model again from scratch.

**Step 1: the design.** The tool's `Box–Behnken` type with three factors and five centre runs gives **17 runs: 12 edge points and 5 centres**, which is the design size the paper used. In a three-factor Box-Behnken design every run sits at the middle of an edge of the cube, never at a corner, which is why it is popular when the corners (all three factors extreme at once) would spoil the sample.

**Step 2: does the model come back?** Feeding the paper's equation through the 17 runs and refitting with the tool returns every coefficient unchanged, to the last digit printed (intercept 28.91, linear 0.063, −0.19, −0.041, quadratic −0.34, −1.33, −0.46, interactions −0.62, 0.017, 0.53), with R² = 1. That is the check that the published equation and the published design agree: a Box-Behnken design with 17 runs can estimate all ten terms of a quadratic, and the printed coefficients are a possible fit to it.

As a second consistency check we compared the equation's prediction at each run with the run responses the paper reports. The largest gap is **0.06** percentage points (at one centre run) and the root-mean-square gap is **0.023**, so the printed equation does describe its own data. The five centre runs themselves have a mean of 28.908 and a standard deviation of 0.041, in line with the 0.035 residual standard deviation in the paper's table.

**Step 3: where is the top of the surface?** This is what the tool adds. The canonical analysis solves for the **stationary point** and looks at the eigenvalues of the quadratic part:

- stationary point, coded: A = 0.2255, B = −0.1492, C = −0.1263
- in real units: **pH 7.113, 49.25 °C, 5,747 U/g**
- predicted peptide extraction rate there: **28.934 %**
- eigenvalues −1.484, −0.419, −0.227: all negative, so it is a **true maximum**, not a saddle, and it lies inside the box that was tested

The paper's optimum (pH 7.1, 49 °C, 5,870 U/g) sits a whisker away: the same model gives **28.926 %** there, 0.008 percentage points below the peak. So the reported optimum is right, and the rounding costs nothing measurable. That is worth saying plainly, because a stationary point that turns out to be a saddle, or to lie outside the tested box, is a common failure in response-surface papers, and three numbers are not enough to tell by eye.

Figure 1 shows why temperature dominates: its quadratic term, −1.33, is three to four times the other two, so the surface is a narrow ridge in temperature and a gentle plateau in enzyme addition.

**Step 4: how precise can that optimum be?** A stationary point is a ratio of fitted coefficients, so its uncertainty is not the uncertainty of any one of them. We ran the same 17-run design **2,000 times in simulation**, adding fresh random scatter of 0.035 (the paper's own residual standard deviation) to the model values, and refitted each time:

| Scatter used (**our simulation**) | Best temperature, 95 % of repeats | Best enzyme addition | Fits that were not a maximum | Optima outside the tested box |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.035 (as reported) | 49.12 to 49.37 °C | 5,669 to 5,817 U/g | 0 of 2,000 | 0 of 2,000 |
| 0.35 (ten times) | 41.3 to 54.3 °C | 1,829 to 7,858 U/g | 261 of 2,000 | 400 of 2,000 |

With the reported scatter, the optimum is pinned to about a **tenth of a degree** and a hundred units per gram, which is why "49 °C" is a fair thing to claim. With ten times that scatter, nothing like it survives: one repeat in eight does not even find a maximum, and the best temperature wanders across the whole range tested. The lesson for a reader of any such paper is that the believability of an optimum is set almost entirely by the replicate scatter, which is why centre runs and a reported residual standard deviation matter more than the F value.

**Step 5: the screening design.** The tool's `Plackett–Burman screening` with 12 runs gives 12 rows and **11 mutually orthogonal columns** (every pair of columns has a dot product of exactly 0). The paper screened four factors, so seven columns go unused, and those seven are where the degrees of freedom for its error estimate come from. Projected onto four factors, the 12-run design is still balanced (six high and six low for every factor) and still orthogonal, and **exactly one of its twelve rows repeats** another row's four-factor settings. That matters for anyone reading the paper's run sheet and suspecting a typo: a repeated row in a four-factor projection of a Plackett-Burman design is expected, not a mistake.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/box-behnken-peptide-extraction-optimum-check.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `boxBehnken`, `quadratic` and `plackettBurman` from `src/lib/sci/doe.ts`, the maths behind the [[experiment-design]] tool.

The design (three factors, five centre runs, the coded levels) and the ten model coefficients come **from the paper's text**. The 0.035 scatter is also the paper's, but the 2,000 simulated repeats, the ten-times-worse scatter and the random draws are **our simulation**. The paper's run-by-run response table is not reproduced here; it was used only to check, in one line, how closely the printed equation matches its own runs.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/experiment-design.mjs`, which pins this maths against an independent Python script (numpy, statsmodels, scipy):

- Minimum-aberration fractional factorials: every 2^(k−p) design from k = 4 to 15 must have the word-length pattern found by exhaustive search, and the stated resolution (for instance 2^(7−4) has resolution 3).
- Plackett-Burman rows for N = 12, 20 and 24 must match the generating rows printed by Plackett and Burman (1946), which the Paley construction reproduces.
- A full quadratic fit of a 15-run Box-Behnken design must match statsmodels' OLS coefficients and standard errors, with the stationary point from `numpy.linalg.solve` (−0.6009, 0.5799, −0.2857), its predicted response 9.0079 and the eigenvalues 0.7865, 1.4262, 2.1935.
- Lenth's pseudo standard error and margins of error for a 2^(8−4) design must match a hand calculation with `scipy.stats.t`.
- New with this article: a three-factor Box-Behnken design with five centre runs must come out as 17 runs, and the model above must refit to a maximum at coded (0.2255, −0.1492, −0.1263) with a predicted 28.9338 and all three eigenvalues negative.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit an optimum before you cite it.** Type the published model's coefficients into your own fit, or evaluate them over the design, and check the stationary point is a maximum and inside the region tested.
- **Plan the design before running anything.** Compare a Box-Behnken with a central composite design of the same factor count, see how many runs each needs and how many centre runs it takes to measure the scatter.
- **Screen honestly.** A Plackett-Burman design with spare columns gives an error estimate without extra runs; the tool shows which columns are free.
- **Quote the precision of an optimum**, not just its position, by simulating your design at your own replicate scatter (as in Figure 2) before you promise a factory a temperature to a tenth of a degree.
- **Check the aliasing of a fractional factorial** with the tool's alias table, so a "significant main effect" is not really a two-factor interaction.

## The fine print

- We never used the paper's run responses as data. The 17 values we fit are the paper's **own equation** evaluated at the design points, so the coefficient recovery in Step 2 is a consistency check, not an independent confirmation of the chemistry.
- Rounding matters a little: the coefficients are printed to two or three digits, so our stationary point is good to about ±0.01 coded units.
- The paper reports its temperature optimum as 49 °C and its enzyme addition as 5,870 U/g. Our 49.25 °C and 5,747 U/g come from the printed equation; a difference of this size can come from the software's own optimiser, which may search a slightly different region or hold a factor fixed.
- The simulation in Step 4 assumes the scatter is the same at every run and normally distributed, which is the model's assumption, not a fact about the experiment.
- Nothing here checks the biology: whether the extracted peptides do what the paper reports is outside what any calculator can say.
