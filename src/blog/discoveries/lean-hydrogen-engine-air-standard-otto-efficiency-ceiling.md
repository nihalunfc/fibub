Burning hydrogen in a piston engine is attractive because hydrogen will burn in very thin mixtures, and thin mixtures are efficient. A 2025 paper in *Energies* measures and simulates a 12.8 litre, six-cylinder hydrogen engine across compression ratios of 9, 11 and 13 and excess air ratios from 1 to 5, and reports a best **indicated thermal efficiency of 41.25 %** at a compression ratio of 13 with two and a half times as much air as the hydrogen needs. The obvious question for a reader is how much room is left: what does the ideal cycle allow at that compression ratio, and does the rest of the paper's numbers hang together with it? Both are a few seconds of work with the [[heat-engine-cycles|heat engine cycle calculator]], and the neighbouring questions — real-gas behaviour of hydrogen at the 200 bar injection pressure, and the dimensionless groups behind the heat transfer — belong to the [[real-gas-calculator]] and the [[dimensionless-numbers]] tool.

## The paper in brief

The engine is turbocharged, direct-injection, 12.82 L, run at 1500 rpm with an intake temperature of 313 K and an intake pressure of 0.6 bar, hydrogen injected at 200 bar and ignition 15 crank-angle degrees before top dead centre. A calibrated one-dimensional model (R² above 0.98 against measured pressure traces, NOx within 8 %) was then swept across 45 combinations of compression ratio and excess air ratio λ.

The findings, in the authors' own numbers:

- **Efficiency** rises with λ and peaks at **41.25 % at a compression ratio of 13 and λ = 2.5**.
- **Volumetric efficiency** falls from a best of 89.7 % to about 55 % as λ grows, and with it the **maximum IMEP of 8.4 bar** at λ = 1.
- **NOx** is non-monotonic: it peaks at **1850 ppm at λ = 1.5** and drops **below 500 ppm at λ = 2.5**.
- The **lean limit** stretches with compression ratio: λ ≈ 4.5 at CR 9, 4.7 at CR 11, **5.0 at CR 13**.

## Checked with the tools

The air-standard Otto cycle is the ceiling every spark-ignition engine is measured against. Its efficiency depends on only two things, the compression ratio *r* and the heat capacity ratio γ of the working gas:

η = 1 − 1/r^(γ−1)

With air (γ = 1.40) the cycle tool gives **58.48 %, 61.68 % and 64.16 %** at the paper's three compression ratios. So the measured best of 41.25 % at CR 13 is **64.3 % of its ideal ceiling** — a normal figure for a good engine, and a useful one, because it says the remaining 23 percentage points are friction, heat loss, finite burn duration and incomplete expansion, not something a higher compression ratio can recover. Note also that the ideal cycle only gains **5.7 points** going from CR 9 to CR 13; an engine that reaches 64 % of the ceiling at each would gain about 3.6 points, which is the size of the improvement the paper reports with compression ratio. Figure 1 also shows that 41.25 % sits below even a γ = 1.30 cycle, so the gap is not an artefact of choosing γ.

**Where the lean-burn gain really comes from.** The formula above has no λ in it, yet the paper's efficiency climbs with λ up to 2.5. That is not a contradiction: leaning out the mixture pushes the working gas towards pure air, so the *effective* γ rises towards 1.40, and the peak temperatures drop, which cuts heat loss and dissociation. On Figure 1 that is a move upwards between the γ = 1.30 and γ = 1.40 lines at a fixed compression ratio: worth about **10 percentage points**, which brackets the real gain comfortably.

**Does the NOx story follow the temperatures?** Feeding the cycle with hydrogen's heating value (120 MJ/kg) and stoichiometric air-fuel ratio (34.3 by mass, both **our assumptions**) gives the heat released per kilogram of trapped mixture, and with it the peak temperature of the ideal cycle at each λ:

| λ | Heat per kg of mixture | Ideal peak temperature (CR 13) | Ideal peak pressure | Ideal MEP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 3399 kJ/kg | 5611 K | 139.8 bar | 15.78 bar |
| 1.5 | 2288 kJ/kg | 4062 K | 101.2 bar | 10.62 bar |
| 2.5 | 1383 kJ/kg | 2801 K | 69.8 bar | 6.42 bar |
| 3.0 | 1155 kJ/kg | 2483 K | 61.9 bar | 5.36 bar |
| 5.0 | 696 kJ/kg | 1843 K | 45.9 bar | 3.23 bar |

Cold-air-standard temperatures are overestimates — the model has no dissociation and a constant heat capacity — but the **fall** is the point: between λ = 1.5, where the paper's NOx peaks at 1850 ppm, and λ = 2.5, where it is under 500 ppm, the ideal peak temperature drops by **31 %**, straight through the band where thermal nitric oxide formation slows by orders of magnitude (Figure 2). By λ = 5 the ideal peak is below 1900 K, which is also why the paper finds exhaust temperatures under 300 °C in the lean region.

**Does the IMEP hang together with the efficiency?** This is the strongest consistency check available from the paper's own text. At CR 13, λ = 1 and a 0.6 bar intake, the ideal cycle gives a mean effective pressure of **15.78 bar**. Multiply by the two measured shortfalls the paper itself reports — the efficiency ratio 41.25/64.16 = 0.643 and the volumetric efficiency 0.897 — and you get **9.10 bar**, within **8 %** of the paper's measured maximum IMEP of **8.4 bar** (Figure 3). Two numbers measured in different ways, from different pages of the paper, agree through the ideal cycle. That is the kind of cross-check a reviewer or a reader can do in a minute.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/lean-hydrogen-engine-air-standard-otto-efficiency-ceiling.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `cycle('otto', { T1, P1, r, Tmax }, gas)` and the closed-form `formula.otto(r, γ)` from `src/lib/sci/phcycles.ts`, the maths behind the [[heat-engine-cycles|heat engine cycle calculator]]. `cycle` returns every state, the heat and work of each process, the efficiency, the mean effective pressure and the peak pressure, per kilogram of gas.

From the paper: compression ratios 9, 11 and 13, intake 313 K and 0.6 bar, λ from 1 to 5, the measured 41.25 %, 8.4 bar IMEP and 89.7 % volumetric efficiency. **Our assumptions:** air as the working gas (R = 287 J/kg·K, γ = 1.40, with 1.35 and 1.30 shown for comparison), hydrogen's lower heating value of 120 MJ/kg, a stoichiometric air-fuel ratio of 34.3, all the fuel energy released at constant volume, and the paper's 0.6 bar read as an absolute intake pressure. Everything here is a **recomputation of an ideal cycle**, not a fit to the paper's data; the paper's own curves stay in its figures.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/heat-engine-cycles.mjs` on every change to fibub, pinning the same function against an independent SciPy calculation:

- An Otto cycle at r = 8 from 300 K and 100 kPa up to 1800 K must give net work 450.077 kJ/kg, heat in 796.985 kJ/kg and an MEP of 597.415 kPa, each to eight figures, and its efficiency must equal 1 − 1/r^(γ−1) exactly.
- A Diesel cycle at r = 18, cut-off 2 must give 604.793 kJ/kg and an efficiency of 0.6315775, matching the textbook closed form; a Brayton cycle at a pressure ratio of 10 must give a back work ratio of 0.4137209.
- Closing the loop by integrating P dv along all four processes with 4000 steps must return the net work, and the first law (net work = heat in − heat out) must hold for every cycle.
- New with this article: the lean hydrogen case must come out right — at r = 13, 313 K, 60 kPa and a peak of 2801 K, the cycle must give an efficiency of 64.155 %, an MEP of 642.098 kPa and a peak pressure of 6980.13 kPa, and the end-of-compression temperature must be 873.216 K, and the closed-form Otto efficiencies at r = 9, 11 and 13 must be 58.48 %, 61.68 % and 64.16 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[heat-engine-cycles|heat engine cycle calculator]] page:

1. Press the **Otto** button. Leave *Gas* on **Air (R 287, γ 1.4)**.
2. Set *Start temperature T1 (K)* to **313**, *Start pressure P1 (kPa)* to **60** and *Compression ratio r = v1/v2* to **13**.
3. Put **2801** in *Peak (hot) temperature (K)* — the lean (λ = 2.5) case worked out above. The headline reads **Efficiency 64.2 % (Carnot limit 88.8 %)**, with *Mean effective pressure* **642.1 kPa**, *Peak pressure* **6980 kPa** and *Net work* **887.4 kJ/kg**.
4. Change *Peak (hot) temperature (K)* to **5611** (the stoichiometric case): the efficiency does **not** move — it is still 64.2 % — but *Mean effective pressure* jumps to **1578 kPa** and *Peak pressure* to **13,980 kPa**. Compression ratio sets efficiency; fuelling sets work.
5. Set *Compression ratio r = v1/v2* to **9**: efficiency falls to **58.5 %**, the whole ideal gain the paper's higher compression ratio can buy.
6. Read the *States* table for the end-of-compression temperature: **873.2 K** at CR 13, **753.8 K** at CR 9. That difference is what widens hydrogen's lean limit, and it is why the paper reaches λ = 5.0 only at CR 13.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Put a measured efficiency in context.** A percentage means little without its ceiling; the ratio to the ideal cycle is comparable across engines and fuels.
- **Sanity-check a reported IMEP or peak pressure** against the cycle the engine is supposed to be running, as we did above.
- **See which knob does what.** The tool separates what compression ratio controls (efficiency, end-of-compression temperature) from what fuelling controls (work, peak pressure).
- **Estimate how far peak temperatures move** when a mixture is leaned out, before any NOx chemistry is run.
- **Teach the air-standard cycles** with P–v and T–s diagrams that redraw as the numbers change, next to real engine data.

## The fine print

- The air-standard cycle is an **ideal** model: one gas with constant heat capacities, instant heat release at constant volume, no friction, no heat loss, no dissociation and no residual gas. Its absolute temperatures are too high and its efficiencies too good by design.
- The heat released per kilogram of mixture uses our rounded hydrogen values; the paper does not state fuel flows in its text, so this is our calculation, not theirs.
- We read the paper's "intake pressure 0.6 bar" as an absolute pressure. If it is boost above atmospheric, every pressure and the MEP scale up by about 2.7, while the efficiencies do not change at all.
- Direct injection of hydrogen late in compression changes the trapped mass and the effective compression; we ignore that.
- The paper's own figures (efficiency and NOx maps) are not reproduced here, and the measurement-versus-model comparison is its own work, not ours.
