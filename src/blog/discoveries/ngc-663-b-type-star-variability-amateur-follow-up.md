NGC 663 is a young open cluster in Cassiopeia, full of hot B stars, and hot B stars rarely sit still: they pulsate, spin, shed discs and eclipse each other. A 2026 paper in *Universe* worked through eight nights of imaging from a 50 cm telescope, plus 2-minute TESS data, and came out with **60 variable candidates**, 46 of them B-type and **29 of them new**. The amplitudes run from 14 millimagnitudes to a third of a magnitude, which raises the question an amateur or a teaching observatory actually cares about: which of these could I confirm myself? This article answers that with the [[variable-star-log]] for the photometry and report format and the [[period-finder]] for the periods; to place the stars on the cluster's main sequence afterwards, use the [[hr-diagram]].

## The paper in brief

The authors imaged the cluster on **eight usable nights in October 2014** with the 50BiN 50 cm telescope at Qinghai Station, taking **3,187 B-band frames** of 80 s and **7,494 V-band frames** of 30 s. They measured differential magnitudes for **881 stars** against six reference stars whose scatter stayed below 0.1 mag, then added **TESS Sector 58** photometry at 2-minute cadence and **Gaia DR3** astrometry to decide membership.

The result is 60 variable candidates: 7 candidate α Cygni variables, 3 β Cephei stars, 10 slowly pulsating B candidates, one possible hybrid, 20 Be stars and a handful of others among the B-type stars, with **31** already in the VSX catalogue and **29 new**. A Gaussian mixture model on the Gaia astrometry makes **40** of the B-type variables likely cluster members. The periods span 0.19 days to about 22 days, and the amplitudes 0.014 to 0.330 magnitudes.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated values, with **simulated** light curves where a dataset is needed. None of the paper's photometry is used.

**Step 1: an amplitude is a change in flux, and small amplitudes are tiny.** The Variable star log works in the same differential form the paper uses: V = C + 2.5 log₁₀(comparison counts ÷ variable counts). Take a comparison star of catalogue magnitude 11.42 measured at 200,000 counts. A variable measured at 190,000, 187,000 and 184,000 counts on three nights comes out at **11.4757, 11.4930 and 11.5105** — a total drop of **0.0348 mag** for a 3.2 % fall in counts between the first and last night. Turned around, the paper's amplitudes are these flux changes:

| Star (paper) | Period | Amplitude | That is a flux change of | Frames needed at 0.03 mag scatter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V8 (β Cephei) | 0.2101 d | 0.014 mag | 1.28 % | 919 |
| V9 (slowly pulsating B) | 1.0981 d | 0.015 mag | 1.37 % | 800 |
| V19 (β Cephei) | 0.1940 d | 0.077 mag | 6.85 % | 31 |
| V38 (rotational) | 21.760 d | 0.280 mag | 22.73 % | 3 |
| V10 (eclipsing binary) | 6.1638 d | 0.330 mag | 26.21 % | 2 |

The last column uses the standard result that a fitted sinusoid's amplitude has an error of σ√(2/N): at a 0.03 mag per-frame scatter, five-sigma detection of V8's 14 mmag needs around 900 frames, while V10's eclipses show up in a handful. Figure 1 draws the whole trade.

**Step 2: why the paper needed TESS.** Eight nights give a frequency resolution of 1 ÷ 8 = **0.125 cycles per day**, so periods beyond a week are barely a single cycle of signal. We simulated the paper's V9 (1.0981 d, 0.015 mag) and V38 (21.760 d, 0.280 mag) on the real sampling pattern — eight nights, 120 points per night, five hours a night, 0.008 mag scatter — and ran the Period finder's periodogram on each (Figure 2). V9 gives a sharp peak at 0.911 cycles per day and a period within 0.6 % of the truth; repeating at eight different starting phases keeps every answer inside **1.087 to 1.125 d**. V38 is luck. The eight-night window sees a third of one cycle, and the periodogram's power piles into the lowest frequencies it can reach; this particular run happened to return 21.99 d, but across eight starting phases the best period swings from **1.11 to 22.83 days**, once collapsing onto a one-day alias. A 27-day TESS sector fixes that; a week of ground data cannot.

**Step 3: the eclipsing binary is a weekend project.** V10's 0.330 mag, 6.1638 d eclipses are a different story. We simulated **six nights, 40 frames a night, 0.03 mag scatter** — a small telescope and a DSLR — and the periodogram found **6.09 d**, 1.2 % from the paper's value, with a false-alarm probability the tool reports as zero. The same run would not see V8 at all. If you want to help with a paper like this one, the large-amplitude candidates are where amateur data has value, and the [[variable-star-log]] exports it in the AAVSO's own formats.

**Step 4: the visual route, for completeness.** The log also does Argelander step estimates: a variable judged 3 steps fainter than an 11.2 mag comparison and 4 steps brighter than an 11.9 one gives **11.5 mag**. That is roughly a tenth-magnitude precision at best — fine for a Mira star, useless for anything in the table above except V10's eclipses.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ngc-663-b-type-star-variability-amateur-follow-up.mjs` in fibub's public code.

- `differential()`, `fractionEstimate()` and `jd()` from `src/lib/astro/aavso.ts`, the maths behind the [[variable-star-log]], give the differential magnitudes, the step estimate and the Julian dates.
- `lombScargle()`, `peaks()` and `falseAlarm()` from `src/lib/sci/lombscargle.ts`, behind the [[period-finder]], give the periodograms and false-alarm probabilities.

From the paper: the five example periods and amplitudes, the eight nights, the 3,187 and 7,494 frame counts and the exposure times. **Our assumptions:** 5 hours of usable night, 120 simulated frames a night, a per-frame scatter of 0.008 mag for the 50 cm run and 0.03 mag for a small telescope, pure sinusoids (real β Cephei and SPB stars have several frequencies at once), and the counts in the worked photometry example.

## How we test the maths

`node tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/variable-star-log.mjs` and the AAVSO block inside `tests/sci.mjs` on every change:

- `jd()` of 2000 January 1, 12:00 UT must be exactly 2451545.0, the definition of J2000.0.
- A step estimate between 11.0 and 11.3 at one step to two must be 11.1, and `differential(15200, 21800, 11.034)` must be 11.4255.
- An AAVSO visual file must come back through `parseAavso()` with its fainter-than flag, comment code and observer code intact, and an extended file must keep the filter, error, comparison magnitude and chart ID.
- New with this article: the worked chain above must give 11.4757 and 11.5105 magnitudes for 190,000 and 184,000 counts against a 200,000-count comparison at 11.42, a 0.0348 mag drop, and the Argelander estimate must give 11.500.

## Check it yourself

On the [[variable-star-log]]:

1. Press **Camera (CCD, DSLR)** at the top, so the form asks for measured counts.
2. In the *Work out the magnitude* card, choose **From measured brightness** and type **184000** in *Variable's counts*, **200000** in *Comparison's counts* and **11.42** in *Comparison's catalogue mag*. It shows **11.5105**. Press **Use this magnitude**.
3. Fill *Star* with the variable's name, set *Date and time (UTC)* (or press **Now**), type **0.02** in *Error (mag)*, choose **V** in *Filter*, and press **Add observation**.
4. Repeat with **190000** counts: the magnitude becomes **11.4757**, and the *Light curve* under your observations shows the 0.035 mag difference.
5. In *Export and import*, type your observer code, set *Camera files are* to **CCD** and download the **AAVSO extended file**. Everything stays in your browser until you upload it yourself.
6. For the period, paste your time and magnitude columns into the [[period-finder]] and look for a peak with a false-alarm probability below 0.001 — our simulated six-night run on a 6.16 d eclipsing binary gives one easily.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Decide whether a target is worth your telescope**: σ√(2/N) answers "how many frames for this amplitude" before you book the nights.
- **Check a published period against the data that found it**: if the period is longer than the observing baseline, look for the space-telescope data that really constrains it.
- **Recruit amateurs properly**: hand out the large-amplitude candidates, with the AAVSO export built into the log, and keep the millimagnitude ones for the big telescope.
- **Teach aliasing**: the same simulated star at eight starting phases shows why a one-day alias appears, and why duty cycle matters as much as baseline.
- **Keep a clean observing record**: the log holds the comparison and check stars, chart ID, filter, airmass and comment codes that a usable report needs.

## The fine print

- Our light curves are **simulated** on the paper's sampling pattern; they test what a run of that length and precision can recover, not the paper's own measurements.
- We modelled each star as one sinusoid. β Cephei and slowly pulsating B stars usually have several close frequencies, which makes real recovery harder than our figures suggest.
- Per-frame scatter (0.008 and 0.03 mag) is **our assumption**. The paper reports only that its six reference stars scatter below 0.1 mag.
- Amplitudes depend on the filter. The paper's B and V amplitudes differ for the same star, and TESS's red band gives smaller ones for hot stars.
- A candidate is not a classification. The paper is careful to call most of its stars candidates, and so are we.
