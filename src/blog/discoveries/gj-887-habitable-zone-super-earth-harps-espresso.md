GJ 887 is a red dwarf only 3.29 parsecs (10.7 light years) from the Sun, and the brightest red dwarf in our sky. In 2020 astronomers found two planets round it. A new study with two of the world's most precise planet-hunting spectrographs, HARPS and ESPRESSO in Chile, adds two more. One of them, GJ 887 d, orbits in the star's **habitable zone**, the range of distances where a rocky planet could keep liquid water on its surface. This article shows how the [[habitable-zone-calculator]] and the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]] let anyone check where the planets sit, how heavy they are and how much starlight they get.

## The paper in brief

The planets of GJ 887 do not pass in front of their star, so they were found by its **wobble**: each planet tugs the star towards and away from us, shifting the colours of its light very slightly. The team used 101 new HARPS and 12 new ESPRESSO measurements, and a statistical model (a Gaussian process) to separate the planets' signals from the star's own spots and activity, which repeat on its 38.7-day rotation.

They confirm four planets, with orbits of **4.42, 9.26, 21.78 and 50.77 days**. The two new ones are an Earth-mass planet on the 4.42-day orbit, whose pull on the star is below 1 m/s, and a super-Earth on the 50.77-day orbit in the habitable zone. The paper calls it the second-closest habitable-zone planet known, after Proxima Centauri b. A fifth signal at 2.22 days, exactly half the 4.42-day period, could be one more small planet, but other causes are possible, so it stays a **candidate**.

## Checked with the tools

We used the paper's periods, wobble sizes (semi-amplitudes, K), star temperature and distance; the star's mass and brightness from the 2020 discovery paper; and fibub's maths. We assumed circular orbits for the masses, which is close enough for the two new planets (the paper fits them as circular) and approximate for the two older ones.

**Step 1: where are the planets?** Kepler's third law with the star's 0.489 solar masses puts the planets at **0.042, 0.068, 0.120 and 0.211 au** from the star (the candidate at 0.026 au). All of them are closer than Mercury is to the Sun (0.39 au). Red dwarfs are dim, so their habitable zones are close in.

**Step 2: the habitable zone.** For a star of 3,688 K and 0.0368 times the Sun's light, the Habitable zone calculator puts the **conservative** zone at **0.191 to 0.381 au** for a super-Earth of 5 Earth masses (0.198 au for the inner edge if the planet were Earth's mass), and the wider **optimistic** zone at 0.157 to 0.402 au. Planet d, at 0.211 au, sits **inside the conservative zone**, near its warm inner edge (Figure 1). That zone corresponds to orbits of about 44 to 123 days.

**Step 3: how much starlight?** Planet d gets **0.82 times** the sunlight Earth gets. With an Earth-like reflectivity (albedo 0.3, our assumption) and no greenhouse warming, its **equilibrium temperature** is **243 K**, close to Earth's own 254 K on the same terms. Planet c, at 21.8 days, gets 2.5 times Earth's sunlight; planet e, at 4.4 days, 21 times.

**Step 4: how heavy?** The mass calculator turns each wobble into a **minimum mass**, the mass if we saw the orbit edge-on (the true mass is larger if the orbit is tilted). Planet d's 1.8 m/s on a 50.8-day orbit gives **6.5 Earth masses**: a super-Earth. Planet e's 0.89 m/s gives **1.4 Earth masses**, and the candidate's 0.37 m/s gives **0.47 Earth masses**, the "half an Earth" the paper mentions. The older planets b and c come out at 4.6 and 6.5 Earth masses with circular orbits; their true minimum masses depend on how eccentric the orbits are.

**Step 5: why this is hard.** Figure 2 adds up the four planets' wobbles. The whole system moves the star by a few metres per second, the speed of a jog, and planet d alone by 1.8 m/s. Spots on the star, carried round every 38.7 days, can fake signals of the same size, which is why the activity model matters.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/gj-887-habitable-zone-super-earth-harps-espresso.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls the tools' own functions:

- `habitableZone`, `auForPeriod`, `insolation` and `tEq` from `src/lib/sci/habitable.ts`, the maths behind the [[habitable-zone-calculator]].
- `minMass` from `src/lib/sci/rvmass.ts`, the maths behind the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]].

Where the inputs come from: the periods, semi-amplitudes, temperature and distance are from the paper; the star's mass (0.489 Suns) and luminosity (0.0368 Suns) are from the 2020 discovery paper by Jeffers and colleagues. ## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks these functions against known answers:

- The habitable zone maths must give the Sun's conservative zone as 0.950 to 1.676 au and its optimistic zone as 0.750 to 1.768 au, the values in Kopparapu and colleagues' 2014 paper.
- Earth's equilibrium temperature with an albedo of 0.306 must come out at 254 K.
- The orbit maths must give Jupiter's pull on the Sun as 12.47 m/s, and 51 Pegasi b's minimum mass as 0.477 Jupiter masses from its measured wobble.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Place a new planet** against its star's habitable zone in seconds, with the edges for an Earth or a super-Earth.
- **Turn a wobble into a minimum mass** and see how small a signal a planet of a given mass makes.
- **Compare starlight and temperature** across a system, to pick targets for future telescopes that will study nearby planets.
- **Check a claim** of "habitable zone" against a standard definition, and see how close to an edge the planet sits.
- **Teach** why the habitable zones of red dwarfs are so close in, and why their planets are easier to find.

## The fine print

- Being in the habitable zone does not mean a planet is habitable. Red dwarfs flare, and close-in planets may be tidally locked, with one side always facing the star.
- The masses are **minimum masses**: the orbits' tilts are unknown because the planets do not transit.
- The masses of planets b and c here assume circular orbits; the paper finds them eccentric, which changes them a little.
- The star's mass and luminosity come from the 2020 paper, not the 2026 one; small differences would shift the zone edges slightly.
- The equilibrium temperatures leave out any greenhouse warming, which on Earth adds about 33 K.
