A power station's hot reheat pipe runs near 600 °C for decades. Nobody can test a pipe for 300 000 hours before deciding whether it is safe for another ten years, so engineers test short at a higher temperature and carry the answer down to the service temperature with a **time-temperature parameter**. The oldest and most used one is the **Larson-Miller parameter**, LMP = T (C + log₁₀ t), with temperature T in kelvin, rupture time t in hours and a constant C that is almost always taken as 20. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Materials* took Grade 91 steel out of real steam pipes, crept it to rupture between 600 and 700 °C, and measured what C actually is. This article recomputes the consequences with the [[larson-miller-calculator]]; for the short-term strength of the same kind of steel see the [[stress-strain-analyser]], and for cycling loads the [[fatigue-sn-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors collected ASME Grade 91 piping that had spent up to about 148 000 hours in ultra-supercritical plants, including welded joints that had served around 117 000 hours at 602 °C and 38 MPa. They then ran **iso-stress tests**: tests at one stress and several temperatures, from 600 to 700 °C, on both base metal and welded joints, at 34 to 80 MPa.

Their central measurement is simple to state. Plot log₁₀ rupture time against 1/T at a fixed stress and the points fall on a straight line, over the whole 600 to 700 °C range and right down to service-level stresses. The slope and intercept of that line give the Larson-Miller parameter and the constant C, and the authors find **C between about 16 and 21 for welded joints and 18 to 21 for base metal**, against reference values near 23 and 21. C barely moves with stress or with how strong the particular heat of steel is. Because of that, they argue, the usual estimation method is safe enough: across the C values they measured, the estimated life moves by only **about 0.75 to 1.45 times**, and their method holds while C is within **±3** of the assumed value.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the values the paper states in its text. There is no new data here, and no measured curve was redrawn.

**Step 1: the straight line in 1/T is the Larson-Miller parameter.** Writing the parameter the other way round, log₁₀ t = LMP/T − C, shows that a single Larson-Miller parameter at a fixed stress *is* a straight line of log₁₀ t against 1/T, of slope LMP and intercept −C. So the paper's main measurement and the Larson-Miller model are the same statement, and C is read off the intercept. Figure 1 draws that line through the paper's own worked example, a 10 000 h rupture at 650 °C, for three constants: the lines meet at the test temperature and spread apart at 600 °C.

| 10 000 h rupture at 650 °C, carried to 600 °C | Paper says | Tool gives |
|---|---|---|
| C = 10 | about 60 000 h | 63 342 h |
| C = 20 | about 240 000 h | 236 774 h |
| C = 30 | about 890 000 h | 885 062 h |

**Step 3: where 0.75 and 1.45 come from.** The ratio of two carried-over lives has a tidy closed form. From log₁₀ t₂ = (T₁/T₂)(C + log₁₀ t₁) − C, changing C by ΔC multiplies the carried-over life by 10^(ΔC (T₁/T₂ − 1)) — and the rupture time itself cancels out. For 650 °C down to 600 °C, T₁/T₂ − 1 = 0.0573, so one unit of C is worth 14 % of life.

Solving for the reference constant that reproduces both of the paper's sensitivity figures gives **C = 18.18**: at C = 21 the tool returns a ratio of **1.45**, and at C = 16 **0.75**, exactly the paper's pair. In other words, the paper's 0.75-to-1.45 window is its measured C span of 16 to 21 seen from a mid-range C of about 18, for a test 50 °C above service. Its ±3 tolerance on C is worth 1.49 times upward and 0.67 times downward on the same basis.

That window is modest only because the tests sit close to the service temperature. Figure 2 shows the same ratio for tests further above 600 °C: a 3-unit error in C is worth 1.49 times for a 650 °C test but **2.19 times** for a 700 °C test. The cheaper the test programme, the more the constant matters.

**Step 4: fitting C instead of assuming it.** To show the whole tool rather than one formula, we built a **simulated** nine-test set (three stresses of 40, 56 and 80 MPa at 625, 650 and 675 °C) from a straight master curve with C = 18.18, chosen so that 40 MPa at 600 °C gives 270 000 h (**our assumption**, loosely consistent with the paper's unfailed 117 000 h service joints). Fitted back with the tool's `fitParam` and `bestC`, the data return **C = 18.19** and a scatter of 0.0004 in log₁₀ t. Forcing C = 16 or C = 21 instead still fits the data with a scatter below 0.07 — small enough to look fine on a plot — yet the predicted life at 600 °C and 40 MPa moves from 202 000 h to 391 000 h. Tests spread over 50 °C simply do not pin C tightly, which is why measuring it on service-exposed material, as the paper does, is worth the trouble.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/grade-91-steel-creep-rupture-larson-miller-constant-service-temperature.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `param`, `logTime`, `fitParam`, `bestC` and `ruptureTime` from `src/lib/sci/creep.ts`, the maths behind the [[larson-miller-calculator]].

From the paper's text: the 600 to 700 °C test range, the 10 000 h rupture at 650 °C and its three converted lives, the C ranges 16 to 21 and 18 to 21, the ±3 tolerance and the 0.75 to 1.45 life window. **Our assumptions**: the reference C of 18.18 (solved so that the tool reproduces the paper's 1.45 and 0.75), the nine simulated tests in step 4 and the straight master curve behind them, and the 270 000 h anchor at 600 °C and 40 MPa. The paper's measured rupture times are in its figures, so none of its data points are used or redrawn.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/larson-miller-calculator.mjs`, which pins the creep maths against NumPy and SciPy:

- On the page's 12 made-up ruptures, the quadratic Larson-Miller fit with C = 20 must give 26 056.089, 1235.753 and −1383.708, with a scatter of 0.07676 in log₁₀ t, as NumPy's `polyfit` does; the best-fit C must be 19.5028, as SciPy's `minimize_scalar` finds.
- The same fit must predict 33 784 h at 650 °C and 120 MPa, and allow 178.05 MPa for 100 000 h at 600 °C, matching SciPy's `brentq`.
- All three parameters must invert exactly: carrying a time to a parameter and back must return it to 1 part in 10¹².
- New with this article: a 10 000 h rupture at 650 °C carried to 600 °C must give 63 342, 236 774 and 885 062 h at C = 10, 20 and 30, and the life ratio from C = 18.18 to C = 21 must be 1.450 and to C = 16 must be 0.750.

## Check it yourself

On the [[larson-miller-calculator]] page, in the *Creep-rupture data* card:

1. Set *Temperatures in* to **°C** and paste our nine simulated tests, one per line as temperature, stress, hours: **625, 40, 59400**; **625, 56, 17400**; **625, 80, 4730**; **650, 40, 14200**; **650, 56, 4300**; **650, 80, 1210**; **675, 40, 3660**; **675, 56, 1140**; **675, 80, 333**.
2. Set *Polynomial in log σ* to a **straight line** and *Larson-Miller C* to **18.18**. The master curve reads about **25 871 − 3279 x** (x = log₁₀ σ) with a scatter near **0.000**, and the best-fit C shown beside it is **18.19**.
3. In *Predict rupture life and allowable stress*, type *Temperature* **600**, *Stress* **40** and *Design life* **100000**. The Larson-Miller life is about **269 850 h**.
4. Change *Larson-Miller C* to **16**. The life at 600 °C and 40 MPa falls to about **202 400 h**: 0.75 times. Change it to **21** and it rises to about **391 300 h**: 1.45 times. Those are the paper's two sensitivity figures, reproduced from its own C range.
5. Leave C at 18.18 and change *Stress* to **60**: about **58 860 h**, under a tenth of the 40 MPa life. Creep life is far more sensitive to stress than to C.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Repeat a published extrapolation** before trusting it: paste the rupture table, set the paper's C, and see whether its 100 000 h stress falls out.
- **Report how much your answer depends on C**: run the fit at C − 3, C and C + 3 and quote the three lives, as this paper effectively does.
- **Design a test programme**: the life ratio 10^(ΔC (T_test/T_service − 1)) says how far above service you can afford to test for a given uncertainty in C.
- **Compare parameters**: fit Sherby-Dorn and Manson-Haferd on the same data; if they disagree with Larson-Miller, the extrapolation is not safe.
- **Teach the method**: a class can see the master curve collapse tests at three temperatures onto one line, then watch it move as C changes.

## The fine print

- Every number here is recomputed from values the paper states in its text. Its measured rupture times are only in its figures, so we have not refitted its data.
- The reference C of 18.18 is our solution for the constant that reproduces the paper's own 1.45 and 0.75 figures; the paper does not state a reference C.
- The nine-test set in step 4 and the master curve behind it are simulated, not Grade 91 data. Real Grade 91 rupture data curve in log stress and scatter far more.
- Grade 91 welds fail in the heat-affected zone, so a weld's life is not the base metal's. The paper treats them separately, and so should any extrapolation.
- A time-temperature parameter assumes the damage mechanism does not change between test and service. The paper notes that for the related Grade 92 the creep activation energy differs by more than a factor of two between short and long times, which is exactly the risk.
