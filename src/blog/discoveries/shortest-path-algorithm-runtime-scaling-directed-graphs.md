Every computer science course teaches that Dijkstra's algorithm costs about (V + E) log V steps, Bellman–Ford costs V·E and Floyd–Warshall costs V³. A 2025 paper in *Engineering Proceedings* put a stopwatch on all three in directed graphs and found something the bounds do not predict: on small graphs Bellman–Ford was only **two to three times** slower than Dijkstra, and on a 1,000-vertex graph it was **3.3 times** slower — not the seventy-odd times the formulas suggest. This article works out why with the [[big-o-plotter]], which turns a growth function into steps, seconds and a fitted class. To watch the algorithms themselves run on a graph you type in, use [[graph-algorithms]] and the [[algorithm-visualiser]].

## The paper in brief

The authors implemented Dijkstra, Bellman–Ford, Floyd–Warshall and Dantzig's method, with a visual front end, and timed them on directed graphs from a handful of vertices up to 1,000. Their headline measurements, with the visualisation switched off, are for a graph of **1,000 vertices and 2,800 edges**: Dijkstra about **3,298.4 µs**, Bellman–Ford about **10,902 µs**, and Floyd–Warshall **4,492,317 µs** — four and a half seconds. On graphs under 20 vertices Dijkstra typically finished in under 10 µs and Bellman–Ford took two to three times as long. They conclude that Dijkstra is the best single-source choice on sparse graphs, that Bellman–Ford "degrades faster than Dijkstra as size grows", and that for graphs above 500 vertices Dijkstra beats Floyd–Warshall by **100 to 1,000 times**, while Floyd–Warshall may win on dense graphs because it answers every pair at once.

Three of those claims are checkable from the numbers alone, with no graph data needed: the size of the Bellman–Ford gap, the size of the Floyd–Warshall gap, and how dense a graph has to be before the all-pairs method pays.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation**, not a simulation. We take the paper's own sparsity — 2,800 edges over 1,000 vertices, so 2.8 edges per vertex — and write the three textbook bounds through the [[big-o-plotter]]'s own growth functions: Dijkstra with a binary heap as (1 + 2.8)·*n* log₂ *n*, Bellman–Ford as 2.8·*n*², Floyd–Warshall as *n*³.

**Step 2: divide the paper's microseconds by our step counts.** This is where the paper's puzzle resolves. At 1,000 vertices the measured times imply **87.1 ns per Dijkstra operation**, **3.89 ns per Bellman–Ford edge relaxation** and **4.49 ns per Floyd–Warshall inner step**. Dijkstra's steps are about **22 times more expensive** than the other two, because each one is a binary-heap push or pop with pointer chasing and cache misses, while Bellman–Ford and Floyd–Warshall sweep flat arrays in order.

So the paper's measurement is not in conflict with the theory at all. Bellman–Ford does 74 times more work but each unit is 22 times cheaper, and 74 ÷ 22 ≈ 3.3 — exactly the ratio the paper measured. The "2 to 3 times slower" that the authors see on small graphs is not a property of Bellman–Ford; it is the point at which the two effects cancel. Push the size up and the quadratic term wins: at 5,000 vertices the same arithmetic predicts about 13 times slower, and at 20,000 vertices about 46 times.

**Step 3: the Floyd–Warshall gap is bigger than the paper says.** The authors report 100 to 1,000 times for graphs over 500 vertices. Their own 1,000-vertex measurement is **1,362 times** (4,492,317 ÷ 3,298.4), already above their stated range, and the step counts with equal cost per step would give **26,406 times**. The cheap-step correction brings 26,406 down to about 1,360 — again a match once constants are taken into account. The stated "100 to 1,000×" looks like a summary across their smaller graphs rather than a result at 1,000 vertices.

**Step 4: when is Floyd–Warshall actually the right choice?** It is an all-pairs method, so compare it against running Dijkstra from every vertex: *V*³ against *V*·(*V* + *E*) log₂ *V*. They tie when *V*² = (*V* + *E*) log₂ *V*, which means the edge density at the turning point is 1 ÷ log₂ *V* − 1 ÷ *V*. At 1,000 vertices that is **9.9 % of all possible edges**, about 99 edges per vertex; at 5,000 vertices, **8.1 %**. The paper's own graph has 2.8 edges per vertex, which is 0.28 % — thirty-five times too sparse. The authors' suggestion that Floyd–Warshall "may beat Dijkstra on dense graphs with several hundred or more vertices" is right, but the threshold is about density, not about size: a graph needs roughly a tenth of all possible edges before it pays, and that threshold falls only slowly as the graph grows.

**Step 5: the fitter gets the class right when the data are clean.** Feeding our Dijkstra step counts for *n* = 100 to 6,400 into the tool's class fitter returns **O(n log n)** with R² = 1 and a fitted coefficient of exactly **3.8**, the mean degree plus one. The leftover sum of squares is 6 × 10⁻²¹ against 1.33 × 10⁸ for the next best class, O(n). Note the log–log slope: **1.154**, not 1. Anyone fitting a straight line through log–log timings and reading "n^1.15" is looking at n log n.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/shortest-path-algorithm-runtime-scaling-directed-graphs.mjs` in fibub's public code. It imports `CLASSES`, `fitClasses`, `fromLog10` and `duration` from `src/lib/sci/bigo.ts`, the maths behind the [[big-o-plotter]], and builds each algorithm's cost out of the tool's own growth functions: `C.nlogn.g(n)` for Dijkstra, `C.n2.g(n)` for Bellman–Ford and `C.n3.g(n)` for Floyd–Warshall.

From the paper we used three measured times (3,298.4, 10,902 and 4,492,317 µs) and one graph size (1,000 vertices, 2,800 edges). Nothing else. **Our assumptions** are the textbook bounds themselves, that the implementation uses a binary heap, that the paper's 2.8 edges per vertex holds as the graph grows, and that the cost per operation stays constant with size (it does not, quite: bigger arrays miss cache more often).

We also tried timing the real `dijkstra` and `bellmanFord` in `src/lib/sci/graph.ts` across sizes, but wall-clock timings on a shared virtual machine were too noisy to fit a class honestly — the measured curve for Dijkstra came out sublinear. That is a lesson about benchmarking, not about the algorithm, and it is why this article counts steps instead. In a real browser on a quiet machine the tool's "Time an algorithm in this browser" card does the job.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/big-o-plotter.mjs`, which pins the plotter against values worked out in NumPy and Python:

- Every class fit (a, b, residual sum of squares, R² and the chosen best class) must match a NumPy `lstsq` solution on several made-up timing sets, and the log–log slope to 1e-9.
- A time that falls as n grows must be refused for every class except O(1), and the O(1) fit must be the mean with R² = 0.
- The log₁₀ sizes must be exact for awkward cases: 2¹⁰ = 1024, 10! = 3,628,800 and log₁₀(1000!) = 2567.6046442; `fromLog10` must print "4.02 × 10^2,567" and `duration` must turn 0.0123 s into "12.3 ms" and 90 s into "1.5 minutes".
- New with this article: on step counts of 3.8·n log₂ n for n = 100 to 6,400 the fitter must pick **O(n log n)** with b = 3.8 to 1e-12, R² = 1 and a log–log slope of 1.15356, and the operation counts at 1,000 vertices must be 37,870 for Dijkstra, 2.8 × 10⁶ for Bellman–Ford and 10⁹ for Floyd–Warshall.

6. It reports **Best fit: O(n log n)**, with *Fitted line* **time = −0.06808 + 3.8 × n log₂ n**, *R²* **1**, *Next best* **O(n), leftover 7.2e+8 × larger** and *Log-log slope* **1.15 (about 1 for n, 2 for n², 3 for n³)**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a complexity bound into a wall-clock estimate** before writing code: pick the class, pick a nanoseconds-per-step figure from a small experiment, and read the time at the size you care about.
- **Explain a surprising benchmark.** Divide measured time by predicted steps for each algorithm; if the nanoseconds per step differ by 20×, the constants are the story.
- **Choose between single-source and all-pairs.** Figure 2 is the whole decision for Floyd–Warshall against repeated Dijkstra, and it depends on density, not size.
- **Fit an unknown implementation's class** from a timing table, with the leftover sums of squares shown so you can see whether n and n log n are really distinguishable at your sizes.
- **Teach that O(·) hides constants**, with a case where a 74-fold difference in work shows up as a 3-fold difference on the clock.

## The fine print

- The paper's times come from its own implementation on its own machine. We used three of them and did not re-time anything.
- Our step counts are the textbook bounds with the constant set to 1, which is a convention, not a measurement. The implied nanoseconds-per-step figures absorb whatever the real constants are.
- Cost per step is not constant with size: bigger graphs miss cache more, so the measured gaps widen a little faster than our arithmetic says.
- The paper reports the paths graph for Figure 4 and some operation estimates (5,890.4 for Dijkstra, 1,078,922 for Bellman–Ford and 29,418,309 for Floyd–Warshall at 659 vertices and 1,538 edges) that we could not reproduce from the standard formulas, which give about 20,600, 1,013,542 and 2.86 × 10⁸. We left them alone. The paper also states in one place that its largest graphs had 1,000 edges and elsewhere 2,800.
- Dantzig's method is in the paper and not here; the tool has no class for it beyond the same V³.
- None of this is about correctness. All four algorithms return the same distances on a graph with non-negative weights.
