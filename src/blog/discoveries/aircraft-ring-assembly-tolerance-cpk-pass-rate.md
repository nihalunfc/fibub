A paper that reports both a pass rate and a capability index is reporting the same thing twice, because for a normal characteristic one number fixes the other. That makes such papers easy to check, and worth checking. A 2025 study in *Machines* modelled the error chain of a helicopter ring assembly, found which error sources mattered with a Sobol sensitivity analysis, re-allocated the tolerances with a genetic algorithm, and reports that the **position pass rate went from 67.4 % to 100.0 % (Cpk 0.31 to 2.19)** and coaxiality from 93.5 % to 100.0 % (Cpk 0.62 to 1.06). This article puts those four pairs through the [[tolerance-stackup|tolerance stack-up calculator]], and then asks what a seven-fold capability gain demands of the hardware. For the day-to-day versions of the same job, see the [[control-charts|control chart calculator]] and [[gauge-rr|gauge R&R calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The assembly is a ring-on-ring aircraft part (the automatic tilt device case of a helicopter), where what matters is the **position** of one ring relative to the other and their **coaxiality**. The authors split the error sources into three families: errors brought in by the components themselves, errors from the automation (robot pose, measurement, fixture), and errors from the process itself. They then build a **directed coupling network** so that an error at one step feeds forward into the next, run a **Monte Carlo** simulation of 20,000 assemblies, use **Sobol global sensitivity analysis** to rank the contributors, and finally re-allocate tolerances with the **NSGA-II** multi-objective genetic algorithm.

The headline result is the capability jump above. The paper also quotes the context: automated drilling and riveting cells reach about ±0.05 mm, while manual assembly or a basic industrial robot gives about ±0.2 mm. The detailed tolerance tables are in the parts of the article we could not read through our tooling, so this piece uses the four published pairs and the paper's own ±0.05 and ±0.2 mm figures.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: do the pass rates and the capability indices agree?** The [[tolerance-stackup|tolerance stack-up calculator]] reports both from the same model, using `fallout`, which turns a mean, a standard deviation and two limits into the fraction outside and into Cp and Cpk. Running it in units of sigma gives the pass rate that each published Cpk implies. Figure 1 shows the two curves, for a characteristic with two limits and the mean in the middle, and for a single upper limit.

Three of the four pairs line up with a **two-sided, nearly centred** characteristic, not a one-sided one: the coaxiality pass rate before optimisation (93.5 %) is within 0.2 points of the 93.71 % a centred Cpk of 0.62 gives, and a one-sided reading would have to be 96.9 %. The position pair needs a little more: a Cpk of 0.31 gives 67.4 % exactly when the mean sits **0.45 σ off the middle** of a band whose Cp is 0.46. That is a sensible picture of an assembly with a small systematic offset on top of its scatter, and it is the kind of detail a paper can state in one line.

**Step 2: a Cpk of 1.06 is not a 100 % pass rate.** The fourth pair is the one that does not fit. Put 1.06 into the tool and the normal model gives **99.853 %** inside the limits: about **1,473 parts per million**, or one assembly in 679. In the paper's own 20,000-run Monte Carlo that is about **30 failing assemblies**, so "100.0 %" is a rounded simulation count rather than a capability statement. For comparison, the position characteristic's Cpk of 2.19 means 0.05 failures per billion, which really is zero for any practical purpose. The gap between the two "100.0 %" entries is a factor of 30,000 in risk, and only the capability indices show it. If you report both numbers, report the pass rate to enough digits to tell them apart.

**Step 3: how much tighter does the chain have to be?** Capability is inversely proportional to the standard deviation of the result, so going from Cpk 0.31 to 2.19 against the same limits means the spread of the position error had to fall by a factor of **7.06**. To see what that asks of the parts, we built a five-link demonstration chain (**our invented numbers**, not the paper's): a frame datum hole at ±0.40 mm, robot pose at ±0.20 mm, fixture location at ±0.20 mm, ring machining at ±0.12 mm and measurement at ±0.08 mm, all normal with the tolerance taken as ±3 σ. The tool gives a worst case of ±1.000 mm, an RSS half-width of ±0.511 mm (σ = 0.1702 mm) and these variance shares: **61.3 %** for the datum hole, 15.3 % and 15.3 % for the robot and fixture, 5.5 % for machining and 2.5 % for measurement. Setting the position band at ±0.158 mm makes this chain start at the paper's Cpk of 0.31.

Now the useful part. **Decoupling cannot do it.** Removing the largest contributor completely, which is the best any clever re-datuming can achieve, multiplies σ by √(1 − 0.613) = **0.622** and lifts Cpk only to **0.50**. The 7.06-fold cut needs the tolerances themselves: multiplying every one by **0.142** gives Cpk 2.19, and turns the chain into ±0.057, ±0.028, ±0.028, ±0.017 and ±0.011 mm. That largest number is almost exactly the **±0.05 mm** the paper quotes for an automated drilling and riveting cell, while the ±0.40 mm we started from is twice its ±0.2 mm manual figure. So the capability jump the paper reports is the size of jump you get by moving the whole chain from hand work to an automated cell: a re-allocation of tolerances alone, without better equipment, could not produce it.

**Step 4: the Monte Carlo agrees with the normal model.** Running the tool's seeded simulation on the same chain, 20,000 assemblies as in the paper, gives **35.38 %** outside the ±0.158 mm band before and **0 of 20,000** after, against the normal model's 35.24 % and 0.00005 %. The simulated standard deviations, 0.17027 mm and 0.02410 mm, match the RSS values to four figures. For a sum of five independent normal parts that is expected; it is worth checking anyway, because an RSS half-width is only as normal as its biggest contributor.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/aircraft-ring-assembly-tolerance-cpk-pass-rate.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `stack`, `fallout`, `monteCarlo` and `summarise` from `src/lib/sci/eng-stack.ts`, the maths behind the [[tolerance-stackup|tolerance stack-up calculator]]: the worst-case and root-sum-of-squares widths, each part's share of the variance, the normal-model fallout with Cp and Cpk, and the seeded Monte Carlo.

From the paper: the four pass rate and Cpk pairs, the 20,000-run simulation size and the ±0.05 and ±0.2 mm accuracy figures. **Our assumptions:** the five-link demonstration chain and its tolerances, a position band of ±0.158 mm (chosen so the chain starts at the paper's Cpk of 0.31), normal parts with the tolerance read as ±3 σ, and independence between the links. The chain is **not** the paper's error model, which is a coupled network; it is the simplest chain that shows what a capability ratio costs in tolerances.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/tolerance-stackup.mjs` on every change to fibub, pinning the stack-up against SciPy and NumPy:

- A five-part chain with normal, uniform and triangular parts must give a nominal gap of 0.2, a mean of 0.26 (the asymmetric bearing shifts it), a worst case of ±0.31 and an RSS σ of 0.0608276, with the variance shares adding to 1.
- Against limits of 0.05 and 0.40 the normal model must give 2.7783 x 10⁻⁴ below and 1.06793 x 10⁻² above, as `scipy.stats.norm` does, with the matching Cp and Cpk.
- A 100,000-run Monte Carlo must match an independent NumPy simulation of 2 million draws (mean 0.26000, sd 0.06078, 0.0088 % below and 0.932 % above) within sampling error, and the same seed must always give the same numbers.
- New with this article: the normal model must give **64.763 %** inside the limits at Cpk 0.31, **99.8527 %** at Cpk 1.06 and **1,472.8 ppm** outside at that index, so the pass-rate curve in Figure 1 cannot drift.

## Check it yourself

On the [[tolerance-stackup|tolerance stack-up calculator]]:

1. Paste our demonstration chain into *Dimension chain*, one part per line (name, nominal, tolerance): **Frame datum hole, 0, 0.40** · **Robot pose, 0, 0.20** · **Fixture location, 0, 0.20** · **Ring machining, 0, 0.12** · **Measurement, 0, 0.08**
2. Set *Gap lower limit* to **-0.158** and *Gap upper limit* to **0.158**. Leave *k* at **3**, *Monte Carlo samples* at **20000** and pick any *Random seed*.
3. *Worst case* reads **-1 to 1**, *RSS (statistical)* reads **-0.5107 to 0.5107** with σ = **0.1702**, and *Cp and Cpk* both read **0.31**: the paper's starting point.
4. *Out of limits (normal estimate)* is about **35.2 %**, and the simulated figure is about **35.4 %**. The share column shows the frame datum hole carrying **61 %** of the variance: that is what to fix first.
5. Delete the frame datum hole row. Cpk rises only to **0.50**, which is what decoupling the worst error source can do on its own.
6. Put it back and multiply every tolerance by 0.142: **0.0566**, **0.0283**, **0.0283**, **0.017**, **0.0113**. Cpk becomes **2.19** and nothing falls outside in 20,000 simulated assemblies, matching the paper's position result.
7. Set both limits to **±0.0766** instead (a Cpk of 1.06 for the same tightened chain) and watch *Out of limits* read about **0.15 %**, not zero: the coaxiality case.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published capability claim** in a minute: a Cpk and a pass rate in the same table must agree, and Figure 1 says what the pass rate should be.
- **Report the number that carries information.** Above Cpk 1, pass rates saturate at "100 %" while the risk still changes by orders of magnitude, so quote parts per million as well.
- **Price a precision target.** Turning a wanted Cpk into the tolerances it needs is one division: σ scales as 1/Cpk, and the tool shows which links must carry the cut.
- **Separate decoupling from tightening.** The variance shares put a hard ceiling on what re-datuming or error compensation can win, √(1 − share), before any money is spent on better hardware.
- **Keep the gain once it is won** with the [[control-charts|control chart calculator]], and check that the measurement itself is not the problem with the [[gauge-rr|gauge R&R calculator]].

## The fine print

- We could not read the paper's tolerance tables, so our five-link chain is **invented** to illustrate the arithmetic of the capability jump. It is not the paper's error model, and the paper's coupled network can behave differently from an independent chain.
- The pass rates implied by a Cpk assume a **normal** characteristic. A true geometric position deviation is a radial distance and so is not normal; for such a characteristic the pass rate at a given Cpk is different, which is one reason to publish the distribution as well as the index.
- The ±0.158 mm band is our choice, made to match the paper's starting Cpk. The paper's real limits may be anything; only the ratios in this article are independent of that choice.
- Cpk says nothing about stability over time. A process can hold a Cpk of 2 for a week and drift the next; that is what control charts are for.
- The mean offset of 0.45 σ that reconciles 67.4 % with Cpk 0.31 is one of many combinations of offset and band width that would do it. We report it as an illustration, not as the paper's value.
