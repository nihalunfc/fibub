Fish sperm sit still inside the male and start swimming when the water around them changes. For a freshwater fish it is the drop in salt concentration that switches them on, so the **osmolality** of the solution you add decides whether you see 10 % or 80 % of the cells moving. A 2024 paper in *Scientific Reports* worked out a complete handling protocol for Japanese medaka, the small laboratory fish used in genetics and toxicology, and reported the best motility after activation at 300 mOsm/kg. Because the paper prints both of its solution recipes, every osmolality in it can be recomputed, and that is what this article does with the [[osmolarity-calculator]]. The same sums with freezing-point depression are in the [[colligative-properties]] page, and the weighing is in the [[solution-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors compared ways of collecting medaka milt (stripping against dissecting the testes), two activating solutions, short-term storage on ice at three osmolalities, and freezing with methanol. Stripping gave higher motility than dissection. Activation worked best around 300 mOsm/kg: in the Kurokura solution labelled **K 300** total motility was 75 ± 5 % against 66 ± 5 % in **HBSS 300** (not a significant difference), but the cells swam significantly faster in K 300 (curvilinear velocity 109 ± 7 against 83 ± 4 µm/s). For storage, the opposite is wanted: a solution that keeps the cells quiet, and HBSS at 600 mOsm/kg kept 97 ± 2 % of them immobile after four hours on ice, with motility and progressive swimming preserved for three hours. Freezing in 16 % methanol with 126 mM sucrose gave about 35 % motility after thawing.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the recipes printed in the paper. Nothing is simulated, and we did not touch the motility measurements.

**Step 1: HBSS 300 is where its label says.** Adding up the eight components with the tool (concentration × particles per formula unit) gives **306.3 mOsm/L** if every salt is treated as fully dissociated, and **285.2 mOsm/L** with an osmotic coefficient of 0.93 for the salts (**our assumption**, a usual value for 0.1 to 0.2 M sodium chloride). The measured 300 mOsm/kg sits between the two, as it should: a real solution is a little less ideal than the simple sum, and osmolality (per kg of water) runs slightly above osmolarity (per litre of solution).

**Step 2: the Kurokura solution labelled "K 300" comes out near 374 mOsm/L.** Its 180 mM sodium chloride alone contributes 360 mOsm/L. The full recipe gives **374.2 mOsm/L** ideal and **348.0** with the same coefficient of 0.93: about **22 % above HBSS 300**. To bring the recipe down to 300 you would need an osmotic coefficient of **0.80** for the salts, which is far lower than sodium chloride shows at this concentration (Figure 1). So the two solutions the paper compares are not equally concentrated, and the significantly faster swimming in K 300 may be an effect of the extra 50 to 70 mOsm rather than of the different ions. That is worth knowing for anyone copying the protocol: the "300" in the two names does not mean the same thing.

In pressure terms the tool puts the difference at **759.3 kPa against 927.7 kPa** of osmotic pressure at 25 °C (7.5 against 9.2 atmospheres): a gap of nearly two atmospheres across the sperm membrane.

**Step 3: methanol's osmoles are not felt.** The freezing mixture is 16 % methanol by volume, which is **3,954 mmol/L** (methanol, 32.042 g/mol, density 0.7918 g/mL). Put that into the tool as a solute that crosses membranes, with the sucrose and salts diluted by the methanol added, and the **total osmolarity is about 4,300 mOsm/L while the effective osmolarity (tonicity) is only about 345** (Figure 2). Both numbers are real, and they answer different questions: an osmometer reading of the mixed solution would be thousands of mOsm, but once the methanol has crossed into the cell it pulls no water, so the cell responds to the few hundred mOsm of salts and sucrose. The paper's "600 mOsm/kg" for the freezing solution must therefore describe the extender it is made in, not the mixture a freezing-point osmometer would read.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/medaka-sperm-activation-solutions-osmolarity-recomputed.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `osmolarity`, `osmoticKPa`, `solute` and `formulaMass` from `src/lib/sci/osmolar.ts`, the maths behind the [[osmolarity-calculator]]: osmolarity = Σ concentration × i × φ, the effective part leaving out solutes that cross membranes, and van 't Hoff's π = cRT.

The two recipes, the methanol percentage and the sucrose concentration come from the paper's text. The osmotic coefficient of 0.93 for the salts, the density of methanol and the choice to dilute the extender by the methanol added are **our assumptions**, and the freezing mixture in Figure 2 is built on HBSS 300 (the paper used a 600 mOsm/kg extender whose recipe it does not give), so that figure is **our construction** showing the shape of the effect, not the paper's exact solution.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/osmolarity-calculator.mjs`, which pins the tool to textbook cases:

- Molar masses worked out from formulas must match a Python rewrite with IUPAC atomic weights typed in separately (sodium chloride 58.43977, calcium chloride dihydrate 147.008, glucose 180.156 g/mol), and every solute in the list must have a formula that parses.
- Unit conversions: 0.9 % saline is 154.0047 mmol/L and 5 % glucose 277.5372 mmol/L.
- A mixture of that saline (φ 0.93) with 100 mM urea must give a total of 386.4488 mOsm/L and an effective osmolarity of 286.4488, because urea crosses membranes, and van 't Hoff's pressure at 37 °C must be 996.5473 kPa, 9.8352 atm and 7474.72 mmHg.
- New with this article: the paper's HBSS recipe must give 306.3 mOsm/L ideal and 285.2 mOsm/L at φ 0.93, the Kurokura recipe 374.2 and 348.0, and a mixture with 3,954 mmol/L methanol must report a total near 4,300 mOsm/L with an effective osmolarity near 345.

## Check it yourself

On the [[osmolarity-calculator]] page:

1. Press **Add a solute** until you have eight rows, and set them to the HBSS recipe: *Sodium chloride* **137**, *Potassium chloride* **5.4**, *Calcium chloride* **1.3**, *Magnesium sulfate* **1.0**, *Disodium phosphate* **0.25**, *Monopotassium phosphate* **0.44**, *Sodium bicarbonate* **4.2**, *Glucose* **5.55**, each with *Unit* **mmol/L**.
2. Leave every *Osmotic coefficient φ* at **1**, set *Temperature (°C)* to **25** and *Reference (mOsm/L)* to **300**.
3. It reads *Osmolarity* **306.3 mOsm/L** ("about equal to the reference of 300"), *Osmotic pressure* **759.3 kPa** and the same figure for *From non-penetrating solutes*, because nothing here crosses membranes.
4. Type **0.93** into φ for the seven salts: the osmolarity falls to **285.2 mOsm/L**.
5. Now the other recipe: four rows, *Sodium chloride* **180**, *Potassium chloride* **2.68**, *Calcium chloride* **1.36**, *Sodium bicarbonate* **2.38**, all at φ 1. It reads **374.2 mOsm/L**, marked as higher than the 300 reference.
6. For the methanol, add a row, choose **Other…**, name it **Methanol**, set *Molar mass (g/mol)* to **32.04**, *Amount* **12.67** with *Unit* **% w/v (g/100 mL)** (that is 16 % by volume), *i* **1**, and tick **Crosses membranes**. The *Osmolarity* line jumps by about 3,950 mOsm/L while *Effective osmolarity (tonicity)* does not move.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published solution before you make it up**, as above: a label and a recipe should agree, and when they do not, the recipe is the thing that gets pipetted.
- **Compare treatments on the same scale**: if one group's solution is 20 % more concentrated, that difference belongs in the discussion of any result.
- **Separate tonicity from osmolality** when a cryoprotectant is present, which is exactly what matters for whether a cell swells, shrinks or bursts on dilution.
- **Work out the weighing** for a target osmolality with the tool's grams-for-a-target box, then scale the recipe with the [[solution-calculator]].
- **Teach activation experiments**: medaka, zebrafish and trout sperm all respond to osmolality, and the arithmetic is the same for insect saline, plant media or a rehydration drink.

## The fine print

- Osmolarity (per litre of solution) and osmolality (per kg of water) are not identical. For these dilute solutions they differ by about 1 %, which does not explain a 22 % gap.
- The osmotic coefficient of 0.93 is our single assumption for all the salts; calcium chloride and magnesium sulfate have lower coefficients, which would lower both totals a little further and would not close the gap.
- We have not seen the authors' osmometer readings for the Kurokura solution, only its name. It is possible the solution was adjusted after mixing, in which case our figure describes the printed recipe rather than what was used.
- Figure 2's freezing mixture is our construction on HBSS 300, because the paper does not print the recipe of its 600 mOsm/kg extender.
- This is laboratory and teaching material about fish gametes; nothing here is veterinary or medical advice.
