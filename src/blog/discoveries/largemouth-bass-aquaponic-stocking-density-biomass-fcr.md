Aquaponics puts fish and vegetables in one water loop, so the fish density sets both the harvest and the fertiliser the plants get. A 2025 paper in *Fishes* ran nine small recirculating units for 176 days with largemouth bass at three nominal densities, 3.1, 4.1 and 6.2 kg per cubic metre, and reported growth, feeding behaviour, flesh quality and the lettuce, basil and chard grown above. This article rebuilds the fish side of that trial with the [[stocking-density-calculator]], which holds the same arithmetic a farm uses: biomass, density, specific growth rate, feed conversion and a day-by-day projection. The growth numbers check out to the last decimal; the feed numbers only hang together once the fish that died are counted. For the next steps, follow a batch's weights with the [[growth-curve-tracker]] and check the water can carry the load with the [[pond-oxygen-budget]].

## The paper in brief

Each unit had a 500 L fish tank in a loop with a biofilter, a settling tank and a storage tank, and nine vertical towers of vegetables above. The 184 bass averaged **109 ± 28 g** at stocking, and the three treatments held **15 to 17, 16 to 20 and 24 to 29** fish per tank, labelled D3, D4 and D6 for their nominal densities. The trial ran **176 days** from June to November at 18 to 27 °C, with feeding twice a day to apparent satiation.

The headline results: daily weight gain fell with crowding, **0.69, 0.61 and 0.50 g per day**, and specific growth rate with it, **0.43, 0.39 and 0.34 % per day**. Feed intake was **0.72 %** of biomass per day in the lightest treatment against **0.60 to 0.61 %** in the other two, and feed conversion was **1.43 to 1.46** in D3 and D4 against **1.76** in D6. Fish in the crowded tanks also ate more slowly (1.23 against 2.21 g per fish per minute) and their tanks held less dissolved oxygen. Survival was **73.9 %** across the trial, 48 fish lost of 184.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's own stated values**, not a simulation of its data. The one thing we had to assume is the shape of the mortality: the paper gives a single survival figure, so we spread the losses evenly as a constant **5.02 % per month** (our assumption), which lands on 73.9 % at day 176.

**Step 1: growth rate from weight gain, three times out of three.** The calculator's *Growth and feed over a period* card turns a start and end weight into a specific growth rate. Take 109 g plus 0.69 g per day for 176 days and the final mean weight is 230.4 g, so SGR = 100 × ln(230.4 ÷ 109) ÷ 176 = **0.425 % per day**, which rounds to the paper's 0.43. The same step gives **0.390** for D4 (against 0.39) and **0.336** for D6 (against 0.34). The paper's two growth measures are exactly consistent with each other.

**Step 2: the nominal densities need fractional fish.** At 109 g in 500 L, a density of 3.1 kg m⁻³ asks for **14.2 fish**, 4.1 for 18.8 and 6.2 for 28.4; the tool's *Target density* field rounds those down to 14, 18 and 28, giving **3.05, 3.92 and 6.10 kg m⁻³**. The stated tank counts imply a wider spread: 15 to 17 fish is **3.27 to 3.71 kg m⁻³**, 16 to 20 fish is **3.49 to 4.36**, and 24 to 29 fish is **5.23 to 6.32**. The heaviest D3 tank (3.71) was therefore more crowded than the lightest D4 tank (3.49). With tanks this small, the integer-fish step is a real part of the treatment's uncertainty.

**Step 3: density at the end, not the start.** A stocking density is a starting condition; what the fish live in is the density on the last day. Running the projection with each treatment's own growth rate, feeding rate and our even mortality gives **4.77, 5.76 and 8.15 kg m⁻³** at day 176 (Figure 1). So D3 finished at a density between the starting points of D3 and D4, and D6 finished a third above the highest density tested. Any claim about "6.2 kg m⁻³" in this trial is really a claim about 6.1 rising to 8.2.

**Step 4: feed conversion only works if the dead fish count.** The projection also adds up the feed: at 0.72 % of biomass per day, D3's tank eats **2.44 kg** over the trial; at 0.605 %, D4 eats 2.55 kg and D6 **3.77 kg**. Divide that by the gain in *surviving* biomass and the feed conversion ratios come out at **2.84, 2.78 and 3.68** — nothing like the published 1.43 to 1.76. Credit the growth of the fish that died (taking each death at the mid-trial mean weight, **our assumption**) and the same function gives **1.65, 1.52 and 1.76**. D6 lands exactly on the paper's 1.76 and D4 within 5 %; D3's 1.65 against 1.43 suggests its feeding rate was nearer 0.63 % than 0.72 % of biomass per day, or that its mortality came later in the trial than our even spread.

**Step 5: how long to a market fish.** Bass are sold near 400 g. Running the same growth rates on past the trial's end, 109 g reaches 400 g in **306 days** at D3's rate, **334** at D4's and **387** at D6's (Figure 2). The 81-day penalty for the crowded tanks is the practical cost of the 0.09 % per day of growth they lose, and it is a bigger effect than it looks in the growth table.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/largemouth-bass-aquaponic-stocking-density-biomass-fcr.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `volume`, `biomass`, `maxFish`, `sgr`, `fcr`, `daysTo` and `project` from `src/lib/sci/aquastock.ts`, the maths behind the [[stocking-density-calculator]].

From the paper: the 500 L tank, the 109 g start weight, the 176 days, the three nominal densities and tank counts, the three daily weight gains, the two feeding rates and the 73.9 % survival. **Our assumptions**, labelled as such above: losses spread evenly at 5.02 % a month, and deaths counted at the mid-trial mean weight. Nothing is simulated from a fitted model, and no table or figure of the paper is reproduced.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/stocking-density-calculator.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the arithmetic against an independent Python version (NumPy, with the cumulative feed from SciPy's `quad`):

- A round tank 4 m across and 1.2 m deep is 15.0796 m³, and 1500 fish of 120 g in it are 180 kg, or 11.9366 kg m⁻³.
- SGR from 40 g to 120 g in 60 days is 1.83102 % per day, and from 120 g the fish reaches 400 g after 65.754 more days.
- A 70-day projection of 1500 fish at 2 % feeding and 1 % lost per month ends at 432.337 g, 1465.23 fish, 633.475 kg and 42.0086 kg m⁻³, with 504.556 kg of feed eaten.
- New with this article: the paper's three treatments must give SGR 0.425, 0.390 and 0.336 % per day; 28 fish of 109 g in 500 L must be 6.10 kg m⁻³, the most that 6.2 kg m⁻³ allows; and D6's projection must reach 197.0 g and 8.15 kg m⁻³ on day 176 with 3.77 kg of feed. The rounded numbers a reader types in the step below must give FCR **1.71** with the dead fish counted and **3.47** without, and 109 g must reach 400 g after 306, 334 and 387 days.

## Check it yourself

On the [[stocking-density-calculator]] page, rebuilding the crowded D6 treatment:

1. In *Tank or pond, and the fish in it*, set *Shape* to **I know the volume** and *Water volume (m³)* to **0.5**.
2. Type **28** in *Number of fish*, **109** in *Mean weight (g)* and **6.2** in *Target density (kg/m³)*. It reads **3.05 kg** of fish at **6.10 kg/m³**, and says 28 is the most the target allows.
3. In *Growth and feed over a period*, put **28** and **109** at the start, **21** and **197** at the end, **176** days, **3.77** kg in *Feed given* and **1.12** kg in *Weight of fish that died*. It shows **SGR 0.336 % per day** and **FCR 1.71**. Clear the dead-fish box and FCR jumps to **3.47**: that one field is the difference between a believable number and a hopeless one.
4. In *Feeding and harvest projection*, leave *SGR* blank (it uses the 0.336 above), set *Feed* to **0.605** % of body weight per day, *Fish lost* to **5.02** % per month and *Harvest weight* to **400** g, with *Show every* **30 days**.
5. The table runs 109 → 120.6 → 133.4 → 147.5 → 163.2 → 180.5 g, with density climbing 6.10 → 6.41 → 6.74 → 7.08 → 7.44 → 7.81 kg/m³ and cumulative feed reaching **3.77 kg** by day 176. It reaches the 400 g harvest weight on day **387**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report the density fish actually experienced**, not only the stocking density: the projection gives the end-of-trial figure in one step.
- **Check a published feed conversion ratio** before citing it, by asking whether it was computed with or without the fish that died — the two differ by a factor of two here.
- **Design a trial that stays inside its treatment**: with small tanks, work out whether the integer-fish step makes two neighbouring densities overlap, as D3 and D4 do.
- **Plan a harvest date** from a measured SGR, and see what a lower growth rate costs in days rather than in percent.
- **Teach the arithmetic** that farm records rest on: biomass, density, SGR, FCR and a stocking limit, all visible at once on one page.

## The fine print

- The paper gives the mean start weight (109 g) and daily gain, not per-tank final weights, so our final weights are the means implied by its own gain values.
- Mortality is the weak point: a single 73.9 % survival figure cannot say when the fish were lost, and FCR depends on that. Our even spread is a neutral choice, not the paper's.
- Feed intake as a percentage of biomass is read here as applying to the whole trial at a constant rate; a satiation-fed tank varies day to day with temperature, which fell from summer to November.
- The 400 g harvest date extends the growth rate far beyond the trial, where it would in reality fall as the fish grow. Treat those days as a comparison between treatments, not a forecast.
- We checked the fish side only. The lettuce, basil and chard yields and the flesh-quality measures are outside what this tool does.
