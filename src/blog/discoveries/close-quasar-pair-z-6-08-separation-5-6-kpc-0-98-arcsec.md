JWST and large ground-based surveys keep finding black holes of a billion solar masses less than a billion years after the Big Bang, which is one of the hottest puzzles in astronomy this month. A 2026 paper adds a pair of them: two quasars, named Nāpōwawā‘enakaulua, at **redshift about 6.08**, only **0.98 arcseconds** apart on the sky. The authors call it the most compact quasar pair candidate known from the epoch when the first galaxies were clearing the fog of neutral hydrogen. An angle and a redshift are all the [[cosmology-calculator]] needs to turn that into kiloparsecs and years, and the [[redshift-calculator]] turns the two quasars' redshifts into how fast they move relative to each other. To go from a measured brightness to a luminosity at that distance, use the [[magnitude-flux-converter]].

## The paper in brief

The pair turned up in the Aether survey, a search for close companions around known quasars at the highest redshifts. The two quasars, A and B, have systemic redshifts of about **6.0781 and 6.0785**, so they sit at the same distance to within a few parts in ten thousand. On the sky they are **0.98 arcseconds** apart, which the paper gives as about **5.56 physical kiloparsecs** (a kiloparsec is about 3,260 light years), with a line-of-sight velocity offset of only about **15 km/s**.

Each holds a supermassive black hole: about **1.5 billion** and **400 million** solar masses. Both shine at or a little above the Eddington limit, with Eddington ratios of about **1.23 and 1.07**, the point where the push of their own light on infalling gas balances gravity. Two such black holes this close, this early, suggests a merger of their host galaxies under way less than a billion years after the Big Bang, and the paper uses the pair to ask how often such encounters happen at cosmic dawn.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated angle and redshifts**. The paper's abstract does not say which cosmology it used, so we ran the calculator's three presets and let the numbers decide.

**Step 1: from arcseconds to kiloparsecs.** At z = 6.08, the calculator gives an angular diameter distance of **1,169.1 Mpc** with round-number cosmology (H0 = 70 km/s/Mpc, Ωm = 0.3, flat), so one arcsecond spans **5.668 kpc**. The pair's 0.98 arcsec is then **5.555 kpc**, which rounds to the paper's **5.56**. With Planck 2018 values the same angle is **5.676 kpc**, and with WMAP 9-year values **5.697 kpc**, both of which would have been written as 5.68 or 5.70. So the paper's number matches the round-number cosmology, the one most quasar papers use, to the last digit it prints. Leaving out radiation, which the calculator includes by default, moves the answer only to 5.557 kpc.

**Step 2: in light years.** 5.555 kpc is about **18,100 light years**, the figure the news coverage used. That is roughly a fifth of the width of the Milky Way's disc: two quasars inside what would be a single galaxy today.

**Step 3: how early.** With the same cosmology the universe was **899 million years** old at z = 6.08, against **13.46 billion years** now, so the light left when the universe was about **6.7 %**, a fifteenth, of its present age. The light has been travelling for **12.56 billion years**. Planck 2018 values give 913 million years, so the "900 million years after the Big Bang" in the coverage holds in either cosmology.

**Step 4: the velocity offset.** Seen from quasar A, quasar B's light is redshifted by (1 + 6.0785)/(1 + 6.0781) − 1 = **5.65 × 10⁻⁵**, and the [[redshift-calculator]] turns that into **16.9 km/s**. The paper says about 15 km/s. The difference is rounding: the redshifts are printed to four decimals, and moving each by half a unit in the last place gives anything from **12.7 to 21.2 km/s**. The tool also shows why the paper needed precise systemic redshifts at all: at z ≈ 6, a velocity of 15 km/s is a redshift difference of only 0.0004.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/close-quasar-pair-z-6-08-separation-5-6-kpc-0-98-arcsec.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `cosmology()`, `omegaR()`, `presets` and `sizeFromArcsec()` from `src/lib/sci/cosmo.ts`, the maths behind the [[cosmology-calculator]], for the angular diameter distance, the scale in kpc per arcsec, the age and the lookback time, and `velocity()` from `src/lib/sci/redshift.ts`, the maths behind the [[redshift-calculator]], for the velocity offset. The distances come from integrals over the expansion history, done by Simpson's rule in the same code the web page runs.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks the cosmology maths against exact solutions: the Einstein-de Sitter universe's comoving distance and age, the empty universe's luminosity distance, the closed-form age of a flat universe with dark energy, and the Planck 2018 age of 13.787 billion years. `tests/sci-tools/redshift-calculator.mjs` pins the pair of tools to published numbers, and gained two checks with this article:

- 0.98 arcsec at z = 6.08 with H0 = 70, Ωm = 0.3 must give 5.555 kpc and an age of 899 million years.
- Redshifts of 6.0781 and 6.0785 must differ by 16.94 km/s.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Find out which cosmology a paper used** when it does not say: run the presets on its angle and redshift, and see which matches the printed kiloparsecs, as here.
- **Convert separations for a pair or a lens** between arcseconds and kiloparsecs at any redshift, in any of three standard cosmologies or your own.
- **Put a high-redshift object on a timeline**: age then, lookback time and the CMB temperature at the time, for talks and outreach as well as papers.
- **Check whether a velocity offset is real or rounding**: the [[redshift-calculator]] makes the size of one unit in the last printed decimal obvious.
- **Go from flux to luminosity** for objects like these with the luminosity distance here and the [[magnitude-flux-converter]].

## The fine print

- We could not read the journal's article page from here. Every value from the paper comes from the abstract in its Crossref record, and the cosmology the authors used is our inference from the match, not something we read in the paper.
- The separation is projected on the sky. The real distance between the black holes could be larger; the small velocity offset says nothing about distance along the line of sight.
- The black hole masses and Eddington ratios are the paper's; fibub has no tool that recomputes them from spectra, so we have not checked them.
- The light-year conversion uses 1 kpc = 3,261.56 light years. News figures may round differently.
