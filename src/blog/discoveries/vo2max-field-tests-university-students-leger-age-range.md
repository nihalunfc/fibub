Two cheap field tests, one group of students, and answers 9 mL/kg/min apart. A 2026 paper in *Physiologia* put 108 active university students through either a **Rockport one-mile walk** or a **20 m shuttle run** (the beep test) and estimated VO2max, the body's top rate of using oxygen, from each. The walk gave 48.85 ± 5.34 mL/kg/min; the shuttle gave 39.80 ± 14.58, with a far worse internal fit. That looks like a verdict on the beep test. Put the same arithmetic through the [[vo2max-estimator]] and most of the gap turns out to come from which of Léger's two equations the shuttle results were read with. For what to do with a VO2max number once you trust it, see [[heart-rate-zones]] and [[training-load]].

## The paper in brief

The author tested two samples of sport-science students at a Spanish university: 48 (12 women, 36 men, age 21.46 ± 4.39 years) did the Rockport walk, and 60 (7 women, 53 men, age 20.63 ± 2.31 years) did the 20 m shuttle run. No laboratory gas analysis was done, so there is no measured VO2max anywhere in the study: every number is an estimate from a published equation, and the "performance" figures (R², RMSE, and their shrunken versions after 200 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation) describe how well each equation's own inputs explain its own output in this sample.

The paper reports an apparent R² of 0.552 and RMSE 3.54 mL/kg/min for the walk model, against R² 0.319 and RMSE 11.93 for the shuttle model, and concludes that the walk test is the more stable choice for this population. Its printed shuttle equation is the **children's** form of Léger's equation, with an age term, and with the coefficient 0.1538 where Léger has 0.1536.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** of published equations at the paper's own stated means. We have none of its individual data.

**Step 1: which stage does the reported mean imply?** Léger's children's equation is VO2max = 31.025 + 3.238 × speed − 3.248 × age + 0.1536 × speed × age, with speed in km/h, and the shuttle run's speed is 8 + 0.5 × stage, so stage 1 is 8.5 km/h. Solving it backwards for the paper's mean estimate of 39.80 at its mean age of 20.63 gives **11.83 km/h**, which is stage 7.7: a believable average run for active students.

**Step 2: the same run through the adult equation.** Léger also published an equation for 18 and over, with no age term: VO2max = 6.0 × speed − 24.4. At 11.83 km/h it gives **46.57 mL/kg/min**, which is **6.77 higher** than the children's equation at age 20.63. The paper's headline gap between the two tests is 9.05 mL/kg/min; read with the adult equation, the gap between its own two samples falls to **2.28**. Figure 1 shows the two equations side by side, with both reported means on it.

**Step 3: why the children's equation misbehaves on adults.** Its age term is not a small correction. The slope with age at 11.83 km/h is −3.248 + 0.1536 × 11.83 = **−1.43 mL/kg/min per year**, and it only reaches zero at 21.1 km/h, a speed no one runs in this test. So every extra year of age subtracts fitness, forever. Two consequences follow. First, a step at the boundary: at age 18 and 11.83 km/h the children's equation gives 43.56 while the adult one gives 46.57, a **3.01 mL/kg/min** jump exactly where you switch. Second, extra spread: an age SD of 2.31 years alone adds about **3.3 mL/kg/min** of variation to the estimates, which the adult equation would not have at all. Figure 2 shows the age term running on past the range Léger fitted it over.

**Step 4: the big standard deviation.** A shuttle SD of 14.58 mL/kg/min needs a spread in running speed of 14.58 ÷ 6.41 = **2.28 km/h**, about four and a half stages, since the children's equation changes by 6.41 mL/kg/min per km/h at age 20.63. That is a wide but real spread for a student group. It is not, by itself, evidence that the test is noisy: the slope of a prediction equation turns the spread of the thing measured into the spread of the estimate, and the shuttle run's slope is steep.

**Step 5: the walk test is extrapolated too.** The Rockport equation was fitted to adults aged **30 to 69**, and these students are 21. Our tool says so on the page. A 66.8 kg man of 21.46 finishing a 14.94-minute mile walk at 140 beats/min gets 48.85 mL/kg/min from it, which is one way to reach the paper's walk mean (**our assumption**: the paper reports neither mass, time nor heart rate). Neither test, then, is being used inside the range it was built for. That is the honest reading of this study: not "the walk test wins", but "both equations are outside their fitted range, and only one of them hides that in an age coefficient".

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/vo2max-field-tests-university-students-leger-age-range.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `legerChild`, `legerAdult`, `shuttleSpeed`, `rockport` and `mets` from `src/lib/sci/vo2max.ts`, the maths behind the [[vo2max-estimator]], and solves the children's equation backwards for a speed by bisection.

The two sample ages, the two reported VO2max means and SDs, the BMI and the printed equations come from the paper. The walk-test mass of 66.8 kg (a 1.75 m man at the sample's BMI), the finishing heart rate of 140 and the 14.94-minute walk time are **our assumptions**, chosen only to land on the paper's reported walk mean. No individual data from the study is used or simulated.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/vo2max-estimator.mjs`, which pins each formula against values worked out by hand from the original papers:

- Cooper's 12-minute run at 2400 m must give 42.37 mL/kg/min, and the 1.5-mile run at 11:40 must give 44.90.
- The Rockport walk for a 75 kg, 40-year-old man walking 14:30 and finishing at 140 beats/min must give 41.69 mL/kg/min, with the mass converted to pounds inside the equation.
- Stage 9 of the shuttle run is 12.5 km/h, and the adult equation must give 50.6 mL/kg/min there.
- New with this article: at 11.828 km/h the children's equation at age 20.63 must give 39.80 while the adult equation gives 46.57, the children's equation at age 18 must give 43.56, and its slope with age must be −1.431 mL/kg/min per year.

## Check it yourself

On the [[vo2max-estimator]] page:

1. In *About you*, set *Age (years)* to **17**, *Sex* to **Male** and leave *Body mass (kg)* at 75.
2. Choose the **20 m shuttle run** test and set *Last stage (level) completed* to **8** (12 km/h). The answer is **46.0 mL/kg/min**, and a note says the children's equation was used.
3. Now change *Age (years)* to **18** and nothing else. The estimate becomes **47.6**: the tool has switched to Léger's adult equation, which has no age term.
4. Put *Age (years)* to **21**. The answer stays **47.6**, because the adult equation ignores age. Reading the same stage 8 run with the children's equation at 21, as the paper's printed formula does, would give **40.4** instead, 7.2 lower.
5. Switch the test to the **Rockport 1-mile walk**, with *Time for 1 mile (min:s)* **14:30** and *Heart rate at the finish* **140**. Note the warning: the walk equation was fitted to adults aged 30 to 69.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check which equation a paper used** before comparing its VO2max values with yours: the children's and adult Léger forms differ by several mL/kg/min at the same run.
- **Report the test, the equation and the age range** in your methods, and say when your sample sits outside the fitted range. The [[vo2max-estimator]] prints that warning for you.
- **Convert a field test to training prescriptions** with [[heart-rate-zones]], and keep weekly loads in [[training-load]], so one estimate is not asked to do everything.
- **Teach measurement error properly**: show students that a prediction equation's slope turns the spread of the measured thing into the spread of the estimate, as in step 4.
- **Compare six tests on one person** on the tool's side-by-side table before choosing a protocol for a study: the spread between tests is usually larger than the difference you are hoping to detect.

## The fine print

- Nothing here is a reanalysis of the study's data, which is not public. Every number is the published equations evaluated at the paper's own stated means.
- The walk-test inputs we used are our own, picked to reproduce the paper's reported mean; other combinations give the same mean.
- The paper measured no laboratory VO2max, so neither it nor we can say which equation is closer to the truth for this group. The point is that the two Léger forms disagree, and that both equations are used outside their fitted ages.
- Field-test VO2max is at best within about ±10 % of a laboratory value.
- General education, not training or medical advice.
