Randomised trials describe how people were allocated so that readers can judge fairness. A 2026 trial of an app for weight gain in pregnancy gives a clear description: stratified, permuted blocks of size 3, ratio 1:1, run by an outside vendor. It also reports how many women ended up in each arm: 216 and 237. Those two statements raise two questions that a randomisation generator can answer in a minute. Can a block of 3 hold a 1:1 ratio at all? And how big a gap between the arms would that design leave in 453 people? This article asks them with the [[randomisation-list]] tool. For planning how many people a trial needs see [[sample-size]], and for judging the size of a difference, [[effect-size-converter]]. We do not claim anything went wrong in the trial; we show what the stated design implies.

## The paper in brief

The GROWell trial tested a smartphone and text-message programme aimed at diet quality in pregnant women in California with a BMI of 25 to 42. Of **567 women who consented**, **114 withdrew before randomisation**, leaving **453 randomised: 216 to the programme and 237 to an educational attention control**. The outcomes were excess gestational weight gain (35 % against 36 %) and weight retained 6 months after birth (23 % against 26 %), and the programme made no clear difference to either. Follow-up was incomplete: 268 women (117 and 151) were reached at 6 months.

The randomisation is described as computer-generated and **stratified** by three variables (BMI category, race and ethnicity, and how the woman was recruited), using **permuted blocks of size 3** at an **allocation ratio of 1:1**. A permuted block is a short run of allocations in which the right number of each arm appears in a shuffled order, so the arms stay close to balanced all the way through. The number of strata and the numbers in each are not reported.

## Checked with the tools

All numbers below are our simulation of the design as stated, with assumptions marked. The paper's data are not used beyond the group sizes and the follow-up counts.

**Step 1: a block of 3 cannot be 1:1.** A permuted block must contain every arm in the exact ratio. With two arms at 1:1, each block holds equal numbers of each, so its size must be a multiple of 2. The tool refuses it: *"Block size 3 does not work: with a 1:1 ratio every block size must be a multiple of 2"*. Blocks of 3 do fit a ratio such as 1:2, where each block has one of one arm and two of the other (12 people in blocks of 3 gives 4 and 8). So the description as printed can't be implemented literally; perhaps the block size, or the ratio, or the number of arms is stated wrongly, or the blocks were of varying size. We cannot tell which.

**Step 2: how big a gap does a proper design leave?** We assigned 453 people at random to a number of strata (our assumption: equally likely), gave each stratum its own list from the tool's generator, and recorded the final difference between the arms. We repeated this 300 times for each design and number of strata.

A stratified blocked design keeps the arms **within a few people** of each other even with 60 strata, because each stratum's last, part-filled block can be off by at most half its size. The gap of **21** seen in the trial is far outside that: in 300 simulated trials per point, the share reaching a gap of 21 was **0 %** at every number of strata for blocks of 2 and 4, and for blocks of 6 only **0.3 %** at 30 strata and **0.7 %** at 60.

**Step 3: what does give a gap of 21?** Simple randomisation, the digital coin toss. For 453 people the standard deviation of the final difference is the square root of 453, or **21.3**, so a gap of 21 is almost exactly one standard deviation, and our simulations show a gap of 21 or more in **about one trial in three** (33 % to 39 % at every number of strata). The reported split is thus entirely ordinary for simple randomisation and unusual for the stated blocked design.

There are innocent explanations the paper's text cannot rule out, and we list them in the fine print. The point is not the cause: it is that the group sizes are a quick check on a described design, and the tool makes the check a one-minute job.

**Step 4: the follow-up gap, with our arithmetic.** At 6 months, 117 of 216 (54.2 %) and 151 of 237 (63.7 %) were reached. A plain two-proportion z test (our arithmetic, not the paper's) gives z of about **2.06** and p of about **0.039**. Even if the arms started balanced, the programme arm lost more women, which is a separate issue from randomisation.

| Check | Paper says | Tool or our arithmetic gives |
|---|---|---|
| Block of 3 with 1:1 | stated | refused: size must be a multiple of 2 |
| Final gap, stratified blocks of 4, 30 strata | 21 observed | about 4 on average, never reached in 300 trials |
| Final gap, simple randomisation | 21 observed | 21 or more in about 1 trial of 3 |
| Retention 117 of 216 against 151 of 237 | not tested in the text we used | z about 2.06, p about 0.039 |

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/growell-trial-stratified-blocks-of-3-allocation-imbalance-237-vs-216.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `stratumList`, `makeList` and `rngFromText` from `src/lib/sci/randomise.ts`, the maths behind the [[randomisation-list]] tool, and `normCdf` from `src/lib/sci/distributions.ts` for the retention arithmetic. From the paper: 453 people, the split 216 and 237, the 1:1 ratio, blocks of 3 and the follow-up numbers. **Ours:** the number of strata (1 to 60), the assumption that people fall into strata equally often, the comparison blocks of 2, 4 and 6, and 300 trials per point.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/randomisation-list.mjs`, which pins the generator against the reference code of xoshiro128** and a Python reimplementation of whole lists, and checks block balance, ratios and uniformity. New with this article, it also checks that a block of 3 with a 1:1 ratio is refused, that blocks of 3 give 4 and 8 of 12 at 1:2, and that 30 strata of 15 people in blocks of 4 (seed "growell-demo") give 224 against 226 with no stratum off by more than one.

## Check it yourself

On the [[randomisation-list]] page:

1. Set *Arms (groups)* to **Intervention, Control**, *Allocation ratio* to **1:1**, *Method* to **Permuted blocks, one size** and *Block sizes* to **3**. The tool shows the error that a block of 3 does not work with 1:1.
2. Change *Block sizes* to **4**, *Participants per stratum* to **15**, and in the strata box type three lines: **BMI: a, b, c** then **Race: a, b, c, d, e** then **Source: clinic, social**. Set *Seed* to **growell-demo**. The table shows 30 strata, each with 8 and 7 (or 7 and 8) people; adding the Intervention column gives **224** and the Control column **226**.
3. Switch *Method* to **Simple (like a coin toss)** and read the same table: single strata now drift by several people, which is what builds a final gap like the trial's.
4. Set *Allocation ratio* to **1:2**, *Block sizes* to **3**, *Participants per stratum* to **12** and empty the strata box: the counts are **4** and **8**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a protocol's own wording.** Block sizes must be multiples of the ratio total; the tool refuses ones that are not, so a sentence in a methods section can be tested directly.
- **Predict the group-size gap** before recruiting, given the number of strata you plan, and decide whether a coarser stratification would keep the arms closer.
- **Check a published split** against the design: simulate the stated design many times and see how often a gap that large arises.
- **Make the list reproducible.** The tool's seed and documented generator let anyone regenerate a list, which helps reviewers and auditors.
- **Teach allocation concealment and balance** with a hands-on comparison of simple and blocked lists.

## The fine print

- Our numbers assume 453 people fall into strata at random with equal chances. Real strata are uneven, with many nearly empty ones, which leaves more part-filled blocks and a larger gap than we show; the paper does not report the strata sizes.
- Innocent explanations for a gap of 21 include a typing slip in the block size or ratio, allocation that varied block sizes or used several lists, allocation continuing after some women were later found ineligible, or the reported arms being counts at a later stage than randomisation. The paper's text does not say.
- The follow-up z test is a plain approximation and ignores that outcomes may be missing for different reasons.
- This article is about trial design and arithmetic. It makes no claim about the app, pregnancy weight, or any person, and is not medical advice.
