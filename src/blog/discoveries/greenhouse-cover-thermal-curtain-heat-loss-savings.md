Greenhouse energy papers usually report savings as percentages: a better side wall saves 6 %, a thermal curtain saves 29 %. A percentage is only meaningful with the greenhouse it belongs to, because the share of the heat that leaves through the side walls depends entirely on how wide the house is. A 2025 study in *Energies* compares three cover configurations and several heat sources for tomato production in four Turkish provinces, and reports its savings and its annual heat per square metre, but not the dimensions, the U values or the degree-days behind them. This article puts those headline numbers back through the physics with the [[greenhouse-heating]] tool and recovers the greenhouse shape and the climate they imply. The carbon side of the same comparison fits the [[carbon-footprint]] tool, and the water side the [[et0-calculator]] and [[irrigation-scheduler]].

## The paper in brief

The authors model heating for a tomato crop in Antalya and Mersin on the Mediterranean coast and in Kırşehir and Kütahya inland, for three configurations: **S-1** polyethylene film on the roof and the side walls, **S-2** the same roof with polycarbonate side walls, and **S-3** S-2 plus a thermal curtain. They then cost the heat from coal, fuel oil, natural gas, geothermal water, biogas and biomass.

Their findings: the annual heat requirement runs from **356.5 kWh m⁻²** (S-1 in Kütahya) down to **46.3 kWh m⁻²** (S-3 in Mersin); S-2 saves about **6 %** against S-1 and S-3 about **29 %**; coal use reaches 69.4 kg m⁻² a year, and the carbon dioxide from coal in the worst case is 253.1 kg m⁻² a year against 1.1 for geothermal. Inland, heating runs for six months of the year, on the coast for about three months of nights.

## Checked with the tools

The tool works from first principles: heat loss is the cover's U times its area plus the air that leaks in, both times the temperature difference, and the seasonal heat is that conductance times the degree-hours. Because the paper gives no geometry, we fixed **our own reference house**: 60 m long, a 3 m eave, a roof whose ridge is the eave plus an eighth of the width (a 14° pitch), one air change an hour, and a design outside temperature of −10 °C with 15 °C inside. Everything below then asks what the paper's numbers mean for a house like that.

**Step 1: a 6 % saving tells you the greenhouse is wide.** Changing only the side walls from film (U 6.5 W m⁻² K⁻¹) to twin-wall polycarbonate (3.5) saves a share of the heat equal to the side walls' share of the cover. In a narrow 6 m tunnel the side walls are 47 % of the cover and the saving is **19.7 %**. In a 9.6 m house it is **14.5 %**, and it keeps falling as the roof takes over. The paper's 6 % is reached at a width of about **27.5 m**, where the side walls are only 15.5 % of the cover (Figure 1). So the figure is not wrong, it is a statement about a wide block of greenhouse: a grower with a 9 m span should expect two or three times the saving the paper reports, and a grower with a 50 m block only half of it.

**Step 2: what the 29 % curtain saving asks of the curtain.** For that 27.5 m house the tool gives a design loss of **450 kW**, 84 % of it through the cover and 16 % through air leaks, or **273 W per m² of floor**. A 29 % cut in the whole loss, with the air leakage unchanged, needs the effective cover U to fall from 6.5 to **4.25 W m⁻² K⁻¹**. That is between a double inflated film (4.0) and a twin-wall sheet (3.5) in fibub's reference list: a demanding but ordinary figure for a well-closed screen. The paper's 29 % is therefore physically consistent, and the tool turns it into the number a grower can actually shop for.

| Saving from the thermal curtain | 29 % | needs the effective cover U to fall from 6.5 to 4.25 W m⁻² K⁻¹ |

**Step 3: the degree-days hidden in the energy figures.** Reading the tool backwards, an annual **356.5 kWh m⁻²** for this house with a film cover needs about **1,362 heating degree-days**. Spread over the paper's six-month inland heating season that is an average deficit of 7.6 K below the 15 °C night set point, which is what a continental Anatolian winter looks like. The coastal **46.3 kWh m⁻²** with the curtain needs about **249 degree-days**, 2.8 K over three months of nights: again plausible. The two extremes of the paper differ by a factor of 7.7 in energy, and the tool shows that about 5.5 of that is climate and the rest is the cover (Figure 2).

**Step 4: the fuel figures hold together.** The paper's worst case burns 69.4 kg of coal per m² for 356.5 kWh m⁻² of heat. That is 18.5 MJ of *delivered* heat per kilogram of coal, so if the coal is a 25 MJ/kg lignite the boiler runs at about **74 %**: a sensible number for a greenhouse coal stove, and a useful sanity check on any paper that reports both energy and fuel. Had it come out above 100 % the table would have been wrong.

## How we made the graphs

Both figures and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/greenhouse-cover-thermal-curtain-heat-loss-savings.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `geometry`, `heating` and `season` from `src/lib/sci/greenhouse.ts`, the maths behind the [[greenhouse-heating]] tool, and the cover U values in that file's `COVERS` list.

From the paper: the three configurations, the 6 % and 29 % savings, the 356.5 and 46.3 kWh m⁻² intensities, the 69.4 kg m⁻² of coal, the 15 °C night set point, the heating season lengths and the 70.8 kWh per W m⁻² K⁻¹ it quotes from Kim and colleagues. **Our assumptions**: the whole geometry (60 m long, 3 m eave, 14° roof), one air change an hour, a −10 °C design temperature, the U values for film and polycarbonate, and the 25 MJ/kg coal. The widths in Figure 1 are ours; the paper reports no dimensions, so none of the shapes here is the paper's greenhouse. Because the saving is a ratio, it depends on the shape and the U values but barely on the design temperature.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/greenhouse-heating.mjs`, which pins this maths against hand-worked textbook sums:

- The gable geometry of a 30 m by 9.6 m house with a 3 m eave and a 4.8 m ridge must give a 288 m² floor, a 562.46 m² cover and a 1,123.2 m³ volume.
- Heat loss must equal U·A·ΔT plus ρ·c_p·V·N/3600·ΔT, with dry-air density from the ideal gas law at the outside temperature and the altitude pressure.
- The seasonal sum must equal the conductance times the degree-hours, and the fuel must follow from the energy content and the burner efficiency.
- New with this article: the 60 m by 27.5 m reference house must give a 1,650 m² floor and a 2,320.5 m² cover; at U 6.5, 15 °C inside and −10 °C outside its loss must be 450.1 kW (273 W per m² of floor, 84 % through the cover); 1,362 degree-days must come to 588,457 kWh, which is 356.6 kWh per m² of floor; and dropping the effective U to 4.25 must cut the loss by 29.0 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[greenhouse-heating]] tool page:

1. In *Greenhouse size*, set *Length (m)* **60**, *Width (m)* **27.5**, *Eave (wall) height (m)* **3** and *Ridge height (m)* **6.44**. The facts should read *Floor area* **1,650 m²**, *Cover area* **2,320.5 m²** and *Roof slope* **14°**.
2. In *Heat loss*, set *Cover U value (W/m² K)* **6.5**, *Inside set point (°C)* **15**, *Coldest outside (°C)* **−10**, *Air changes per hour* **1** and *Safety margin (%)* **0**. It should show *Heat loss* **450.1 kW**, with **84 %** through the cover and **273 W/m²** of floor.
3. Set *Heating degree-days (°C·day)* to **1362**, choose *Fuel* **Electricity** (3.6 MJ per kWh at 100 %, so the fuel figure is the heat itself) and read *Heat needed* **588,457 kWh**, which is **356.6 kWh per m² of floor**: the paper's worst case.
4. Change *Cover U value* to **4.25**, the value the curtain has to reach, and the loss falls to **194 W/m²**, 29 % less.
5. Now change *Width (m)* to **9.6** and *Ridge height (m)* to **4.2**, keeping U 6.5, and watch *Per m² of floor* rise: a narrow house loses more per square metre of crop, which is why the same curtain saves a different percentage in a different shape.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a published percentage into your own greenhouse**: enter your dimensions and U values and see what the same cover change saves for you.
- **Specify a screen or a second layer**: work backwards from the saving you need to the effective U you must buy.
- **Sanity-check an energy table**: divide the reported heat by the reported fuel and see whether the implied boiler efficiency is possible.
- **Recover the climate behind a published intensity**, as the degree-day inversion above does, so figures from different provinces can be compared.
- **Size the fans too**: the same tool's ventilation panel gives the summer air flow from the solar load, which decides the other half of a greenhouse's energy bill.

## The fine print

- The paper publishes no dimensions, U values or degree-days, so every shape here is **ours**. The widths that match its percentages are inferences about its model greenhouse, not measurements of it.
- The tool takes one U value for the whole cover, so the mixed configuration is handled as an area-weighted average. That is exact for steady-state conduction and ignores how wind and screens differ between roof and wall.
- A thermal curtain is drawn only at night. Treating it as a lower cover U is right for a night-heated season and would overstate the saving if the house were heated through the day.
- Degree-days are to the 15 °C night set point; a day set point of 21 °C would raise every energy figure, which is one reason our inferred degree-days are a lower bound.
- The tool sizes for heat loss, not for crop growth, humidity or carbon dioxide enrichment.
