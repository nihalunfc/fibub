Two-phase olive pomace is the wet sludge left after olive oil is pressed, and mills produce mountains of it. Drying it is the first step to using it, and a 2025 paper in *Foods* dried thin layers of pomace under near-infrared lamps between 70 and 140 °C, fitted the drying curves, and reported an **effective moisture diffusivity of 1.417 to 5.807 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹** with an **activation energy of 23.732 kJ/mol**. Those three numbers are enough to rebuild the whole temperature dependence, because an activation energy is just the slope of a straight line. The [[diffusion-calculator]] does the rebuilding, and then says what the diffusivity means for a real layer of pomace. For the heat side of drying see the [[heat-conduction]] calculator, and for what diffusion looks like particle by particle see the [[random-walk-diffusion]] tool.

## The paper in brief

The authors dried two-phase olive pomace in thin layers under near-infrared radiation, in bursts rather than continuously (**intermittent** drying), at 70, and up to 140 °C. They fitted the usual thin-layer drying models and found the **Midilli** model best (r² of at least 0.99839, RMSE at most 0.01349). From the drying curves they extracted an **effective moisture diffusivity**, the single number that stands in for all the ways water leaves a porous solid, and from its temperature dependence an activation energy of **23.732 kJ/mol**. Drying at 140 °C took **68 % less time** than at 70 °C, and near-infrared drying was **3.2 times faster** than an oven at the same temperature. They then checked what the heat did to the pomace's phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity.

An activation energy near 24 kJ/mol is low for a solid-state process but normal for moisture in a wet food: water is not breaking chemical bonds on its way out, it is finding its way through pores.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation from the values in the paper's abstract**, not new drying experiments. One pairing is **our assumption**: that the smallest diffusivity (1.417 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹) belongs to the lowest temperature, 70 °C, and the largest (5.807 × 10⁻⁹) to 140 °C. Diffusivity always rises with temperature, so the pairing is safe even though the abstract gives the range without saying so.

**Step 1: two points give the activation energy.** The Arrhenius law says D = D₀·exp(−Q/RT). With two (temperature, diffusivity) pairs, Q = R·ln(D₂/D₁) ÷ (1/T₁ − 1/T₂). The tool's maths gives **Q = 23.75 kJ/mol** (0.246 eV per molecule) and a pre-factor **D₀ = 5.847 × 10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹**. The paper's own fit, which used every temperature it measured, gives **23.732 kJ/mol**: a difference of **0.09 %**. The two ends of a range carry nearly all the information in an Arrhenius fit, and this one is internally consistent, which is a real check: a mis-typed exponent or a confusion of °C and K in such a table usually shows up as a factor of several.

**Step 2: the line fills in the gaps.** With that D₀ and Q, the tool gives the diffusivity at any temperature in between: **2.008 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹ at 85 °C**, **2.767 × 10⁻⁹ at 100 °C** and **4.085 × 10⁻⁹ at 120 °C**. Near 140 °C the diffusivity doubles for every **41 K** of extra temperature, which is a useful rule of thumb for anyone planning a drier: a 10 °C rise buys about 18 %.

**Step 3: what the diffusivity means for a layer.** The reported diffusivity only becomes intuitive when it is turned into a time. Treating drying as Fick's second law with the surface held at the dry end (our own normalised scale: moisture 1 inside, 0 at the surface), the tool says the **half-dried front reaches 1.52 mm in 30 minutes at 70 °C and 3.08 mm at 140 °C**, and that the moisture 3 mm down halves after **116 minutes at 70 °C** but **28 minutes at 140 °C**.

**Step 4: a cross-check on the paper's own time saving.** If drying were purely diffusion-limited, time would scale as 1/D, so going from 70 to 140 °C should cut it by **75.6 %** (the diffusivity ratio is 4.10). The paper measured **68 %**. The two agree in size but not exactly, and the gap is the interesting part: in a real drier, surface evaporation, shrinkage and the intermittency of the lamps all take a share, so the time saving is a little smaller than diffusion alone predicts. That is also a warning about reading too much into an "effective" diffusivity: it is a fitted number for one geometry, not a material constant.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number here come from `scripts/blog-figures/olive-pomace-nir-drying-effective-diffusivity-arrhenius.mjs` in fibub's public code. The finite-difference run agrees with the erfc profile at 3 mm and 140 °C to within 0.005 of the normalised moisture (0.4883 against 0.4927 on 200 cells).

From the paper: the two diffusivities, the two temperatures, the stated activation energy and the 68 % time saving. **Our own choices**: pairing each diffusivity with the end of the temperature range, the normalised moisture scale (1 inside, 0 at the surface), the 3 mm depth and the 30-minute snapshot.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/diffusion-calculator.mjs`, which pins this maths against SciPy and a NumPy finite-difference solver:

- The error function and its inverse must match `scipy.special.erf` and `erfinv` to about 10⁻¹⁵, including far out in the tails where a naive series loses all its digits.
- A carburising case (D 1 × 10⁻¹¹ m² s⁻¹, surface 1.0 %, core 0.2 % carbon) must put the 0.4 % contour at the depth SciPy's `brentq` finds, and the finite-difference solution must agree with the erfc profile across the grid.
- New with this article: the Arrhenius law with D₀ = 5.846772 × 10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹ and Q = 23.7524 kJ/mol must return 1.417 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹ at 70 °C and 5.807 × 10⁻⁹ at 140 °C, the paper's two values, and the half-moisture depth after 30 minutes at 140 °C must be 3.084 mm.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published diffusivity table** in a minute: fit the Arrhenius line to its ends and see whether the stated activation energy comes back. A unit slip in D or a Celsius-for-kelvin error shows up at once.
- **Fill in temperatures nobody measured**, for a drier or furnace setting between the ones in the paper, instead of interpolating a curve by eye.
- **Turn a diffusivity into a schedule**: the time for a given depth to reach a target moisture, and the fact that twice the depth needs four times the time.
- **Design the experiment**: pick layer thicknesses and sampling times so that the fitted diffusivity is well determined, and check whether your layer is thin enough for a one-dimensional model.
- **Compare materials across fields** on the same footing: the same page handles moisture in food, carbon in steel and dopants in silicon, because it is the same equation.

## The fine print

- An **effective** diffusivity is a fitted parameter for one sample geometry and one drier, not a property of olive pomace. Numbers from different papers are only loosely comparable.
- The paper's diffusivities come from its own thin-layer model; our Fickian depths and times use the semi-infinite erfc solution, so they describe the early part of drying, before the fronts from both surfaces meet.
- We paired the ends of the diffusivity range with the ends of the temperature range. If the paper's intermediate values are not evenly spread in 1/T, its own fit is the better one; ours is a check, not a replacement.
- The moisture scale in our figures is normalised to the starting moisture. We do not use the paper's moisture data, only the values quoted above.
