Soot starts as flat aromatic molecules that stick to each other, and how readily they stick depends on how easily their electrons move: the **HOMO-LUMO gap**, the energy step from the highest filled molecular orbital to the lowest empty one. A study in *Frontiers in Mechanical Engineering* computed that gap with density functional theory for **323 polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)** of 6 to 96 carbon atoms, and asked what in a molecule's structure sets it. This article asks a cheeky question: how much of that answer can you get from **Hückel theory**, a 1931 model that needs nothing but a list of which π atoms are bonded to which? We put it to the [[huckel-mo-calculator]] and find the paper's two main trends come straight out. Draw the molecules with the [[molecule-viewer]], and count their rings and double bonds with the [[unsaturation-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors collected 323 PAH structures, optimised each with B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) and a dispersion correction, and took the gap as the energy difference between the Kohn-Sham HOMO and LUMO. Their headline results:

- Gaps **shrink as the molecule grows**: across the whole set they run from 6.59 eV down to 0.64 eV.
- **Structure matters as much as size.** At a fixed carbon number a structural change can move the gap by about **2 eV**.
- Among the structural features tested, **five-membered rings** that make the molecule non-planar had the largest effect, and ketone (=O) substitution lowered gaps most among the side groups.
- A machine-learning model trained on the set predicts gaps to a mean absolute error of 0.19 eV.

The paper reports the gaps by group rather than molecule by molecule. For its **linear** group, 9 molecules with 10 to 26 carbon atoms, the gaps span **1.45 to 5.97 eV**. That range is the hook for what follows.

## Checked with the tools

Hückel theory keeps one π orbital per atom, sets every atom's energy to α and every bond's interaction to β, and solves the resulting matrix. Orbital energies come out as α + xβ, so **all gaps are pure numbers times |β|**, with no geometry, no basis set and no computer time. The fibub tool takes a number of π atoms and a bond list and returns the whole orbital diagram, the π energy, the delocalisation energy and the gap.

**Step 1: the size trend.** We built the linear acene series (benzene, naphthalene, anthracene, tetracene, pentacene, hexacene, heptacene) as bond lists and solved each. The gaps fall smoothly: 2.000, 1.236, 0.828, 0.590, 0.439, 0.339 and 0.268 |β|. From benzene (C6) to heptacene (C30) that is a factor of **7.5**; the paper's whole set falls by a factor of 10.3, over a much wider size range (C6 to C96). The shape of the fall is the same: fast at first, then flattening.

How big is |β| in electronvolts? Hückel theory does not say; it is fitted. Taking the paper's linear-group range and **assuming** its ends are the plain acenes naphthalene (C10) and hexacene (C26), each end fixes a value: 5.97 ÷ 1.236 = **4.83 eV** and 1.45 ÷ 0.339 = **4.28 eV**. One parameter therefore matches both ends of a range that spans a factor of four, to within **13 %**. That is the whole of the paper's size trend recovered from bond lists. Figure 1 uses the mean, |β| = 4.56 eV.

**Step 2: shape at a fixed size.** This is the paper's more interesting claim, and Hückel theory is unusually good at it. At 14 carbon atoms, anthracene (three rings in a row) and phenanthrene (the same three rings bent) give gaps of 0.828 and 1.211 |β|: a split of **0.382 |β|, or 1.74 eV** on our scale. At 18 carbon atoms, tetracene and chrysene give 0.590 and 1.040 |β|: **0.450 |β|, or 2.05 eV**. Those are exactly the paper's "about 2 eV at the same size", and the tool needed only the connectivity to find them. Triphenylene, also C18 but with its rings arranged around a centre, comes out at 1.368 |β| (6.2 eV), above even benzene's neighbours: compact, branched aromatics stay wide-gapped.

| Check | Paper | Hückel MO calculator |
|---|---|---|
| Gap falls with size | 6.59 → 0.64 eV over C6-C96 | 2.000 → 0.268 \|β\| over C6-C30 (factor 7.5) |
| Linear group's range | 1.45-5.97 eV, C10-C26 | one \|β\| fits both ends to 13 % (4.28 and 4.83 eV) |
| Shape shifts the gap at fixed size | about 2 eV | 1.74 eV (C14 pair), 2.05 eV (C18 pair) |
| Five-membered rings matter most | largest single effect | breaks the alternant symmetry: LUMO drops 0.35 \|β\| on its own |

**Step 3: why a five-membered ring is special.** Benzenoid PAHs are *alternant*: their carbons can be coloured in two sets with no bond inside a set, and the orbital energies then come in ± pairs, so the HOMO and the LUMO move together and the gap closes slowly. Add an odd-membered ring and that pairing breaks. We built **acenaphthylene** (naphthalene plus a two-carbon bridge across its peri positions, closing a five-membered ring) and the tool gives HOMO +0.638 and LUMO −0.285 |β|: no longer mirror images. The empty level has dropped on its own, which is precisely the mechanism behind the paper's finding that five-membered rings pull gaps down hardest, and it is visible in Figure 2 as a lopsided ladder.

**Where Hückel theory fails, and it should be said plainly.** Our fitted |β| of about 4.5 eV is nearly double the 2.4 eV usually fitted from UV-visible spectra, because a B3LYP Kohn-Sham gap is not an excitation energy; the tool's own note uses the spectroscopic value. With a single |β| fitted to the large acenes, benzene comes out at 9.1 eV, far above any computed or measured value: one β cannot cover the whole size range. Hückel theory also knows nothing about side groups, so the paper's ketone result is out of reach, and nothing about bending, so for a non-planar molecule it gives the gap of its flat graph.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/pah-homo-lumo-gap-size-trend-huckel.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `build` and `solve` from `src/lib/sci/huckel.ts`, the maths behind the [[huckel-mo-calculator]]: `solve` builds the Hückel matrix, diagonalises it with cyclic Jacobi rotations, fills the orbitals (sharing electrons within a degenerate set), and returns energies, charges, bond orders and the delocalisation energy.

Bond lists for the benzenoids are generated by placing hexagon vertices at a bond length of 1 around each ring centre, merging shared vertices and bonding every pair closer than 1.05: a convenience for building the graph, not geometry the model uses. Acenaphthylene is the tool's own naphthalene preset plus three bonds. From the paper we take only the group gap ranges, the carbon-number ranges and the method; the identification of the linear group's ends with naphthalene and hexacene, and therefore |β| = 4.56 eV, is **our assumption**.

## How we test the maths

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/huckel-mo-calculator.mjs` runs inside `tests/sci.mjs` on every change and pins the solver against the standard published Hückel results:

- ethylene ±1 β and zero delocalisation; butadiene ±0.618 and ±1.618 β (the golden ratio) with a delocalisation energy of 0.472 β; the allyl radical √2, 0, −√2 with a half-filled middle orbital and no net charge anywhere;
- benzene 2, 1, 1, −1, −1, −2 β, a 2 β gap, 2 β of delocalisation and every bond order exactly ⅔; square cyclobutadiene with two electrons in a degenerate non-bonding pair (gap 0);
- naphthalene's HOMO at exactly φ − 1 = 0.618 β and anthracene's at √2 − 1 = 0.4142 β; pyrene 0.4450 β and triphenylene 0.6840 β;
- the numbers in this article: acene gaps 0.5899, 0.4394 and 0.3387 β, the isomer splits 0.3820 and 0.4504 β, and acenaphthylene's unsymmetric +0.6375 / −0.2846 β.

## Check it yourself

On the [[huckel-mo-calculator]] page, in the *π system* card:

1. Set *Molecule* to **Naphthalene**. The facts read **10 π electrons, Eπ = 10α + 13.683β, delocalisation energy 3.683β**, and *HOMO and LUMO* **α + 0.618β and α − 0.618β**, gap **1.236|β|**.
2. Set *Molecule* to **My own**, *π atoms* to **14**, *Net charge* **0**, and paste this anthracene bond list into *Bonds between π atoms, numbered from 1*: `1-2, 1-6, 2-3, 2-7, 3-4, 3-10, 4-5, 5-6, 7-8, 8-9, 8-11, 9-10, 9-14, 11-12, 12-13, 13-14`. The gap is **0.828|β|** and the delocalisation energy **5.314β**.
3. Now bend it into phenanthrene by changing four bonds: `1-2, 1-6, 2-3, 2-7, 3-4, 3-10, 4-5, 5-6, 7-8, 7-14, 8-9, 8-13, 9-10, 11-12, 11-14, 12-13`. Same 14 atoms, same 16 bonds, but the gap jumps to **1.211|β|** and the delocalisation energy to **5.448β**. That is the paper's "structure at the same size" effect, in two edits.
4. For the five-membered ring, set *π atoms* to **12** and use `1-2, 2-3, 3-4, 4-10, 10-5, 5-6, 6-7, 7-8, 8-9, 9-1, 9-10, 1-11, 11-12, 12-8` (acenaphthylene). *HOMO and LUMO* read **α + 0.638β and α − 0.285β**: the ladder has gone lopsided.
5. The page reports the gap in |β| and also in electronvolts with β ≈ −2.4 eV, the value fitted to UV spectra. To compare with a DFT Kohn-Sham gap instead, multiply the |β| figure by about 4.5 yourself, as we did here, and say which scale you used.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a DFT gap before you trust it**: a Hückel gap takes seconds, and a B3LYP result that breaks the Hückel ordering of two isomers is worth re-running.
- **Screen candidate structures** in a growth mechanism: Hückel gaps rank hundreds of bond lists in the time one DFT job takes, so the expensive calculations can be spent on the interesting ones.
- **Tell size from shape** when you explain a trend: run the acene series for the size part, then isomers at a fixed size for the shape part.
- **Spot non-alternant systems**, where a five- or seven-membered ring breaks the ± symmetry of the levels, as the tool shows directly.
- **Teach the result**: two bond lists, one number each, reproduce the central claim of a 323-molecule DFT survey.

## The fine print

- We reproduce **trends and splittings**, not the paper's individual numbers: the paper publishes its gaps as group ranges and in figures, and no per-molecule value is used here.
- Identifying the linear group's extremes with naphthalene and hexacene is **our assumption**; the group has 9 members and may include substituted chains.
- |β| is a fitted parameter. The 4.56 eV we use is specific to B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) Kohn-Sham gaps; the usual spectroscopic value is about 2.4 eV, and the tool's own estimate uses that.
- Hückel theory treats a molecule as a flat graph: it cannot see non-planarity, side groups, solvent or dispersion, all of which the paper handles.
- A Kohn-Sham HOMO-LUMO gap is itself not an observable; comparing it with optical gaps needs excited-state methods.
