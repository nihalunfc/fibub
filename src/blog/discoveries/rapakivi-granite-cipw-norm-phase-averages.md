Rapakivi granites are the big, coarse, 1.6-billion-year-old granites of the Baltic shield, famous for feldspar crystals ringed by a paler rim. Because most of western Estonia's rapakivi is buried under younger rock, the only way to follow how its magma changed is through drill-core chemistry. A 2026 review in the *Estonian Journal of Earth Sciences* does exactly that for the Märjamaa and Kloostri intrusions, using **CIPW normative minerals**, the virtual mineral list a rock would crystallise if it were perfectly dry and simple, to compare three magmatic phases. All of that maths is in fibub's [[cipw-norm]], so we recomputed the paper's three phase norms from its own oxide averages. For the next steps, turn a mineral analysis into a formula with the [[mineral-formula]] and plot the phases against each other with the [[ternary-plot-maker]].

## The paper in brief

The authors gather 47 whole-rock major-element analyses from Märjamaa and Kloostri drill cores and group them into three intrusive phases. **Phase I** is the least evolved (average 66.94 wt% SiO₂) and collapsed into a piston-like cauldron. **Phase II** is a more fractionated granite ring (69.93 wt% SiO₂, and the most potassic, 6.35 wt% K₂O). **Phase III** is the youngest and most silica-rich leucogranite (71.78 wt% SiO₂, 3.35 wt% Na₂O).

Their reading of the chemistry: titanium, magnesium, calcium and phosphorus all fall from Phase I to Phase III, while silica and alkalis rise, which is ordinary fractional crystallisation. The iron tells a second story. The ferric share of the iron, Fe³⁺# = Fe₂O₃/(FeO + Fe₂O₃) by weight, averages 0.45 in Phase I, drops to 0.28 in Phase II and rises to 0.54 in Phase III, so Phase II crystallised under the most reduced conditions. The authors are careful to call this semi-quantitative, because normative iron-titanium oxides fall mostly through differentiation, not redox. Silica-in-melt thermometry gives about 775 °C for Phase I down to 707 °C for Phase III, and quartz barometry mid-crustal pressures of roughly 3 to 5 kbar.

## Checked with the tools

The paper prints both sides of the sum we need: the average oxides of each phase and the average normative minerals of each phase. So this is a **recomputation from the paper's stated values**, not a simulation. We fed each phase's average analysis to the CIPW norm calculator's maths, with the iron left exactly as analysed (no Le Maitre correction) and the oxides closed to 100 %. MnO is the one oxide the paper does not average by phase, so we used **0.05 wt% for all three phases (our assumption)**, taken from the single Table 1 row we read, where MnO is 0.04 wt%.

The tool also returns numbers the paper does not quote: a differentiation index (quartz + alkali feldspars) rising 78.1 → 84.7 → 88.6 through the phases, a colour index (the dark minerals) falling 11.9 → 8.7 → 6.8, and anorthite in normative plagioclase falling from An 28 to An 22 to An 14. All three say the same thing as the paper's oxide trends, in one line each.

**The two points off the line are the interesting part.** Phase II magnetite comes out 1.06 wt% low and Phase II quartz 0.81 wt% low. That is not a bug, it is the order of operations. Normative magnetite is `min(Fe₂O₃, FeO)` in moles: in Phase II the average Fe₂O₃ (1.05 wt%) is small next to FeO (2.67 wt%), so magnetite is capped by ferric iron and nothing else. Averaging the oxides first and then taking the norm is therefore not the same as taking each sample's norm and averaging: samples with more ferric iron contribute extra magnetite, and that extra magnetite steals FeO from hypersthene, which in turn frees silica for quartz.

Figure 2 shows that trade with total iron held fixed and only the ferric share moved.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/rapakivi-granite-cipw-norm-phase-averages.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `cipw` and the molar masses `M` from `src/lib/sci/cipw.ts`, the maths behind the [[cipw-norm]], and nothing else: no curve is drawn by hand.

The oxide averages, the normative averages, the Fe³⁺# averages and the one sample row are the paper's stated values. The MnO of 0.05 wt% is **our assumption**. The ferric sweep in Figure 2 keeps the paper's total iron and changes only the split between Fe₂O₃ and FeO, solved by bisection so that Fe³⁺# lands on each value exactly; the ratio that reproduces the paper's magnetite is found the same way.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/cipw-norm.mjs`, which pins the norm against the Python library `pyrolite`:

- Seven made-up compositions (basalt, granite, nephelinite, peraluminous, peralkaline, ultrapotassic and an extremely silica-poor rock), in eight combinations with no iron correction and with Le Maitre's volcanic and plutonic corrections, must match `pyrolite`'s `CIPW_norm` to 0.002 wt% on every normative mineral.
- Hand-checkable cases: the composition of pure albite must give 100 % normative albite, and 3 MgO + 2 SiO₂ must split one-to-one in moles between hypersthene and olivine.
- Mass balance: the norm must total 100 wt% once any borrowed silica is added back.
- New with this article: the Phase II average analysis above must give quartz 24.85 and magnetite 1.53 wt%, and raising the ferric ratio to 0.468 with the same total iron must give 25.65 and 2.59, so the quartz-magnetite trade stays pinned.

3. Read down the three columns: quartz **23.3, 24.9, 27.0**, orthoclase **30.7, 37.8, 33.1**, albite **24.1, 22.1, 28.5**, anorthite **9.3, 6.3, 4.6**, magnetite **3.4, 1.5, 2.0**, ilmenite **1.5, 0.8, 0.6**, apatite **0.8, 0.3, 0.1**. *Differentiation index* reads **78.1, 84.7, 88.6** and *Colour index* **11.9, 8.7, 6.8**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published norm against the published analysis** before you build an argument on it, as we did here: a mismatch usually means a different iron assumption or a mean of norms.
- **Report whether your phase averages are the norm of the mean or the mean of the norms.** For magnetite, ilmenite and quartz they are not the same, and the gap can be a whole weight per cent.
- **Test how much your redox assumption matters**: sweep Fe₂O₃/FeO and watch the oxides and silica move before you read anything into normative magnetite.
- **Prepare plotting variables** for classification diagrams (differentiation index, normative An, colour index) in one pass, then draw them with the [[ternary-plot-maker]].
- **Teach the norm** with one real granite: the step order is visible in the output, which is hard to show with a black-box spreadsheet.

## The fine print

- The paper's MnO is not averaged by phase; our 0.05 wt% changes the norm by well under 0.1 wt%.
- The paper's analyses are already normalised to 100 wt%, so our closure step changes almost nothing (the phase averages sum to 99.2 to 99.4 wt% because of rounding and the missing MnO).
- We used only major elements. The paper's thermometry, barometry and trace-element arguments are outside what a norm can check.
- Phase averages are averages. Individual samples span 63.5 to 75.5 wt% SiO₂, so no single norm describes a phase.
- The normative "magnetite" of a norm is not the magnetite you would see under a microscope; it is a bookkeeping mineral for ferric iron.
