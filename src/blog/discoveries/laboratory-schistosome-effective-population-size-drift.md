*Schistosoma mansoni* is a parasitic flatworm that infects hundreds of millions of people, and most of what laboratories know about it comes from a handful of lines kept in snails and mice for decades. A 2025 paper in *PLOS Pathogens* sequenced five of those lines and found something reassuring: they still carry a lot of genetic variation, about 62 % less than wild parasites rather than the near-total loss you might fear. The question that invites is arithmetic: **how large must a line's effective population have been to still hold 38 % of its diversity after 400 generations?** That is one line of algebra and one simulation, both in the [[wright-fisher-simulator]]. For the companion checks on the same kind of data, see [[hardy-weinberg-tester]] and [[molecular-clock]].

## The paper in brief

The authors sequenced parasites from five laboratory populations: **SmNMRI** (from Puerto Rico in the 1940s), **SmEG** (Egypt), **SmLE** (Brazil, 1965), **SmBRE** (Brazil, 1975) and **SmOR** (Puerto Rico, 1971). Taking five complete generations a year, that is roughly 160 generations for the youngest line and about 400 for the oldest.

Their headline numbers: nucleotide diversity of 1.52 × 10⁻⁴ to 7.15 × 10⁻⁴ in the laboratory lines, against roughly 2 × 10⁻³ to 9 × 10⁻³ in field populations, which they describe as **62 % lower**. Current effective population size, estimated from linkage patterns, is **2 to 258** (NeEstimator) or 5 to 123 (COLONY), against 3,174 or more in the field. They then ran the simulation program BottleSim at fixed population sizes and reported a **63.4 % reduction at N = 100** and 39.9 % at N = 200 after 400 generations, concluding that the observed loss is "generally compatible" with N ≈ 100.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation and a simulation** from the paper's stated numbers. We did not touch its sequence data.

**Step 1: the one-line answer.** Under neutral drift in a population of constant effective size Ne, expected heterozygosity shrinks by a factor 1 − 1/(2Ne) each generation, so after t generations H_t / H_0 = (1 − 1/2Ne)^t. Setting that to 0.38 at t = 400 and solving for Ne gives **Ne ≈ 207**, not 100. At Ne = 100 the same 400 generations would leave only **13.5 %** of the starting diversity, a 86.5 % loss, far more than observed.

**Step 2: the simulator agrees with the algebra.** We ran fibub's Wright-Fisher simulator on 2,000 independent neutral sites per line, each starting at a frequency of 0.5, and tracked the mean heterozygosity 2p(1 − p) across sites (Figure 1). After 400 generations it leaves 1.8 % at Ne = 50, **13.8 %** at Ne = 100, 35.7 % at Ne = 200 and 60.1 % at Ne = 400, against 1.8, 13.5, 36.7 and 60.6 % from the formula. The simulation is doing exactly what the textbook says, which matters here: the gap with the paper is not simulation noise.

**Step 3: where the factor of two comes from.** Look at the paper's own BottleSim output through the same lens. A 63.4 % reduction leaves 36.6 %, which a plain Wright-Fisher model reaches at **Ne = 199**, not 100. Its 39.9 % reduction leaves 60.1 %, which needs **Ne = 393**, not 200. Both of its sizes map onto twice themselves, which is the signature of counting gene copies instead of diploid individuals (or breeding pairs instead of parents) somewhere in the setup.

| Reduction after 400 generations | Paper's label | Share of diversity left | Ne in a plain Wright-Fisher model |
|---|---|---|---|
| 63.4 % | BottleSim N = 100 | 36.6 % | **199** |
| 39.9 % | BottleSim N = 200 | 60.1 % | **393** |
| 62 % (observed) | "compatible with N ≈ 100" | 38 % | **207** |
| 38 % (Figure 6 caption) | – | 62 % | **419** |

**Step 4: this changes the conclusion, mildly but usefully.** An Ne of about 200 sits at the very top of the paper's own NeEstimator range of 2 to 258 and well above the COLONY range of 5 to 123. So either these lines have been kept at effective sizes near the best end of what the paper measures, or their size has not been constant: a line that spent most of its history large and only recently shrank loses far less diversity than its current Ne suggests, because drift is a cumulative sum over generations and recent generations contribute little. Both readings support the paper's main message, that the lines are more variable than expected, while making its stated N ≈ 100 look too small.

**Step 5: how fast is fast?** The half-life of diversity is ln(0.5) / ln(1 − 1/2Ne) generations: **69** at Ne = 50, **138** at Ne = 100, **277** at Ne = 200 and **554** at Ne = 400. At five parasite generations a year that is 14, 28, 55 and 111 years of laboratory time. This is why the paper's small-Ne result is not in dispute: at Ne = 5, 99 % of the diversity is gone in **44** generations, under nine years. Figure 2 reads the whole relationship backwards, from the share of diversity left to the Ne it needs.

Two caveats of our own. First, we treat nucleotide diversity as behaving like expected heterozygosity, which is standard but ignores mutation putting variation back; over 400 generations in a small population that is a small correction, and it works in the direction of making our Ne estimate an underestimate. Second, these lines also pass through snails and mice, so a "generation" is not a clean Wright-Fisher one; the paper's own five-generations-a-year figure is the assumption we inherit.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/laboratory-schistosome-effective-population-size-drift.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `simulate` from `src/lib/sci/wrightfisher.ts`, the maths behind the [[wright-fisher-simulator]], with 2,000 replicate lines, no selection (s = 0), a starting frequency of 0.5 and seed 7, so the figures are the same on every run. The expected-heterozygosity curve and the inverse (the Ne that leaves a given share) are three lines of algebra in the same script.

The 400 generations, the five generations a year, the 62 % reduction, the Ne ranges and the BottleSim results are the paper's. The starting frequency of 0.5 and the 2,000 sites are **our choices**; the decay of mean heterozygosity does not depend on the starting frequency, only on Ne and t.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/wright-fisher-simulator.mjs`, which pins the simulator against exact theory computed in SciPy:

- Kimura's fixation probability for a new favourable allele must match SciPy's numeric integral: 0.20964108 for p = 0.1, N = 50, s = 0.02, h = 0.5, and six more cases including recessive, dominant and deleterious alleles.
- The binomial sampler must pass a chi-square goodness-of-fit test against the exact probability mass function at n = 40, 200, 500 and 1000.
- The neutral mean time to fixation from p = 0.5 at N = 100 must equal 4N ln 2 generations.
- New with this article: after 400 generations at Ne = 100 and 200 the mean heterozygosity across 2,000 simulated sites must be 0.1376 and 0.3571 of its starting value, within 0.02 of the analytic (1 − 1/2Ne)^400, and the Ne that leaves 38 % must be 207.

## Check it yourself

On the [[wright-fisher-simulator]] page:

1. Set *Population size N (diploid individuals)* to **100**, *Start* to **At a frequency** with *Starting frequency of A* **0.5**, *Selection s* to **0**, *Generations* to **400** and *Lines (replicates)* to **1000**.
2. Untick *Keep going until every line is fixed or lost*, so the run stops at generation 400, and press nothing else: the page recomputes as you type.
3. Read *Still segregating*. With the page's default *Seed* of 1, **195 of 1000** lines are still segregating: four fifths of the sites have already gone to 0 or 1. Most of the diversity is gone, which is the Ne = 100 case the paper calls compatible with its data.
4. Change *Population size N* to **200** and watch *Still segregating* rise to **543 of 1000**, and to **866** at N = 400.
5. In the frequency chart, look at how flat the mean line stays while individual lines wander: drift destroys variation between lines' frequencies without moving their average, which is why heterozygosity, not mean frequency, is the thing to measure.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check an Ne claim** before citing it: (1 − 1/2Ne)^t takes ten seconds and catches factor-of-two slips in simulation setups.
- **Plan how to keep a laboratory line**: the half-life table in step 5 says how many breeders a colony needs to hold its variation for a given number of years.
- **Decide how many loci you need**: run the simulator with your Ne and generations and see how many sites are still segregating, which is the number your assay can actually use.
- **Separate drift from selection**: rerun the same line with a small s and see whether the observed frequency change needs selection at all.
- **Teach why current Ne and historical Ne differ**, with a line that was large for 300 generations and small for 20.

## The fine print

- The paper's sequence data is public, but nothing here uses it: every number is recomputed from values stated in its text, or simulated.
- We read the laboratory-versus-field diversity gap as 62 %, as the paper's text says. Its Figure 6 caption says 38 %, which would imply Ne ≈ 419; we report both.
- Expected heterozygosity and nucleotide diversity are not the same statistic, and we ignore new mutation, so our Ne is a lower bound rather than an estimate.
- A parasite with an asexual stage in snails and a sexual stage in mammals breaks the Wright-Fisher assumption of one clean generation of random mating; the paper's five generations a year is an approximation we keep.
- Nothing here bears on the clinical or control use of these parasite lines.
