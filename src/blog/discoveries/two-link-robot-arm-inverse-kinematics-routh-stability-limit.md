The two-link planar arm, a shoulder and an elbow moving in one plane, is where most robotics courses start, because every idea of the subject shows up in it: forward kinematics (from joint angles to the tip), inverse kinematics (from a target to the joint angles), the workspace, and the control loop that drives each joint. A 2026 conference paper works all of this by hand for an arm with links of **2 m and 1 m**, then sets up a Routh stability test for the joint controller. Its numbers are small enough to check exactly, and the [[inverse-kinematics]] tool and the [[pid-simulator]] do it in seconds. For arms with more joints, use [[dh-kinematics]]; to plan the route between targets, try the [[path-planner]].

## The paper in brief

The authors write the forward kinematics of the arm, x = L1 cos θ1 + L2 cos(θ1 + θ2) and y = L1 sin θ1 + L2 sin(θ1 + θ2), and work one example: with **θ1 = 45°** and **θ2 = 30°** the tip is at **(1.67, 2.38)**. They then invert it, using the law of cosines for the elbow (cos θ2 = 0.8633) and recovering **θ2 = 30°** and **θ1 = 45°**, after checking that the point's distance, **2.9**, lies between |L1 − L2| and L1 + L2.

Each joint is driven by a DC motor (armature resistance 4 Ω, inductance 0.7 H, inertia 0.05, friction 0.2). The motor and joint together give a third-order model, **0.4 / (0.035 s³ + 0.34 s² + 0.84 s)**, with a pure integrator, since a motor turns an angle. The design targets are a settling time of **5 s or less**, overshoot **below 25 %** and **no steady-state error**. The paper builds a Routh table for a proportional gain K, with first-column entry (0.2856 − 0.014 K)/0.34, before moving to a PID controller.

## Checked with the tools

We redid the kinematics with the Inverse kinematics tool's maths and the joint loop with the PID tuning simulator's maths. Both are **recomputations** from the paper's printed values.

**Step 1: forward and inverse kinematics.** The tool's forward kinematics put the tip at **(1.6730, 2.3801)** for 45° and 30°, which the paper rounds to (1.67, 2.38). Working back from the rounded point, the tool finds **cos θ2 = 0.8633**, as the paper does, and the angles **θ1 = 44.948°, θ2 = 30.308°**, with cos θ1 = 0.7077 and sin θ1 = 0.7065 (the paper: 0.7078 and 0.7065). The 0.3° gap from 30° is the rounding of the target to two decimals; from the unrounded tip the tool gives exactly 45° and 30°.

**Step 2: the second solution.** A two-link arm reaches most points in two ways, mirror images about the line from the base to the target. The paper gives one; the tool also finds **θ1 = 64.939°, θ2 = −30.308°** (Figure 1). Both are valid unless the joints have limits, so a controller must be told which one to use, or the arm can flip between them.

**Step 3: the workspace.** The arm reaches from **1 m** to **3 m** from its base. The target is **2.907 m** away, so it is inside, but only 9 cm from the outer edge, where the arm is nearly straight and the elbow angle changes quickly for small moves of the target.

**Step 4: the Routh limit.** The paper's Routh entry turns negative when 0.014 K > 0.2856, so the loop is stable for **0 < K < 20.4**. The PID tuning simulator finds the same number another way, as the **ultimate gain Ku = 20.4**: the gain at which the joint's phase reaches −180°, at **4.899 rad/s** (a period of **1.28 s**). With K = 1, the gain margin is a factor of 20.4 (26.2 dB) and the phase margin 79°.

**Step 5: which gains meet the specs?** Because the joint model already contains an integrator, a plain proportional controller leaves **no steady-state error** for a step. Scanning K in steps of 0.01, the gains from **1.27 to 5.14** settle within 2 % in 5 s or less with less than 25 % overshoot (Figure 2). For example, K = 3 rises in **0.91 s**, overshoots by **7.99 %** and settles in **2.85 s**; K = 5 overshoots by **23.9 %**; K = 8 is still stable but overshoots **43 %** and takes **5.04 s**. So all three of the paper's targets can be met before any integral or derivative action is added; those then buy robustness to loads and disturbances.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/two-link-robot-arm-inverse-kinematics-routh-stability-limit.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `ik2`, `fk` and `reach2` from `src/lib/sci/ik.ts`, the maths behind the [[inverse-kinematics]] tool, and `ultimate`, `closedLoopPoles`, `simulate`, `stepInfo` and `margins` from `src/lib/sci/ctl-pid.ts`, the maths behind the [[pid-simulator]].

The link lengths, joint angles, target, joint model and specifications come from the paper's text. The 0.01 step of the gain scan and the run length of each simulation (8 divided by the slowest pole's rate, as the PID tool chooses it) are **our choices**. We do not know the paper's final PID gains, so we checked only proportional control.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/inverse-kinematics.mjs` and the PID checks:

- For a 2-link arm with links 1 and 0.8 reaching (1.2, 0.6), and for two more arms, both elbow solutions must match an independent SciPy least-squares solve from 60 random starts.
- 400 random targets must round-trip through inverse and forward kinematics to within 10⁻¹² of the target, and points beyond 1.8 or closer than 0.2 must be refused as out of reach.
- New with this article: the paper's arm (2 and 1) reaching (1.67, 2.38) must give 44.948° and 30.308°, and the mirror pose 64.939° and −30.308°, and from the unrounded tip exactly 45° and 30°.
- The PID tuning simulator's tests (`tests/sci-tools/pid-simulator.mjs`) pin its step responses and margins against python-control on 8 loops, and its ultimate gain for 2e^(−s)/(5s + 1) at **Ku = 4.2512** with period **3.7208 s**, as SciPy's root finder gives.

## Check it yourself

On the [[inverse-kinematics]] page:

1. Keep *Links* on **2 links (position only)**, set *Link 1 length L1* to **2** and *Link 2 length L2* to **1**.
2. Set the target *x* to **1.67** and *y* to **2.38**, and set *Draw and list* to **Elbow down**.
3. The headline reads **θ1 = 44.948°, θ2 = 30.308°**, and the distance fact reads **2.9075**, with the reach given as **1 to 3**.
4. Switch *Draw and list* to **Elbow up**: **θ1 = 64.939°, θ2 = −30.308°**, the other way to reach the same point.

Then on the [[pid-simulator]]:

5. Choose *Model* **Transfer function + dead time**, type **0.4** in *Numerator*, **0.035 0.34 0.84 0** in *Denominator*, and set *Dead time L* to **0**.
6. Set *Kp* to **3**, *Ki* to **0** and *Kd* to **0**. The step response shows *Overshoot* **7.99 %**, *Settling time (±2 %)* **2.85 s** and *Steady-state error* **0**.
7. Below the tuning table, the note reads *Ultimate gain Ku = 20.4*: the paper's Routh limit. Set *Kp* to **21** to watch the loop go unstable.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a hand calculation:** type a paper's link lengths and target into the [[inverse-kinematics]] tool and compare both elbow solutions with its angles.
- **Spot rounding:** a 0.3° difference here comes only from a target rounded to two decimals; the tool shows how much a published rounding moves the answer.
- **Find a gain range before tuning:** the [[pid-simulator]] gives the ultimate gain (the Routh limit) and the step response for any gain, so the range that meets the specs can be found before adding integral and derivative terms.
- **Go beyond two joints:** build the forward kinematics of a spatial arm from its Denavit-Hartenberg table with [[dh-kinematics]].
- **Teach:** the arm and joint model in this paper make a complete one-lecture example, from geometry to stability.

## The fine print

- These are **recomputations** from the paper's printed values. Its final PID gains and simulated responses were not readable to us, so we checked proportional control only.
- The joint model ignores gravity and the coupling between the two links; a real arm's joints load each other.
- Which solution is called "elbow up" or "elbow down" depends on the convention; the tool names the solution with a positive elbow angle "elbow down".
- Settling and overshoot come from simulations with 2000 time steps; values may differ in the last digit from other software.
