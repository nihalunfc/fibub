Corpus linguistics has a favourite number: **6.63**. Put two bodies of text side by side, count a word in each, and if the log-likelihood statistic beats 6.63 the word is "key" — more typical of one corpus than the other at p < 0.01. A 2025 paper in *PLOS ONE* uses exactly that cut-off to compare how male and female entrepreneurs write on X, with corpora of 31,124 and 27,773 words. This article redoes that arithmetic with the [[corpus-concordancer]], whose collocation tables run on the same log-likelihood formula, and asks the question a reader should always ask of a keyness table: at these corpus sizes, how often does a word have to appear before the test can say anything at all? For the other two ways of comparing texts, try the [[lexical-diversity-readability]] tool and [[stylometry-delta]].

## The paper in brief

The authors built two corpora of posts, one from female entrepreneurs (31,124 words) and one from male entrepreneurs (27,773 words), and ran keyness in Wmatrix 5.0 on semantic categories and parts of speech, keeping anything with a log-likelihood above 6.63. They report **shared** categories, where both groups score highly — helping and supporting (*help*, *support*, *helping*, *inspiring*), and thanking (*thanks*, *grateful*) — and **preferential** categories, where one group stands out: for the women, work and career, family, technology, leadership, gratitude and sharing (*work* 76, *thank_you* 90, *women* 67, *technology* 39, *family* 37, *leadership* 28); for the men, money and finance, government, education, climate and negation (*telegram* 61, *climate* 41, *money* 14, *students* 13, *privacy* 11).

Their reading is a "double bind with a twist": women entrepreneurs perform both the warm, communal register expected of them and the competent, professional one expected of founders, while men lean on technical and financial topics and on negation. That is a discourse argument, and we are not qualified to settle it. What we can do is check the counting underneath it.

## Checked with the tools

Keyness is a 2×2 table. For a word that appears *a* times in a corpus of *N*<sub>A</sub> words and *b* times in one of *N*<sub>B</sub> words, the table is [*a*, *N*<sub>A</sub> − *a*; *b*, *N*<sub>B</sub> − *b*], and the statistic is G² = 2 Σ O ln(O/E) over the four cells. That is the same function the [[corpus-concordancer]] uses for its collocation column, so we called it directly on the frequencies the paper prints in its running text. This is a **recomputation from the paper's own numbers**, not a simulation.

**Step 1: the shared categories really are shared.** Six words have a count in both corpora. None of them comes close to the cut-off:

| Word | Female (per million) | Male (per million) | G² we get | Key at 6.63? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| help | 60 (1,928) | 46 (1,656) | 0.60 | no |
| support | 30 (964) | 31 (1,116) | 0.33 | no |
| helping | 25 (803) | 11 (396) | 4.12 | no |
| inspiring | 19 (610) | 10 (360) | 1.91 | no |
| thanks | 32 (1,028) | 41 (1,476) | 2.38 | no |
| grateful | 14 (450) | 17 (612) | 0.73 | no |

Add the four helping-and-supporting words together (134 against 98; **our grouping**, for illustration) and the total is still only **G² = 2.27**. So the paper's "shared" label is the right one: on these counts there is no evidence either group helps or thanks more than the other, and a reader should not be tempted by the raw 60-against-46 to think otherwise.

**Step 2: the preferential contrasts are real, and large.** Treating the words listed for one corpus as absent from the other — the most generous possible reading, since the paper does not print the other count (**our assumption**) — the big items are overwhelming: *thank_you* 90 gives **G² = 114.9**, *work* 76 gives **97.0**, *women* 67 gives **85.5**, *telegram* 61 gives **91.8**, *climate* 41 gives **61.7**, *money* 14 gives **21.1**. All far past 6.63. The paper's central contrast does not depend on a delicate statistical judgement; it is visible in the counts.

**Step 3: but some listed words cannot be key at all.** At these corpus sizes a word that is completely missing from the comparison corpus still needs **6 occurrences** in the 31,124-word female corpus (G² = 7.65 at 6, only 6.38 at 5) or **5** in the 27,773-word male corpus (7.52 at 5, 6.01 at 4) to clear 6.63. Items the paper lists with 2 to 5 tokens — *female* 5, *mother* 4, *bravo* 5, *objective* 2, *encrypted* 2 — therefore cannot pass the test on their own. They are not keywords; they are members of a semantic category whose **total** was tested. That is a legitimate method, and the paper says it tests categories, but a table that prints word-level frequencies invites word-level reading, and word-level reading of a 2-token item is not supported.

**Step 4: read the test backwards and it becomes a planning tool.** Fix the cut-off at 6.63 and ask how many tokens you need to prove a given difference in rate. A word twice as common in one corpus as the other needs **42** occurrences; 1.5 times as common needs **105**; three times as common needs **21**; ten times needs **9**. Below those counts the honest answer is "this corpus is too small to tell", however suggestive the words look.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/keyness-log-likelihood-thresholds-entrepreneur-twitter-corpus.mjs` in fibub's public code. It imports one function, `g2`, from `src/lib/sci/kwic.ts` — the maths behind the [[corpus-concordancer]]'s Collocations view — and feeds it the 2×2 keyness tables built from the two corpus sizes and the word counts the paper prints.

The corpus sizes (31,124 and 27,773 words), the cut-off (6.63) and every word frequency come from the paper's text. **Our assumptions** are that a word listed for only one corpus has a count of zero in the other, and the grouping of four words in step 1. Nothing here is simulated: it is the paper's own counts put through the same formula the paper used.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/corpus-concordancer.mjs`, which pins the concordancer against values worked out in Python:

- On the tool's own 151-token example text, the word list must give *the* 22, *and* 8 and *ferry* 8, and the tokeniser must keep *Don't* and *well-known* whole.
- For the node *ferry* with a three-word window each side, the window must hold 46 words and the collocate *and* must give O = 5, E = 2.437086, MI = 1.036771, t = 1.146170 and **G² = 3.700616**; a left-only window must give *strong* MI = 3.594549 and G² = 18.560354.
- G² of a perfectly independent table must be exactly 0.
- New with this article: at the paper's corpus sizes, G² must be **0.60** for 60 against 46, **4.12** for 25 against 11 and **85.53** for 67 against 0, and the smallest count that reaches 6.63 with nothing in the other corpus must be **6** words in 31,124 and **5** in 27,773.

## Check it yourself

The cut-off is about counts, not about any particular corpus, so you can watch it work on a passage small enough to paste. On the [[corpus-concordancer]], in *Your texts*:

1. Replace the example with this passage (our own words, 106 tokens):

   *Women founders thank their mentors in public. Women founders thank investors, and thank the teams who stayed late. Women founders thank customers by name. ## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a corpus before you collect it.** Decide the smallest rate difference you care about, read the token count off Figure 2, and you know how much text you need.
- **Audit a keyness table you are reading.** Any row with a count under about six in corpora this size cannot be significant on its own; check whether the test was run on the category instead.
- **Report rates, not raw counts.** The concordancer's word list gives frequencies you can turn into per-million rates, which is the only fair comparison between corpora of different sizes.
- **Check a collocation claim.** The same G² column ranks collocates inside one corpus, with MI and t-score beside it, so you can see whether a "strong collocation" is strong or merely frequent.
- **Teach the difference between size of effect and strength of evidence.** MI is large for rare pairs, G² only for well-attested ones; the tool shows both in the same table.

## The fine print

- We used only the frequencies the paper prints in its running text. Its full keyness tables are published as images, and we did not read numbers off them.
- Where the paper lists a word for one corpus only, we took the other count as zero. If the word does occur there, its real G² is **lower** than ours, so our step-2 conclusions are upper bounds.
- The paper tests **semantic categories and parts of speech**, assigned by Wmatrix. Our step-3 point is about how word-level frequencies in such a table can be misread, not a claim that the paper tested single words.
- Log-likelihood assumes words fall independently, which they do not: a word repeated in one long post is not six independent pieces of evidence. The real token threshold is therefore higher than 6, not lower.
- Nothing here speaks to the paper's discourse analysis, its sampling of accounts, or its reading of gender performance.
