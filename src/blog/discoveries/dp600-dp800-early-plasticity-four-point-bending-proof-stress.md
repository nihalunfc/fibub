Every data sheet says a steel yields at one stress, and every designer below that stress treats the metal as perfectly springy. A 2026 paper in *Mechanics & Industry* put long sheet strips of three car-body steels into a large 4-point bending rig, loaded and unloaded them, and measured the strain that did not come back. Permanent strain showed up at **127 to 135 MPa in DP600**, a steel whose yield strength the same authors measure as 305 MPa. In other words, the first irreversible deformation starts at about **42 % of the yield strength**. This article rebuilds that elastoplastic transition with the [[stress-strain-analyser|stress-strain curve analyser]], shows what the paper's two measurements imply about the shape of the curve, and shows why an ordinary tensile machine cannot see the effect at all. For what it means downstream, take the yield strength into a design with the [[beam-calculator]] and look at repeated loading with the [[fatigue-sn-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors tested three sheet steels: **DP600** and **DP800** (dual-phase steels, the high-strength grades used in car bodies, 2 mm thick) and **DC01** (a plain cold-rolled mild steel, 3 mm thick). Strips 1200 mm long and 35 mm wide were bent in a 4-point rig with spans of 250 and 500 mm, so the middle section sees an almost constant bending moment. A laser profilometer measured the shape of the strip between the inner punches, which gives the curvature and so the strain, with a standard deviation of 7 x 10⁻⁸ strain over 20 repeated profile measurements.

The trick is the loading-unloading cycle. After each load step the strip is unloaded and its residual curvature measured. While the steel is truly elastic, the residual strain stays inside the noise band (the mean plus or minus three standard deviations). The first stress at which it steps out of that band is the **onset of irreversible strain**. In bending the authors find 127 to 135 MPa for DP600, 154 to 169 MPa for DP800 and 64 to 68 MPa for DC01: in every case 41 to 47 % of the yield strength they report from a normal tensile test (305, 360 and 158 MPa).

They also ran tensile tests with an infrared camera, looking for the moment the temperature rise stops following the stress (plastic work heats the metal). That method puts the onset much higher, at 345 to 396 MPa for DP600. The two methods disagree by a factor of nearly three, and the bending rig is the more sensitive of the two. The paper's practical message: the elastic limit is not a single number but a detection threshold, and the yield strength on a data sheet is only the stress at which the metal has already taken 0.2 % of permanent strain.

## Checked with the tools

The paper's measured curves are in its figures, so we did not have them. What we did instead is take the two stresses the paper reports for each sheet, **the onset and the yield strength**, and ask the tool's maths what single stress-strain curve passes through both. For the shape we used the Ramberg-Osgood form, the oldest and simplest description of a smooth elastoplastic transition:

**ε = σ / E + 0.002 (σ / R<sub>e</sub>)ⁿ**

Here ε is total strain, σ is stress, E is Young's modulus (**our assumption: 210 GPa** for all three sheets, the usual value for steel), R<sub>e</sub> is the paper's yield strength and n is the exponent that controls how sharply the curve bends. The second term is plastic strain, and by construction it equals 0.002 (0.2 %) exactly at R<sub>e</sub>, which is what a proof stress means.

**Step 1: the paper's two numbers fix the exponent.** The bending rig calls a strain irreversible when it leaves a three-sigma band, so its detection threshold is 3 x 7 x 10⁻⁸ = **2.1 x 10⁻⁷ of plastic strain** (**our reading** of the paper's method). Setting the plastic term equal to that at the measured onset gives n = ln(2.1 x 10⁻⁷ / 0.002) / ln(σ<sub>onset</sub> / R<sub>e</sub>). The answers are strikingly alike:

| Steel | Yield strength R<sub>e</sub> | Onset in bending | Onset / R<sub>e</sub> | Exponent n implied |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DP600 | 305 MPa | 127 to 135 MPa | 0.42 to 0.44 | 10.8 (10.5 to 11.2) |
| DP800 | 360 MPa | 154 to 169 MPa | 0.43 to 0.47 | 11.4 (10.8 to 12.1) |
| DC01 | 158 MPa | 64 to 68 MPa | 0.41 to 0.43 | 10.5 (10.1 to 10.9) |

Three very different steels, one shape. An exponent near 11 is a fairly gradual knee; sharply yielding metals run from 20 upwards. So the paper's finding is not three separate surprises but one property of a smooth transition: once the knee is that gentle, a strain resolution of 10⁻⁷ will always find plasticity at a little over 40 % of the proof stress.

**Step 2: the offset decides the answer.** Figure 1 plots the proof stress of each simulated curve against the offset strain used to measure it. The left-hand dots are the paper's bending onsets, the right-hand dots its yield strengths; they are the same curves read at two offsets five orders of magnitude apart.

**Step 3: what the Stress-strain curve analyser makes of such a curve.** We fed each simulated curve (501 points, stress and strain) to the tool's `analyse` function with the paper's own modulus fit range. For DP600, fitting between 20 and 150 MPa, it returns **E = 209.85 GPa** (r² 0.999999) against the 210 GPa we put in, and a 0.2 % proof stress of **305.0 MPa**, the paper's value back to four figures. Pushed down to the bending rig's offset of 2.1 x 10⁻⁷, the same tool reports **141.6 MPa** instead of the 131 MPa that is really there.

That 8 % error is the point. At a 0.2 % offset the proof stress hardly cares about the modulus fit; at 2.1 x 10⁻⁷ it is almost entirely modulus-fit error:

| Modulus fit range | Fitted E (DP600) | 0.2 % proof stress | Proof stress at 2.1 x 10⁻⁷ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 to 80 MPa | 210.0 GPa | 305.0 MPa | 131.0 MPa (right) |
| 40 to 120 MPa | 210.0 GPa | 305.0 MPa | 133.2 MPa |
| 20 to 150 MPa | 209.9 GPa | 305.0 MPa | 141.6 MPa |
| 60 to 200 MPa | 206.3 GPa | 305.2 MPa | 181.1 MPa |

A modulus wrong by 1.8 % moves the micro-offset proof stress by 38 %, while the 0.2 % value moves by 0.07 %. This is why the paper needed a dedicated rig: on a tensile curve the early-plasticity signal is smaller than the uncertainty of the straight line you measure it from. The bending method sidesteps the modulus completely, because it compares the shape of the strip before and after unloading.

**Step 4: the soft steel punishes a careless fit.** The paper fits the modulus of DP600 and DP800 between 20 and 150 MPa, but of DC01 only between 20 and 50 MPa. Figure 2 shows why, worked out by the tool: the fitted modulus against the top of the fit range. DC01 yields at 158 MPa, so a range reaching 150 MPa is already deep in the knee, and the tool returns **87.6 GPa** instead of 210 GPa, with a 0.2 % proof stress 4 MPa too high. The DP steels hold their modulus to 150 MPa and lose it above about 200. If you take one habit from this paper, take that one: scale the modulus range to the material's own yield strength, not to a fixed stress.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/dp600-dp800-early-plasticity-four-point-bending-proof-stress.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `analyse` from `src/lib/sci/tensile.ts`, the maths behind the [[stress-strain-analyser|stress-strain curve analyser]]: the least-squares modulus fit over a stress band, the offset (proof) yield strength found where the curve crosses the shifted elastic line, and the true-stress conversion.

From the paper: the yield strengths (305, 360, 158 MPa), the onsets in bending (127 to 135, 154 to 169, 64 to 68 MPa), the thermal-method onsets, the modulus fit ranges (20 to 150 and 20 to 50 MPa), the strain standard deviation of 7 x 10⁻⁸, the specimen sizes and the 250 mm span. **Our assumptions:** E = 210 GPa, the Ramberg-Osgood form of the transition, a detection threshold of three standard deviations of strain, and simulated curves that stop 25 % above the yield strength. The curves are **simulated**, not the paper's measurements, and the "tensile strength" and elongation the tool prints for them mean nothing, because the simulated test never reaches necking.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/stress-strain-analyser.mjs` on every change to fibub. It pins the analyser against NumPy and against curves whose answer is known:

- On the page's own made-up ductile-metal test, the modulus must come out as 199.7478 GPa, matching NumPy's `polyfit` over the same 13 points, and within 0.2 % of the 200 GPa used to build the curve.
- The 0.2 % proof stress of that curve must be 304.695 MPa at a strain of 0.0035254, the tensile strength 437.048 MPa and the toughness 105.6517 MJ/m³ (NumPy's trapezoid rule), with a Hollomon exponent of 0.15688.
- A straight line with a soft toe must be corrected to start at exactly 0.001 strain and return a modulus of 1000 MPa.
- New with this article: the 21-point DP600 curve printed below must give **E = 209.88 GPa**, a 0.2 % proof stress of **305.0 MPa** and, at an offset of 2.1 x 10⁻⁷, **140.3 MPa**, so the modulus-fit sensitivity shown above cannot drift unnoticed.

## Check it yourself

On the [[stress-strain-analyser|stress-strain curve analyser]]:

1. Set *Columns are* to **stress and strain**, *Stress unit* to **MPa** and *Strain unit* to **mm/mm (1 = 100 %)**.
2. Paste our simulated DP600 curve into *Your test data*, one pair per line: **0, 0** · **20, 0.0000952** · **40, 0.0001905** · **60, 0.0002857** · **80, 0.000381** · **100, 0.0004762** · **120, 0.0005715** · **140, 0.0006671** · **160, 0.0007637** · **180, 0.0008637** · **200, 0.000973** · **220, 0.0011056** · **240, 0.0012917** · **260, 0.0015925** · **280, 0.0021247** · **290, 0.0025387** · **300, 0.0031005** · **305, 0.0034524** · **310, 0.0038617** · **320, 0.0048894** · **330, 0.0062696**
3. Set *Modulus fit from (% of UTS)* to **6** and *to (% of UTS)* to **45**. That is the paper's own 20 to 150 MPa window, because the curve's highest stress is 330 MPa.
4. The headline line reads **E 209.9 GPa · yield 305 MPa · UTS 330 MPa**, with r² 1.00000 over 7 points from 20 to 140 MPa, and a yield-to-tensile ratio of 0.924.
5. Now put **0.000021** in *Yield offset (% strain)*: that is the bending rig's 2.1 x 10⁻⁷. The proof stress drops to about **140 MPa**, close to the paper's 127 to 135 MPa band but about 8 % high, because the modulus line is 0.06 % off.
6. Change the modulus window to **6** and **24** (20 to 80 MPa) and watch that number fall to **131 MPa**, while the 0.2 % value stays at 305 MPa. The close-up plot shows the offset line sliding along the elastic part.
7. Try *Yield offset* at **0.001** (a 10⁻⁵ offset): **185 MPa**. One curve, three published "elastic limits".

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report a proof stress with its offset**, not just a number. The analyser takes any offset, so the same curve can be read at 0.2 %, 0.05 % or 10⁻⁵ and the three values quoted together.
- **Choose the modulus window for the material.** Fit the modulus with the tool at several upper limits, as in Figure 2, and keep the range where the fitted E stops moving: for a 158 MPa steel that is about a third of the yield strength.
- **Judge whether a tensile frame can see your effect.** If a claimed elastic-limit shift is smaller than the proof-stress change you get by moving the modulus window, the claim needs a different measurement, as this paper's bending rig provides.
- **Turn an onset stress into a rig design** with the [[beam-calculator]]: for the paper's 35 x 2 mm strip on a 250 mm span, 127 MPa needs only 12 N of load, so load-cell resolution, not force, is the limit.
- **Feed fatigue models.** A steel that takes permanent strain at 42 % of yield is a steel whose high-cycle fatigue limit sits in the plastic range; the [[fatigue-sn-calculator]] shows how much life that costs.

## The fine print

- The curves here are **simulated** from two stresses per steel in the paper's text. They are not the paper's measurements, and the paper's own measured curves may bend differently.
- E = 210 GPa and the Ramberg-Osgood form are our assumptions. A different form with the same two anchor points would give the same picture but a different exponent.
- The detection threshold of 2.1 x 10⁻⁷ plastic strain is our reading of "mean plus three standard deviations" applied to the paper's strain standard deviation of 7 x 10⁻⁸. If their threshold was wider, the implied exponent falls a little.
- The paper reports a 0.2 % proof stress above its R<sub>e</sub> in a table we could not read; we anchored our curves on the R<sub>e</sub> values printed in the text, so our proof stresses are those.
- The paper's thermal-camera onsets (345 to 396 MPa for DP600) are far above the bending onsets. On our curves those stresses correspond to plastic strains of about 1 to 2 % (1.7 % for DP600 at 370 MPa), which is a different phenomenon entirely: the heat signal needs enough plastic work to warm the specimen.
- Early plasticity at low stress does not mean a part will fail there. It is a one-off, tiny permanent set, not progressive deformation.
