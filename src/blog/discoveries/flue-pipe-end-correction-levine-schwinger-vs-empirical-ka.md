A pipe sounds as if it were a little longer than it is. The air just outside an open end still moves with the sound wave inside, so the wave "reflects" a short distance beyond the end. That extra distance is the **end correction**, and every flute maker, organ builder and physics student who works out a pipe's pitch has to add it. For a plain round tube end, the textbook value is **0.6133 times the radius**, worked out by Levine and Schwinger in 1948 for low frequencies. Some experiments have suggested it shrinks as the frequency rises. A 2025 paper in *Acta Acustica* put a tiny pressure-and-velocity probe right at the end of flue pipes to test both ideas. Here we use the [[standing-waves]] calculator, which uses the 0.6133 value, to work out how much the choice matters for a pipe's pitch. To measure the notes of a real pipe, record it with the [[audio-spectrum-analyser]]; to hear a small tuning difference as beats, try [[doppler-beats]].

## The paper in brief

The team made two flue pipes, like recorder or organ pipes, each with a 3D-printed mouthpiece and fipple on a clear plastic tube: a round one 0.48 m long with a 10 mm inner radius, and a square one 0.36 m long with 10 mm sides. Blown by compressed air, the round pipe was played near **870 and 2056 Hz** and the square pipe near **474 and 875 Hz**. A small Microflown acoustic probe stepped in 0.127 mm steps along 22.86 mm across the open end, from just inside the pipe to about 2 cm outside.

They fitted the pressure pattern inside the pipe (a standing wave) and outside it (a spreading wave that falls off as 1/r) with two end corrections: the classical **δ = 0.6133a**, and an empirical one, **δ = a(0.6133 − 0.1168(ka)²)**, that falls with frequency. Here **ka** is the wavenumber k = 2πf/c times the radius a, the pipe's width compared with the wavelength. Across the tested range, ka from 0.049 to 0.377, both fitted almost equally well (fit errors within 5 × 10⁻⁴ of each other in three of four cases). In the fourth, the square pipe at 875 Hz, the classical model fitted clearly better. The probe itself shifted the notes by at most 2.5 Hz, too little to matter.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: how far apart are the two models?** We worked out ka and both end corrections for the four tests. This is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated sizes, tones and speed of sound:

| Test | ka | Classical δ (tool) | Empirical δ | Difference | Pitch difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Round, 870 Hz | 0.159 | 6.133 mm | 6.103 mm | 0.48 % | 0.21 cents |
| Round, 2056 Hz | 0.377 | 6.133 mm | 5.967 mm | 2.70 % | 1.17 cents |
| Square, 474 Hz | 0.049 | 3.460 mm | 3.459 mm | 0.05 % | 0.01 cents |
| Square, 875 Hz | 0.090 | 3.460 mm | 3.455 mm | 0.16 % | 0.05 cents |

The largest gap, 0.17 mm at 2056 Hz, is the paper's "under 3 %". Figure 1 shows why: the empirical curve only bends away from 0.6133 as ka grows, and the tests sit mostly at small ka.

**Step 2: what it does to the note.** A **cent** is a hundredth of a semitone; most listeners need a few cents' difference before they hear two tones as out of tune. Using the calculator's resonance formula, f = nc / 2(L + 2δ) for a pipe open at both ends, and swapping in the empirical correction, the pitch rises by at most **1.17 cents** in the paper's pipes (the table's last column). That is why the two models fitted equally well: in narrow pipes they barely differ. For a wide pipe the story changes. In a 50 mm radius pipe of the same length (**our example**), the gap reaches **3.37 cents** at 328 Hz and **5.32 cents** at ka 0.377 (412 Hz), around the point where a careful listener could hear it (Figure 2).

For scale, the calculator shows that swapping the plain tube end for a **flanged** one (0.8216a, an end set in a wide flat face) lowers the round pipe's note by **14.6 cents**. How the end is shaped matters far more than which of the two plain-end models you pick.

**Step 3: a tube is not the whole instrument.** The plain 0.36 m square tube with its equivalent 5.64 mm radius should sound at **467.4 Hz** with c = 343 m/s, against the paper's 474 Hz; turned round, the calculator says 474 Hz needs a tube 354.9 mm long. The round pipe's tones do not fall on the plain-tube harmonic ladder (348.4 Hz apart) at all. The mouthpiece and fipple, which the calculator does not model, add their own length and resonance. That is why the paper tested the end correction at the open end only, where the plain-tube theory applies.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/flue-pipe-end-correction-levine-schwinger-vs-empirical-ka.mjs` in fibub's public code. It uses `END_FACTOR` (0.6133 for a plain end, 0.8216 flanged), `pipeResonance`, `effLength` and `lengthFor` from `src/lib/sci/phstand.ts`, and `soundSpeed` from `src/lib/sci/phair.ts`, the maths behind the [[standing-waves]] calculator. The empirical end correction is the paper's comparison formula, written into the script; to get its pitch we pass the calculator a radius scaled so its 0.6133 factor gives the empirical length.

The pipe sizes, tones, speed of sound and the tested ka range come from the paper's text. The 50 mm radius pipe and the 5-cent hearing threshold are **our examples**.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/standing-waves.mjs`, which pins the calculator against exact formulas evaluated with SymPy:

- A 0.6 m pipe open at both ends with a 9.5 mm radius and plain ends must have an acoustic length of 611.65 mm and a fundamental of 280.56 Hz.
- A 0.3 m pipe closed at one end with a 10 mm radius and a flanged end must give 278.39 Hz, and its second resonance must be the third harmonic.
- The length of a pipe closed at one end that sounds A4 (440 Hz) with a 10 mm radius must be 188.9 mm.
- New with this article: the paper's square pipe (0.36 m, equivalent radius √(10⁻⁴/π) m, c = 343 m/s) must give 467.4 Hz, and the round pipe's acoustic length must be 492.27 mm (0.48 m plus 2 × 6.133 mm).

## Check it yourself

On the [[standing-waves]] calculator:

1. Set *System* to **Air in a pipe** and keep *I want to find* on **The notes it plays**.
2. In the *Pipe* card choose *Ends* **Open at both ends (flute)**, type *Length (mm)* **480**, *Inside diameter (mm)* **20**, keep *Open ends are* on **Plain tube ends (×0.61 radius)** and *Air temperature (°C)* **20**.
3. The *Fundamental* reads **348.61 Hz**, with *Acoustic length* **492.3 mm** (plus 6.13 mm at each end) and *Speed of sound* **343.2 m/s**.
4. Switch *Open ends are* to **In a wide flat face (×0.82 radius)**: the fundamental drops to **345.68 Hz**, the 14.6-cent change from the flanged end.
5. Back on plain ends, type *Length (mm)* **360** and *Inside diameter (mm)* **11.28** (the square pipe's equivalent): the fundamental is **467.70 Hz** at 20 °C, a little below the 474 Hz the paper's blown pipe played.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Decide whether an end-correction model matters** for your pipe: work out ka at your highest note and see how far the pitch moves, with the [[standing-waves]] calculator.
- **Separate the tube from the mouthpiece**: compare a measured tone with the plain-tube prediction, then use *What gives a target note* to find the equivalent length the mouthpiece adds.
- **Design pipes and resonators** for a target note, with plain or flanged ends, then check the build by recording it with the [[audio-spectrum-analyser]].
- **Teach end corrections**: show students that the effect is millimetres, the model choice tenths of a millimetre, and the end's shape much more.
- **Tune by ear**: play the predicted and the measured note together and count the beats with [[doppler-beats]].

## The fine print

- The empirical formula is meant for small ka (well under 1). We stop our curves at ka = 0.5.
- Both models describe a plain, thin-walled, unflanged end. Thick walls, chamfers or a nearby surface change the correction.
- The paper compared fits to the sound field at the end, not the pitches of the pipes. Our cents are a translation of its end-correction gap into pitch, using the calculator's simple open–open model.
- We treat the square pipe with its equivalent radius, as the paper does; a square end radiates a little differently from a round one, which may be why the classical model fitted better there.
