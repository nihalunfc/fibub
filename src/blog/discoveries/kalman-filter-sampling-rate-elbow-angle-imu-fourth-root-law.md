A wearable that has to last a day on a 100 mAh battery spends most of its energy reading its sensor and doing arithmetic. Read the sensor ten times less often and you save roughly ten times the energy — but how much accuracy does that cost? A 2025 paper in the *Journal of Low Power Electronics and Applications* answers it for a single inertial device strapped to the forearm, estimating elbow angle for home rehabilitation: dropping from **100 Hz to 10 Hz raised the error by a factor of 1.8**. That number is not an accident of their hardware. This article uses the [[kalman-filter-playground]] to show that 1.8 is almost exactly the ceiling for any filter of this kind, and where the ceiling comes from. For the 2 Hz low-pass in front of it see the [[digital-filter-designer]], and for combining the error terms, the [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors put one SensorTile module — an LSM303AGR accelerometer, an LSM6DSL gyroscope and an STM32L476 Cortex-M4 — on the forearm and compared five ways of getting the elbow angle out of it: the accelerometer alone, raw gyroscope integration, a bias-corrected gyroscope, a complementary filter and a Kalman filter. The signals are low-pass filtered at 2 Hz, since rehabilitation movements are slow.

Their results are about what a cheap single-device setup can do: **RMSE between 1.83° and 5.52° at 100 Hz**, depending on the test configuration and how fast the arm moves. The lightest algorithm costs 58 processor cycles, and the heavier fusion algorithms at least twice that. The engineering conclusion is the trade-off: cutting the data rate from 100 Hz to 10 Hz **cuts sensor and computation power by about ten** and **multiplies the error by about 1.8**.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **simulation and a steady-state calculation**, not a reanalysis of the paper's recordings. The point is to test whether the paper's 1.8 is the expected size, and what sets it.

**Step 1: the fourth-root law.** Take the simplest sensible model of a slowly moving joint: the true angle wanders like a random walk of variance q per second, and every sample measures it with noise of variance R. The tool's `steadyGainConstant` solves the steady-state Kalman equations for a sample interval Δt. When the per-step wandering is small compared with the sensor noise — qΔt ≪ R, true for a slow joint and a 100 Hz sensor — the answer simplifies to

**error ≈ (q Δt R)^(1/4)**

so the error falls as the **fourth root** of the data rate. Ten times more samples buys 10^(1/4) = **1.778 times** less error, no more. That is the paper's 1.8.

**Step 2: putting the paper's numbers in.** Take the sensor noise on one accelerometer-derived angle sample as **8°** (**our assumption**: the paper gives filtered errors, not raw sample noise). Solving for the process noise that puts the 100 Hz steady-state error at the paper's best figure of 1.83° gives **q = 18.5 deg² per second**, that is an angle that wanders by about 4.3° in a second — a slow, deliberate rehabilitation movement. At that setting the tool's exact steady state, with no small-qΔt approximation, gives:

| | Paper | Tool (steady state) |
|---|---|---|
| Error at 100 Hz | 1.83° (best case) | 1.830° (fitted) |
| Error at 10 Hz | 1.8 × the 100 Hz value | 3.161°, that is **1.727 ×** |
| Ceiling for a tenfold rate cut | — | 10^(1/4) = **1.778 ×** |

**Step 3: the same thing from a simulation.** Running the real filter rather than the formula, with the tool's seeded simulator over one minute of data (6001 samples at 100 Hz, 601 at 10 Hz) and the filter's Q and R matched to the truth, the measured RMSE averaged over 20 seeds is **1.85° at 100 Hz** and **3.158° at 10 Hz**, a ratio of **1.707**. The raw measurements have an RMS error of about 8° at either rate, so the filter is doing the work: at 100 Hz it is averaging roughly 20 samples' worth of noise.

**Step 4: why 1.8 is a ceiling, not a coincidence.** The fourth-root law only holds while the sensor noise dominates. Figure 2 sweeps the process noise q across six orders of magnitude and shows the penalty for a tenfold rate cut. It rises towards 1.778 for a slow-moving joint and falls towards **1.0** for a fast one, because a filter tracking a fast-wandering angle has to believe each new sample anyway and gains little from having more of them. There is no setting in which cutting the rate tenfold costs more than 1.778 times the error.

That makes the paper's measured 1.8 informative in a way its authors do not claim: it says their error at 100 Hz is almost entirely **sensor noise being averaged away**, not drift being tracked. If their error had been dominated by fast arm motion or gyroscope bias, the same rate cut would have cost much less than 1.8, and 10 Hz would have been nearly free. The flip side is that buying accuracy by sampling faster is expensive: going from 10 Hz to 100 Hz costs ten times the power for 1.8 times less error, and reaching 1° would need about 600 Hz.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/kalman-filter-sampling-rate-elbow-angle-imu-fourth-root-law.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `steadyGainConstant`, `simulate`, `kalman` and `summary` from `src/lib/sci/kalman.ts`, the maths behind the [[kalman-filter-playground]].

From the paper: the two data rates (100 Hz and 10 Hz), the 1.8 error factor, the tenfold power saving and the 1.83° best-case RMSE. **Our assumptions**: the constant-level (random-walk) model, 8° of noise on one angle sample, the process noise q = 18.5 deg²/s solved from the paper's 1.83°, a one-minute record, and the filter's Q and R set to the true values. The paper's own recordings are not used; its rate-against-error curve is in a figure, not in its text.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/kalman-filter-playground.mjs`, which pins the filter against an independent NumPy implementation written with full matrices:

- On 40 made-up measurements at uneven times, every state, variance, innovation and innovation variance must match NumPy to 1 part in 10¹⁰, for the constant-level model and two constant-velocity settings.
- The steady-state gain must match NumPy's iteration of the Riccati equation to 1 part in 10¹², and a long run of the filter must settle onto it.
- With Q and R matched to the simulated truth, the mean normalised innovation squared must come out near 1 and the estimate must beat the raw measurements.
- New with this article: for 8° sample noise and q = 18.491 deg²/s, the steady-state error must be 1.830° at a 10 ms step and 3.161° at a 100 ms step, and the ratio of the two must stay below 10^(1/4) for every process noise from 0.01 to 10 000 deg²/s.

## Check it yourself

On the [[kalman-filter-playground]] page:

1. In *Measurements*, leave *Data* on **Simulate a noisy sensor** and *Model* on **Constant level (random walk)**.
2. Set *Steps* to **6001**, *Time step Δt (s)* to **0.01**, *True process noise q* to **18.491**, *Sensor noise σ* to **8**, *Start value* to **90** and *Random seed* to **7**.
3. Press *Set Q and R to the true values* so the filter is honest about the data it was given.
4. In *Result*, read the RMS error of the estimate: about **1.95°**, against about **8.07°** for the raw measurements, with the normalised innovation squared near **1.0**.
5. Now set *Steps* to **601** and *Time step Δt (s)* to **0.1**, keeping everything else, and press *Set Q and R to the true values* again. The estimate's RMS error rises to about **3.10°** — the same one minute of arm movement, a tenth of the samples, about 1.6 times the error on this seed.
6. Press *New noise* a few times at each setting. Single runs wander by a few tenths of a degree; the average over many seeds settles near 1.85° and 3.16°.
7. Raise *True process noise q* to **5000** at both rates and repeat. The gap between the two rates nearly closes: a fast-wandering angle does not reward a fast sensor.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Choose a sampling rate before building the hardware**: the fourth-root law turns an accuracy target into a rate, and shows when a faster sensor is not worth the battery.
- **Sanity-check a reported trade-off**: if a paper claims a tenfold rate cut cost 3 times the error, something other than this filter is going on — aliasing, a gyroscope integration drift, or a mismatched Q.
- **Diagnose what dominates your error**: measure the error at two rates; a ratio near 10^(1/4) means sensor noise, a ratio near 1 means real motion or bias.
- **Tune Q and R honestly** using the normalised innovation squared, which should sit near 1; a value far from 1 means the filter's noise settings do not match the data.
- **Teach the estimation trade-off** with one slider, which is hard to do with equations alone.

## The fine print

- The paper's 1.8 is a measurement across its test configurations; our 1.73 and 1.71 are a steady-state calculation and a simulation for a model we chose. They agree in size, which is the claim, not to the last digit.
- A real elbow is not a random walk, and a real inertial device adds gyroscope bias, axis misalignment and soft-tissue movement. The fourth-root law describes the noise-averaging part of the error only.
- The paper low-pass filters at 2 Hz. Sampling at 10 Hz is still above the Nyquist rate for that, so aliasing should be small, but it is another reason a measured factor can exceed the filter's own ceiling.
- The 8° sample noise is our assumption. A different value changes q but not the ratio, which is what this article is about.
- Power scaling with data rate is rarely exactly tenfold: radio, housekeeping and leakage do not scale with sampling.
