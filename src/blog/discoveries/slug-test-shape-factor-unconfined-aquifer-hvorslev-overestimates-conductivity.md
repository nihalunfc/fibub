A **slug test** is the quickest way to measure how easily water moves through the ground around a well: drop a weight (the slug) into the well, or pull it out, and time how fast the water level returns. Turning that time into **hydraulic conductivity** K needs one number that depends only on the shape of the well screen and the aquifer, the **shape factor**. A 2024 paper in *Water* solves the flow around a partly penetrating screen in an unconfined aquifer more carefully than the classic formulas do and finds that Hvorslev's shape factor, used by many field hydrologists, is **about 10 % too large**. This article recomputes Hvorslev's value with the [[slug-test-analyser]] and shows how the paper's value can be used in the same tool. For the bigger picture, compare with a pumping test in the [[pump-test-analyser]] and turn K into a flow with the [[darcy-flow-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

All slug-test formulas share one form: K = rc² × S ÷ (2 L T0), where rc is the radius of the casing where the water level moves, L the screen length, T0 the **basic time lag** (the time for the displacement to fall to 37 % of its start) and S the shape factor, written as ln(Re/rw), the logarithm of an "effective radius" over the screen radius. The methods differ only in S.

Hvorslev (1951) took the flow into the screen to be uniform along its length. Bouwer and Rice (1976) used an electrical model to give S from charts. The author instead lets the casing carry no flow and holds the head fixed along the screen, so the water enters faster near the screen ends, as it really does, and solves this mixed boundary problem in series form.

The headline result: for a 0.1 m radius screen in the middle of a 100 m thick aquifer, Hvorslev gives **4.61** for a 10 m screen and **3.00** for a 2 m screen, while the new solution gives **4.21** and **2.71**. Because K is proportional to S, Hvorslev's K comes out about 10 % high. The new values agree with the Zlotnik et al. method to about 3 % (except near the water table, about 30 %) and can differ from Bouwer and Rice by 20 % or more.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: Hvorslev's shape factor.** The [[slug-test-analyser]] uses Hvorslev's formula in the form S = ln(L/R), valid when the screen is more than about eight times longer than its radius. For R = 0.1 m it gives **4.605** for a 10 m screen and **2.996** for a 2 m screen: the paper's 4.61 and 3.00, exactly. Figure 1 shows the curve for screens from 1 to 100 m (2.30 to 6.91) with the paper's two values below it.

**Step 2: how big is the error in K?** Since K scales with S, the ratio of the shape factors is the ratio of the conductivities. Hvorslev over the paper's solution is 4.605 ÷ 4.21 = **1.094** for the 10 m screen and 2.996 ÷ 2.71 = **1.105** for the 2 m screen: Hvorslev's K is **9 % and 11 % too high**. The author's earlier predictor (4.31 and 2.74) is only 2.4 % and 1.1 % above the new values.

**Step 3: a simulated slug test.** To see this in practice we **simulated** a test (our numbers, not the paper's): a 0.05 m casing over the paper's 0.1 m by 10 m screen, a true K of 1 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, a 0.5 m start and 2 mm of reading scatter. With the paper's shape factor of 4.21, that K gives a basic time lag of 52.63 s. The analyser fits the straight part of the log recovery (H/H0 from 0.9 to 0.1, 20 readings, r² 0.9985) and finds **T0 = 52.51 s** (Figure 2). Its Hvorslev result is then **K = 1.096 × 10⁻⁵ m/s**, 9.6 % above the true value. Typing the paper's 4.21 into the tool's Bouwer-Rice box gives **1.002 × 10⁻⁵ m/s**, within 0.2 %. For the 2 m screen the same exercise gives 1.098 × 10⁻⁵ (Hvorslev) and 0.993 × 10⁻⁵ m/s (paper's factor).

A 10 % error is small next to the factor-of-two spread usual between slug and pumping tests, but it is a **bias**, always in the same direction, so it does not average out over many wells.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/slug-test-shape-factor-unconfined-aquifer-hvorslev-overestimates-conductivity.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitRecovery` (the straight-line fit of ln(H/H0) against time), `hvorslev` and `bouwerRice` from `src/lib/sci/slug.ts`, the maths behind the [[slug-test-analyser]].

The screen radius, screen lengths and the four shape factors come from the paper's text. The casing radius (0.05 m), the true K (1 × 10⁻⁵ m/s), the 0.5 m start, the reading times and the 2 mm scatter are **our assumptions**. The recovery data are **simulated**, not field data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs the slug test checks:

- A made-up recovery with a known time lag must give back **T0 = 70 s** exactly, and the fitted line must start at H/H0 = 1.
- Bouwer-Rice must equal Hvorslev when ln(Re/R) is set to ln(Le/R), and the effective casing radius for a filter pack must follow rc² = (1 − n) rc² + n R².
- New with this article (`tests/sci-tools/slug-test-analyser.mjs`): ln(Le/R) must give 4.605 and 2.996 for the paper's screens, and the simulated 10 m test must give T0 = 52.51 s, Hvorslev K = 1.096 × 10⁻⁵ m/s and K = 1.002 × 10⁻⁵ m/s with ln(Re/R) = 4.21.

## Check it yourself

On the [[slug-test-analyser]]:

1. Keep *Lengths in* **metres** and *Times in* **seconds**. Type **0.05** for *Casing radius rc*, **0.1** for *Screen or borehole radius R* and **10** for *Screen length Le*. Leave *Filter pack porosity* blank.
2. Clear the data box and paste our simulated readings, one pair per line: **0, 0.5**; **6, 0.4481**; **12, 0.4018**; **18, 0.3573**; **24, 0.3151**; **30, 0.2829**; **36, 0.2486**; **42, 0.2251**; **48, 0.2009**; **54, 0.1775**; **60, 0.1589**; **66, 0.1444**; **72, 0.1294**; **78, 0.1137**; **84, 0.106**; **90, 0.0926**; **96, 0.0745**; **102, 0.0685**; **108, 0.0647**; **114, 0.0579**; **120, 0.0522**.
3. Keep *Second column holds* on **displacement from the static level** and the fit window at **0.1** to **0.9**.
4. *Basic time lag T0* reads **52.5 s** and the big line **K = 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ m/s (0.947 m/day), Hvorslev**; the *Hvorslev* line notes Le/R = 100.
5. Type **4.21**, the paper's shape factor, in *Bouwer-Rice ln(Re/R)*. The *Bouwer-Rice* line reads **K = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ m/s (0.866 m/day)**: the 9 % bias is gone.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Correct old slug-test results**: rerun archived time lags in the [[slug-test-analyser]] with a better shape factor in the Bouwer-Rice box, and see how K changes.
- **Compare methods on one test**: Hvorslev, Bouwer-Rice and your own shape factor side by side, from the same fitted time lag.
- **Plan a test**: simulate a recovery for the K you expect and choose a reading interval that puts enough points between H/H0 of 0.9 and 0.1.
- **Set slug-test K beside a pumping test**: fit the drawdown in the [[pump-test-analyser]] and see whether the two agree within the usual factor of two.
- **Teach where the formulas come from**: the shape factor is the only thing that differs, so students can see each method's assumption in one number.

## The fine print

- We checked the Hvorslev values and the size of the bias; we did not re-solve the paper's mixed boundary problem, so its 4.21 and 2.71 are taken as given.
- The tool's Hvorslev formula is the long-screen form ln(L/R); for screens shorter than about eight radii it is only rough, and the tool says so.
- The paper assumes a uniform aquifer with negligible storage; layered or anisotropic ground changes the shape factor too.
- Near the water table the paper's values differ more from other methods (about 30 % from Zlotnik et al.).
- Our slug test is simulated; real tests often start with a fast drop as the filter pack drains, which the fit window should skip.
