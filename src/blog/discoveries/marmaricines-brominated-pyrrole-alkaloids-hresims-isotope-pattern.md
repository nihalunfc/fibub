Marine sponges are among the richest sources of new antibiotic leads, and sponges of the genus *Agelas* are famous for **bromopyrrole alkaloids**, small nitrogen compounds that carry one or two bromine atoms on a pyrrole ring. A 2025 paper in *Marine Drugs* describes three of them, **marmaricines A, B and C**, from a Red Sea *Agelas* sponge, and finds two that stop methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) at 8 µg/mL. The first step in naming any new compound is its **molecular formula**, read from a high-resolution mass spectrum. This article shows how the [[isotope-pattern-calculator]] and the [[unsaturation-calculator]] check those formulas from the numbers in the paper. When you go on to make or modify such compounds, the [[reaction-equivalents]] table works out the amounts.

## The paper in brief

The authors extracted the sponge, separated the extract by chromatography and solved the structures with NMR spectroscopy and **high-resolution electrospray mass spectrometry (HRESIMS)**, which measures the mass of an ion to a few parts per million (ppm). All three compounds were seen as sodium adducts, written [M+Na]⁺: the molecule plus a sodium ion.

- **Marmaricine A**, C₁₀H₁₂Br₂N₂O₃, found at m/z 388.9111, with three peaks in a 1:2:1 pattern, the signature of two bromines. It is new from nature.
- **Marmaricine B**, C₁₀H₁₃BrN₂O₅, found at m/z 342.9906, with two equal peaks: one bromine. It shows no optical rotation, so it is likely a 50:50 mix of mirror-image forms.
- **Marmaricine C**, C₁₃H₁₄Br₂N₄O₃, found at m/z 454.9327, again 1:2:1, with eight **degrees of unsaturation** (rings plus double bonds).

In tests, A and B stopped MRSA at 8 µg/mL (killing it at 16), C at 16 µg/mL; B and C also stopped the yeast *Candida albicans* at 8 µg/mL, and none acted on *E. coli*.

## Checked with the tools

These are **recomputations** from the formulas and masses stated in the paper. Nothing here is the paper's spectra.

**Step 1: the exact masses.** The Isotope pattern calculator builds each sodium adduct from the neutral formula, adds the exact masses of the lightest isotopes and takes off the mass of the missing electron. The paper's calculated values agree with the tool's to within 0.1 mDa, and the measured masses sit 0.5 to 1.7 ppm from the tool's values, well inside the usual 5 ppm limit for a formula assignment.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marmaricine A | 388.9111 / 388.9106 | 388.9107 | 1.06 | 5 |
| Marmaricine B | 342.9906 / 342.9900 | 342.9900 | 1.74 | 5 |
| Marmaricine C | 454.9327 / 454.9324 | 454.9325 | 0.47 | 8 (paper: eight) |

**Step 2: the bromine clusters.** Bromine has two isotopes, ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br, in almost equal amounts, so each bromine splits a peak in two, 2 mass units apart. The tool's full isotope calculation gives, for marmaricine A, peaks at M, M+2 and M+4 of **51.1 : 100 : 49.6**, the "1:2:1" the paper describes; for marmaricine C, 50.9 : 100 : 50.0; and for marmaricine B, **100 : 99.0**, the 1:1 of one bromine (Figure 1). The small peaks at M+1, M+3 and M+5 come from carbon-13: 6.0 % of the tallest for A's ten carbons, 8.0 % for C's thirteen.

**Step 3: how unique is the formula?** A mass alone fits more than one formula. We searched every formula made of 1 to 30 carbons, up to 8 nitrogens, up to 12 oxygens, hydrogen and exactly two bromines (the number the cluster fixes), as a sodium adduct with a whole number of rings plus double bonds. Only **3** such formulas lie within 10 ppm of 388.9111, and only **one**, the paper's C₁₀H₁₂Br₂N₂O₃, lies within 5 ppm (Figure 2). The other two, C₆H₈Br₂N₈O at +8.0 ppm and C₁₅H₁₂Br₂O at −9.3 ppm, are well outside the instrument's accuracy. So the cluster pattern and the exact mass together pin down marmaricine A's formula within this element set.

**Step 4: ring and double-bond counts.** The Unsaturation calculator gives 5 rings plus double bonds for A and B and 8 for C. The pyrrole ring that all three share accounts for 3 of these (one ring and two C=C bonds), so A and B each have two more rings or double bonds, such as C=O groups, and C has five more. For C, eight matches the value the paper states; which bonds they are comes from the paper's NMR data.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/marmaricines-brominated-pyrrole-alkaloids-hresims-isotope-pattern.mjs` imports `parseFormula`, `adductFormula`, `monoMass`, `isotopePattern`, `clusterPattern` and `ppm` from `src/lib/sci/chem.ts`, the maths behind the Isotope pattern calculator, and `dbeOf` from `src/lib/sci/dbe.ts`, the maths behind the Unsaturation calculator. It prints every number in this article and draws Figure 1 (the three clusters) and Figure 2 (the formula search, each candidate's mass computed with the same `monoMass`).

Inputs from the paper: the three formulas, the sodium adduct and the measured and calculated m/z values. Our choices: the element set and ranges of the formula search (C, H, N, O and two Br; C up to 30, N up to 8, O up to 12), and isotope peaks merged by whole mass number, as a time-of-flight instrument sees them.

- **New with this article** (`tests/sci-tools/isotope-pattern-calculator.mjs`): marmaricine A's [M+Na]⁺ is 388.9107, B's is 342.9900 with the paper's 1.74 ppm error, and two bromines give 51 : 100 : 50.

3. In **Compare with your spectrum**, type `388.9111 51` on one line and `390.9087 100` on the next. The first row shows an error of **1.1 ppm**.
4. Change the formula to C10H13BrN2O5: the headline becomes **m/z 342.9900**, and the plot shows two peaks of nearly equal height.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Checking a new formula before submission.** Type the formula and ion into the Isotope pattern calculator: the calculated m/z, the ppm error and the cluster shape take seconds, and a mismatch in any of them flags a typo or a wrong adduct.
- **Counting halogens from a cluster.** Compare a measured cluster with the calculated ones for one, two or three bromines or chlorines in the compare box.
- **Picking the ion to report.** The "Every common ion" table lists [M+H]⁺, [M+Na]⁺, [M+K]⁺ and negative ions at once, which helps decide what a peak is.
- **Sanity-checking structures.** The Unsaturation calculator gives rings plus double bonds and lists the combinations of rings, double and triple bonds that add up to it, a quick filter on proposed structures.
- **Teaching.** Bromine's near 1:1 isotopes make the clearest classroom example of an isotope pattern.

## The fine print

- We checked the formulas' arithmetic and how unique they are within one element set. The structures themselves rest on the paper's NMR data, which we did not re-examine.
- The formula search assumed no sulfur, phosphorus or other elements, and an even-electron sodium adduct. Allowing more elements would give more candidates; the bromine cluster and the NMR data rule most of them out.
- Measured cluster heights depend on the instrument and on peak overlap, so a real "1:2:1" will rarely be exactly 51:100:50.
- The antibacterial results are from lab tests on microbes. They are early research, not a medicine.
