A child on chemotherapy is fed to a number, and that number usually comes from an equation written for healthy adults a century ago. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Nutrition* measured resting energy expenditure (REE) by indirect calorimetry in 203 children and teenagers with cancer, compared five published equations and built two of its own. Its headline is that the old equations run high: Harris-Benedict by 134 kcal a day, Schofield by 185. This article shows that the same gap falls out of the cohort's average height, weight and age with the [[energy-needs]] calculator, and what that means when you read any equation-versus-calorimetry paper. For the agreement statistics behind such comparisons, see [[bland-altman]]; for the spread around a single prediction, [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors studied children aged 6 to under 18 at a paediatric oncology referral hospital: 226 evaluated, 203 analysed, 57.6 % boys, with solid tumours (68.5 %), leukaemias (20.2 %) and brain tumours (11.3 %). On average they were 12.2 ± 3 years old, 45.7 ± 16.6 kg and 147.6 ± 23.6 cm, with a normal BMI-for-age z-score of 0.64 ± 1.5. REE was measured with a mask-and-calorimeter system, lying still for 10 minutes beforehand and recording for 20 minutes, with the usual stability criteria on the respiratory quotient.

Measured REE averaged **1,200 ± 306 kcal/day** (the paper's Table 1 gives 1,180 ± 319 for the same cohort and does not explain the difference). Every published equation predicted more: Harris-Benedict 1,334, FAO/WHO 1,379, Schofield 1,385, Oxford 1,311 — biases of −110 to −185 kcal/day, where a negative bias means the equation reads high. The limits of agreement were wide in every case, several hundred kcal either way. The authors then fitted two new equations, INP-simple and INP-Morpho, which cut the mean absolute error from about 196 to 161–169 kcal/day and include an offset for the diagnosis.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from the cohort's published mean height, weight, age and sex split**. We have no individual data, so we feed the averages to the equations; the limits of that short cut are in The fine print.

**Step 1: Harris-Benedict, from three averages.** Typing the cohort means into the [[energy-needs]] calculator (45.7 kg, 147.6 cm, 12.2 years) gives Harris-Benedict **1,351.0 kcal/day for a boy** and **1,308.1 for a girl**. Weighted by the paper's 57.6 % boys, that is **1,332.8 kcal/day**, against the paper's reported mean of **1,334 ± 269**. The two agree to 1.2 kcal, or 0.1 %. The bias follows: 1,200 − 1,332.8 = **−132.8 kcal/day**, against the paper's **−133.6**.

So the paper's headline overestimate is not a property of these children at all. It is what the 1919 equation says for a body of that size, and any reader with the three averages could have predicted it.

Roza-Shizgal gives 1,312.1, barely better than the 1919 original. Figure 1 shows all three against body weight, with the measured mean marked.

**Step 3: where Harris-Benedict goes wrong.** The 1919 equation was fitted to adults, and its female version leans much harder on height and much less on weight than its male version. At this body size the two versions nearly meet: 1,351 for a boy against 1,308 for a girl, a gap of 43 kcal/day. Mifflin-St Jeor keeps a 166 kcal/day gap (1,323.5 against 1,157.5) at the same height, weight and age. Figure 2 shows the four lines. The practical consequence is that Harris-Benedict reads high for girls of this size, and a cohort that is 42 % girls carries that error into its mean.

**Step 4: the new equation, and a coding question answered.** The paper's INP-simple equation is published in full, so we can run it too: 11 × weight + 22 × age − 3 × height, plus a diagnosis offset and 640 for girls or 520 for boys. With the cohort means and the diagnosis mix (68.5 % solid at +195, 20.2 % leukaemia at +219, 11.3 % brain at +95, a weighted offset of 188.5) it gives **1,087.7 kcal/day**, against the published **1,086 ± 196**. That settles an ambiguity in the paper: its methods describe leukaemia as the reference category, which would make its offset zero, but Table 3 prints +219. With the printed +219 the mean lands on the published value; with a zero offset for leukaemia it would be 1,043.5, some 42 kcal below. Use the table.

It also shows how the new equation earns its accuracy: it predicts **1,088** where measurement gives **1,200**, so it is biased low: the paper reports +114.8 kcal/day, and our recomputation from the means gives +112.3. It wins on *mean absolute error*, not on bias. For an individual child that trade is worth stating out loud.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/paediatric-oncology-resting-energy-expenditure-harris-benedict-bias.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `mifflin`, `harrisBenedict` and `rozaShizgal` from `src/lib/sci/energy.ts`, the maths behind the [[energy-needs]] calculator, and implements the paper's own INP-simple formula from the coefficients printed in its Table 3.

The inputs are the cohort's published means (45.7 kg, 147.6 cm, 12.2 years), the 57.6 % / 42.4 % sex split, the diagnosis mix and the measured REE of 1,200 kcal/day from the paper's Table 4. Weighting the two sex-specific predictions by the published percentages is **our method**, since we have no individual data; the weight range of 25 to 75 kg in the figures is **our choice**, roughly the cohort mean ± 1.8 standard deviations.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/energy-needs.mjs`, which pins each equation against an independent Python implementation and, for the first case, hand arithmetic:

- A 35-year-old woman of 65 kg and 165 cm must give Mifflin-St Jeor 1,345.25, Harris-Benedict 1,418.2545, Roza-Shizgal 1,408.268 and Katch-McArdle 1,352.8 kcal/day at 30 % body fat.
- A 40-year-old man of 80 kg and 180 cm must give 1,730, 1,796.995, 1,796.862 and 1,752.4; two further cases cover a 19-year-old man and a 67-year-old woman.
- The activity factor must simply multiply (1,345.25 × 1.55 = 2,085.1375), Katch-McArdle must refuse to answer without a body-fat percentage, and impossible inputs must be rejected.
- New with this article: the cohort means 45.7 kg, 147.6 cm and 12.2 years must give Harris-Benedict 1,350.9972 for a male and 1,308.1015 for a female, a 57.6 / 42.4 weighted mean of 1,332.81 kcal/day, and Mifflin-St Jeor 1,323.5 and 1,157.5.

## Check it yourself

On the [[energy-needs]] calculator, in *The person* card:

1. Set *Sex used by the equations* to **Male**, *Age (years)* to **12.2**, *Weight* to **45.7** kg and *Height* to **147.6** cm.
2. Leave *Activity factor* at **1** (so the results are resting energy, not a daily total) and *Show energy in* on **kcal per day**.
3. *Results* reads Mifflin-St Jeor **1,324**, Harris-Benedict (1919) **1,351**, Roza-Shizgal (1984) **1,340**. Katch-McArdle stays blank until you give a body-fat percentage.
4. Switch *Sex used by the equations* to **Female**: Mifflin-St Jeor falls to **1,158** but Harris-Benedict only to **1,308**. That 1919 equation barely distinguishes a girl from a boy of this size.
5. Average the two Harris-Benedict numbers in the paper's proportions (0.576 × 1,351 + 0.424 × 1,308.1) and you have **1,333**, the paper's 1,334.
6. For the daily energy of one patient, set *Activity factor* to the stress or activity factor your protocol uses and read the total; the tool shows the weight-change arithmetic underneath.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published equation table** before citing it: if the recomputed mean does not match the paper's, one of the two has an error in units, sex coding or body size.
- **Choose which equations to test** in a new validation study, and include Mifflin-St Jeor: it is the adult standard and, here, it would have beaten four of the five equations the paper tried.
- **See how much of a reported bias is body size** rather than illness, by running the equations on a healthy group with the same averages.
- **Plan a validation study's size** with [[sample-size]], using the published limits of agreement as the spread you must beat.
- **Report agreement properly**, with bias and limits of agreement from [[bland-altman]] rather than a correlation coefficient.

## The fine print

- Running an equation on the group's averages is not the same as averaging each child's prediction. Harris-Benedict, Mifflin and the INP equations are linear in weight, height and age, so the two agree exactly for those; they would not for an equation with a square or a ratio in it.
- The predictions cover resting energy only. Nothing here is a feeding recommendation, and none of these equations is validated for a child on treatment: that is the paper's own point.
- Harris-Benedict, Mifflin-St Jeor and Roza-Shizgal were all built on adults. Applying them to 6 to 18-year-olds is what the paper set out to test, not something the tool endorses.
- The paper reports two different measured means (1,180 and 1,200 kcal/day) in two tables. We used 1,200, which is the one consistent with its published biases.
- The tool's Katch-McArdle equation needs a body-fat percentage, which this paper does not publish as a mean, so we left it out.
