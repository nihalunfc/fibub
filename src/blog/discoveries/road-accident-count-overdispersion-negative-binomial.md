Count models come with a number almost nobody interprets. A paper says "the dispersion parameter was 0.1499 (p < 0.001)", a reader nods, and the model moves on. But that one number decides how often the model expects a crash with five casualties instead of one, and road safety policy is made in that tail. A 2026 paper in *PLOS ONE* fits negative binomial models to ten years of Bangladeshi police accident records and reports four such parameters. This article turns each of them into probabilities with the [[distribution-explorer]], so you can see what the paper's model actually believes. For the testing and modelling either side of it, see [[hypothesis-tests]] and the [[regression-workbench]].

## The paper in brief

The authors used 64,050 police-reported road accident records from Bangladesh for 2006 to 2015, analysed in two subsets: 11,888 records with driver and vehicle details, and 24,078 with road and environmental details. For each crash they modelled the **number of fatalities** and the **number of injuries**. A Poisson model assumes the variance of a count equals its mean; crash counts are far more variable than that, so the authors fitted **negative binomial** models instead, which add one dispersion parameter and let the variance rise above the mean.

They report that the dispersion parameter is significant (p < 0.001) in all four models, with values **4.306** and **0.6065** for fatalities and injuries in the first dataset and **1.5665** and **0.1499** in the second, and that the negative binomial beats Poisson on AIC in both datasets. Their substantive findings — which driver, vehicle, road and weather factors raise the counts — sit in a table we did not read. The dispersion parameters are in the running text, and they are enough to check the paper's central claim: that these counts are overdispersed, and badly.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated numbers**. Nothing is simulated.

**Step 1: how strong is the evidence for overdispersion?** The paper reports a significant Z-test but no statistic. We can get one anyway. Poisson and negative binomial are nested models differing by exactly one parameter, and AIC = −2 log *L* + 2*k*, so an AIC gap of *g* means a likelihood-ratio statistic of *g* + 2. The first dataset's gap of **264.04** gives a statistic of **266.04** on one degree of freedom; the second dataset's gap of **381.92** gives **383.92**. Put either into a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom and the p-values are **8.28 × 10⁻⁶⁰** and **1.74 × 10⁻⁸⁵**. The authors' "p < 0.001" is a dramatic understatement: these counts are not Poisson by any stretch.

**Step 2: what does each θ do to the tail?** A negative binomial with mean μ and dispersion θ has variance μ + μ²/θ, so small θ means heavy dispersion. The paper does not report a mean count, so we worked each dispersion parameter out at a mean of **1 casualty per record** (**our assumption**, chosen so the comparison with Poisson is easy to read) using the tool's own survival function:

| Model | θ | Variance at mean 1 | P(no casualty) | P(2 or more) | P(5 or more) | Tail vs Poisson |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Poisson | — | 1.000 | 0.368 | 0.264 | 0.00366 | 1× |
| Fatalities, set 1 | 4.306 | 1.232 | 0.407 | 0.263 | 0.00989 | 2.7× |
| Fatalities, set 2 | 1.5665 | 1.638 | 0.462 | 0.257 | 0.0218 | 6.0× |
| Injuries, set 1 | 0.6065 | 2.649 | 0.554 | 0.237 | 0.0442 | 12.1× |
| Injuries, set 2 | 0.1499 | 7.671 | 0.737 | 0.167 | 0.0687 | 18.8× |

Read the "2 or more" column and the four models look almost identical — every one sits between 0.17 and 0.27. Read the "5 or more" column and they are worlds apart. **Overdispersion is a statement about the tail, not about the middle.** A planner using the Poisson model would expect a five-casualty crash about once in 273 records; the paper's most dispersed model expects one about once in 15.

The other half of the same effect is the zero. At a mean of 1, the Poisson puts 37 % of its weight on no casualty and the θ = 0.1499 model puts **74 %**. The dispersed model says most records are harmless and a few are terrible, which is what accident data look like, and it gets there without any zero-inflation term — worth knowing, because the paper says it did not fit zero-inflated models.

**Step 3: say which dispersion parameter you mean.** There are two conventions in common use and software reports both. R's `glm.nb` reports **θ** with variance μ + μ²/θ, so a *small* value means heavy dispersion. Stata's `nbreg` reports **α = 1/θ** with variance μ(1 + αμ), so a *large* value means heavy dispersion. The paper calls its numbers "the dispersion parameter" without saying which. The difference is not cosmetic: at a mean of 2, a reported 0.1499 means a variance of **28.68** under the θ reading and **2.60** under the α reading — a standard deviation of 5.4 against 1.6. Taken as θ, the paper's four numbers say injuries are much more dispersed than fatalities, which is what you would expect, since a crash that kills one person may injure ten. Taken as α they would say the opposite. We read them as θ (**our assumption**), and we would ask any count-model paper to name the convention and the software.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/road-accident-count-overdispersion-negative-binomial.mjs` in fibub's public code. It imports `DIST` from `src/lib/sci/distributions.ts`, the maths behind the [[distribution-explorer]], and uses the Poisson and negative-binomial `pdf`, `sf` and `moments`, plus the chi-squared `sf` for the likelihood-ratio p-values. A count-model negative binomial with mean μ and dispersion θ is entered as the tool's `nbinom` with **r = θ** and **p = θ ÷ (θ + μ)**, which is the standard reparametrisation.

From the paper we took four dispersion parameters and four AIC values, all from its running text. **Our assumptions** are the mean of 1 casualty per record (the paper reports none), the θ convention, and the use of the AIC gap as a likelihood-ratio statistic, which is exact for nested models differing by one parameter.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/distribution-explorer.mjs`, which pins all 35 distributions against SciPy:

- For each distribution and several parameter sets, the pdf, cdf, survival function and quantiles must match `scipy.stats` — including `nbinom(r=5, p=0.4)` and the awkward `nbinom(r=0.8, p=0.1)`, `poisson(0.05)` and `poisson(10000)`, and `chi2` with 1, 3 and 150 degrees of freedom.
- The analytic mean, variance, skewness and kurtosis must agree with numerical integration or summation of the same pdf.
- Random samples from every distribution must pass a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test at 1 % against their own cdf.
- New with this article: at a mean of 1, the negative binomial with θ = 0.1499 must give a variance of 7.671114, P(X = 0) = 0.7368171 and P(X ≥ 5) = 0.0686797, that is 18.8 times the Poisson value; and chi-squared with one degree of freedom must put 8.2794 × 10⁻⁶⁰ above 266.04.

## Check it yourself

On the [[distribution-explorer]]:

1. Set *Distribution* to **Negative binomial**. Put **0.6065** in *Successes r* and **0.377529** in *Probability p* — that is θ and θ ÷ (θ + μ) for the injuries model of the first dataset at a mean of 1.
2. The *Result* card reads *Mean* **0.999999**, *Variance* **2.6488** and *Standard deviation* **1.62751**. (The mean is a whisker off 1 because we rounded p to six figures.)
3. Set *Shade* to **Above a (right tail)** and *a* to **5**. The big line reads **P(X ≥ 5) = 0.0441719**.
4. Change *Distribution* to **Poisson** and *Mean λ* to **1**, keeping the same shading. Now **P(X ≥ 5) = 0.00365985** — twelve times smaller for the same average count.
5. Put **0.5, 0.9, 0.99** in *Quantiles for probabilities*. The Poisson gives **1, 2, 4**; switch back to the negative binomial of step 1 and it gives **0, 3, 7**. The 99th-percentile record almost doubles.
6. For the evidence, set *Distribution* to **Chi-squared (χ²)**, *Degrees of freedom k* to **1**, *Shade* to **Above a (right tail)** and *a* to **266.04**. It reads **8.2794 × 10⁻⁶⁰**: the p-value hidden inside the paper's AIC gap.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a dispersion parameter into a sentence.** Enter r = θ and p = θ ÷ (θ + μ) and read the probability of the outcome you care about, instead of quoting the parameter.
- **Recover a p-value from an AIC table.** For nested models one parameter apart, the gap plus 2 is a chi-squared statistic on one degree of freedom.
- **Decide whether you need zero inflation.** Compare the share of zeros your fitted negative binomial predicts with the share your data show; if they match, the extra term is unnecessary.
- **Pick a sample size for rare counts.** The tool's quantiles and tail probabilities tell you how many records you need before a five-casualty event is likely to appear at all.
- **Sanity-check a reported variance.** If a paper's stated variance does not equal μ + μ²/θ at its own mean, one of the three numbers is wrong or the convention is α, not θ.

## The fine print

- The paper reports no mean count, so the probabilities in step 2 are at a mean of **1 casualty per record**, our own choice. Figure 1 shows the whole range from 0.2 to 3 so nothing depends on it.
- We read the reported parameters as θ in the variance μ + μ²/θ. If they are α = 1/θ, every tail probability here is far too large, and the ordering of the four models reverses.
- Treating the AIC gap as a likelihood-ratio statistic assumes the two models were fitted to the same records with the same covariates and differ only in the dispersion parameter. That is what the paper describes.
- The paper's Poisson AIC and BIC (27731.65 and 27526.68) cannot both be right for 11,888 records, and the same BIC is printed for both models. We used only the AIC pair, which is internally consistent.
- The negative binomial is one of several ways to handle overdispersion; others are quasi-Poisson, zero-inflated and hurdle models. Nothing here tests the paper's choice of covariates or its conclusions about which factors matter.
