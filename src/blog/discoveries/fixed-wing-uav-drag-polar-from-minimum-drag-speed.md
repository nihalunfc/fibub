Papers about home-built unmanned aircraft nearly always leave out the two numbers another engineer needs: the zero-lift drag coefficient and the Oswald efficiency. Without them you cannot redo any of the performance claims. A 2024 paper in *Drones* describes a 6 kg electric fixed-wing UAV with a 2.21 m span, built and flown, and reports a long list of speeds and powers but no drag coefficients. This article shows that one of those speeds is enough: the [[aircraft-performance|aircraft performance calculator]] turns the **least-drag speed** back into a whole drag polar, which then reproduces the paper's least-power speed, cruise power and endurance to within a few per cent. For the propeller and battery ends of the same job, see [[propeller-thrust]] and [[drone-flight-time]], with air density from [[standard-atmosphere]].

## The paper in brief

The aircraft is a conventional pusher with a NACA 4412 wing of **0.49 m² area** and **2.21 m span** (aspect ratio 10), **6 kg** all-up with a 1 kg payload, powered by an 800 kV motor on a **3S 13,000 mAh** battery, so about **144 Wh** of energy. The authors sized the wing from a target stall speed, built the airframe, ran CFD with and without winglets, and flew an autonomous 3 km circuit.

The performance numbers they report include a **stall speed of 10.3 m/s**, a cruise of **15.86 m/s** without winglets and 14.64 m/s with them, a **least-power speed of 12.71 m/s** and a **least-drag speed of 16.73 m/s**, a maximum of **28.8 m/s**, a cruise power of **about 75 W** against a maximum demand of **287.5 W**, and an endurance of **1.5 to 2 hours**. Nowhere do they give CD0, the Oswald efficiency or the lift-to-drag ratio.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the two speeds are consistent, which is the first thing to check.** On a parabolic drag polar the least-drag speed is always 3^(1/4) = **1.3161** times the least-power speed, whatever the aircraft. The paper's pair gives 16.73 ÷ 12.71 = **1.3163**. They agree to two parts in ten thousand, so both came from the same consistent polar, and either one fixes the other.

**Step 2: recovering the polar.** At the least-drag speed the lift coefficient is CL = √(CD0/k). Working backwards from 16.73 m/s at 6 kg and 0.49 m² at sea level gives **CL = 0.7005**, so **CD0 = k × 0.7005²**. Only the split between CD0 and the induced term is left, and that needs the Oswald efficiency, which the paper does not state. Taking **e = 0.85** (**our assumption**, typical for a clean straight wing) gives k = 0.0376 and

- **CD0 = 0.0184**
- **best lift-to-drag ratio 19.0**, at CL = 0.70

Over the plausible range e = 0.75 to 0.95, CD0 runs from 0.0209 to 0.0165 and the best lift-to-drag ratio from 16.8 to 21.2. Feeding that polar back into the tool returns the least-power speed as **12.71 m/s**, the paper's own figure, which it never saw.

**Step 3: the power curve reproduces the paper's cruise power.** With the recovered polar, level flight at the paper's 15.86 m/s cruise needs CL = 0.78, **3.11 N of drag** and **49.4 W** of thrust power. At a 65 % efficient propeller and drive (**our assumption**) that is **76 W** of shaft power against the paper's "about 75 W". Nothing in our chain used that number, so it is an independent check on both the paper and the polar.

**Step 5: two numbers that do not quite fit.** The paper's stall speed of 10.3 m/s needs a maximum lift coefficient of **1.848**, not the 1.724 it quotes; with 1.724 the tool gives **10.66 m/s**. And the 287.5 W maximum demand would carry this aircraft to **31.5 m/s** in level flight, 9 % faster than the quoted 28.8 m/s, where it needs only 227 W. The likely reason is the propeller: static thrust, not shaft power, is what runs out at high speed.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/fixed-wing-uav-drag-polar-from-minimum-drag-speed.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `performance()`, `dragAt()` and `takeoff()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-aircraft.ts`, the maths behind the [[aircraft-performance|aircraft performance calculator]], with air density from `atmosphere()` in `src/lib/sci/eng-atmos.ts`, which powers [[standard-atmosphere]].

Mass, wing area, span, CLmax, the speeds, the two power figures and the battery size are the paper's own stated values. The **Oswald efficiency of 0.85** and the **65 % propeller and drive efficiency** are **our assumptions**, and they are the only free numbers in the chain; the drag polar itself is derived from the paper's least-drag speed. The ground roll the tool also reports (26 m at 0.2875 kW with a 1.2 × stall lift-off at 12.8 m/s) is ours, not the paper's: the aircraft is hand- or catapult-launched.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/aircraft-performance.mjs` runs on every change to fibub:

- The characteristic speeds and the best lift-to-drag ratio of two made-up aircraft must match an independent SciPy calculation that finds them by numerically minimising drag and power, and the identity "least-drag speed = 3^(1/4) × least-power speed" is checked directly.
- Breguet range and endurance must match SciPy's numerical integration over the fuel burned, for both the jet and the propeller forms, and the ground roll must match an integration of the equation of motion.
- New with this article: the recovered polar for this UAV (CD0 = 0.018428, e = 0.85, 6 kg, 0.49 m², 2.21 m span) must give a least-drag speed of 16.73 m/s, a least-power speed of 12.71 m/s, a best lift-to-drag ratio of 19.0, a stall speed of 10.66 m/s at CLmax 1.724, and 49.4 W of drag power at 15.86 m/s.

## Check it yourself

On the [[aircraft-performance|aircraft performance calculator]]:

1. Set *Example* to **Keep my numbers** and *Engine* to **Propeller (piston or turboprop)**, and *Speeds in* to **m/s**.
2. Type *Mass at start (kg)* **6**, *Wing area S (m²)* **0.49**, *Wing span b (m)* **2.21**, *Zero-lift drag CD0* **0.01843**, *Oswald efficiency e* **0.85**, *Maximum lift CLmax* **1.724**.
3. Under *Engine and fuel*, type *Take-off power (kW)* **0.2875**, *Propeller efficiency η* **0.65**, *Fuel burned in cruise (kg)* **0** (it is electric, so the Breguet range and endurance lines will read zero), *Cruise altitude (m, ISA)* **0**.
4. The results read *Aspect ratio and k* **AR 9.97, k 0.0376**, *Best lift-to-drag ratio* **19**, *Stall speed* **10.7 m/s**, *Least-drag speed* **16.7 m/s** and *Least-power speed* **12.7 m/s** with about **0.0454 kW needed**. Divide that by the propeller efficiency and you have the 70 W of shaft power behind the two-hour endurance.
5. *Take-off ground roll* shows about **26 m** at a lift-off speed of 12.8 m/s, with a thrust-to-weight of 0.36.
6. Change *Maximum lift CLmax* to **1.848** and the stall speed falls to **10.3 m/s**, the paper's figure: that is the lift coefficient its stall speed really implies.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recover a missing drag polar** from any one characteristic speed (least drag, least power or best glide) plus mass and wing area, so a published aircraft can be reanalysed.
- **Cross-check a design table before publishing it**: the 3^(1/4) ratio, the stall speed and the cruise power must all agree with one polar. Ours did not at two points.
- **Separate aerodynamics from propulsion.** When the maximum speed falls short of what the power allows, the propeller is the limit; size it with [[propeller-thrust]].
- **Plan an electric mission** by reading endurance off the power curve at the least-power speed rather than at cruise, then check the battery with [[drone-flight-time]].
- **Trace a wing change through performance**: get CD0 and CLmax for a new section from [[airfoil-calculator]], then re-run the whole table.

## The fine print

- The polar here is **derived, not measured**. It rests on the paper's least-drag speed and on our assumed Oswald efficiency of 0.85; another value of e moves CD0 and the best lift-to-drag ratio, as the ranges above show.
- The 65 % propeller and drive efficiency is also ours. At 70 % the cruise shaft power becomes 71 W and the endurance 2.05 h.
- The parabolic polar is a model. Near the stall and above about CL 1.2 the real drag rises faster than it predicts.
- The paper gives several stall speeds (10.3, 11.5 and 11.7 m/s) and two cruise speeds; we used the sea-level 10.3 m/s and the no-winglet 15.86 m/s.
- Battery energy is taken as nominal voltage times capacity. A real pack delivers less under load and should never be flown flat.
