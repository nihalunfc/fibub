Low oxygen in water is almost always reported as a concentration: below **2 mg/L** is called hypoxia, below **0.5 mg/L** severe hypoxia. Those lines come from fish and benthic animals, but the water's own chemistry does not care about concentration alone. Cold, salty water can hold far more oxygen than warm fresh water, so the same 2 mg/L is a different distance from full saturation at every site. A 2025 paper in *Oceans* puts that problem on the table by following two very different hypoxic waters for a year with the same thresholds, and we used the [[dissolved-oxygen|dissolved oxygen tool]] to translate its numbers into saturation and oxygen debt. For the stratification side of the same casts, see the [[ctd-cast-viewer]] and the [[carbonate-system]] calculator.

## The paper in brief

The authors compared the **Baltic Proper**, a brackish sea with a permanent halocline, and the **Shuikou Reservoir** in China, fresh and much warmer. Over a year the Baltic transect ran from 3.04 to 21.26 °C and salinity 6.98 to 23.18, with oxygen from 0 to 9.9 mg/L; the reservoir ran from 14.52 to 32.58 °C at a salinity of 0.02 to 0.04, with oxygen from 0.3 to 8.7 mg/L.

Their headline results: the Baltic Proper is **persistently hypoxic below 63 m**, with the hypoxic water at 5.37 to 10.14 °C and the deepest stations down to **0.0076 to 0.2503 mg/L**, essentially anoxic. The reservoir is **seasonally hypoxic below 11.5 m**, reaching **0.23 mg/L** in September at 25.52 to 31.80 °C, and recovering to 2.33 mg/L by November. Oxygen also changed much faster in the reservoir: up to 0.048 mg/L per day in the bottom layer, against 0.005 mg/L per day in the Baltic's deep water. Stratification strength and nitrogen species tracked the oxygen at both sites, while temperature and turbidity acted in opposite directions at the two.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the temperatures, salinities and oxygen concentrations the paper states in its text**, using the Garcia and Gordon (1992) solubility equation that the tool implements, the same one in the TEOS-10 oceanographic toolbox. The paper's own profiles are in its figures, so we did not refit anything.

**Step 1: how much oxygen each water could hold.** At 5.37 °C and salinity 12 (the Baltic's hypoxic layer sits below the permanent halocline; the paper gives only the whole-transect salinity range, so **salinity 12 is our assumption**) saturation is **11.68 mg/L**. At 31.80 °C in the reservoir's fresh water it is **7.33 mg/L** — only 63 % as much (Figure 1).

**Step 2: the same threshold, a different shortfall.** Two milligrams per litre is therefore **17.1 % of saturation** in the coldest Baltic hypoxic water and **27.3 % of saturation** in the warmest reservoir water: the same number means a 1.6 times milder relative shortfall in the cold sea. Salinity matters far less than temperature here; across the paper's whole Baltic salinity range, 6.98 to 23.18, the 2 mg/L line moves only from 17.6 % to 19.5 % of saturation at the same temperature. The severe-hypoxia line, 0.5 mg/L, is **4.3 %** of saturation in the cold Baltic water and **6.8 %** in the warm reservoir (Figure 2).

**Step 3: oxygen debt, which is the quantity biogeochemists actually use.** **Apparent oxygen utilisation** (AOU) is saturation minus measurement: how much oxygen has been consumed since the water last touched the air. The Baltic's deepest water, 0.0076 to 0.2503 mg/L, has an AOU of **341.0 to 333.5 µmol/kg** (taken at 7.75 °C, the middle of the paper's hypoxic temperature range, and salinity 12). The reservoir's September low of 0.23 mg/L has an AOU of **222.9 µmol/kg** at 31.80 °C. So although both are near zero in mg/L, the Baltic's deep water has consumed about **1.5 times more oxygen per kilogram** on its way down — exactly what a persistent, decades-old stagnant layer should look like, and a cleaner way to say it than "both are anoxic".

**Step 4: the reservoir's recovery is less of a recovery than it looks.** November's 2.33 mg/L is above the 2 mg/L line, but in fresh water at 20 °C (**our assumption**, inside the reservoir's range) that is only **25.6 % of saturation**. The threshold crossing is a book-keeping event, not a return to health.

**Step 5: temperature does different work at the two sites.** The paper notes that temperature pushed oxygen in opposite directions at the two sites. Part of that is pure solubility and can be read straight off the curve: warming by 1 °C near 7.75 °C in the Baltic's deep water expels **0.263 mg/L**, while warming by 1 °C near 28.7 °C in the reservoir expels only **0.136 mg/L**. The solubility curve is nearly twice as steep in the cold sea, so a given warming does twice as much to the Baltic's oxygen budget per litre.

**Step 6: a check on the maxima.** The highest oxygen reported anywhere on the Baltic transect, 9.9 mg/L, would be exactly saturation at **12.4 °C** (salinity 12) or **13.9 °C** at the freshest salinity in the study; at the coldest water the paper saw, 3.04 °C, it is only **77 %** saturated. The reservoir's highest, 8.7 mg/L, is saturation at **22.2 °C** and **85 %** of saturation at the coldest reservoir water, 14.52 °C. Neither site ever reached full saturation anywhere in the record. That is a useful cross-check on a sensor calibration and takes ten seconds in the tool.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/baltic-reservoir-hypoxia-oxygen-saturation-same-threshold-different-meaning.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `saturation`, `satState` (percent saturation and AOU), `convert` (the density chain between mg/L, µmol/L, µmol/kg and mL/L) and `density` from `src/lib/sci/oxygen.ts`, the maths behind the [[dissolved-oxygen|dissolved oxygen tool]].

Inputs from the paper's text: every temperature, salinity and oxygen concentration above, and the 2 and 0.5 mg/L thresholds. **Our assumption**: salinity 12 for the Baltic's hypoxic layer, where the paper gives only the transect range (Step 2 shows how little that choice matters). Barometric pressure is taken as 1 atm throughout, which is right for water at the surface in contact with normal air.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/dissolved-oxygen.mjs`, which pins the solubility and the unit chain against published and independently computed values:

- Solubility must match the TEOS-10 toolbox's `O2sol_SP_pt` to 1 part in 10¹²: 274.5957 µmol/kg at 10 °C and salinity 35, 352.8441 in fresh water at 10 °C, 347.9029 at 0 °C and salinity 35. It was checked against `gsw` 3.6 on 2000 random temperatures and salinities.
- Sea-water density must be within 0.01 kg/m³ of TEOS-10 (1025.97 kg/m³ at 15 °C and salinity 35), and 1 mL/L must be exactly 44.6596 µmol/L and 1.429053 mg/L.
- A measurement of 230 µmol/kg at 15 °C and salinity 35 must read 92.8 % saturated with an AOU of 17.8 µmol/kg, and saturated water must read exactly 100 % with an AOU of zero.
- New with this article: 2 mg/L must come out as 17.12 % of saturation at 5.37 °C and salinity 12, and 27.29 % at 31.80 °C in fresh water, and the AOU of 0.0076 mg/L in Baltic deep water must be 340.99 µmol/kg.

## Check it yourself

On the [[dissolved-oxygen|dissolved oxygen]] page, in the *Water* card:

1. Type *Temperature (°C)* **5.37** and *Salinity* **12**, and leave *Barometric pressure (hPa)* at **1013.25**. *Oxygen at saturation* reads **11.68 mg/L** (361.6 µmol/kg).
2. In *Measured oxygen* type **2** and set *Unit* to **mg/L**. It reads **17.1 %** saturation, with the AOU beside it.
3. Now press **Fresh water (S 0)** and type *Temperature* **31.8**. Saturation drops to **7.33 mg/L** and the same 2 mg/L becomes **27.3 %**.
4. Type *Measured oxygen* **0.23**, the reservoir's September low: *Saturation* **3.1 %** and *AOU* **222.9 µmol/kg**.
5. Now type *Temperature* **7.75** and *Salinity* **12**, the middle of the Baltic's hypoxic layer, with *Measured oxygen* **0.0076**: *Saturation* **0.1 %** and *AOU* **341.0 µmol/kg**, half again as much consumed oxygen as the reservoir's low.
6. In *Solubility against temperature*, set *Show in* to **mg/L** and use **Download table CSV (0 to 40 °C)** to get the whole curve for your own salinity.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report percent saturation and AOU alongside mg/L.** One line in a methods section, and your low-oxygen numbers become comparable with any other site, in any climate.
- **Translate a legal or ecological threshold** into the saturation it means at your own temperature and salinity, before comparing two water bodies.
- **Check a sensor.** If a reading is above saturation for its own temperature and salinity, either the water is genuinely supersaturated by photosynthesis or bubbles, or the calibration has drifted.
- **Convert units for a data archive** (mg/L, µmol/L, µmol/kg, mL/L) with the density link done properly rather than with a single fixed factor.
- **Budget an experiment or a pond**: the solubility curve gives the ceiling that aeration is working against, and the [[pond-oxygen-budget]] tool takes it from there.

## The fine print

- Every number here is **recomputed from values stated in the paper's text**. We did not use its data tables or figures and we did not refit anything.
- The salinity of the Baltic's hypoxic layer is our assumption; Step 2 brackets the effect across the paper's whole salinity range.
- The AOU values are quoted at a single temperature inside each site's stated range (7.75 °C for the Baltic's hypoxic layer, 31.80 °C for the reservoir in September), because the paper gives ranges rather than paired readings.
- Solubility assumes contact with moist air at 1 atm. Water at 63 m is under about 7 atm of total pressure, but its oxygen content was set at the surface, so surface saturation is the right reference for AOU.
- AOU ignores any oxygen gained or lost by mixing with other water masses; it is "apparent" for that reason.
- We say nothing about what the oxygen means for particular fish or benthic animals. That is the paper's domain and depends on species, not chemistry.
