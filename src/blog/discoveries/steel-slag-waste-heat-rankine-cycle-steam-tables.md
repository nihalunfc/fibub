Molten steel slag leaves the furnace at well over 1,000 °C and is usually cooled with water, throwing away the heat. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Energy Research* encloses the **hot disintegration** step in a cylindrical radiative receiver, raises steam from it, superheats that steam, and runs it through a turbine: **2068 kW of shaft power at 20.4 % thermal efficiency**. The paper states four conditions for the cycle (0.2 MPa and 556.8 °C at the turbine, 0.004 MPa in the condenser, a 72 % efficient turbine) and three results (power, heat input and condenser duty). That is exactly the situation the [[steam-tables|steam tables]] are for: from the four conditions, the three results follow, and anyone can check them in a browser. For the equipment either side of the cycle, see the [[heat-exchanger-sizing|heat exchanger sizing calculator]] and the [[lcoe-calculator|cost of energy calculator]].

## The paper in brief

Hot disintegration is the stage where slag at high temperature is broken up and cooled. The authors' idea is to surround it with a **cylindrical** water-cooled receiver rather than a rectangular one: for the same receiver area they model a mean radiative heat-transfer rate of 17.54 MW against 13.83 MW, about **27 % higher**, because a cylinder sees the hot bed from more directions.

That heat raises steam, which the paper then **superheats to 556.8 °C** while leaving it at only **0.2 MPa**, about two atmospheres. The steam flow is 10,400 kg/h, the turbine is 72 % efficient, and the condenser works at 0.004 MPa, which corresponds to condensate at about 29 °C. Of the 10,127 kW of heat entering the water, 7,247 kW comes from the disintegration enclosure, 267 kW from an economizer and 2,613 kW from the superheater. The authors also run a small water-chemistry test, cutting equivalent hardness from 3.944 to 0.188 mmol/L with trisodium phosphate dosing, and are careful to say that the long-term behaviour of that treatment is untested.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below comes from the [[steam-tables|steam tables]], whose property functions are the **IAPWS-IF97** industrial formulation, the standard used in power-plant engineering. We gave it only the paper's four conditions, plus a pump efficiency of 75 % (**our assumption**; the paper gives the pump's work, 0.66 kW, not its efficiency, and it is far too small to matter).

**Step 1: the condenser temperature.** IF97's saturation line puts the boiling point of water at 0.004 MPa at **28.96 °C**. The paper's condensate leaves at 28.96 °C. The two agree exactly, which tells us the paper's condenser is modelled as saturated, with no subcooling, and gives us the first cycle state for free.

**Step 3: the paper's three results, recomputed.** The turbine drops 716.08 kJ/kg (an ideal drop of 994.6 to an exit at 61.63 °C, of which the turbine takes 72 %), the pump adds 0.26 kJ/kg, the boiler and superheater add 3488.5 kJ/kg, and the condenser rejects 2772.7 kJ/kg. Multiply by the paper's flow of 10,400 kg/h (2.889 kg/s) and the agreement is close to exact:

| Check | Paper | Steam tables (IF97) |
|---|---|---|
| Condensate temperature | 28.96 °C | 28.96 °C (saturation at 0.004 MPa) |
| Cycle power | 2068.4 kW | **2067.9 kW** (0.02 % apart) |
| Heat input | 10,127 kW | **10,078 kW** (0.5 % apart) |
| Condenser duty | about 8.01 MW | **8.010 MW** |
| Thermal efficiency | 20.4 % | **20.52 %** |
| Feed-pump work | about 0.66 kW | 0.76 kW (at our assumed 75 % pump efficiency) |

The only visible gap is the 0.5 % in heat input, and it has a simple explanation: 10,127 kW of heat at 3488.5 kJ/kg needs **10,451 kg/h** of steam, not 10,400. Either the paper's flow is rounded, or a little heat goes to blowdown or losses. A reader can decide which, because the arithmetic is now in the open.

**Step 4: where the energy goes, and what it would take to do better.** Three numbers from the tool explain this cycle completely:

- The **Carnot limit** between 556.8 °C and 28.96 °C is **63.6 %**. The cycle reaches 20.5 %, less than a third of it.
- The turbine exhaust leaves at **207 °C**, which is **178 K above the boiling point** at 0.004 MPa. All of that superheat is thrown into the cooling water: the condenser duty is 8.0 MW against 2.1 MW of work.
- The **back work ratio** is 0.037 %. The pump is free, which is the one luxury of a low-pressure cycle.

The reason is the 0.2 MPa. Superheating at two atmospheres gives the turbine a large temperature drop but a small pressure ratio, so the steam leaves hot. Figure 2 shows what the tool makes of the same cycle at higher boiler pressures, with the turbine inlet still at 556.8 °C: **23.2 % at 0.5 MPa, 25.1 % at 1 MPa, 27.0 % at 2 MPa and 31.4 % at 12 MPa**. From the paper's own 10,127 kW of heat, 2 MPa would give about **2,739 kW** instead of 2,068 kW, a third more power from the same slag. Above about 8 MPa the exhaust finally becomes wet (quality 0.973 at 8 MPa, 0.948 at 12), which is where blade erosion starts to matter and reheat becomes worth its cost.

Two other sensitivities, for anyone planning such a plant: raising the turbine's isentropic efficiency from 72 % to 80 % lifts the output from 2,068 kW to **2,298 kW**, and letting the condenser warm from 0.004 MPa (28.96 °C) to 0.01 MPa (45.81 °C), which is what a hot summer or a fouled condenser does, drops it to **1,738 kW**, a 16 % loss. In a steel works, condenser cooling is likely the easier of the two to get wrong.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/steel-slag-waste-heat-rankine-cycle-steam-tables.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `rankine`, `dome`, `isobar`, `tsat` and `pT` from `src/lib/sci/if97.ts`, the maths behind the [[steam-tables|steam tables]]: IF97 regions 1, 2 and 4 for the water, steam and saturation properties, the entropy inversions that give the isentropic turbine exit, and the cycle bookkeeping.

From the paper: 0.2 MPa, 556.8 °C, 0.004 MPa, a turbine efficiency of 0.72, a flow of 10,400 kg/h, and the three results we compare against (2068.4 kW, 10,127 kW, 8.01 MW). **Our assumption:** a pump isentropic efficiency of 75 %. Nothing is fitted: the cycle is solved once from the four conditions.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/steam-tables.mjs` on every change to fibub. IF97 is a published standard with its own verification tables, so the checks are strict:

- The region 1, 2 and 3 property values at the release's verification points (for example 3 MPa and 300 K, 0.0035 MPa and 300 K, and the near-critical points) must match the published figures.
- The saturation pressure and temperature must match the IAPWS equations and invert each other, and the properties are compared against the Python `iapws` package at about 100 points and five complete cycles.
- New with this article: the paper's cycle (0.2 MPa, 556.8 °C, 0.004 MPa, 72 % turbine, 75 % pump) must give a turbine work of 716.08 kJ/kg, a heat input of 3488.5 kJ/kg, a thermal efficiency of 20.52 % and a turbine exit at 207.31 °C, so the numbers printed above stay reproducible.

## Check it yourself

On the [[steam-tables|steam tables]] page, in the *Rankine cycle* card:

1. Set *Boiler pressure* to **0.2** MPa and *Turbine inlet temperature* to **556.8** °C.
2. Set *Condenser pressure* to **0.004** MPa.
3. Set *Turbine efficiency* to **72** % and *Pump efficiency* to **75** %.
4. Put **2.068** into *Net power (MW, for the flow rates)*.
5. The headline reads **Thermal efficiency 20.52 %**. *Net work* is **715.8 kJ/kg** (turbine 716.1 minus pump 0.262), *Heat in* is **3488.5 kJ/kg** with **2772.7 kJ/kg** rejected in the condenser, the *Steam rate* is **5.03 kg/kWh** and the *Carnot limit* is **63.6 %**.
6. *For 2.068 MW net* reads **2.889 kg/s of steam** (that is 10,400 kg/h, the paper's flow), with a **10.08 MW** heat input and **8.01 MW** to the cooling water: the paper's two other results.
7. The state table shows the turbine exit at **207.3 °C** and *Turbine exit* reads **superheated steam**, not a quality: there is no moisture at all in this turbine.
8. Now change *Boiler pressure* to **2** and watch the efficiency climb to **27.04 %** while everything else stays put.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published cycle in two minutes.** Four conditions fix the whole thing, so a power, a heat input and a condenser duty are checkable claims, as the table above shows.
- **Find the implied steam flow.** Dividing a stated heat input by the IF97 heat per kilogram, as in step 3, catches rounded or inconsistent flow rates.
- **Rank the improvements before modelling them**: pressure, turbine efficiency and condenser temperature each have a clear slope, and the tool gives all three in a minute.
- **Teach where the exergy goes.** A 63.6 % Carnot limit against a 20.5 % cycle, with 178 K of superheat going into the cooling water, is a clearer lesson than any textbook diagram.
- **Size the equipment next**: the receiver and the condenser with the [[heat-exchanger-sizing|heat exchanger sizing calculator]], and the money with the [[lcoe-calculator|cost of energy calculator]].

## The fine print

- The 2068 kW is **shaft power**, as the paper is careful to say: generator and auxiliary losses are not modelled, so the exported electricity would be lower.
- Our pump efficiency of 75 % is an assumption. At this pressure the pump work is 0.26 kJ/kg out of 716 kJ/kg, so any sensible value gives the same answers.
- A simple Rankine cycle with no feedwater heating, no blowdown and no pressure drops is what the paper describes; a real plant would differ in each.
- The 27 % gain for the cylindrical receiver and the water-treatment result are modelling and bench results in the paper, and neither is something a steam table can check.
- Slag-heat recovery is intermittent by nature: hot disintegration is a batch process, and the steady-state cycle above is the best case, not an annual average.
