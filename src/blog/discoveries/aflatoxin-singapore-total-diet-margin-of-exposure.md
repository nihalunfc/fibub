Aflatoxins are made by moulds that grow on nuts, grains and spices, and they are among the few food contaminants with no safe intake: regulators compare the dose that caused tumours in studies with the dose people actually eat, and ask how wide the gap is. That gap is the **margin of exposure**. A 2025 total diet study measured aflatoxins in 642 foods bought in Singapore and worked out margins of exposure for average and high consumers; for high consumers of some foods the margin fell to 51, far below the 10,000 that is usually taken as low concern. This article rebuilds those margins from the paper's own stated values with the [[margin-of-exposure]] tool, and shows what the number is most sensitive to. The point of departure itself comes from animal data fitted with a model like the [[benchmark-dose]] calculator, from curves like the ones the [[dose-response]] fitter handles.

## The paper in brief

The authors ran Singapore's first total diet study for aflatoxins: 642 food samples, prepared as people eat them, measured by mass spectrometry with a limit of detection of 0.025 µg/kg and a limit of quantification of 0.05 µg/kg. Aflatoxins turned up in 25 samples (4 %), at mean concentrations of 0.01 to 0.07 µg/kg across categories, with nuts and seeds (0.27 to 0.30 µg/kg) and sauces and condiments (0.09 to 0.14 µg/kg) highest. One satay sauce sample held 0.83 µg/kg of total aflatoxins, the paper's single highest-risk food.

They then combined each concentration with national food consumption data from 2,014 people and divided by body weight to get an estimated daily intake, and divided the **BMDL10 of 0.17 µg/kg bw/day** (the dose giving a 10 % extra tumour incidence at its lower confidence limit, credited to JECFA and EFSA) by that intake to get the margin of exposure. For average consumers the margins ran from about 2,819 to 7,101. For high consumers among people who actually eat the food, they ran from **51 to 725**: 51 for sauces and condiments, 80 for legumes, 122 for bakery and confectionery. All are below 10,000, which is the conventional comfort line for a genotoxic carcinogen.

## Checked with the tools

Everything the margin needs is in the paper's text for one food, so this is a **recomputation**, not a simulation: satay sauce at 0.83 µg/kg, a 95th-percentile consumption of 3.894 g/day among people who eat it, and a BMDL10 of 0.17 µg/kg bw/day.

**Step 1: which reading of the intake reproduces the paper?** The paper's formula divides the daily intake by each participant's body weight, and elsewhere it describes the consumption data as grams per kilogram of body weight. Read that way (body weight 1 kg in the tool, because the intake is already per kilogram), the tool gives an exposure of **0.00323 µg/kg bw/day, that is 3.2 ng/kg bw/day**, and a margin of exposure of **52.6**. The paper reports 3.4 ng/kg bw/day and a margin of 51 for that category: a match within rounding. Read instead as 3.894 g per person per day for a 60 kg adult (**our assumption** for the body weight; the paper does not give one), the same numbers give an exposure of 5.39 × 10⁻⁵ µg/kg bw/day and a margin of **3,156**, sixty times wider. So the published margins only make sense with the per-kilogram reading, and a reader who takes "3.894 g/day" at face value will be wrong by a factor of body weight. It is worth knowing that a 60 kg adult eating at that rate would be getting about 230 g of satay sauce a day, which is a lot of sauce; the margin is driven by the consumption figure far more than by the concentration.

**Step 2: how the margin moves with concentration.** Because exposure is linear in concentration, the margin is a straight line on log axes (Figure 1). At the paper's consumption rate, a food would have to be at **0.0044 µg/kg** to reach a margin of 10,000, and even a food exactly at the study's limit of quantification (0.05 µg/kg) would give a margin of **873**.

**Step 3: the measurement limit sets what can be proved.** That last number is the useful one for a lab. Figure 2 turns the sum around: for each consumption rate it plots the highest concentration that would still reach a margin of 10,000. The line crosses the study's limit of quantification at about **0.34 g per kg of body weight per day**, roughly 20 g a day for a 60 kg adult. Above that, "not detected" no longer proves a margin of 10,000, because the toxin could be present just under the limit and still close the gap. For a food eaten at the satay sauce rate the limit of quantification is more than ten times above the concentration that the convention would need. This is not a flaw in the paper, which is explicit about using upper-bound substitution; it is the arithmetic behind why aflatoxin margins in total diet studies rarely reach 10,000.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/aflatoxin-singapore-total-diet-margin-of-exposure.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `marginOfExposure` from `src/lib/sci/moe.ts`, the maths behind the [[margin-of-exposure]] tool: exposure = concentration × intake ÷ body weight with unit conversion, margin = point of departure ÷ exposure, and the concentration at which a scenario would just meet a target margin.

The BMDL10, the concentration, the consumption rate, the limits of detection and quantification and the target of 10,000 all come from the paper's text. The 60 kg body weight used for the alternative reading, and the extra consumption rates of 1 and 0.1 g per kg of body weight per day in Figure 1, are **our assumptions** for comparison.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/margin-of-exposure.mjs` on every change to fibub:

- A worked example by hand: a made-up substance with a BMDL10 of 0.3 mg/kg bw/day at 50 µg/kg in a food gives margins of 2,100 (adult, 200 g/day, 70 kg), 700 (600 g/day) and 400 (child, 300 g/day, 20 kg), and the child's scenario would just meet a target of 100 at 200 µg/kg.
- Forty random cases with mixed concentration, intake and dose units, checked against an independent Python calculation of the exposure, the margin and the concentration meeting the target.
- New with this article: the satay sauce scenario must give an exposure of 0.0032320 µg/kg bw/day, a margin of 52.5987 and 0.0043657 µg/kg as the concentration meeting a target of 10,000, and the same intake at the limit of quantification must give a margin of 873.138.

## Check it yourself

On the [[margin-of-exposure]] page:

1. In *Point of departure*, type **Total aflatoxins** as the *Substance*, leave *Type* on **BMDL10**, set *Value* to **0.17** and *Unit* to **µg/kg bw/day**.
2. Set *Concentration unit* to **µg/kg** and leave *Intake unit* on **g/day**.
3. Replace the scenario box with three lines, each a name and then the concentration, the intake and the body weight, separated by commas (so keep commas out of the names):
   **Satay sauce high consumer per kg bw, 0.83, 3.894, 1**
   **Same numbers read as g per person, 0.83, 3.894, 60**
   **At the quantification limit per kg bw, 0.05, 3.894, 1**
4. Press **Use 10 000** under the uncertainty factors.
5. The headline reads **Lowest MOE 52.6**, and the table gives margins of **52.6**, **3,160** and **873**, with "no" in the target column for all three and **0.00437** µg/kg as the concentration meeting the target for the two per-kilogram rows.
6. Press **Use 10 × 10 = 100** and only the middle row meets the target: the conclusion depends entirely on which convention you apply.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Re-derive a published margin of exposure** before citing it, and find out which units the authors really used, as above.
- **Turn a margin into a laboratory specification**: the "concentration meeting the target" column says what a method must be able to measure for a given consumption, which is a fair way to choose a limit of quantification.
- **Compare population groups** (children, high consumers) in one table, since exposure scales with intake over body weight and small children usually set the lowest margin.
- **Teach the difference between threshold and non-threshold conventions** with the two target buttons: 10 × 10 = 100 for a NOAEL-type effect, 10,000 for a genotoxic carcinogen from a BMDL10.
- **Fit your own point of departure** with the [[benchmark-dose]] calculator or the [[dose-response]] fitter, then bring it here.

## The fine print

- This is a recomputation of one food from values stated in the paper's text. The paper's own margins are built on the whole consumption distribution, with upper-bound substitution for non-detects, and its category values come from tables we have not reproduced.
- The 60 kg body weight is our assumption, used only to show how much the reading of the intake unit matters.
- A margin of exposure is not a risk, and nothing here is health advice. Interpretation depends on the data and on each country's guidance: check the official source.
- Aflatoxin limits in food are set by law in most countries, separately from any margin of exposure; this article is about the arithmetic, not about compliance.
