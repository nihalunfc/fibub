When people work for decades next to buried industrial waste, the obvious question is whether they get more cancer than everyone else. A 2024 cohort study in *BMC Public Health* followed 590 employees of a German toxic-waste landfill and found the opposite: **fewer** cancers and fewer deaths than the surrounding population would predict. That kind of result lives or dies on one calculation, the ratio of observed events to expected events and its confidence interval, and the [[rate-standardisation]] tool does exactly that calculation in a browser. For the rest of a cohort analysis, the [[odds-risk-ratio]] tool compares two groups directly, [[survival-analysis]] handles time to event, and [[nnt-calculator]] turns a risk difference into people.

## The paper in brief

The landfill took industrial and household waste from 1983 onwards. The authors assembled everyone who had worked there for at least three months up to 2018: 590 people, 432 men and 158 women. Cancer registry and death records were then matched for 2009 to 2021, giving 6,374 person-years of follow-up for new cancers and 6,556 for deaths, a mean of 11.9 years each.

Because the cohort is small, the authors used **indirect standardisation**. The method takes the age-specific and sex-specific rates of a reference population (here the surrounding district for cancers, and the federal state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania for deaths), applies them to the cohort's own age and sex mix, and adds up how many events that would produce. Divide the real count by that expected count and you get a **standardised incidence ratio** (SIR) for new disease or a **standardised mortality ratio** (SMR) for deaths. One means "exactly as many as expected".

They counted 31 incident cancers against 44.8 expected, an SIR of 0.69, and 31 deaths against 60.3 expected, an SMR of 0.51. Both are below one, and the authors read this as the familiar **healthy-worker effect**: people well enough to hold a physical job are, as a group, healthier than the general population, which includes everyone too ill to work. They say plainly that the study cannot rule out a modest cancer risk, because the cohort is small and the follow-up short relative to the latency of most cancers.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the counts printed in the paper**, not a simulation. Put the observed count and the expected count into the tool's Indirect (SMR) mode and it returns the ratio, the exact Poisson interval and an exact test of "ratio = 1".

**Step 1: the three ratios the paper states with their counts.** All three come back to the last printed digit.

| Ratio | Observed / expected | Paper | Tool (exact Poisson) | Exact test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SIR, all cancers | 31 / 44.8 | 0.69 (0.47, 0.98) | **0.692 (0.470, 0.982)** | p = 0.038 |
| SMR, all causes | 31 / 60.3 | 0.51 (0.35, 0.73) | **0.514 (0.349, 0.730)** | p = 0.000049 |
| SMR, at least a year worked | 30 / 56.2 | 0.53 (0.36, 0.76) | **0.534 (0.360, 0.762)** | p = 0.00019 |

The exact test is worth noticing. The cancer deficit only just clears the usual line (p = 0.038, and the upper limit 0.982 only just misses one), while the mortality deficit is far clearer (p = 0.000049). Writing both as "significantly reduced" would flatten a real difference between them.

**Step 2: reading back the counts the paper did not print.** The paper gives the men's and women's ratios with intervals but not the counts behind them. An exact Poisson interval for a count divided by an expected number keeps its shape: the *ratio* of its upper to its lower limit depends only on the observed count. So we searched every whole count, and every expected count still allowed by the rounding of the printed ratio, and kept those whose limits round to exactly the three printed numbers.

| Printed in the paper | Counts that fit | Our reading | Tool from that reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| SIR men 0.72 (0.47, 1.05) | 26 or 27 | 26 observed, 36.1 expected | 0.720 (0.470, 1.055) |
| SIR women 0.59 (0.19, 1.38) | 5 only | 5 observed, 8.5 expected | 0.590 (0.192, 1.377) |
| SMR men 0.54 (0.36, 0.78) | 27, 28 or 29 | 29 observed, 53.7 expected | 0.540 (0.362, 0.776) |
| SMR women 0.30 (0.04, 1.09) | 2 only | 2 observed, 6.7 expected | 0.300 (0.036, 1.084) |

The choice inside each set is settled by the totals: 26 cancers in men plus 5 in women make the 31 the paper reports, and 29 deaths in men plus 2 in women make the other 31. Two deaths in women is the whole reason their SMR of 0.30 carries an interval reaching past one.

**Step 3: what a cohort of that size could ever have shown.** With so few expected events, one interval reaching past one is not evidence of "no effect"; it may simply be arithmetic. For any expected count we can ask the smallest shortfall and the smallest excess whose exact 95 % interval still clears one.

| Expected events | A shortfall is provable only at or below | An excess only at or above |
|---|---|---|
| 7 (women's deaths) | 0.14 | 2.00 |
| 9 (women's cancers) | 0.33 | 1.78 |
| 20 | 0.55 | 1.50 |
| 44.8 (the paper's cancers) | 0.69 | 1.33 |
| 60.3 (the paper's deaths) | 0.73 | 1.28 |
| 100 | 0.80 | 1.21 |

Read the women's row: with about seven expected deaths, nothing between 0.14 and 2.00 could have been shown either way, so their 0.30 was never going to be conclusive. The cohort's full-sample cancer result sits right at the edge of what 44.8 expected cases allows (0.69 against a limit of 0.69), which is why its interval almost touches one.

**Step 4: the crude rates, for context.** The tool's Crude rate mode turns the same counts into rates with exact Poisson limits: 31 cancers in 6,374 person-years is **486 per 100,000 person-years (330 to 690)**, and 31 deaths in 6,556 person-years is **473 per 100,000 person-years (321 to 671)**. These are crude, so they say nothing about age; they are the raw material the standardisation works on.

## How we made the graphs

Every number and both charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/german-landfill-workers-cancer-sir-smr-exact-poisson.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `indirect` and `crude` from `src/lib/sci/ratestd.ts`, the maths behind the [[rate-standardisation]] tool. `indirect` normally takes one row per age group (population and standard rate) and adds up the expected events; to feed it a total expected count straight from the paper we pass a single row of 100,000 people at a standard rate of E per 100,000, which makes the expected count exactly E.

The observed and expected counts, person-years and printed ratios are the paper's. The **sex-specific counts are our reading**, recovered as described in Step 2, not values the paper prints. The detection curves in Figure 2 are ours, computed from the same exact Poisson limits.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/rate-standardisation.mjs` on every change to fibub, pinning this maths against SciPy 1.18:

- A made-up cohort of 260 observed events against 199.5 expected must give an SMR of 1.3032581, exact Poisson limits of 1.1496445 and 1.4716814 (SciPy `chi2.ppf`) and an exact two-sided p of 4.7164e-05 (SciPy `poisson.cdf`/`sf`).
- 17 cases in 5,230.5 person-years must give 3.2501673 per 1,000 with exact limits 1.8933422 and 5.2038327.
- A directly standardised rate of 295.2222 per 100,000 must carry Fay-Feuer gamma limits of 257.4245 and 337.1825, as R's `epitools::ageadjust.direct` gives them.
- New with this article: this paper's three published ratios, pinned to 0.692/0.470/0.982 (cancers), 0.514/0.349/0.730 (deaths) and 0.534/0.360/0.762 (the one-year subset), each to four decimal places.

## Check it yourself

On the [[rate-standardisation]] tool page:

1. Press **Indirect (SMR)** in the *What do you want?* row. Leave *Show rates per* on **100 000** and *Confidence* on **95 %**.
2. Replace everything in the big box (*One row per age group...*) with one line: **All ages**, tab, **100000**, tab, **44.8**. A single row of 100,000 people at 44.8 per 100,000 makes the expected count 44.8, the paper's figure.
3. Put **31** in *Observed events in your study*.
4. The result reads **SMR 0.692 (69)**, with *95 % interval: 0.470 to 0.982 (exact Poisson)*. The facts list shows *Expected* **44.8**, *Excess (observed − expected)* **−13.8** and *Exact test of SMR = 1* **p = 0.038**.
5. Change the standard rate to **60.3** and the observed events to **31** for the mortality result: **SMR 0.514 (51)**, interval **0.349 to 0.730**, *p < 0.001*.
6. Now try the women's deaths: standard rate **6.7**, observed events **2**. You get **SMR 0.299**, interval **0.036 to 1.078** — the same two deaths, and an interval that cannot settle anything.
7. For the crude rate, press **Crude rate**, leave *Measure* on **Incidence rate (per person-time)**, put **31** in *Cases or events* and **6374** in *Person-years*: **486.4 per 100 000 person-years**, interval **330.5 to 690.3**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published SMR or SIR** in seconds: the counts are usually printed, and a ratio whose interval does not match its counts is worth a second look.
- **Recover counts from printed intervals** when a paper gives only ratios, using the trick in Step 2, before pooling results in the [[meta-analysis]] tool.
- **Size a cohort before you start**: Figure 2 says what deficit or excess a given number of expected events can ever establish, which is often the honest answer to "is this worth doing?".
- **Teach the healthy-worker effect** with real numbers: an all-cause SMR near 0.5 in an employed cohort is normal, not reassuring.
- **Standardise the other way** with the tool's Direct mode when your groups are big enough, so rates can be compared with each other rather than each with a standard.

## The fine print

- The paper's own expected counts come from age-specific and sex-specific reference rates we do not have; we take them as given and recompute only the ratios and their limits.
- Different reference populations give different expected counts. The authors show this themselves: using Schleswig-Holstein instead of the local district moves the cancer SIR from 0.69 to 0.71.
- The sex-specific counts in Step 2 are inferred, not published. The sets of counts that fit are exact; the single count we chose inside each set rests on the men and women adding to the printed totals.
- Indirectly standardised ratios from different cohorts each use their own age mix, so compare each with its standard, never with each other.
- An SMR below one is not a clean bill of health. Follow-up here is a mean of 11.9 years, short for most solid tumours, and 48 workers had unknown outcomes.
- General education, not medical or occupational-health advice.
