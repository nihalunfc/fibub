Every living cell is slightly negative inside, and the **resting membrane potential** sets how easily a nerve fires or a muscle contracts. Textbooks explain it with potassium and sodium. Calcium, at only a millimolar or two outside the cell, is usually left out. A 2024 paper in *Biology* shows it should not be: in fruit fly (*Drosophila*) larvae, raising the calcium around a body-wall muscle from 0.5 to 3 mM makes the muscle about 7.5 mV more negative. The authors suggest calcium plugs a small sodium leak channel. This article puts that idea into the [[membrane-potential]] calculator, which uses the **Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) equation**, and checks which of the paper's results it can and cannot explain. To see what the resting potential does to firing, try the [[hodgkin-huxley-simulator]]; to check that a swapped saline keeps its strength, the [[osmolarity-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors recorded from muscle 6 of third-instar larvae with sharp glass electrodes, in a standard saline called HL3 (sodium 80 mM, potassium 5 mM). They changed only the calcium, from 0.5 to 3 mM, and found:

- **Normal saline:** a hyperpolarisation (the inside became more negative) of **7.5 mV**.
- **Choline chloride saline**, where the 70 mM NaCl is replaced by choline, a large ion that cannot pass most channels: still **7.4 mV**.
- **Lithium chloride saline:** a smaller **2.9 mV**.
- **Barium instead of calcium:** a depolarisation of about 7 mV.
- **Muscles with extra ORK1**, a potassium "leak" channel that pins the cell near the potassium potential: **no clear change**.

The authors' leading explanation is that calcium blocks **NALCN**, a channel that lets a little sodium leak in at rest; with less sodium leaking in, the cell sits closer to the potassium potential. They say plainly that they did not test this directly.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **model built on our assumptions**, not a refit of the paper's recordings. The paper gives the outside concentrations and cites a potassium potential (E_K) of about −90 mV and a chloride potential of about −40 mV. We set the inside concentrations to match those, which gives **174.2 mM potassium** and **24.15 mM chloride** inside at 21 °C. We also assumed 10 mM sodium inside, a chloride permeability one tenth of potassium's, and a resting potential of **−60 mV** in normal saline at low calcium (**all our assumptions**; the paper reports only the changes). The GHK equation then gives the sodium leak, as a permeability ratio PNa/PK, that produces −60 mV: **0.127**.

**Step 1: how big a block?** To move from −60 to −67.5 mV, the calculator's maths needs PNa/PK cut to **0.070**, a **44.7 %** cut in the sodium leak. If the muscle starts at −65 or −70 mV instead, the cut needed is 53.3 % or 69.5 %. So a partial block of the leak is enough; nothing needs to close completely.

**Step 2: the choline result does not fit.** In choline saline only 10 mM sodium is left outside, the same as inside, so the sodium potential is **0 mV** and the leak barely pushes the cell. The model's resting potential there is −77.8 mV, and the same 44.7 % cut moves it only **1.6 mV** (Figure 1), not the 7.4 mV the paper measured. Starting from −65 or −70 mV gives 1.4 and 1.2 mV. Under the GHK picture, a calcium block of a sodium-only leak cannot explain most of the choline result. Either choline passes the leak channel, or calcium also acts another way, for example by changing the electric charge on the membrane surface, which shifts channel gating. The model cannot tell these apart, but it shows that the choline experiment is the key one.

**Step 3: the ORK1 result fits well.** Extra ORK1 channels raise the potassium permeability. With the same 44.7 % cut in the sodium leak, doubling PK shrinks the hyperpolarisation from 7.5 to **5.65 mV**; five times PK, to **3.25 mV**; ten times, to **1.9 mV** (Figure 2). When potassium dominates, the cell already sits near E_K and a change in a small sodium leak hardly matters. That matches the paper's explanation for why ORK1 muscles showed no clear effect.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/drosophila-larval-muscle-calcium-resting-membrane-potential-ghk.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `ghkVoltage`, `nernst` and `rtf` from `src/lib/sci/ghk.ts`, the maths of the [[membrane-potential]] calculator.

The outside concentrations come from the paper's saline recipe (sodium 70 + 10, potassium 5, chloride 70 + 5 + 40 + 2 = 117 mM), and the potassium and chloride potentials from values the paper cites. The temperature (21 °C), inside sodium, chloride permeability, starting potential and the idea that choline does not pass the leak are **our assumptions**. The script also reruns everything from −65 and −70 mV to show how much the conclusions depend on the starting point.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/membrane-potential.mjs`, which checks the tool against an independent NumPy and SciPy version:

- Nernst potentials at 37 °C: potassium 5/140 mM must give **−89.06 mV** and sodium 145/12 mM **+66.60 mV**.
- The GHK voltage for a textbook neuron (P 1 : 0.05 : 0.45) must be **−64.92 mV** at 37 °C, and **+51.13 mV** at the spike peak (PNa 20).
- The zero-current potential with calcium also permeant must match SciPy's `brentq` root: **−63.78 mV**.
- New with this article: the fly muscle model must give **−60.0 mV** with PNa/PK 0.127 and **−67.5 mV** with 0.070 in normal saline, but only **−77.8 and −79.5 mV** in choline saline, matching the GHK formula written out in the test.

## Check it yourself

On the [[membrane-potential]] calculator:

1. Set *Temperature (°C)* to **21** and *Permeabilities* to **As typed**.
2. In the *Ions* table type, for K⁺: outside **5**, inside **174.2**, permeability **1**; Na⁺: **80**, **10**, **0.127**; Cl⁻: **117**, **24.15**, **0.1**; and give Ca²⁺ a permeability of **0**.
3. The result reads *Membrane potential (GHK)* about **−60.0 mV**, and the K⁺ row's Nernst E about **−90 mV**.
4. Change the Na⁺ permeability to **0.07** (the calcium block): about **−67.5 mV**.
5. Now set Na⁺ outside to **10** (choline saline): **−79.5 mV**; put the permeability back to **0.127**: **−77.8 mV**. The same block moves it only about 1.7 mV with these rounded inputs.
6. For five times the K⁺ permeability, keep K⁺ at 1 (the table is relative to K⁺) and divide the others by 5: Na⁺ outside **80** with **0.0254**, Cl⁻ **0.02**. It reads about **−80.1 mV**, and with Na⁺ **0.014** about **−83.3 mV**: a step of about 3 mV, not 7.5.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan an ion-substitution experiment**: before swapping sodium for choline, lithium or NMDG, see how much a channel block can move the potential in each saline with the [[membrane-potential]] calculator.
- **Sanity-check a mechanism**: does a proposed leak channel explain the size of an effect, or would it need an impossible block?
- **Work out inside concentrations** from a measured or cited reversal potential with the Nernst equation, then reuse them.
- **Link rest to firing**: move the resting potential in the [[hodgkin-huxley-simulator]] and see how the threshold and spike change.
- **Teach** why a cell full of potassium channels ignores a small sodium leak, with numbers students can change.

## The fine print

- This is a GHK model with assumed inside concentrations, chloride permeability and starting potential. It is not the paper's data, and it leaves out pumps, calcium's own current and any surface-charge effects.
- The paper reports only changes in potential, so the starting point is our assumption; we show how the conclusions change from −60 to −70 mV.
- The paper does not give the recording temperature; we used 21 °C, the rearing temperature, which changes the numbers only slightly.
- Whether calcium really blocks NALCN in this muscle is the authors' hypothesis, still to be tested directly.
