Many papers in structural geology quote a fold's hinge, its interlimb angle and the lines where joints cut the bedding, and give them as bare numbers. Can you get them back from the bedding readings alone? A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Earth Science* describes a folded outcrop in the southern Yidun Arc, north-west Yunnan, China, and prints enough orientations that we can try. The sums are a job for the [[fold-axis-fit]] tool, with the [[stereonet]] for single planes and lines, the [[apparent-dip]] tool for true thickness, and the [[phone-clinometer]] for taking the readings in the field.

## The paper in brief

The authors studied a well-exposed outcrop of low-grade folded metasedimentary rock. Their question: how does slip between layers (**interlayer slip**) change cleavage and fractures that formed earlier or at the same time? They found that late **axial planar cleavage** (cleavage parallel to the fold's mirror plane) bends close to bedding surfaces, that small folds between layers share the big fold's hinge line, and that two joint sets, J1 and J2, formed after folding. They propose three stages: folding with flow in the rock, late cleavage changed by slip, and brittle jointing as the rocks were brought towards the surface.

The orientations in the text, which we read as **dip direction / dip** (our assumption): the fold's limbs have bedding of 230/56, 225/51 and 050/47; the hinge line plunges 3 degrees towards 320; the interlimb angle is about 77 to 80 degrees; the axial plane is 230/86 and the cleavage 220/85. J1 is 235/70 and J2 is 035/75; their intersections with bedding plunge 16 degrees towards 331 and 15 degrees towards 129. The paper gives no data table: all of these are in the text.

## Checked with the tools

We typed the two limbs into the Fold axis tool: the south-west limb as A and the north-east limb as B. With only two readings, the fold axis is the line common to both planes (the pole of the great circle that passes through both poles).

**Step 1: the hinge.** The tool gives a **horizontal axis trending 320**, exactly the trend the paper quotes; the paper's 3 degree plunge is 3.0 degrees away from our horizontal line. Using all three readings (the second south-west bedding 225/51 added to limb A), the best-fit axis plunges 1.6 degrees (a line along 139, pointing slightly up towards 319), within 4.8 degrees of the paper's hinge, and the three poles lie within 1.6 degrees (RMS) of one great circle: the fold is close to cylindrical. A fold axis from two or three readings is a first estimate; dozens of readings tighten it.

**Step 2: the interlimb angle.** For limbs dipping 56 degrees and 47 degrees in opposite directions about a horizontal axis, the angle between the limbs is 180 − 56 − 47 = **77.0 degrees**: the bottom of the paper's range, and an **open** fold in Fleuty's classes. With the third reading included, the tool gives **79.6 degrees**, near the top of the range. So the paper's 77 to 80 span is just two ways of averaging the same limbs, and Figure 2 shows how touchy it is: if the north-east limb dipped 44 to 47 degrees (with the other fixed at 56) the answer would be 77 to 80 degrees; a limb dip off by 5 degrees moves the interlimb angle by 5 degrees.

**Step 3: the axial plane.** The plane that contains the axis and splits the two limbs equally (the bisector) is, in the tool, strike 320, dip 85.5 degrees to the north-east. The paper's axial plane, 230/86, is a plane dipping 86 degrees to the south-west: the same strike (140 is the same line as 320), tilted the other way. The two planes are 8.5 degrees apart, a small angle for planes that are nearly upright, where a tiny tilt flips the dip direction. The paper's cleavage (220/85) tilts south-west too. So here the simple bisector and the field axial plane agree on strike and steepness but not on the side to which the plane tilts; a reader should not trust a bisector's tilt direction to a few degrees.

**Step 4: the joints.** The line where a joint plane cuts a bedding plane is the cross product of their poles. For J1 (235/70) and the south-west bedding (230/56) we get a line of **15.5 degrees** plunge along the 151 direction, or the same line pointing the other way up from 331. The paper quotes 16 degrees towards 331. For J2 (035/75) and the same bedding the line is 15.5 degrees towards 309, or pointing the other way, 129.2 degrees: the paper says 15 degrees towards 129. The match to within half a degree in plunge and a fraction of a degree in direction is what shows our reading of the notation (dip direction / dip) is right. The only difference: the paper quotes the end of the line as it rises, we as it plunges (a line has two ends, so we cannot say which end the authors meant).

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/fold-hinge-two-limbs-interlimb-angle-yidun-outcrop.mjs` calls `parsePlanes`, `fit` and `limbs` from `src/lib/sci/foldaxis.ts`, the maths of the [[fold-axis-fit]] tool, and `vec`, `pole`, `line`, `project` and `greatCircle` from `src/lib/sci/stereonet.ts`, the geometry the net and the [[stereonet]] share.

Every orientation comes from the paper's text. The reading as dip direction / dip is **our assumption**, backed by the matches. We drew our own net from the readings; the paper's stereonet is not copied.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/fold-axis-fit.mjs`, which compares the tool with an independent NumPy implementation (eigenvectors by `numpy.linalg.eigh`, Woodcock K and C, Vollmer indices, limb means, interlimb angle and axial plane) on 40 random sets and the page's example, and with folds worked by hand (an upright anticline, an overturned fold, a perfect cylinder). For this article we added two checks: the limbs 230/56 and 050/47 must give a horizontal axis trending 320, an interlimb angle of 77.00 degrees (open) and an axial plane of 320/85.5; and adding 225/51 to the first limb must give 79.56 degrees with the axis within 2 degrees of horizontal.

## Check it yourself

On the [[fold-axis-fit]] page, in the card *Bedding or foliation planes*:

1. Set *Planes typed as* to **Dip direction, dip**.
2. Clear the box and type, one per line: **230 56 A**, **50 47 B**. Look at *Fold axis* and *Limbs*: the axis reads about **0.0° towards 320**, the interlimb angle about **77.0°** and the fold is called open; the axial plane is about **320 / 85.5**.
3. Add a third line, **225 51 A**. The interlimb angle becomes about **79.6°** and the fold axis is a line plunging about **1.6°** (towards 139, as the tool shows the lower-hemisphere end).
4. Change the last line to **50 44 B**. The interlimb angle becomes about **82.6°** with three beds (**80.0°** if you delete the **225 51 A** line again): a 3 degree change in one dip moves the angle by about 3 degrees.
5. To get a joint-bedding intersection, clear the box and type **235 70 A** and **230 56 B**. With only two planes the fold axis is the line they share: it reads about **15.5° towards 151**. For J2, type **35 75 A** and **230 56 B**: about **15.5° towards 309**. Plot the same planes on the [[stereonet]] to see the great circles cross there.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Checking a quoted hinge or interlimb angle** against the readings in a figure or table before using it in a model.
- **Asking how many readings you need:** add or remove beds and watch the fold axis and interlimb angle move, as in step 4 above.
- **Cross-checking joint-bedding intersections** (the lines that control rock-fall wedges and fluid pathways) with the stereonet.
- **Teaching:** a two-reading fold, a one-page net and one cross product are enough to show what a hinge and an interlimb angle are.
- **Planning a section:** use the true dip and strike with the [[apparent-dip]] tool to see how steep the beds will look in a cliff face that is not across the strike.

## The fine print

- We used only the attitudes the authors print in their text; the measured poles are in their figures and we did not use them.
- With two or three readings a fold axis is a rough estimate; a cylindrical fold needs a spread of readings around the hinge.
- The paper's "∠" notation is not defined; we read it as dip direction / dip, and the matches support that.
- The axial plane we compute is a geometric bisector of two limbs; the field axial plane comes from cleavage and hinge-zone measurements, so a small difference is not an error in either.
