When the James Webb Space Telescope found JADES-GS-z14-0 in 2024, its redshift came from the **Lyman break**, the sharp edge where hydrogen blots out a galaxy's ultraviolet light. An edge is blurry: the estimate was 14.32, give or take about 0.1 to 0.2. A 2025 paper in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* by Stefano Carniani and colleagues pointed the ALMA radio array at the galaxy and caught a single sharp line from doubly ionised oxygen, [O III] at a rest wavelength of 88 µm. A line has an exact rest frequency, so it pins the redshift at **14.1796 ± 0.0007**, from oxygen seen from less than 300 million years after the Big Bang. This article reworks that redshift, the age of the universe it implies and the physical scale of the ALMA image with the [[redshift-calculator]] and the [[cosmology-calculator]]. To match other lines in an optical or infrared spectrum, use the [[spectral-line-identifier]]. A fainter neighbour at a similar redshift is covered in [[blog:discoveries/jades-gs-z14-1-faint-galaxy-redshift-14|our article on JADES-GS-z14-1]].

## The paper in brief

The team observed with ALMA's Band 6 receivers, tuned to cover 218 to 229 GHz. That range was chosen so the oxygen line would fall inside it anywhere in the Lyman-break redshift range. They found a line at **223.524 GHz**, with a width of 102 km/s (full width at half maximum). They saw no glow from dust, and the 3σ upper limit is 13 µJy.

From the oxygen line's strength, the galaxy's starlight and the missing dust, they argue three things. The gas already holds more than a tenth of the Sun's share of heavy elements, only about 290 million years after the Big Bang. The galaxy holds little cold gas. And strong outflows from star formation have probably pushed dust and gas out of it. The title's "eventful life" is that story of rapid enrichment and feedback.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the redshift from one frequency.** For a line seen at frequency ν_obs with rest frequency ν_rest, 1 + z = ν_rest / ν_obs. With the [O III] 88 µm rest frequency of 3393.006244 GHz and the observed 223.524 GHz, the redshift calculator's maths gives **z = 14.17961**, the paper's 14.1796. Light from the galaxy has been stretched **15.18 times**.

**Step 2: why one tuning was enough.** Figure 1 runs the same sum the other way: for each redshift, where would the line land? Across the Lyman-break range of 14.12 to 14.40 the line falls between **224.41 and 220.33 GHz**, inside the 218 to 229 GHz tuning with room to spare. The detection, at 223.524 GHz, sits near the low-redshift end of that range.

**Step 3: how far off the Lyman break was.** The break said 14.32; oxygen says 14.18. Put in the galaxy's own frame, that gap is c × Δz / (1 + z) = **2,773 km/s**. That is still inside the break's own −0.20 error, but it shows why a line is worth the telescope time. One cause of the offset, discussed for several galaxies of this kind, is that dense neutral gas around the galaxy softens the break and pushes it to the red.

**Step 4: age and scale.** With the paper's round-number cosmology (H₀ = 70 km/s/Mpc, Ωm = 0.3, flat), the cosmology maths gives:

| Quantity | Paper | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Redshift from 223.524 GHz | 14.1796 | 14.17961 |
| Kiloparsecs per arcsecond at z = 14 | 3.268 | 3.270 |
| Kiloparsecs per arcsecond at z = 14.18 | not given | 3.239 |
| Age of the universe then | "300 to 500 Myr" for this class | 287 million years (292 with Planck 2018) |
| Light-travel time | not given | 13.18 billion years |
| ALMA beam, 0.42″ × 0.38″ | not given | 1.36 × 1.23 kpc |

The small gap in the scale (3.270 against 3.268) comes from details of the cosmology code, such as how radiation is treated. The ALMA beam is more than a kiloparsec across, larger than the compact galaxy JWST sees, so ALMA does not show where inside it the oxygen sits.

**Step 5: the next lines to look for.** At the same redshift the [C II] 158 µm line, the other workhorse of high-redshift ALMA work, would land at **125.20 GHz** (ALMA Band 4), and the optical [O III] 500.7 nm line at **7.600 µm**, in reach of JWST's MIRI. The 102 km/s line width is **76 MHz** of sky frequency at 223.5 GHz, which tells you how fine the spectral channels must be.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/jades-gs-z14-0-alma-oxygen-88-micron-redshift-14-18.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `zFromFreq` and `velocity` from `src/lib/sci/redshift.ts`, the maths behind the [[redshift-calculator]], and `cosmology` with its presets from `src/lib/sci/cosmo.ts`, the maths behind the [[cosmology-calculator]]. It uses a flat universe with no radiation term, as the redshift calculator does.

The observed frequency, tuning range, Lyman-break redshift, line width and beam come from the paper's text. The rest frequencies come from the standard line lists. The direction of the break offset (neutral gas) is general background, not a result of this paper.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` checks the redshift maths: at z = 1 the relativistic velocity must be 179,875.47 km/s and the radio velocity 149,896.229 km/s, every convention must survive a round trip, and the 21 cm line must sit at 1.420405752 GHz. The cosmology checks include the exact age of a flat universe with matter and Λ, and the closed-form distances of an empty universe.

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/redshift-calculator.mjs` pins the [O III] 88 µm sum: 223.524 GHz must give z = 14.1796 within 0.0001, and one arcsecond at z = 14 must be 3.268 kpc within 0.005 with H₀ 70 and Ωm 0.3.

## Check it yourself

On the [[redshift-calculator]], in the *What you know* card:

1. Set *I know* to **Where a line is seen (frequency)** and *Unit* to **GHz**.
2. Set *Line* to **Another line** and type *Rest value* **3393.006244**.
3. In the value box type **223.524**. The result reads **z = 14.1796**, with wavelengths *stretched by* 15.1796 times.
4. Open *More options*, set *Cosmology for distances* to **Round numbers (H₀ 70, Ωm 0.3)**. *Age of the universe then* reads **0.287 billion years**, and *Angular size distance* says 1 arcsecond across is **3.24 kpc**.
5. Switch the cosmology to **Planck 2018**: the age becomes **0.292 billion years**. Then in *Any wavelength (nm)* type **500.684**: it *arrives at* about **7600 nm**, where MIRI looks.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a line search:** turn a photometric or Lyman-break redshift range into the frequency range a receiver must cover, with the [[redshift-calculator]].
- **Confirm a single-line redshift:** check that one line's frequency gives a redshift consistent with the break, then predict where a second line ([C II], CO) should be to confirm it.
- **Size things on the sky:** convert beams, apertures and half-light radii to kiloparsecs with the [[cosmology-calculator]], in the paper's cosmology or in Planck 2018.
- **Identify lines in a spectrum:** once the redshift is known, match the remaining features with the [[spectral-line-identifier]].
- **Teach redshift conventions:** the radio, optical and relativistic velocities of z = 14 differ by thousands of km/s, which is why astronomers quote z, not a speed.

## The fine print

- We recomputed values from the paper's text. We did not reprocess the ALMA data, and the paper's line detection and its physical models (metals, gas, dust) are beyond what these tools check.
- The rest frequency of the oxygen line is not printed in the paper; we used the standard value from the line lists.
- The age of the universe depends on the cosmology: 287 million years with H₀ 70 and Ωm 0.3, 292 million with Planck 2018.
- The galaxy is slightly magnified by a foreground galaxy (the paper uses a factor of 1.17 for luminosities); that does not change the redshift.
