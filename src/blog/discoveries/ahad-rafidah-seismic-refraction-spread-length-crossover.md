Before a new district is built, somebody has to find out how deep the soil goes and how strong the rock is underneath. A 2025 paper in *Open Geosciences* does that for Ahad Rafidah, about 18 km north-west of Khamis Mushait in southern Saudi Arabia, with ten **seismic refraction** spreads of 24 geophones at 5 m spacing — a 115 m line — plus two surface-wave profiles. It reports three velocity layers, alluvium 2 to 8 m thick and weathered basement reaching 30 m. Those are good, usable numbers, and they are also exactly the kind a forward model can audit. This article runs them through the [[seismic-refraction]] interpreter. To take the result further, measure the outcrop dips with the [[phone-clinometer]] and draw the ground with the [[cross-section-profile]] tool.

## The paper in brief

Each refraction profile is 115 m long: 24 geophones of 4.5 Hz at 5 m spacing, hit with a 10 kg sledgehammer at five positions (two off the end, then forward, middle and reverse). Two 60 m surface-wave (MASW) profiles with 1 m spacing give shear-wave speeds.

The authors find three layers: **alluvium at 300 to 940 m/s**, **weathered basement at 1,350 to 2,630 m/s** and **fresh basement at 2,400 to 6,876 m/s**. The alluvium is about 2 m thick in the north-east and thickens to about 8 m in the south-west, with 3 to 5 m under most profiles; the weathered basement reaches **30 m** under profiles 3 and 4. From the speeds they derive density (1.29 to 1.7 g/cm³), Poisson's ratio (0.235 to 0.238), elastic moduli and an ultimate bearing capacity of 0.215 to 6.17 kg/cm², and conclude the site is fit to build on with shallow foundations once the loose alluvium is accounted for.

## Checked with the tools

The picks themselves are not published, so we do the useful thing instead: build the model the paper describes, compute the travel times it must produce, and see what a 115 m spread would actually record. Our model takes **V1 = 530 m/s** (the median of the paper's ten per-profile values), **V2 = 1,890 m/s**, **V3 = 2,630 m/s**, **4 m** of alluvium and basement at **30 m** — the paper's own numbers, with the choice of a single representative profile as **our assumption**.

**Step 1: the shallow refractor is easy; the deep one never arrives.** The tool's forward model gives an intercept time of **14.49 ms** for the weathered-basement head wave, which overtakes the direct wave at a crossover of **10.7 m** — the third geophone. The fresh-basement head wave has an intercept of **33.92 ms** and a critical distance of 55.4 m, but it only becomes the **first arrival at 130.5 m** (Figure 1). On a 115 m spread it is always hidden behind the layer above it, so the depth to fresh basement is not measured by refraction on this geometry at all.

**Step 2: how fast would the basement have to be?** Keeping basement at 30 m and sweeping its speed, the crossover falls inside the 115 m spread only once V3 passes about **3,000 m/s**: 153 m at 2,400 m/s, 133 m at 2,600, 120 m at 2,800, 111 m at 3,000 and 89 m at 4,000 (Figure 2). The paper's range reaches 6,876 m/s, and at that end the branch appears at 70 m with room to fit a line — so profiles in hard, fresh granite are fine. It is the slow end of the reported range, 2,400 to 2,800 m/s, that a 115 m spread cannot reach at 30 m depth.

**Step 3: the interpreter gets the model back exactly, when the branch is there.** Feed the tool the synthetic picks and ask for two layers: it returns **530 and 1,890 m/s** with **4.00 m** of alluvium, a fit RMS of 0.00 ms. Given both crossovers (10.7 m and 130.5 m) and the three speeds, the crossover-depth method returns **4.01 m and 26.00 m** — the full model. The maths is not the limit here; the spread length is.

**Step 4: 1 ms of pick error moves the top-layer speed by 80 m/s.** With the crossover at 10.7 m, only the geophones at 0, 5 and 10 m record the direct wave, so V1 rests on three picks. Adding a 1 ms standard deviation to every pick (**our assumption**, a fair figure for a sledgehammer source) and re-interpreting 120 times gives **V1 = 554 ± 81 m/s** (364 to 793 m/s across the draws), **V2 = 1,888 ± 25 m/s** and an alluvium thickness of **4.20 ± 0.70 m** (2.65 to 6.45 m). The paper's spread of top-layer speeds between profiles, **300 to 940 m/s**, is barely wider than what pick error alone produces on one profile (Figure 3). Thicker alluvium pushes the crossover out and fixes this: at 8 m the crossover is 21.5 m and five geophones see the direct wave.

**Step 5: Poisson's ratio here is an assumption, not a measurement.** The paper's per-profile table lists Vp and Vs side by side. Divide them: **1.7003, 1.6998, 1.7010, 1.6993, 1.6987, 1.7029, 1.7021, 1.6987, 1.6981, 1.7045** — the ratio is 1.70 to within 0.3 % on every profile, so Vs has been set to Vp ÷ 1.7 rather than taken from the MASW profiles. Poisson's ratio follows arithmetically: σ = (Vp² − 2Vs²) ÷ 2(Vp² − Vs²) gives **0.2345 to 0.2376**, which is the 0.235 to 0.238 the paper reports. That is self-consistent, but it means the ratio, and the moduli and bearing capacities built on it, carry no independent information about the ground.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ahad-rafidah-seismic-refraction-spread-length-crossover.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `forwardTimes`, `segment`, `crossover`, `interceptDepths` and `crossoverDepths` from `src/lib/sci/refraction.ts`, the maths behind the [[seismic-refraction]] interpreter; Poisson's ratio is the standard formula applied to the paper's own table.

From the paper: the geometry (24 geophones, 5 m spacing, 115 m spread), the three velocity ranges, the per-profile Vp and Vs, the alluvium thickness and the 30 m basement. **Our assumptions:** the single representative profile (530, 1,890 and 2,630 m/s with 4 m and 26 m thicknesses), the 1 ms pick scatter and the 120 random draws. The picks are **simulated**; the paper's own first arrivals are not published.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/seismic-refraction.mjs`, which pins the interpreter against hand-worked geometry:

- first arrivals for a 500/1,800/4,000 m/s model with 4 m and 10 m layers must match ray paths found by Fermat's principle in SciPy, to **1e-9 s**;
- from those noise-free picks the automatic break finder must return all three speeds to 1e-6 m/s, and both depth methods must give back **4 m and 10 m**; the two-layer crossover must satisfy h₁ = (xc ÷ 2) × √((V2 − V1) ÷ (V2 + V1));
- a dipping two-layer case must return **V2 = 2,400 m/s and a dip of 4°** with vertical depths of 6 m and 6 + 125 tan 4° under the two shots, and swapping the shots must flip the dip;
- a layer slower than the one above it must be reported as hidden, and too few picks must be refused;
- **new with this article:** the three-layer model above must give intercepts of **14.49 ms and 33.92 ms**, crossovers of **10.7 m and 130.5 m**, a critical distance of 55.4 m for layer 3, and `crossoverDepths` must return **4.01 m and 26.00 m**; within a 115 m spread only two branches may appear as first arrivals.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Design the spread before you drive the geophones in.** Put your expected speeds and depths in the forward model and read the crossover: if it is longer than your line, the layer will not appear, whatever you do with the data.
- **Quote a thickness with its pick error.** Re-interpret your own picks with a millisecond of scatter and report the spread; with the [[seismic-refraction]] tool that is a minute's work.
- **Check somebody else's three-layer section.** A deep refractor with a modest velocity contrast needs a long line; the crossover tells you whether the section can be believed.
- **Spot derived numbers in a table.** Divide Vp by Vs down a column: a constant ratio means one of them was assumed, and every modulus built on it inherits that.
- **Pair methods properly.** Surface waves see the slow layers refraction skips; a radargram from the [[gpr-viewer]] fills in the top few metres.

## The fine print

- Our picks are **simulated** from the paper's own velocity and thickness figures; its real first arrivals are not published, so nothing here is a fit to its data.
- A single flat three-layer model stands in for ten profiles over sloping ground. The paper's five-shot layout, including the two offset shots and the tomography, recovers more than our simple model allows for — our crossover arithmetic applies to the classical intercept-time reading, which is what the reported layer speeds and depths come from.
- The 1 ms pick scatter is our assumption. Better sources and stacking do better; a single hammer blow in dry gravel often does worse.
- The paper's bearing capacities depend on density and modulus relations we have not checked here; our Poisson's ratio point is about where the number comes from, not whether the site is safe to build on.
