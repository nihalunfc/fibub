A lump of fissile metal is critical when each generation of neutrons makes exactly as many neutrons as the last. For simple shapes the one-group diffusion equation gives that size in closed form, but as soon as the shape is awkward, like a hemisphere, the closed form runs out and you need a series or a numerical solve. A 2023 paper in *Frontiers in Physics* does exactly that with a **residual power series**, and reports critical radii for a sphere and a hemisphere of pure plutonium-239 under three different boundary conditions. The sphere is the case anyone can check, so we rebuilt it with the [[neutron-diffusion]] calculator. For what happens after such a core is switched off, see the [[decay-heat|decay heat estimator]] and the [[point-kinetics|point kinetics simulator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors write the one-group, steady-state diffusion equation for a homogeneous ball of fissile metal and solve it as a power series in the radius, choosing the coefficients so that the residual of the equation vanishes term by term. For a sphere the answer is the textbook one; the point of the method is that the same machinery also handles a **hemisphere**, where the flux depends on both the radius and the polar angle, and where an extra parameter describes how much the flat face is coupled to a neighbouring hemisphere.

The material is pure plutonium-239 treated with a single neutron energy group: a fission cross section of 0.0816 per cm, scattering 0.225216 per cm, capture 0.019584 per cm, and 2.84 neutrons per fission. The paper gives three answers for the sphere, one per boundary condition: the flux forced to zero at the metal surface (**8.7864 cm**), an extrapolated boundary (**6.9138 cm**), and a reflective, partial-current boundary (**6.5021 cm**), which is the most realistic of the three. The hemisphere needs 9.0956 to 12.5674 cm, depending on the same choice. The series converges to about five decimal places with roughly 22 terms.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the cross sections printed in the paper**, not a refit of any data. The paper's numbers and ours should agree exactly where the physics is the same, and the interesting part is where they do not.

**Step 1: the material constants.** From the three cross sections, the total is 0.3264 per cm, so the diffusion coefficient is D = 1/(3Σt) = **1.02124183 cm**, matching the paper's 1.021241834 to nine figures. Absorption is fission plus capture, 0.101184 per cm, so the infinite-medium multiplication is k∞ = νΣf/Σa = **2.29032** and the diffusion area is L² = D/Σa = **10.09292 cm²**. The tool's `materialB2` then gives the buckling that makes k_eff exactly 1:

B² = (k∞ − 1)/L² = 0.12784435 per cm², so B = **0.357553286 per cm**.

The paper quotes 0.357553294. The two agree to eight figures, which is the strongest kind of check: the same formula, independently coded, on the same numbers.

**Step 2: the critical radius.** For a sphere whose flux is forced to zero at radius R, the geometric buckling is π²/R², so the critical radius is π/B. The tool returns **8.7864 cm**, exactly the paper's zero-flux value.

Real metal does not have zero flux at its surface; neutrons leak out and the flux only extrapolates to zero a little way outside. The tool uses the standard extrapolation distance of 2.13 D, here 2.175 cm, and so makes the **metal** critical at a radius of **6.6111 cm**. That sits 1.7 % above the paper's reflective-boundary answer of 6.5021 cm and 4.4 % below its extrapolated-boundary entry of 6.9138 cm. (The paper's text and its Table 5 disagree about which radius belongs to the extrapolated condition, so we compare with both.) Put the paper's 6.5021 cm into the tool and it reads k_eff = **0.98596**: 1.4 % subcritical, which is the price of the simple extrapolation rule.

**Step 3: the flux shape and a numerical cross-check.** The same tool solves the problem a second way, with finite volumes and power iteration, which is useful because that is the method the paper's series is competing with. On the critical sphere, 120 cells give k = **1.0000191** and a flux profile that lies on top of the sin(Br)/Br formula to better than 0.1 %. The flux at the metal surface is still **30 %** of the centre value, which is why the boundary condition matters so much.

**Step 4: other shapes for free.** The paper needed a series for the hemisphere. For any shape whose buckling is known the tool gives the critical size at once from the same B²: a slab **4.436 cm** thick, an infinitely tall cylinder of radius **4.551 cm**, a cylinder as tall as it is wide with radius **5.858 cm**, or a cube of side **10.868 cm** (all as physical sizes, after the extrapolation distance is taken off). The critical sphere holds about 1,210 cm³ of metal. A hemisphere is not in that list, and that is exactly the gap the paper's method fills.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/plutonium-sphere-critical-radius-neutron-diffusion.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `materialB2`, `kInf`, `L2`, `keff1`, `buckling`, `extrapolated`, `extrap`, `criticalSize`, `shape`, `volume` and `fdSolve` from `src/lib/sci/eng-diffusion.ts`, the maths behind the [[neutron-diffusion]] calculator.

The three cross sections and the 2.84 neutrons per fission come from the paper's text. The extrapolation distance of 2.13 D is the tool's own default, not the paper's boundary condition: that difference is the point of step 2. Nothing here is fitted and no data are simulated.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/neutron-diffusion.mjs`, which pins this tool against SciPy and against an independent eigenvalue solve:

- the first zero of the Bessel function J₀ to 2.4048255576957724 and J₀ itself at eight points to 1 part in 10¹³;
- the critical slab, sphere, cylinder and cube sizes of the tool's example material against SciPy root-finding of k_eff(size) = 1, to 1 part in 10¹⁰, and the two-group critical buckling to 1 part in 10⁹;
- the finite-volume solve against the closed form, with k within 1 part in 10⁵, the flux shape within 10⁻³, and the error falling fourfold when the number of cells doubles.
- New with this article: the paper's plutonium data must give D = 1.0212418, B = 0.3575533 per cm, a zero-flux critical radius of 8.78636 cm and a physical critical radius of 6.61112 cm with the 2.13 D rule, and the finite-volume solve must put k within 3 × 10⁻⁵ of 1 there.

## Check it yourself

On the [[neutron-diffusion]] page, in the *Material* card:

1. Leave *Model* on **One group**. Type *Diffusion coefficient D (cm)* **1.0212418**, *Absorption Σa (1/cm)* **0.101184** and *Neutron production νΣf (1/cm)* **0.231744**.
2. In *Shape and size*, choose **Sphere** and type *Radius R (cm)* **6.6111**.
3. Leave **Add the extrapolation distance (2.13 D) to each outer face** ticked. The *Criticality* card reads k_eff ≈ **1.000** (critical), with k∞ **2.2903** and L² **10.09 cm²**.
4. Change *Radius R (cm)* to **6.5021**, the paper's reflective-boundary answer: k_eff falls to about **0.986**.
5. Untick the extrapolation box and type **8.7864**: k_eff is back to **1.000**, the paper's flux-zero radius.
6. Look at *Critical sizes* for the slab, cylinder and box figures quoted above, and at the *Flux shape* chart for the sin(Br)/Br profile.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published critical size** in a minute, before trusting a longer calculation: the buckling and k∞ follow from three cross sections.
- **See how much the boundary condition is worth.** Switching the extrapolation distance on and off here moves the critical radius by 25 %, which dwarfs most cross-section uncertainties.
- **Compare a series or Monte Carlo result with finite volumes** on the same material, and watch the numerical error shrink as cells are added.
- **Teach leakage.** The flux is still 30 % of its peak at the surface of a bare core, so a reflector is not a detail.
- **Plan a two-group study**: the same page does the fast and thermal model, which is where thermal-reactor work starts.

## The fine print

- One-group diffusion theory is a teaching model. For bare fast metal, where neutrons travel far compared with the core, transport theory is the right tool, and published transport critical radii are smaller than diffusion ones.
- The paper lists its cross sections without units; they only make sense as per-cm macroscopic values, and we read them that way, which reproduces the paper's own D and B.
- We checked the **sphere** only. The hemisphere, the paper's real contribution, has no closed form in the tool.
- The tool's extrapolation distance is a fixed 2.13 D, not the paper's exact boundary conditions, so a 1 to 4 % difference in the metal's critical radius is expected, not an error in either.
