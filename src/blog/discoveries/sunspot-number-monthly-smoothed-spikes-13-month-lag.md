Every space-weather chart shows two sunspot curves: the **monthly** number, which jumps about, and the **13-month smoothed** number, which is what people mean by "the solar cycle". A 2026 paper in *Universe* measures the gap between them across the whole record from 1749, and sets a clear rule for a **spike**: a month whose unsmoothed number sits beyond the 95th or 5th percentile of that gap, which is **+51.6** or **−41.0**. This article shows that most of the paper's statistics follow from two ingredients — a smooth cycle and month-to-month scatter that grows with activity — using the [[solar-cycle-fitter]]'s own smoothing and shape maths. Along the way the [[distribution-explorer]] and the [[time-series-analyser]] are the tools to reach for if you want to repeat it on the real series.

## The paper in brief

The authors take SILSO version 2 monthly and 13-month smoothed sunspot numbers from 1749 to October 2025, form the difference DIF = SNm − SNsm for every month, and describe it. The distribution is narrow and long-tailed: **74 %** of months fall between −20 and +20, about **1 %** lie beyond ±70, **54.5 %** are negative against 44.9 % positive, the mean is **0.0** and the median **−1.5**, with skewness **−0.55** and kurtosis **5.95** — not a Gaussian.

Spikes, by their percentile rule, appear in almost every cycle and cluster near maxima: about 55 % negative and 45 % positive, with five positive spikes above 100 (the largest, 133.9, in May 1778) and none below −100. Cycles 5 and 20 have none; Cycles 2, 8, 9, 11, 18 and 19 have ten or more. Across cycles, the monthly maximum and the smoothed maximum track each other closely (r = 0.95), and the median gap between them is **64.0**. For Cycle 25 that gap looked unusually large at first — **76.1** in early 2024 — and then shrank to **55.1** once the smoothed curve had caught up a year later.

## Checked with the tools

Nothing here uses SILSO data: this is a **simulation** built from the Solar cycle fitter's own cycle shape and its example noise model, then analysed with the same 13-month smoothing and quantile code the tool runs. The question we ask is: how much of the paper's description is solar physics, and how much is arithmetic?

**Step 1: the smoothing has a six-month blind spot.** The smoothed value for a month needs the six months before and after it, as a tapered mean: (Rₙ₋₆ + 2 × the eleven middle months + Rₙ₊₆) ÷ 24. Run the tool's `smooth13` on any series and the first **six** and last **six** months come back empty. That is the "intrinsic lag" the paper mentions, and it is the whole explanation of Cycle 25's shrinking gap: in February 2024 the smoothed maximum could not yet include the months that followed, so it read 136.9 and the gap looked like 76.1. A year later the smoothed maximum had grown to 160.9 and the gap had fallen to 55.1 — close to the historical median of 64.0, which is to say Cycle 25 was never unusual in this respect.

**Step 2: a cycle plus scatter.** We built 24 made-up cycles with the fitter's shape function (amplitudes 60 to 250, lengths 10.4 to 12 years, 3,348 months in all — about the length of the real record), added monthly scatter of σ = 4 + f × level, smoothed with `smooth13`, and measured the same quantities the paper reports. The only free number is **f**, the scatter as a share of the activity level. Sweeping it:

| Scatter f | 95th percentile | 5th percentile | Within ±20 | Beyond ±70 | Median gap at maximum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.15 (the tool's example) | +26.0 | −24.6 | 84.5 % | 0.3 % | 32.0 |
| 0.20 | +32.6 | −30.8 | 79.3 % | 1.3 % | 41.6 |
| 0.25 | +39.4 | −37.5 | 74.7 % | 2.5 % | 51.2 |
| **0.30** | **+46.0** | **−44.4** | **70.6 %** | 3.9 % | **60.9** |
| 0.35 | +52.7 | −50.9 | 67.6 % | 5.2 % | 71.1 |
| The paper (real Sun) | **+51.6** | **−41.0** | **74 %** | about 1 % | **64.0** |

Monthly sunspot numbers scatter by **about 30 % of the current level**, and that single number reproduces the paper's spike thresholds to within about 10 %, its share of negative months and its typical gap at cycle maximum.

**Step 3: what the arithmetic explains, and what it does not.**

| Check | Paper (real series) | Our simulation (f = 0.30) |
|---|---|---|
| Mean of SNm − SNsm | 0.0 | 0.00 |
| Median | −1.5 | −0.75 |
| Share negative | 54.5 % | 53.8 % |
| 95th / 5th percentile | +51.6 / −41.0 | +46.0 / −44.4 |
| Within ±20 | 74 % | 70.6 % |
| Kurtosis | 5.95 | 8.51 |
| Skewness | −0.55 | +0.12 |
| SNm-max against SNsm-max | slope 1.2 ± 0.9, r = 0.95 | slope 1.5, r = 0.986 |
| Median SNm-max − SNsm-max | 64.0 | 60.9 |

Four results need no solar physics at all. The **mean of zero** is forced: a running mean has to have the deviations around it average out. The **median slightly below zero with more negative months than positive** follows from scatter that grows with activity: a few large positive excursions near maxima balance many small negative ones in the quiet years. The **fat tails** (kurtosis well above 3) come from mixing quiet months and active months in one distribution. And **spikes clustering near maxima** is just the scatter being proportional to the level.

Two results do not fall out of the arithmetic. Our simulation gives a slightly **positive** skew where the real series is **negative** (−0.55), and our tails are heavier than the real ones (kurtosis 8.5 against 5.95, and 3.9 % beyond ±70 against about 1 %). Both point the same way: real monthly scatter is not symmetric Gaussian noise on the cycle. The paper's own Student-t and skew exponential power fits say the same thing with more care, and the asymmetry — a long negative tail after smoothing — is where the physics (and the observing history behind the earliest cycles) actually lives.

**Step 4: the percentile rule is self-calibrating, and that matters.** Because the thresholds are percentiles of the record itself, 5 % of months are spikes by construction: our simulation gives exactly 167 high and 167 low spikes out of 3,336 months. So "how many spikes in this cycle" is a statement about how that cycle compares with the whole record, never about an absolute level — worth remembering before reading a spike count as a physical event.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/sunspot-number-monthly-smoothed-spikes-13-month-lag.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `shape()`, `peakX()`, `smooth13()`, `findExtremes()`, `quantile()`, `decYear()` and `rng()` from `src/lib/sci/solarcycle.ts`, the maths behind the [[solar-cycle-fitter]]: the same tapered 13-month mean, cycle shape, minimum and maximum finder and interpolated quantiles the tool uses on a pasted series.

From the paper: the spike thresholds, the distribution shares, the mean, median, skewness and kurtosis, the regression of SNm-max on SNsm-max, the median gap of 64.0 and the Cycle 25 figures. **Our assumptions:** 24 cycles with amplitudes drawn between 60 and 250 and shape constant c = 0.7 (the fitter's example value), spacing 10.4 to 12 years, Gaussian monthly scatter of σ = 4 + f × level, and the sweep over f. The series is **made up**; it is not the sunspot record.

## How we test the maths

`node tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/solar-cycle-fitter.mjs` on every change to fibub:

- `smooth13` of a constant series must return that constant, and the first and last six months must be empty — the tapered 13-month mean, weights 1 : 2 × 11 : 1 over 24.
- The fitted shape must put each cycle's peak at the root of e^x²(3 − 2x²) = 3c, and a fit to values generated from a known shape must recover its amplitude and peak time.
- `quantile` must match numpy's linear interpolation on a short list.
- New with this article: a 13-point ramp 0, 1, … 12 must smooth to 6.0 at its middle month; and for our 24-cycle simulated record the 95th and 5th percentiles of monthly minus smoothed must be +46.04 and −44.40, with 53.8 % of months negative.

## Check it yourself

On the [[solar-cycle-fitter]]:

1. Press **Use the made-up example** in the *Monthly numbers* card. It loads 50 years of made-up monthly values (not real sunspot data) in `YYYY-MM,value` form.
2. In *Settings*, leave *What the numbers are* on monthly values, *Shortest time between minima (years)* on its default and *Shape constant c* at **0.7**.
3. The chart draws your monthly points with the 13-month smoothed curve through them. Note that the curve stops **six months before the last data point**: that is the lag that made Cycle 25's gap look large in early 2024.
4. In *Cycle by cycle*, read each cycle's smoothed maximum and its date. Compare it with the highest monthly point in the same cycle: in the example the four complete cycles give gaps of 27.9, 75.2, 35.3 and 25.4, which straddles the paper's historical median of 64.0 for the real Sun.
5. Change *Fit each cycle to* between **Monthly values** and the smoothed curve and watch the fitted peak heights move: fitting monthly values chases the spikes.
6. To repeat the paper's distribution work on your own series, paste the monthly minus smoothed differences into the [[distribution-explorer]] and compare a normal, a Student-t and a skew distribution, as the paper does.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Judge whether a "record" gap is real**: the smoothed curve near the end of a series is provisional, so any difference from it is too.
- **Set a spike rule and say what it is**: percentile thresholds move with the record you apply them to, so quote the record and the percentile.
- **Separate arithmetic from physics**: simulate a cycle with proportional scatter first, and only treat what the simulation cannot reproduce as a solar result.
- **Estimate month-to-month variability from published summaries**: matching the gap at cycle maximum gave about 30 % scatter without touching the data.
- **Teach smoothing honestly**: the same tool shows the lag, the edge effects and what a running mean does to a spiky series.

## The fine print

- Our series is **made up**. It tests which of the paper's statistics follow from smoothing plus proportional scatter, and says nothing about any individual cycle.
- Our cycle amplitudes are drawn uniformly between 60 and 250, which is wider and flatter than the real distribution of cycle strengths.
- Gaussian scatter is **our assumption**, and the places our numbers miss the paper's (skewness, the exact tail weight) are exactly where that assumption fails.
- The earliest cycles in the real record rest on far fewer observers, which the paper is careful about when discussing its three largest eighteenth-century spikes. Nothing in our simulation captures that.
- We have not re-derived the paper's parametric fits (Gaussian, Student-t, skew exponential power); their published parameters are quoted as facts only.
