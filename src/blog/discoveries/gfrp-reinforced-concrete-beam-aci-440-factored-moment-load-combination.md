Steel rebar rusts. Bars made of glass fibre in a plastic resin (GFRP) do not, which is why they are used in marine structures, bridge decks and wherever salt is a problem. They also behave differently from steel: they are much less stiff than steel (about 45 GPa against 200 GPa), they do not yield, and so a beam is designed to be crushed in the concrete first rather than to snap the bars. A 2023 conference paper walks through one complete design to the American ACI 440 guide, printing each intermediate number. The first of those numbers is the design moment, which the [[load-combination-helper|load combination helper]] works out from the dead and live moments; the next ones, bar areas and weights, belong to the [[rebar-calculator|rebar calculator]]. Beam bending itself is a job for the [[beam-calculator|beam deflection calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The design example is a rectangular beam **300 mm wide** and 500 mm deep (effective depth **414 mm**) in 28 MPa concrete, carrying a dead-load moment of **76 kN·m** and a live-load moment of **47 kN·m**. The factored design moment is `1.2 × 76 + 1.6 × 47` = **166.4 kN·m**. GFRP bars with a guaranteed strength of 550 MPa are reduced by an environmental factor of 0.8 to a design strength of 440 MPa, and a modulus of 45 GPa. The authors work out a balanced reinforcement ratio of **0.01079** (the amount at which concrete and bars fail together), choose 1.5 times that, find a required area of about **2011 mm²** and provide **four No. 25 bars** (2040 mm² by the table value of 510 mm² per bar). A strength factor of 0.65 applies because the section is compression-controlled. The paper does not print the beam's capacity, span or deflection, so this article closes that gap.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the design moment, and what other codes would say.** The [[load-combination-helper|load combination helper]], given the two moments and the combination `1.2D + 1.6L`, returns **166.4 kN·m**, the paper's number. The same loads under other rules give different answers: `1.35D + 1.5L` (the European form) gives **173.1 kN·m**, 4 % higher; `1.4D` gives 106.4 kN·m, far lower; and the unfactored service moment is 123 kN·m. Which of the American combinations governs depends on the live share: `1.4D` wins only when live load is below about **11 %** of the total (live over dead below 0.125). Here it is 38 %, so `1.2D + 1.6L` governs. Figure 1 shows the lines cross at 11 %.

**Step 2: the bar areas.** A 25.4 mm bar has a true area of `π d² / 4` = **506.7 mm²**, a little less than the 510 mm² the paper takes from a table. Four bars therefore give **2026.8 mm²**, against a required **2011 mm²**: the margin is 0.8 %, and three bars (1520 mm²) would not do (Figure 2). The rebar calculator uses the true areas, so a design checked against it and against a manufacturer's table can differ by about 0.7 %.

**Step 3: does the design close?** Using the same ACI 440 equations the paper uses (our own evaluation in the script, not a fibub tool), the balanced ratio is **0.01080**, the design ratio 0.01619 gives a required area of **2011 mm²** (the paper's number), the fibre stress at failure is **348.7 MPa** (paper: 349) and the nominal moment is 255.8 kN·m. Multiplied by the strength factor of 0.65 this gives a design capacity of **166.3 kN·m**, against a demand of 166.4: the paper sized the bars to the demand with almost nothing to spare. With the four bars actually provided (2040 mm² by the paper, 2027 mm² by true area) the capacity is **167.1** or **166.7 kN·m**: it passes, by 0.4 % and 0.2 %.

**Step 4: the weight of the bars.** The rebar calculator's mass per metre formula gives **15.9 kg per metre** for four steel bars of 25.4 mm. GFRP is about 3.7 times lighter for the same volume: with a density of 2100 kg/m³ (**our assumption**, typical of fibre-reinforced bars; the paper states none), four bars weigh **4.3 kg per metre**. That is one reason these bars are easier to handle on site.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/gfrp-reinforced-concrete-beam-aci-440-factored-moment-load-combination.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `parseLoads()`, `parseCombos()` and `evaluate()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-loadcomb.ts` (the [[load-combination-helper|load combination helper]]) and `area()` and `massPerM()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-rebar.ts` (the [[rebar-calculator|rebar calculator]]).

The moments, geometry, material strengths and the paper's reported results come from the paper. The ACI 440 balanced ratio, fibre stress and nominal moment equations are evaluated directly in the script from the paper's inputs (they are plain formulas, not part of a fibub tool), and the GFRP density and the 123 kN·m fixed service moment in Figure 1 are **our assumptions**.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/load-combination-helper.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the tool against an independent NumPy calculation (factor vectors times the load matrix for every sign of the reversible loads):

- The example set of seven combinations on five load cases must give 11 rows (reversible wind and earthquake loads each tried both ways), with maxima 860, 190.75 and 87.3 in rows 1, 3 and 3.
- Minima 304, −101.5 and −42.6 must all fall in row 8.
- New with this article: moments of 76 and 47 kN·m must give 166.4 (1.2D + 1.6L), 106.4 (1.4D), 173.1 (1.35D + 1.5L) and 123 kN·m (D + L), with the European combination as the largest.

The rebar formulas (area and mass per metre) are pinned in `tests/sci-tools/rebar-calculator.mjs`.

## Check it yourself

On the [[load-combination-helper|load combination helper]]:

1. In the loads box type three lines: `Load, M (kN·m)`, `D, 76` and `L, 47`.
2. In the combinations box type `ACI: 1.2D + 1.6L`, then add `EN: 1.35D + 1.5L` and `Service: D + L` on lines of their own.
3. Read the table: **166.4**, **173.1** and **123** kN·m, with the largest design moment coming from the second combination.

Then on the [[rebar-calculator|rebar calculator]]: a bar of 25.4 mm diameter has an area of 506.7 mm² and, in steel, a mass of 3.98 kg per metre, so four bars come to 15.9 kg per metre.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check which load combination governs** before designing: if your live share is high, the factored values differ by several per cent between codes.
- **Audit a worked design:** rebuild each printed number (demand, required area, provided area, capacity) and see how much margin the paper really left.
- **Quote materials fairly:** compare bar weights and areas between steel and GFRP with the [[rebar-calculator]], using each product's own data sheet.
- **Follow up on bending and deflection** with the [[beam-calculator]], and check the concrete with the [[concrete-mix-design|concrete mix calculator]].

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation** of a worked example, not a test of a beam. The paper's own equations were garbled in the text available to us, so we used the standard ACI 440 equations for the same inputs and checked that they return the paper's printed numbers (balanced ratio, required area, fibre stress).
- The paper does not explain how it gets from its calculated depth to an effective depth of 414 mm, and gives no span, capacity or deflection values. Our capacity of 166.3 to 167.1 kN·m is our own, from the stated area and strengths, and is not a value from the paper.
- GFRP beams are often governed by deflection and crack width, not strength; these serviceability checks are not covered here.
- Code factors (1.2, 1.6, 0.65 and the rest) change with the edition and country. This is general education: check the official source, and use a qualified structural engineer for anything built.
