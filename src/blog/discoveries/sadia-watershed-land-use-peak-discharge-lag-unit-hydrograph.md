When a city grows over its own catchment, two things happen to the flood hydrograph at once: more rain runs off instead of soaking in, and what does run off arrives sooner. A 2025 paper in *TEKNIK* modelled both for the downstream Sadia watershed in Bima City, Indonesia, using HEC-HMS with the SCS curve number and an SCS synthetic unit hydrograph, and reported a **7 % rise in the 25-year peak discharge** between the 2015 and 2022 land cover, with the **time lag shortening by about a quarter of an hour**. The paper gives both numbers but not the split between them, and the split is what a drainage engineer needs. The [[unit-hydrograph]] builder can do that separation from the two published numbers alone. For the steps either side, the [[runoff-calculator]] turns rain into runoff and the [[flood-frequency]] tool picks the design storm.

## The paper in brief

The authors mapped land cover for 2015, 2019 and 2022 in a GIS, turned each map into SCS curve numbers, and ran a 25-year design storm through HEC-HMS. They calibrated against the Sadia River's bank-full discharge. The results they report are:

| Land cover | Modelled peak, 25-year storm | Modelled time lag |
|---|---|---|
| 2015 | 156.20 m³/s | 445.15 min |
| 2019 | 164.80 m³/s | 431.84 min |
| 2022 | 167.00 m³/s | 429.88 min |

Their conclusion is that a higher curve number and more sealed surface raised the peak, with extra rainfall intensity from climate factors also playing a part.

Only the abstract could be read from here, so the catchment area, the curve numbers and the design rainfall depth are not used below. That turns out to matter less than you would expect, because the two numbers that *are* published pin down a great deal.

## Checked with the tools

The SCS synthetic unit hydrograph has one useful property: its peak is

> qp = 1000 · A · Q / (3600 · Tp · G)

where A is the catchment area in km², Q the depth of excess rain in mm, Tp the time to peak in hours, and G a pure number that depends only on the peak rate factor (G = 1.3333 at the standard factor of 484, which the tool gets from a shape parameter of m = 3.697). Everything about the land cover enters through just two routes: Q, how much rain runs off, and Tp = D/2 + lag, how fast it gets to the outlet. So the paper's own pair of numbers can be separated.

**Step 1: what the shorter lag alone would have done.** Hold the runoff volume at its 2015 value and shorten the lag to the 2019 and 2022 figures. The peak would have risen to **160.70** and **161.39 m³/s**, against the paper's modelled **164.80** and **167.00**. The lag change therefore explains about **52 %** of the 2019 rise and **48 %** of the 2022 rise. Roughly half, not all.

**Step 2: the rest is volume.** Rearranging for A · Q, the product of area and runoff depth, gives **5,937, 6,089 and 6,144 km²·mm** for the three years, that is **5.94, 6.09 and 6.14 million m³** of direct runoff. In other words the paper's model is putting **2.6 %** and **3.5 %** more water through the outlet in 2019 and 2022 than in 2015. We assumed a one-hour rain step to get Tp from the published lag, so we checked how much that matters: over rain steps from 15 minutes to 2 hours the rises stay at **2.4 to 2.7 %** and **3.3 to 3.7 %**. The volume conclusion does not depend on the assumption.

**Step 3: draw the unit hydrographs.** Figure 1 shows the gamma unit hydrographs the tool builds for the three lags. Each holds exactly one millimetre of runoff, so the curves cross: the 2022 one is taller (3.261 against 3.156 m³/s per mm) and peaks earlier (7.75 against 8.00 hours in quarter-hour steps) but has a thinner tail. A 15-minute shorter lag is worth about 3 % on the peak here, which is small because the lag itself is more than seven hours. In a small, steep catchment with a one-hour lag, the same quarter of an hour would be worth 20 %.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/sadia-watershed-land-use-peak-discharge-lag-unit-hydrograph.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `shapeFor`, `gammaShapeArea`, `gammaUH`, `convolve` and `impliedArea` from `src/lib/sci/uh.ts`, the maths behind the [[unit-hydrograph]] builder.

Where each input comes from:

- The three peak discharges and the three time lags: **the paper's abstract**.
- The SCS lag relation Tp = D/2 + 0.6 tc and the peak rate factor of 484: **the standard SCS method**, as built into the tool.
- The rain step D = 1 h: **our assumption** (the paper does not state it). The checked range of 0.25 to 2 h is also ours.
- The catchment area of 120 km²: **our assumption**, used only to set the vertical scale of Figure 1 and to turn the fixed product A · Q into a drawable millimetre figure. Nothing in Steps 1, 2 or 4 depends on it; at 120 km² the implied excess rain is 49.5 to 51.2 mm, which is plausible for a 25-year storm in that climate but is not the paper's number.
- The runoff volumes and the lag-only peaks: **worked out by the tool's maths** from the published peaks and lags.

## How we test the maths

Every change to fibub runs `tests/sci.mjs`, which now loads `tests/sci-tools/unit-hydrograph.mjs`. It pins the builder against values that can be checked by hand:

- The gamma shape for a peak rate factor of 484 must be m = 3.6969, giving a dimensionless area under the curve of 1.33333, and `prfOf` must invert `shapeFor` exactly. (645.33 / 1.33333 = 484 is the textbook relation in US units.)
- Every unit hydrograph must hold exactly one millimetre of runoff over its catchment: summing the ordinates and multiplying by the step must give 1000 · A m³ to one part in 10⁹, for the gamma curve and for a Nash cascade alike, and `impliedArea` must return the area it was given.
- A Nash cascade with one reservoir must decay exactly as exp(−t/k), and convolution must be linear: doubling the excess rain must double every ordinate.
- Non-negative least squares must recover a unit hydrograph exactly from noise-free data: convolve a known unit hydrograph with a known rain series, feed the result back in, and get the same ordinates to one part in 10⁶.
- New with this article: the paper's three lags, with a one-hour rain step and a peak rate factor of 484, must give unit hydrograph peaks of 3.1564, 3.2477 and 3.2611 m³/s per mm for a 120 km² catchment; excess rains of 49.48, 50.74 and 51.20 mm must convolve back to the paper's 156.2, 164.8 and 167.0 m³/s within 0.05 m³/s; and the lag-only peaks must be 160.70 and 161.39 m³/s.

## Check it yourself

On the [[unit-hydrograph]] page, in the *Catchment and unit hydrograph* card:

1. Type *Catchment area (km²)* **120**, *Rain step D (h)* **1**, leave *Shape* on **Gamma curve (SCS type)** and *Peak rate factor* on **484**.
2. Type *Time of concentration tc (h)* **12.37**. That is the paper's 2015 lag of 445.15 minutes divided by 0.6, because the SCS lag is 0.6 tc. The unit hydrograph peaks at about **3.16 m³/s per mm**, about **8 hours** after the rain starts.
3. In *Storm hydrograph*, clear *Excess rain in each step* and type a single value, **49.48**, with *Baseflow (m³/s)* **0**. The storm peak reads about **156 m³/s**, the paper's 2015 figure.
4. Now change *Time of concentration tc (h)* to **11.94**, the 2022 lag of 429.88 minutes over 0.6, and leave the rain at **49.48**. The peak rises to about **161 m³/s**: that is the whole effect of the shorter lag.
5. Raise *Excess rain in each step* to **51.2** and the peak reaches about **167 m³/s**, the paper's 2022 figure. Steps 4 and 5 are the split: roughly half the rise from a faster catchment, half from more water.
6. For a feel for how much the peak rate factor matters, set it to **300** (flat, swampy land): the 2022 peak falls to roughly two-thirds. Always say which factor you used when you publish a synthetic unit hydrograph.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Separate speed from volume** in any published pair of peak and lag figures, as above, with no access to the original model. Two numbers are enough.
- **Recover the runoff volume behind a published peak**, so you can check whether a reported curve number and design rainfall are consistent with the hydrograph that was drawn from them.
- **Test how sensitive a conclusion is to the rain step** by rerunning with D from 15 minutes to 2 hours, as we did; a conclusion that survives that is worth reporting.
- **Derive a unit hydrograph from a storm you measured** in the third card: paste rain and flow, and the tool separates baseflow, sets a φ-index loss and fits non-negative ordinates. Compare it with the synthetic SCS curve before trusting either.
- **Decide whether a synthetic method is suitable at all.** Build the gamma curve and a Nash cascade for the same catchment and see how far apart they put the peak; in a long-lag catchment like Sadia the choice matters less than the lag estimate.
- **Carry the hydrograph downstream** with the [[manning-weir-calculator]] for a channel check, or convert it to a level with the [[rating-curve]] fitter.

## The fine print

- Only the paper's abstract was readable from here. We check the arithmetic that links its published peaks and lags; we do not reproduce its HEC-HMS run, its curve numbers or its design rainfall.
- The rain step and the catchment area are **our assumptions**. The lag-versus-volume split is almost unaffected by both; the millimetre figures in Step 2 and Figure 1 scale with the area.
- The tool's gamma curve stands in for the SCS dimensionless unit hydrograph. It is the usual closed-form substitute and matches it closely, but it is not the tabulated ordinates, so peaks can differ by a per cent or two from HEC-HMS.
- The paper reports a rise in rainfall intensity as well as in curve number, so the extra runoff volume we recover is the two effects together.
- Design flood work must follow your local manual. These numbers are for understanding a published result, not for sizing a culvert.
