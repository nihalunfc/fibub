A germanium gamma detector cooled with liquid nitrogen gives peaks about **550 eV** wide near 100 keV. A **microcalorimeter** — a tiny absorber held below 0.1 K, read out by a superconducting thermometer that senses the heat each photon deposits — gives peaks of **53 to 100 eV**. A 2025 review in *Frontiers in Nuclear Engineering* collects what three such instruments have measured, from fresh fission products to plutonium in used fuel. The claim at its centre is simple: sharper peaks let you separate lines that germanium smears into one. This article puts a number on that with the [[spectrum-peak-fitter]], which fits Gaussian peaks on a background by Poisson-weighted least squares. Plan how long to count with the [[counting-statistics|counting statistics helper]], and work out what gets through a collimator or a shield with the [[gamma-shielding|gamma shielding calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors describe three transition-edge-sensor (TES) microcalorimeter arrays: **SOFIA**, a portable 256-pixel instrument, and **HERMES-400** and **HERMES-700**, larger arrays installed at national laboratories. Each pixel is a small tin absorber (1.3 × 1.3 × 0.5 mm) glued to a superconducting film biased inside its 100 to 150 mK transition, so a 100 keV photon raises the film's resistance measurably. Peak efficiency is about 54 % near 100 keV, and above about 20 counts per second per pixel the resolution starts to degrade, which is why the arrays need hundreds of pixels rather than one good one.

The review's applications include identifying short-lived fission products minutes after a reactor irradiation, measuring uranium enrichment and plutonium isotope ratios without dissolving the sample, reading the cooling time of used nuclear fuel from europium lines, and checking actinium-227 impurity in medical actinium-225. Along the way it gives the numbers an experimenter actually needs: **about 12,000 counts in a peak for a 1 % statistical uncertainty**, about 600 for 10 %, and a worked case where a **131 eV** peak width was reached on the 50.13 keV thorium-227 line.

The example that makes the resolution argument concrete is a pair of lines from a freshly irradiated uranium foil: **barium-140 at 132.687 keV and cerium-144 at 133.515 keV**, only **828 eV** apart. At 100 eV resolution they are two separate peaks. At 550 eV they are not, and that is exactly the kind of statement a fitter can test.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **simulation built from the paper's stated numbers**, not the paper's data: its spectra are shown as figures, and we never copy a figure. We generate the doublet at the paper's own binning (0.01 keV per channel) with **12,000 counts in the barium line and 3,000 in the weaker cerium line on a flat background of 20 counts per channel** (**our assumptions**), add Poisson noise from a fixed seed, and fit it with the tool's maths.

**Step 1: the paper's resolution claim is arithmetic.** 550 ÷ 100 = **5.5** and 550 ÷ 53 = **10.4**, so "5 to 10 times better than germanium" checks out exactly. In units that matter for a doublet: the 828 eV gap is **8.3 peak widths** at 100 eV, and only **1.5** at 550 eV. Figure 1 shows what that looks like.

**Step 2: the fitter resolves both, but pays for it.** At 100 eV the fit returns centroids **132.6879 ± 0.0004** and **133.5143 ± 0.0009 keV**, widths **100.2 ± 0.7** and **100.2 ± 1.8 eV**, and areas **12,162 ± 113** (0.93 %) and **3,035 ± 60** (1.97 %), with χ²/ν = 1.09 on 333 degrees of freedom. At 550 eV it still finds both, because we told it where to look: areas **11,781 ± 148** (1.25 %) and **3,102 ± 116** (3.73 %). So germanium is not helpless when you know the two energies in advance — but the weak line's uncertainty nearly doubles, and the strong line's rises by a third.

The honest version of the resolution argument is about *knowing* rather than *fitting*. Fit the 100 eV spectrum as a single peak and the fit collapses: χ²/ν jumps to **43.6**, and the single Gaussian cannot even sit on the data. Do the same to the 550 eV spectrum and you get a plausible-looking single peak of 605 eV width and 12,048 counts with χ²/ν = **5.21** — high, but the kind of number people explain away as a detector tail. A sharp detector shows you the second line; a blunt one makes you suspect it.

**Step 3: how far does blurring have to go before the weak line is lost?** We repeated the simulation and fit at seventeen detector widths from 50 to 900 eV (Figure 2). The uncertainty on the weak line's area creeps up slowly, from **1.9 % at 50 eV to 2.0 % at 100 eV, 3.0 % at 500 eV and 3.4 % at 550 eV**, then runs away: **4.8 % at 600 eV, 19.7 % at 800 eV and 31.9 % at 900 eV**. The knee is near 600 eV, where the gap drops below 1.4 peak widths. A commercial germanium detector sits just on the good side of that cliff for this particular pair, which is why the paper's example is well chosen rather than unfair.

**Step 4: the counting rule of thumb.** With a single clean 100 eV peak on the same background, the fitter gives **0.9 % for 12,000 counts**, matching the paper's figure, **2.0 % for 3,000** and **5.2 % for 600**. The Poisson floor 1/√N is 0.9 %, 1.8 % and 4.1 %, so above a few thousand counts the background costs almost nothing, while at 600 counts it adds a quarter. The paper's "600 counts for 10 %" is worse than our 5.2 %, which tells you its background under that peak is heavier than the flat continuum we assumed.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/microcalorimeter-gamma-doublet-peak-fitting.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitPeaks`, `sumArea` and the constant `FWHM` from `src/lib/sci/phpeak.ts`, the maths behind the [[spectrum-peak-fitter]]. `fitPeaks` builds a model of one or more Gaussians on a constant, straight or quadratic background, fits it by Levenberg-Marquardt with Poisson weights (each channel weighted by 1/counts), and returns the centroid, width and net area of each peak with the standard errors from the unscaled covariance matrix.

Where the numbers come from: the two line energies, the 0.01 keV binning, the 100 eV and 550 eV peak widths and the counting rules of thumb are **from the paper's text**. The 12,000 and 3,000 count areas, the flat background of 20 counts per channel, the 131.4 to 134.8 keV window and the random seed are **our assumptions**. The spectra are **simulated**; the paper's own spectra are not reproduced here.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/spectrum-peak-fitter.mjs`, which pins the fitter against SciPy's `curve_fit` with absolute Poisson sigmas on a made-up 1,024-channel spectrum:

- The doublet in the region 380 to 460 must return the eight fitted parameters of SciPy's solution to within 2 parts in a million, including the background slope −0.33784708.
- Its net areas must be 4610.3226 ± 203.3056 and 2467.0460 ± 186.2599 counts, from the same covariance matrix SciPy reports, and χ² must be 92.85864121.
- A single peak at channel 180 must come out at 179.98706171 with 9006.7486 counts, and the straight-line energy calibration must match NumPy's `polyfit` to ten digits.
- New with this article: a noise-free doublet at 132.687 and 133.515 keV on a flat background of 20 counts per 0.01 keV channel, with 12,000 and 3,000 counts in the peaks, must be recovered exactly at both 100 eV and 550 eV input width: centroids to 1 µeV, widths to 0.001 eV and areas to 0.001 counts.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Decide whether your detector can separate two lines** before buying beam time: the gap in peak widths is the number that matters, and under about 1.4 widths the weak line's uncertainty runs away.
- **Refit a published doublet** with your own background order and see how much the quoted areas move: the fitter reports the covariance between the two peaks, which is where the risk hides.
- **Turn a resolution specification into an uncertainty**: simulate your peak at your detector's FWHM and counting time, and read the area error off the fit.
- **Check a counting plan** with the [[counting-statistics|counting statistics helper]], then confirm it against a fit rather than trusting 1/√N, which is optimistic whenever there is background.
- **Teach why resolution matters** with two spectra of the same physics, one sharp and one blurred.

## The fine print

- Every spectrum here is **simulated** from values stated in the paper's text. We did not use, copy or digitise any of its figures, and our background is a flat continuum, not a real Compton background.
- We gave the germanium case the paper's 0.01 keV binning, which real germanium electronics would not provide. That is generous to germanium, so the gap we measure is a lower bound on the real one.
- The microcalorimeter's line shape is close to Gaussian, which is why a Gaussian fit works so well here. Germanium peaks have a low-energy tail that a pure Gaussian does not describe, so a real 550 eV fit would be slightly worse than ours.
- The 12,000 and 3,000 count areas are our choice. A doublet of equal lines is easier to fit than an unequal one.
- The paper is a review of several instruments and campaigns; the efficiency, count-rate and resolution figures belong to particular detectors and are not specifications for microcalorimeters in general.
