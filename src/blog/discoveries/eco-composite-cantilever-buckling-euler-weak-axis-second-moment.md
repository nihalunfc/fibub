Recycled-plastic profiles are turning up in fences, walkways and furniture, and the first question a designer asks is how much compression a post can take before it bows sideways. A 2025 paper in *Applied Computer Science* measured a new composite pressed from 100 % recycled polymer, then built finite-element models of nine cantilever columns and compared their buckling loads with Euler's 270-year-old formula. The agreement is within 4 %. This article rebuilds all nine cases with the [[column-buckling]] calculator, shows where the 4 % lives, and takes apart one of the paper's headline numbers. For the tensile test behind the modulus see the [[stress-strain-analyser]], and for the same profile loaded sideways the [[beam-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors made the composite entirely from recycled polymer waste and measured it: **Young's modulus 493 ± 15 MPa**, tensile strength 13.5 ± 0.5 MPa, Poisson's ratio 0.32 and density 969 kg/m³. That is a soft material, about a fortieth as stiff as aluminium, so stability rather than strength decides how much load a slim post carries.

They then modelled a **cantilever**: one end clamped, the other free and pushed straight down, which is the worst of the standard end conditions (effective length factor K = 2). Three rectangular cross-sections, 40 × 20, 40 × 40 and 40 × 60 mm, were each run at free lengths of 150, 300 and 450 mm, giving nine first-mode buckling loads from 160 N up to about 16.8 kN. Every one of them sits within 4 % of Euler's formula, and the agreement is best for the longest columns. The paper also reports how the load responds to size: **+685 %** for doubling the section area, **+53 %** for a half-again larger area, **−75 %** for doubling the length and **−55 %** for a half-again longer column.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the material properties and geometry the paper states. We use the measured modulus of 493 MPa, K = 2 for the fixed-free end condition, and the tool's own section properties.

**Step 1: all nine loads.** The tool's `sectionProps` and `eulerLoad` reproduce the paper's Euler column to **0.10 % on every one of the nine cases** — the same offset each time, which is the signature of a slightly different modulus rather than a different formula. Inverting the paper's 1440.23 N for the 40 × 20 × 150 mm column gives **E = 492.50 MPa**, so the paper rounded its measured 493 MPa down a notch. **Step 2: the 4 % is not random.** The two worst cases, 4.03 % and 3.09 %, are both the **stocky** ones: the 40 × 40 and 40 × 60 sections at only 150 mm free length. The tool explains why. Their slenderness λ = KL/r is **25.98**, and for this material the Johnson parabola takes over from Euler below a transition slenderness of **26.85**, worked out as √(2π²E/f_y) with f_y = 13.5 MPa. So those two columns are the only ones in the set that are no longer purely elastic columns, and the tool's blended curve gives **7.179 MPa** instead of Euler's 7.208 MPa — 11 487 N and 17 230 N. That alone closes about a seventh of the gap; the rest is the shell model's shear and local flattening, which Euler's formula ignores. The other seven columns, at λ from 52 to 156, sit deep in the Euler region, and there the paper's own agreement is 0.03 % to 1.7 %.

**Step 3: "doubling the area" has three answers.** The paper's +685 % is for going from 40 × 20 to 40 × 40, which doubles the area from 800 to 1600 mm². But Euler's load follows the **smallest** second moment of area, not the area, and for a rectangle the weak axis is whichever side is shorter. The tool gives three different answers for three ways of doubling the same 800 mm²:

| How the area is doubled | Weak-axis I | Load multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| Double the depth, 40 × 40 | 213 333 mm⁴ | ×8.0 (+700 %) |
| Double the width, 80 × 20 | 53 333 mm⁴ | ×2.0 (+100 %) |
| Scale both by √2, 56.6 × 28.3 | 106 667 mm⁴ | ×4.0 (+300 %) |

The paper's own finite-element pair gives ×7.79, or +679 %, against Euler's exact ×8. Its next step, 40 × 40 to 40 × 60 (+50 % area), gives ×1.514 against Euler's ×1.50, because once the 40 mm side is the short one, growing the long side raises the weak-axis I in simple proportion. **The practical rule: thicken the thin direction.** Adding material to the already-wide direction of a recycled-plastic post buys you almost nothing against buckling.

**Step 4: the length claims are exact.** Euler's load falls as 1/(KL)², so doubling the length must give exactly **×0.25**, a 75 % drop, and a half-again longer column **×0.4444**, a 55.6 % drop. The paper quotes −75 % and −55 %, and its own finite-element pair for the 40 × 20 section gives ×0.2526. Length is the one lever with no ambiguity.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every computed number come from `scripts/blog-figures/eco-composite-cantilever-buckling-euler-weak-axis-second-moment.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `sectionProps`, `eulerLoad`, `column`, `transition` and `criticalStress` from `src/lib/sci/eng-buckling.ts`, the maths behind the [[column-buckling]] calculator.

From the paper: the modulus 493 MPa, the tensile strength 13.5 MPa, the fixed-free end condition, the three cross-sections, the three lengths, and the nine finite-element and Euler values in the comparison table. **Our assumption**: using the tensile strength of 13.5 MPa as the yield strength f_y that sets the transition slenderness, since the paper reports no compressive yield stress. No measured curve from the paper has been redrawn; the dots in Figure 1 are its nine stated critical forces.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/column-buckling.mjs`, which pins the buckling maths against an independent Python calculation:

- Section properties are checked against fine grid integration with NumPy (within 0.3 %) and against the closed forms exactly: an I-section 200 × 150 with 10 mm flanges and a 6 mm web must give A = 4080 mm², I_x = 30 016 000 mm⁴ and I_y = 5 628 240 mm⁴, and the tool must pick the weak axis.
- Euler loads are checked against a finite-difference eigenvalue solution of EI w'''' + P w'' = 0 with `scipy.linalg.eigh`, within 0.01 %: 694 352.6 N pinned-pinned and 2 777 368 N fixed-fixed for that section over 4 m.
- The transition slenderness √(2π²E/f_y) must be 125.664 for 200 GPa and 250 MPa, the Johnson and Euler curves must meet at f_y/2 with the same slope, and a stocky column must tend to f_y.
- New with this article: a 40 × 20 mm fixed-free column of 150 mm with E = 493 MPa must give an Euler load of 1441.69 N, λ = 51.96; the 40 × 40 and 40 × 60 sections at 150 mm must both land at λ = 25.98, just inside the Johnson range, with a critical stress of 7.179 MPa.

## Check it yourself

On the [[column-buckling]] page:

1. In *Section*, set *Shape* to **Solid rectangle**, *Width b (mm)* to **40** and *Depth h (mm)* to **20**.
2. In *Column and material*, set *Length L between supports (mm)* to **150**, *Ends* to **Fixed – free, a flagpole (K = 2)**, *Young's modulus E (MPa)* to **493** and *Yield strength fy (MPa)* to **13.5**.
3. Read the buckling load: about **1.44 kN**, with slenderness λ ≈ **52** and the mode given as Euler. The paper's finite-element answer for this column is 1.42 kN.
4. Change *Depth h (mm)* to **40**. The load jumps to about **11.5 kN**, eight times as much, and λ drops to **26** — just below the transition, so the page now shows the Johnson value, about **11.49 kN**.
5. Now undo that and instead change *Width b (mm)* to **80**, leaving the depth at 20. The area is the same 1600 mm², but the load is only about **2.88 kN**: twice the original, not eight times.
6. Set the section back to 40 × 20 and change *Length L* to **300**. The load falls to about **0.36 kN**, a quarter of the 150 mm value.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a finite-element buckling run** before trusting it: the Euler load is the answer any slender-column model must approach, and the tool gives it in seconds.
- **Spot an inelastic case**: if λ falls below √(2π²E/f_y), Euler is optimistic and the paper's own error grows, as it does here.
- **Choose a profile** for a soft recycled material: compare load multipliers for the same material cost by trying each way of adding area.
- **Design a test matrix**: pick lengths that keep every specimen on the same side of the transition slenderness, so one formula describes the whole set.
- **Teach stability** with real numbers: a 400 mm recycled-plastic post 40 × 20 mm buckles at about 160 N, light enough to demonstrate by hand.

## The fine print

- Euler's formula assumes a perfectly straight, centrally loaded, homogeneous column. Recycled composites are neither perfectly homogeneous nor perfectly straight, so real posts buckle below both numbers here.
- We used the tensile strength as the yield strength for the transition slenderness. Polymers are usually stronger in compression than in tension, which would push the transition further left and leave all nine columns in the Euler region.
- The paper's modulus carries ±15 MPa, about ±3 %, and the buckling load is directly proportional to it. That uncertainty is larger than the Euler-versus-FEM gap for seven of the nine columns.
- The composite's behaviour over time (creep) is not covered. A plastic post under steady load will keep bending for years, so a long-term allowable load is well below the buckling load.
- Nothing here is a design check. Real codes add safety factors, initial crookedness and load eccentricity.
