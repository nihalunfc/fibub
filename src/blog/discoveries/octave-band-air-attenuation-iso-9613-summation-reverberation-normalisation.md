Air soaks up sound, and it soaks up high notes much faster than low ones. The international standard ISO 9613-1 gives a formula for how many decibels a **pure tone** loses per kilometre at any temperature and humidity. But room acousticians measure in **octave bands**, each spanning a doubling of frequency, and a band is not a tone: its top end dies away first. A 2024 paper in *Applied Sciences* works out what that does to a measured reverberation decay and proposes how to correct concert-hall measurements to one standard atmosphere, 20 °C and 50 % humidity. Here we rebuild its key curves with the same ISO formula that drives the [[decibel-distance]] calculator. The [[room-acoustics]] calculator uses that formula for its air term, and the [[audio-spectrum-analyser]] shows you which bands your own recording holds.

## The paper in brief

A room's reverberation time is read from how fast the sound decays. Late in the decay, the sound has travelled hundreds of metres, bouncing between walls, so the air has had plenty of time to absorb it. That air term depends on temperature and humidity, so the same hall measured on a dry winter day and a humid summer day gives different results at high frequencies.

The author's reference method splits each octave band into **nine pure tones**, attenuates each with the ISO coefficient, and adds the energies back up at every moment of the decay (fifteen tones changed nothing noticeable). His findings: using the pure-tone value at the band's **centre frequency** is fine only for short paths, up to **roughly 15 dB** of attenuation; using the tone one-third octave lower, as one European standard does, is not accurate; and an approximation in an American standard is systematically too low for long paths. For practical work he proposes a straight-line shortcut with a **knee** at 14 dB of attenuation (6 dB in the 500 Hz band), and a way to normalise reverberation times to 20 °C and 50 %.

## Checked with the tools

The paper gives its octave-band curves only as figures, so we **recomputed** them: nine tones per band, each attenuated with `airAbsorption` (the ISO 9613-1 formula in fibub), energies averaged. Where the paper is silent on exactly where the nine tones sit, we placed them at the centres of nine equal slices of the octave on a log scale with equal weight, **our assumption** (a flat spectrum within the band). Travel time is turned into distance with the paper's 343.2 m/s.

**Step 1: the band falls slower than its centre tone.** At 20 °C and 50 %, the 8 kHz tone loses 105.3 dB per km. After 2 s of travel (687 m) that would be **72.3 dB**, but the summed 8 kHz band has lost only **49.3 dB**: the top of the band is gone, and what is left is the cheaper-to-carry bottom. The 4 kHz band shows the same effect more gently, 17.3 dB against 20.4 dB at 2 s (Figure 1).

**Step 2: where the shortcut breaks.** We asked when the centre-tone value drifts 1 dB from the summed band. For 2, 4 and 8 kHz that happens after **14.5, 11.0 and 10.2 dB** of band attenuation; once the band has lost 15 dB, the centre tone claims **16.1, 17.2 and 17.6 dB**. That backs the paper's rule of thumb of about 15 dB, with a knee near 14 dB. The 0.5 dB drift comes at r·fc² of about 5 km·kHz², a little beyond the ISO standard's quoted ±0.5 dB limit of 3, so the standard's limit is on the safe side.

**Step 3: the 8 kHz band slides towards 6.3 kHz.** Figure 2 tracks the 8 kHz band's rate of loss. Right at the start it is **112.2 dB per km**, slightly *above* the centre tone, because the top half of the band costs more than the bottom half saves. By 2 s it has fallen to **62.1 dB per km**. The average rate since the start reaches the 6.3 kHz tone's **67.6 dB per km** after **3.3 s**, when the band has lost **76 dB**. The paper says the band reaches the 6.3 kHz coefficient after more than 2 s, with more than 60 dB lost: our recomputation agrees.

**Step 4: normalising a dry day.** Measured at 20 °C and 30 % humidity, the 4 kHz band loses more than at the 50 % reference. The correction to add back, by summation, is 4.9 dB at 1 s, **8.5 dB at 2 s** and 15.3 dB at 4 s, passing **10 dB at 2.4 s**. The centre tone alone would say 13.2 dB at 2 s, overcorrecting by more than half.

| Check | Paper says | Our recomputation (ISO 9613-1 maths, 9 tones) |
|---|---|---|
| Centre tone is fine up to | roughly 15 dB | within 1 dB up to 10 to 14.5 dB; 1.1 to 2.6 dB high at 15 dB |
| 8 kHz band reaches the 6.3 kHz coefficient | after more than 2 s, over 60 dB | after 3.3 s, at 76 dB |
| 30 % → 50 % correction at 4 kHz | over 10 dB after 2 s | 8.5 dB at 2 s, 10 dB at 2.4 s |
| 9 versus 15 tones per band | no noticeable change | at most 0.2 dB up to 2 s |

The last row is ours: with fifteen tones the 8 kHz band at 2 s reads 49.1 dB instead of 49.3 dB.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/octave-band-air-attenuation-iso-9613-summation-reverberation-normalisation.mjs`. It calls `airAbsorption` from `src/lib/sci/phair.ts` (the ISO 9613-1 formula behind [[decibel-distance]] and [[room-acoustics]]) for every tone, and `levelAt` from `src/lib/sci/phdb.ts` for the single-tone values in "Check it yourself". The only extra step is the energy average of the nine tones.

The reference atmosphere, c₀ = 343.2 m/s, N = 9 and the comparison values come from the paper's text. The placement and equal weighting of the nine tones, and the 30 % humidity case, are **our assumptions**. The curves are **recomputed**, not read from the paper's figures.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/decibel-distance.mjs`, which pins the calculator against an independent NumPy version of the ISO formula:

- 90 dB at 1 m from a point source at 1 kHz, 20 °C and 50 %, must be **49.54 dB** at 100 m, and fall to 45 dB at **163.0 m** (SciPy root-finding).
- All eight octave bands of a sample spectrum carried from 10 m to 500 m at 15 °C and 70 % must match NumPy, and the A-weighted total must drop from 91.26 to **54.64 dB(A)**.
- New with this article: an 8 kHz tone at 20 °C and 50 % must lose **72.23 dB** over 686 m (105.3 dB per km), and a 6.3 kHz tone **46.39 dB** (67.6 dB per km).

## Check it yourself

On the [[decibel-distance]] calculator:

1. Under *Sound and distances*, set *Sound given as* to **One level at one frequency**, *Sound level L₁ (dB)* **90**, *Frequency (Hz)* **8000**.
2. Type **1** in *Measured at distance r₁ (m)* and **687** in *Find the level at r₂ (m)*: 686 m, or 2 s of travel. Choose *Source* **Large flat surface, close up: no spreading**, so only the air acts.
3. Under *Air*, keep **20** °C, **50** % and **101.325** kPa, with *Include sound absorbed by the air* ticked.
4. *Air absorption* shows **−72.23 dB** at **105 dB per km**: the centre-tone answer.
5. Change *Frequency (Hz)* to **6300**: **−46.39 dB** at **67.6 dB per km**. The summed 8 kHz band, 49.3 dB, sits between the two and much nearer the lower tone, which is the paper's point.
6. Set *Relative humidity (%)* to **30** and see how much more an 8 kHz tone loses on a dry day.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Correct reverberation measurements** to 20 °C and 50 % before comparing halls or seasons, with the air term from the [[room-acoustics]] calculator and the band behaviour shown here.
- **Check when a centre-frequency value is safe**: if the band attenuation stays under about 10 dB, the [[decibel-distance]] calculator's single-tone answer is within 1 dB.
- **Plan outdoor noise predictions** for long paths, where high bands lose tens of decibels and the band shape matters.
- **Scale-model acoustics**: at ten times the frequency, air absorption dominates, so test the humidity in the model room with the same maths.
- **Teach relaxation absorption**: the dips in the ISO curve come from oxygen and nitrogen molecules taking up and giving back sound energy.

## The fine print

- Our nine tones are placed and weighted by **our assumption**; the paper's exact choice may shift our numbers by a few tenths of a decibel.
- A real source is not flat across a band. A spectrum that falls with frequency makes the band behave even more like its lower edge.
- We used still, uniform air at sea-level pressure; temperature layers and wind matter outdoors.
- We did not rebuild the paper's knee-point approximation or its EDT, T20 and T30 corrections, only the summation reference they are tested against.
