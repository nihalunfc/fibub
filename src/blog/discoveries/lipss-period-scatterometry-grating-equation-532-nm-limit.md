Shine an ultrashort laser pulse train on stainless steel and the surface ripples itself into a grating, all by itself, with a period close to the laser wavelength. Those **laser-induced periodic surface structures** (LIPSS) colour the metal, change how it wets and how it slides, and so people want to watch them form in real time rather than take the part to an electron microscope afterwards. A 2025 paper in *Sensors* does it the cheapest possible way: a green laser pointer, a camera, and the grating equation. This article recomputes all of that with the [[diffraction-patterns]] generator, and finds a hard limit in the method that the paper's own data runs into. For the companion jobs of calibrating a wavelength scale and sizing the probe beam, see the [[spectrometer-calibration]] tool and the [[gaussian-beam]] calculator.

## The paper in brief

The authors structure stainless steel with a picosecond laser focused to a 40 µm spot, varying the number of scans from a few to five hundred. In the same machine a **532 nm probe laser diode** lights a 1 mm patch of the structured surface at normal incidence, and a camera records where the diffracted light lands. From the pixel distance Δx between the zero order and a diffracted order they get the period from

Λ = (175.46 ± 0.64) µm·px ÷ Δx,

which is the grating equation sin θ_m = mλ/Λ with the geometry folded into one constant. They also pull a "period range" and a structure height out of the same images, and check all three against atomic force microscopy.

The findings: as the scan count rises the surface goes from shallow fine ripples to **low-spatial-frequency ripples of about 1130 nm**, then to bulges near 983 nm and finally to a molten, irregular surface. The average period falls from about 1000 nm to 800 nm, the heights climb from tens of nanometres to 420 nm at 95 scans and 880 ± 290 nm at 500. Scatterometry recovers the period to about **5 %** and the height to **11.4 %** on average, which for a camera and a laser pointer is a good bargain.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the probe wavelength, the stated periods and the stated spot size. No measurement of ours is involved and the paper's images are not used.

**Step 1: where each order goes.** At normal incidence the orders sit at sin θ_m = mλ/Λ. With λ = 532 nm the tool gives:

| Structure | Period Λ | First order | Second order |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shallow fine ripples (HSFL) | 415 nm | none at all | none |
| Just above the floor | 580 nm | 66.53° | none |
| Average, late in the process | 800 nm | 41.68° | none |
| Bulges | 983 nm | 32.77° | none |
| Coarse ripples (LSFL) | 1130 nm | 28.09° | 70.32° |

Two things fall out of that table. A **second order needs Λ ≥ 1064 nm** (twice the probe wavelength), so only the coarsest ripples ever produce two rings; seeing a second ring is by itself evidence that the period is above 1064 nm. And a first order needs **Λ ≥ 532 nm**: below the probe wavelength, nothing is diffracted at all.

**Step 2: the floor is the probe wavelength, and the calibration constant says so.** The paper's own calibration constant contains the limit. The furthest an order can ever land is where sin θ = 1, and since Λ = C/Δx with C = 175.46 µm·px, that extreme Δx is C/λ = **329.8 px**. Put that back in and the smallest period the instrument can read is C ÷ 329.8 px = **532.0 nm**, the probe wavelength exactly. So the 415 nm fine ripples the paper reports **cannot** have come from a diffracted order; they must come from the microscope, and the "period range starting near 400 nm" is likewise outside what the camera can see. Figure 2 shows the same thing as a profile: the 415 nm grating simply has no far-field order to find.

**Step 3: the optics are far sharper than the 5 % error.** With the paper's 1 mm spot, a 1130 nm grating has **885 grooves lit**. The tool's grating maths puts the first-order peak half-width at **0.603 mrad**, which corresponds to a period uncertainty of **0.113 %**, about 1.3 nm. For the 580 nm structure it is 0.058 %, about 0.3 nm. The instrument's own resolution is therefore **forty to a hundred times better** than the 5 % error the paper reports. The error is not in the optics: it is in the surface, which is a bundle of periods rather than one, so the order is broadened and shifted by the real spread of ripple spacings. That is a useful conclusion for anyone building such a monitor, because it says that a better camera or a longer lens will not help, while a narrower probe spot (fewer, more uniform ripples) or fitting the order's shape rather than its centroid might.

**Step 4: the second order carries profile information for free.** The relative strength of the orders depends on the groove shape, not just the period. With a groove width 40 % of the period (**our assumption**), the tool puts the first order at 0.573 and the second at 0.055 of the zero-order peak. Make the grooves exactly half the period and the second order vanishes completely (relative strength 1 × 10⁻³²): a 50:50 square profile has no even orders. So the mere presence of a visible second order says the ripple profile is not a symmetric 50:50 square, which is a free extra measurement sitting in the same camera frame.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/lipss-period-scatterometry-grating-equation-532-nm-limit.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls two functions from `src/lib/sci/phdiff.ts`, the maths behind the [[diffraction-patterns]] generator:

- `fraunhofer`, the far-field irradiance of an N-slit grating at screen position x, which uses the exact sin θ = x/√(x² + z²) rather than a small-angle approximation (that matters here, because the interesting orders are at 60 to 70°);
- `fresnelNumber`, which confirms the far-field picture applies to the geometry we draw.

From the paper we take the probe wavelength (532 nm), the probe spot (1 mm), normal incidence, the calibration constant (175.46 µm·px) and the four reported periods (415, 580, 983 and 1130 nm). Two things are **our assumptions**: the groove width is 40 % of the period, which changes the relative order strengths but no angle; and Figure 2 is drawn with a 20 µm illuminated patch instead of 1 mm, because at 1 mm the orders are narrower than one pixel of the chart. Treating a reflective rippled surface as a transmission grating is also a simplification: at normal incidence the order angles are identical, while the relative strengths would need a full vector calculation.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/diffraction-patterns.mjs` on every change to fibub. It pins the maths against SciPy and against closed forms:

- the Bessel functions J0 and J1 must match `scipy.special` at 14 points from 0 to 1000 to 1e-10, and the Fresnel integrals C and S at 11 points to 1e-12;
- a 10-slit grating of 1.6 µm pitch at 532 nm must put its first order exactly at sin θ = λ/d with a peak equal to the single-slit envelope there, and its central peak must be exactly N² = 100 times one slit;
- the Fresnel circular-hole integral must match `scipy.integrate.quad` at five radii to 1e-8, and the on-axis value must equal the closed form 4 sin²(πR²/2λz).
- New with this article: a 1.13 µm grating at 532 nm must give orders only at sin θ = 0.4708 and 0.9416 (28.09° and 70.32°), a 415 nm grating must give no order at all, and a grating with grooves exactly half its period must suppress the second order to below 1e-20 of the zero order.

## Check it yourself

On the [[diffraction-patterns]] page:

1. Set *Aperture* to **Grating**, *Wavelength (nm)* to **532**, *Slit width a (µm)* to **0.452**, *Slit spacing d, centre to centre (µm)* to **1.13**, *Number of slits N* to **885**, *Screen distance z (m)* to **1** and *Screen half-width shown (mm)* to **3000**.
2. Set *Pattern* to **Far field (Fraunhofer) only** (the Fresnel number is 0.469, so the far field is the right picture but not an exact one at this distance).
3. The headline reads **885-slit grating: 1st order at 28.1°, 534 mm from centre**.
4. *Grating orders (angle)* reads **1: 28.1°, 2: 70.3°**, with **885.0 lines per mm** underneath, and *Resolving power* reads **885 × order**, resolving **0.601 nm** in the first order.
5. *Envelope first minimum* reads **none (slit narrower than λ)**: the grooves are smaller than the wavelength, so there is no envelope zero to create a missing order.
6. Now change *Slit spacing d* to **0.415** and *Slit width a* to **0.166**. *Grating orders (angle)* reads **none beyond 0**: the fine ripples are invisible to a green probe.
7. Change *Slit spacing d* to **0.58** and the first order reappears at **66.5°**, 2.3 m from the centre on a 1 m screen. That is why a real instrument needs a collecting lens, not a flat screen.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Checking that a scatterometry set-up can see the structure at all.** Before building a monitor, put the expected period and the probe wavelength into the [[diffraction-patterns]] generator: if it says "none beyond 0", choose a shorter probe wavelength.
- **Choosing the probe wavelength.** A blue 405 nm probe would push the floor down to 405 nm and bring the fine ripples into view; the tool shows at once where their order would land (41.1° for a 616 nm period, for instance) and whether the camera reaches that far.
- **Sizing the detector.** The order angles decide the collection numerical aperture and the sensor's field of view. At 66° you need a real lens; at 28° a bare camera will do.
- **Separating instrument spread from sample spread.** The peak half-width for your illuminated spot is the best period resolution you can have; anything wider is the sample talking, as it is in this paper.
- **Reading the profile, not just the period.** Comparing measured order strengths against the tool's values for different groove widths turns a bare period measurement into a rough shape measurement.

## The fine print

A rippled metal surface is a reflection grating with a shallow, roughly sinusoidal profile, not a set of clean slits in an opaque screen. The **angles** of the orders are the same in both pictures at normal incidence, and those are what we checked. The **strengths** are not: a rigorous answer needs a vector diffraction calculation with the metal's complex refractive index, especially once the ripple height approaches the probe wavelength, as it does at 880 nm in the paper's late samples. Our 532 nm floor is also an idealisation of a perfect far field; in practice an order within a degree or two of grazing is lost long before the formula says so, which is one reason the paper's useful range starts nearer 580 nm than 532 nm. None of this changes the two headline points: the probe wavelength is a hard floor on what scatterometry can see, and the optics in this paper are far sharper than its quoted accuracy, so the remaining error belongs to the surface.
