The biggest tides of the month do not arrive on the day of the new or full Moon. At most coasts they come a day or so later, at some they come before, and the delay is called the **age of the tide**. A 2026 paper in *Ocean Science* worked out this age, the **form factor** (how much of the tide is twice-daily and how much once-daily) and other tidal measures for 3,591 tide gauges around the world. This article rebuilds three of the places it names, New York, San Francisco and Honolulu, with the [[tide-predictor]], using the tidal constants that NOAA publishes for them, and checks that a tide predicted from those constants really does peak when the age says it should. For the sea around the tide, see the [[wave-calculator]] for waves and the [[ocean-currents|ocean currents calculator]] for wind-driven and geostrophic flow.

## The paper in brief

A tide gauge records the sea level every few minutes. **Harmonic analysis** splits that record into **constituents**: pure waves with speeds fixed by the Moon and Sun, each with an **amplitude** H (half its range) and a **phase lag** G (how many degrees it runs behind the pull that drives it, counted at Greenwich). The two largest are usually M2 (the main lunar twice-daily tide, period 12.42 h) and S2 (the solar one, exactly 12 h). Because they run at slightly different speeds, they drift in and out of step every 14.8 days: spring tides when they add up, neap tides when they partly cancel.

The authors took the GESLA-4 collection of tide-gauge records (hourly or faster, from 45 agencies in 119 countries) and the TICON-4 set of 50 constituents worked out from every record at least a year long. From these they mapped tidal datums, the age of the tide, the form factor, how long high water stands at spring tides, and long-term trends in the constituents.

Some results named in the text: the age of the twice-daily tide is about **18 h at New York**, **8 h at San Francisco**, roughly **2 days at Liverpool** and **negative at Honolulu**, where the spring tide comes before the new or full Moon. During spring tides the water stays within 20 cm of its peak for **1 to 14 hours**, depending on the place. And **125 of 237 long records** show a statistically significant trend in at least one constituent, between −1.47 and +1.80 mm per year.

Two formulas carry most of the paper's maps:

- **Age of the tide** = (G_S2 − G_M2) ÷ (speed of S2 − speed of M2). The speeds are 30 and 28.984 degrees per hour, so the bottom line is 1.016 °/h. Each degree that S2 lags behind M2 delays the spring tide by about an hour.
- **Form factor** F = (K1 + O1) ÷ (M2 + S2), the ratio of the two main once-daily amplitudes to the two main twice-daily ones. Below 0.25 the tide is semidiurnal (two nearly equal highs a day); 0.25 to 1.5 mixed, mainly semidiurnal; 1.5 to 3 mixed, mainly diurnal; above 3 diurnal (one high a day).

## Checked with the tools

The paper's constants come from TICON-4, which we did not download. Instead we used the constants NOAA publishes for the same three places (public data, listed in `sources:`), and fed them to the tide predictor's maths. This is a **recomputation from independent published constants**, plus a **prediction** of future tides; it is not the paper's data.

**Step 1: the two formulas.** From NOAA's amplitudes and phases:

| Place | Form factor | Class | Age of the tide (formula) | Paper says |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New York (The Battery) | 0.19 | semidiurnal | 24.3 h | about 18 h |
| San Francisco | 0.84 | mixed, mainly semidiurnal | 7.9 h | about 8 h |
| Honolulu | 1.01 | mixed, mainly semidiurnal | −4.6 h | negative |

San Francisco and Honolulu agree with the paper. New York comes out about 6 hours later than the paper's 18 h. S2 lags M2 by 24.7° in NOAA's numbers, and an 18 h age would need about 18°. NOAA's accepted constants come from its own analysis epoch at The Battery, while TICON-4 analyses the GESLA-4 record; we cannot tell from the text which record or years the paper's New York value uses, so treat the difference as a reminder that the age of the tide depends on a few degrees of phase.

**Step 2: does a real prediction peak at that delay?** The age formula is a shortcut that looks only at M2 and S2. A full prediction also has N2 (the Moon's changing distance), K2 and the once-daily tides, all of which move the peaks around. So we predicted every high and low water for ten years, 2027 to 2036, with nodal corrections on (as the tool does), took the range of each rise and fall, smoothed it over one lunar day (about 25 hours, so the difference between the two daily tides cancels), and found the biggest range within five days of each of the 247 new and full Moons.

Averaged over those 247 spring tides, the range peaks **25.1 ± 2.9 h** after the new or full Moon at New York, **9.0 ± 2.2 h** at San Francisco and **−3.9 ± 1.7 h** at Honolulu (± one standard error). All three match the shortcut formula within about one standard error. Any single month can be a day or two off, because the perigee (the Moon at its closest, through N2) can pull the biggest tide away from new or full Moon; only the average shows the age cleanly. Figure 1 shows six weeks of it.

**Step 3: the once-daily age.** The same formula with K1 and O1 gives the delay between the Moon's greatest north or south **declination** and the biggest once-daily tide: 2.0 h at New York, 15.5 h at San Francisco and 9.9 h at Honolulu. It matters most where the form factor is high, as at Honolulu, where the twice-daily and once-daily tides are about equal.

**Step 4: how long high water stands.** For every spring tide of 2027 we took the highest high water within three days of the new or full Moon and measured, on a 2-minute predicted curve, how long the level stays within 20 cm of that peak (the paper's rule). On average it is **2.9 h at New York** (2.7 to 3.4), **3.0 h at San Francisco** (2.7 to 3.5) and **5.1 h at Honolulu** (4.7 to 5.8). All sit inside the paper's global range of 1 to 14 hours. Honolulu stands longest because its tide is small (a spring range well under a metre), so 20 cm is a large share of it, and the once-daily part flattens the crest.

## How we made the graphs

Every number and both charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/tide-age-form-factor-gesla-tide-gauges-harmonic-prediction.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `predict` (harmonic prediction with nodal factors f and angles u), `extremes` (high and low waters, refined by bisection), `windows` (time above a level), `speed` (constituent speeds) and `astro` (the mean longitudes of the Moon and Sun) from `src/lib/sci/tide.ts`, the maths behind the [[tide-predictor]].

Inputs: NOAA's published amplitudes and Greenwich phases for the eight main constituents (M2, S2, N2, K2, K1, O1, P1, Q1) plus M4 at New York and San Francisco. The new and full Moons are the times when the Moon's and Sun's **mean** longitudes differ by 0° or 180°, which can be up to about 14 hours from the true instant; over 247 events that scatter averages out. The ten-year window, the lunar-day smoothing and the five-day search are **our choices**. Mean sea level is set to zero, so heights are relative to it, not to a chart datum.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/tide-predictor.mjs`, which pins the tool against UTide (a standard tidal analysis package) and Schureman's manual:

- Constituent speeds match UTide's table to 2 × 10⁻⁶ degrees per hour (M2 28.9841043, K1 15.0410686).
- The nodal factor of M2 is 0.963 and 1.038 at the two ends of the 18.6-year cycle, and of K1 1.113 and 0.882, as Schureman tabulates; predictions with nodal corrections match UTide's reconstruction within 1 mm.
- New with this article: NOAA's New York constants give a spring high water at 01:45 UTC on 10 March 2027 of 0.80 m, a 2.9 h window above 0.60 m, and an age of 24.3 h.

## Check it yourself

On the [[tide-predictor]]:

1. In *Tidal constituents*, clear the box and paste NOAA's New York numbers, one per line: **M2 0.671 18.2**, **S2 0.128 42.9**, **N2 0.156 0.5**, **K2 0.036 44.5**, **K1 0.103 178.5**, **O1 0.051 176.3**, **P1 0.033 178.8**, **Q1 0.011 196.6**, **M4 0.024 264.1**.
2. Set *Mean level Z0 above datum* to **0**, *Units* to **metres** and *Nodal corrections* to **on**.
3. Under *Prediction*, type *Start date* **2027-03-09**, *Days* **2**, *Show times at UTC offset (hours)* **0** and *Needed water level* **0.6**.
4. In *High and low waters* the highest is **0.80 m at 01:45 on 10 March 2027**, about 28 hours after the new Moon (22:00 UTC on 7 March by the mean longitudes the tool uses): the age of the tide at work. The highs of 9 March are 0.72 and 0.75 m.
5. *Draft windows* shows **2.9 hours** above 0.60 m around that high water (00:20 to 03:13), and about 2.3 to 2.5 hours around the others.
6. Swap in the San Francisco or Honolulu constants from `sources:` and watch the two daily high waters become unequal: that is the form factor showing.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check an age or form factor in a table** from published constants in a minute, and see whether a full prediction agrees with the shortcut ([[tide-predictor]]).
- **Plan fieldwork at spring tides**, such as intertidal surveys or sediment sampling, from the real peak, not the Moon's phase.
- **Estimate how long a site floods** at a given level, the tool's *Draft windows* being the same calculation as the paper's high-water stand.
- **Analyse your own gauge record**: paste a month or more of levels into *Harmonic analysis of a record* to get your own constants, then work out the age and form factor from them.
- **Teach tides**: put in M2 and S2 only, change the S2 phase by 10°, and the spring tide moves by about 10 hours.

## The fine print

- Our constants are NOAA's, not the paper's TICON-4 values, and our New York age (24.3 h) differs from the paper's (about 18 h); San Francisco and Honolulu agree.
- Predictions are astronomical tides only. Storm surges, river flow and sea-level rise change real water levels. **Not for navigation**: use official tide tables.
- We used nine constituents or fewer; the paper uses 50, and NOAA lists 37. Shallow-water constituents matter more in estuaries.
- The paper's trend results (125 of 237 records) need decades of data and are not recomputed here.
