An antenna etched on a scrap of circuit board looks simple, and the first few numbers behind it really are: how fast a wave travels along a copper strip over a ground plane, and how long the strip must be to resonate. A 2026 paper in *Electronics* designs such an antenna for **ambient RF energy harvesting**, picking up stray radio power from mobile base stations and Wi-Fi and turning it into a few volts of direct current. Its board is ordinary 1.6 mm FR-4. That makes every design step checkable with the [[pcb-trace-calculator|PCB trace tool]], which carries the same closed-form microstrip model that antenna designers use before they open a field solver. Below we reproduce the paper's own effective permittivity and resonance, and work out the one number its text omits: how wide a 50 Ω feed line has to be on that board. For the copper losses at these frequencies see the [[skin-depth-calculator]], and for what the harvested power means at a distance, the [[link-budget-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The antenna is a rectangular copper patch, **36 mm wide and 29.5 mm long**, on a 40 × 47 × 1.6 mm FR-4 substrate with a relative permittivity of 4.4, a loss tangent of 0.02 and 0.035 mm copper. A plain patch of that size has one useful resonance; the authors cut a slot in the **ground plane** behind it to add a second, lower band, so that one small antenna covers both the GSM-1800 uplink near **1.75 GHz** and Wi-Fi at **2.45 GHz**.

Their analytical starting point gives an **effective permittivity of about 4.07**, an **edge extension ΔL of about 0.74 mm** and a fundamental patch mode near **2.40 GHz**. Simulation puts the unslotted resonance at about 2.36 GHz and the slotted upper band at about 2.42 GHz; measurement on the built board gives minima near **1.85 GHz** and **2.44 GHz**, with −10 dB bandwidths of about 210 and 155 MHz. Simulated peak gains are 1.07 dBi at 1.75 GHz and 2.54 dBi at 2.45 GHz. Fed with weak signals the rectifier still produced 3.60 V and 3.85 V from about −13 and −12 dBm.

Two terms, because the rest of the article uses them. The **effective permittivity** εeff is the single number that says how much slower a wave travels along a strip than in free space: part of the field runs inside the board and part in the air above it, so εeff lands between 1 and the board's own 4.4. The **edge extension** ΔL allows for the field that fringes past each end of the patch, which makes the patch behave as if it were slightly longer than the copper.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from the paper's stated geometry**: the board thickness, permittivity, copper thickness and the two patch dimensions. No measured curve is reproduced and nothing is fitted.

**The effective permittivity matches to three digits.** Ask the tool for a microstrip 36 mm wide on 1.6 mm FR-4 with 35 µm copper and εr 4.4 and it returns **εeff = 4.0724**. The paper's own estimate is 4.07. This is not a trivial agreement: the tool uses the Hammerstad and Jensen model with its strip-thickness correction, while a patch designer normally uses the simpler wide-strip formula, which here gives 4.0729. For a patch this wide (width ÷ height = 22.5) the two agree to four digits, so either is safe; on a narrow feed line they part company.

**The 2.40 GHz patch mode falls out of the same two numbers.** A half-wave patch resonates where its effective length is half a wavelength in that slower medium. With the tool's εeff and the paper's 0.74 mm extension, a 29.5 mm patch resonates at **2.3976 GHz**, which is the paper's 2.40. Leave the extension out and you get 2.5179 GHz, 5 % high: the fringing field is not a detail.

**It also shows why the patch is a little short of its target.** To sit exactly on 2.45 GHz the patch would need to be **28.84 mm**, not 29.5 mm; 29.5 mm aims at 2.40 GHz. That is consistent with the paper's own simulated 2.36 to 2.42 GHz and with the measured 2.44 GHz, which came out slightly higher again, most likely because the real FR-4 permittivity is a little below the catalogue 4.4. Figure 2 shows the whole curve. It also shows why the ground slot exists: a plain patch for **1.75 GHz would need to be 40.97 mm long**, which will not fit on a 40 × 47 mm board. A slot in the ground plane is the compact way to buy the lower band.

**The number the paper leaves out: the feed width.** The text sets a 50 Ω reference impedance but gives no feed-line dimensions (they are in a table image). The tool's *Width for an impedance* answers it: on this stack-up a 50 Ω microstrip is **3.02 mm wide**, with an effective permittivity of 3.30, a delay of 6.06 ps/mm, 0.303 nH/mm and 0.121 pF/mm. A guided wavelength at 2.45 GHz is then **67.3 mm**, so a quarter-wave matching section would be 16.8 mm. Two useful warnings come with it (Figure 1):

- A 2 mm line, a common guess, is **62.8 Ω** on this board, not 50 Ω. Impedance moves fast at these widths.
- The patch edge itself is only **7.2 Ω** as a transmission line, which is why a patch is never fed by butting a 50 Ω line against its middle: an inset, a quarter-wave section or, as here, a slot and an offset feed is needed.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/fr4-patch-antenna-effective-permittivity-microstrip-50-ohm-width.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `microstrip`, `widthFor`, `perLength`, `traceR` and `ipcCurrent` from `src/lib/sci/eng-pcb.ts`, the maths behind the [[pcb-trace-calculator|PCB trace tool]].

From the paper: εr 4.4, a 1.6 mm substrate, 0.035 mm copper, the 36 × 29.5 mm patch, the 0.74 mm edge extension and the 50 Ω reference. **Our assumption:** that the resonance is the plain half-wave patch relation, with the paper's extension applied at both ends, and that the quoted 4.4 is the permittivity at these frequencies (FR-4 is not a controlled material; 4.2 to 4.6 is normal, and a 1 % change in εeff moves the resonance by half a per cent).

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/pcb-trace-calculator.mjs` on every change to fibub. The impedance model is pinned against an independent two-dimensional finite-volume solve of Laplace's equation in SciPy (two grids, Richardson extrapolated) and, for stripline, against the exact conformal-mapping formula:

- Five microstrip cases must match the field solve to 1.2 %, for example W/h = 2 with εr 4.4 giving Z₀ ≈ 48.4 Ω and εeff ≈ 3.32, and a thick-copper case W/h = 4, t/h = 0.1, εr 3.5 giving 33.0 Ω.
- Centred stripline at W/b = 0.5, εr 4 must give 50.216 Ω, the exact elliptic-integral value, to 0.6 %.
- The IPC-2221 fit must give 11.898 mil for 1 A at a 10 °C rise on 1 oz outer copper, and inner layers must need 2^(1/0.725) times the cross-section.
- New with this article: the paper's board. A 36 mm microstrip on 1.6 mm FR-4 with 1 oz copper and εr 4.4 must give εeff 4.0724 and Z₀ 7.187 Ω; the width for 50 Ω must be 3.017 mm with εeff 3.303; and the resulting half-wave patch frequency for 29.5 mm plus 2 × 0.74 mm must be 2.3976 GHz.

## Check it yourself

On the [[pcb-trace-calculator|PCB trace tool]], in the **Impedance** card:

1. Leave *Line* on **Microstrip (outer layer over a plane)** and *Find* on **Impedance from the width**.
2. Set *Dielectric height h to the plane* to **1.6**, *Trace width W* to **36**, *Relative permittivity εr* to **4.4** and *Copper weight* to **1 oz (35 µm)**.
3. It reads **7.19 Ω** with *Effective εr* **4.07**, a delay of **6.73 ps/mm** and *Width ÷ height* 22.5. The 4.07 is the paper's own number.
4. Change *Find* to **Width for an impedance** and type **50** in *Wanted impedance (Ω)*. The answer is about **3.02 mm**, with *Effective εr* **3.3**: the feed line the paper does not print.
5. Type **2** in *Trace width W* with *Find* back on **Impedance from the width**: **62.8 Ω**. That is how far a guessed width can miss.
6. For the DC side, in the **Trace width for current** card set *Find* to **Current for a width**, *Trace width* **3.02** mm, *Allowed temperature rise* **10** °C, *Copper weight* **1 oz** and *Layer* **Outer layer (in air)**: about **5.3 A**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published antenna's analytical step** before trusting its simulation: the effective permittivity and the resonance follow from the stack-up in two lines, as above.
- **Size a feed line or a matching section** for a board you are about to etch, including the guided wavelength for a quarter-wave transformer.
- **Judge how much a patch will move** if the real permittivity differs from the catalogue value, by rerunning the width with εr 4.2 and 4.6.
- **Decide when a plain patch cannot reach a band** on the board you have, as with the 41 mm this design would have needed at 1.75 GHz, and plan a slot or a meander instead.
- **Keep the DC wiring honest** on a harvester or sensor board with the IPC-2221 width and the trace resistance, in the same tool.

## The fine print

- Closed-form microstrip models are approximations. They are excellent for planning, but a patch with a ground slot, an offset feed and a finite board is a full three-dimensional problem; the paper's simulation and measurement are the authority on the finished antenna.
- The paper's dimension table is published as an image, so the feed-line width, the slot length and the slot offset were not readable. Our 3.02 mm is what 50 Ω needs, not necessarily what the authors used.
- The half-wave patch relation with an edge extension is the textbook model; the paper's 0.74 mm extension is used as given, not recomputed.
- FR-4's permittivity and loss both drift with frequency and between batches. Treat 4.4 as a label, not a measurement.
- Nothing here checks the paper's gains, efficiencies, radiation patterns or rectifier results.
