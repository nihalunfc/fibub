Game theory's safest answer to an opponent who might want to hurt you is the **maximin strategy**: pick the mix of moves whose worst case is as good as possible, assuming the other side will do whatever harms you most. But a real opponent who wants to hurt you usually also cares about not getting hurt itself. A 2025 paper in *Games* turns that idea into a **linear program**, an optimisation with straight-line constraints, and shows that it can guarantee you more than the classical maximin. This article rebuilds the paper's worked example in the [[lp-solver]] and checks every number it states, then looks at the same game's ordinary equilibria in the [[nash-equilibrium-solver]]. For counting how many strategy supports such a search has to try, the [[combinatorics-calculator]] helps.

## The paper in brief

The authors look at players who want an **adequate** payoff rather than the largest one. Their first scenario is a two-player game with a "partially malicious" row player: it would like the column player to do badly, but only among strategies that keep its own payoff above a minimum it sets for itself. They measure that minimum with a **risk threshold** θ between 0 (no requirement at all) and 1 (the row player insists on its own maximin value).

Their worked example is a game with 4 row strategies and 3 column strategies, with θ = 0.22, which they say gives the row player a minimum requirement of about −53.89. Against a fully malicious row player the column player's classical maximin mix is about (0.61, 0, 0.39), guaranteeing about −33.48. Knowing the row player must protect itself, a **generalised maximin** linear program gives the column player about (0.54, 0, 0.46), guaranteeing about **−31.73**. The row player's matching **generalised minimax** strategy, the safe mix that hurts the column player most, is about (0.10, 0.13, 0.77, 0), and it holds the column player to the same −31.73. The paper's second scenario applies similar linear programs to **truncation selection** in evolutionary games, where strategies whose payoff falls below a threshold are culled.

## Checked with the tools

We typed the paper's payoff matrices into the LP solver's maths and solved three linear programs: the column player's classical maximin, the row player's own maximin, and the row player's generalised minimax with the requirement written in as three extra constraints (one per column strategy: the row player's expected payoff against it must stay at or above the requirement). This is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated game, not a simulation.

**Step 1: the classical maximin.** The LP solver finds the column player's best worst case at **−33.479**, with the mix (0.6134, 0, 0.3866). In exact fractions that is −3984/119 with weights 73/119 and 46/119, the paper's −33.48 and (0.61, 0, 0.39).

**Step 2: where −53.89 comes from.** The paper does not print the rule that turns θ into a requirement. The row player's own maximin, found by the same solver, is **28** (always play row 4), and its worst payoff anywhere in the game is −77. Reading θ as the fraction of the way from the worst payoff to the maximin value gives −77 + 0.22 × 105 = **−53.9**, which matches the paper's "about −53.89". That rule is **our reading**, modelled on the paper's definition of the column player's risk measure.

**Step 3: the generalised minimax.** With the requirement at −53.89, the LP solver gives the row player's most harmful safe mix as **(0.1019, 0.1330, 0.7650, 0)** and the column player's guarantee as **−31.736**, the paper's −31.73. With −53.9 it is −31.737, so the rounding does not matter. The **shadow prices** on the three column-payoff constraints, how much the optimum moves if each constraint is loosened by one unit, are 0.5436, 0 and 0.4564. By linear programming duality those are exactly the column player's defensive mix, the paper's (0.54, 0, 0.46). One linear program gives both players' answers.

**Step 4: the whole range of thresholds.** The paper quantifies the gain only at θ = 0.22. We solved the same linear program at 101 thresholds from 0 to 1 (Figure 1). The guarantee equals the classical maximin up to θ = 0.05; from **θ = 0.06** it starts to climb: −31.95 at 0.2, −28.72 at 0.5, −13.92 at 0.7 and +28.77 at 0.9. At θ = 1 the row player has only one safe move left, row 4, and the column player can count on **85** by replying with column 1. This bears out the paper's statement that the gain depends on how tightly the requirement restricts the row player.

**Step 5: the ordinary Nash equilibria, for contrast.** The Nash equilibrium solver lists four extreme equilibria of the same game: (row 2, column 3) paying 62 and 34; (row 4, column 1) paying 28 and 85; a mix of rows 1 and 2 (0.5914 and 0.4086) against column 3, paying 62 and −23.37; and a mix of rows 2 and 4 (0.4977, 0.5023) against columns 1 and 3 (0.1139, 0.8861), paying 50.15 and 4.87. ## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/malicious-opponent-generalised-maximin-linear-programming-bimatrix-game.mjs` in fibub's public code. It writes each model as plain text, exactly as you would type it into the [[lp-solver]], and solves it with `parseModel` and `solve` from `src/lib/sci/lp.ts`, the two-phase simplex method behind that tool. The Nash equilibria come from `supportEnumeration` in `src/lib/sci/nash.ts`, the maths of the [[nash-equilibrium-solver]].

The payoff matrices, θ = 0.22, the requirement of −53.89 and the paper's rounded results come from the paper's text. The rule linking θ to the requirement (worst payoff plus θ times the distance to the maximin value) is **our reading**. The thresholds other than 0.22 are our own sweep.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/lp-solver.mjs`, which pins the solver against SciPy's `linprog` and `milp` (HiGHS):

- The feed-mix example must give 2.0407643 with shadow prices 0.00707006, 0.03458599, 0 and −0.24783439, as SciPy's marginals do.
- The shipping example must cost 620 with duals 0, −2, 4, 5 and 9, and 36 random models must match SciPy's status and objective.
- New with this article: the Zhang and Morsky game must give the classical maximin −3984/119 at weights 73/119 and 46/119, and the generalised minimax −31.7357 with mix (0.1019, 0.1330, 0.7650, 0) and duals 0.5436, 0, 0.4564.

The Nash solver's checks in `tests/sci-tools/nash-equilibrium-solver.mjs` compare its equilibria with nashpy's vertex enumeration on textbook and random games up to 6 × 6.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recompute a game-theory paper's maximin or minimax** in a few lines of text with the [[lp-solver]], and read the opponent's mix from the shadow prices.
- **Add a "safety requirement" to any matrix game**: one extra constraint per opponent strategy, then sweep the requirement as we did.
- **List every Nash equilibrium** of the same game in the [[nash-equilibrium-solver]] and compare it with the cautious answers.
- **Teach LP duality** with a case where the dual values have a meaning students can see: the other player's strategy.
- **Plan an experiment** on spiteful play: compute in advance what a cautious player should do at each threshold you plan to test.

## The fine print

- We checked the paper's first scenario, the partially malicious opponent. Its second scenario, safe spaces under truncation selection, uses polyhedral vertex enumeration that our tools do not do.
- The rule from θ to the requirement is our reading; the paper gives only the resulting value, about −53.89, which our reading matches to the stated precision.
- Our guarantee rounds to −31.74 where the paper writes about −31.73; the paper seems to cut off rather than round.
- The game has a degenerate equilibrium (a whole segment of row mixes against column 3); the Nash solver lists its end points.
