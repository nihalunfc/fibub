The **transient climate response** (TCR) is the warming at the moment carbon dioxide doubles, if it has been rising 1 % a year — 70 years of rise, with the ocean still absorbing heat. It is the number that matters for this century, and it is not the same as the **equilibrium climate sensitivity** (ECS), the warming once everything has settled. A 2026 paper in *Earth System Dynamics* puts an observational constraint on the TCR using records through 2024 and gets **1.81 K**. We took that number into the [[energy-balance-model|climate balance model]] to see what it fixes and what it leaves open. For the records behind such a constraint, see the [[trend-analyser]]; for the sunlight side of the energy budget, the [[insolation-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors use an **emergent constraint**: a relationship across climate models between something observable and something you want to know, calibrated on the models and then read off with the observations. With warming records extended to 2024 they get a TCR of **1.81 K**, very likely between **1.28 and 2.33 K**, slightly above estimates that stopped at 2019 (by under 0.05 K, and about ±0.04 K for the choice of scenario). That sits inside but low in the IPCC AR6 very likely range of 1.2 to 2.4 K, and it is about 0.1 K above three earlier emergent-constraint studies.

They are careful not to turn this into an ECS. The paper reviews how far apart ECS estimates still are: AR6's very likely 2.0 to 5.0 K, Sherwood et al.'s 2.3 to 4.5 K with a best estimate near 3 K, an aerosol-based inference of about 2.2 K, paleoclimate evidence around 4 K, and an argument for 4.5 K. Their warming projections sit in the low-to-middle part of the CMIP6 spread, with a late-century SSP5-8.5 central estimate of **4.3 K** against AR6's constrained 4.4 K, and they conclude that staying below 2 °C is still feasible.

## Checked with the tools

Every number below comes from putting the paper's own values into the **linear feedback model** the tool runs: C dΔT/dt = F − λΔT, where λ is the climate feedback parameter in W/m²/K and C the heat capacity of a water layer of a given depth. The forcing is the tool's Myhre expression ΔF = 5.35 ln(C/C₀), so a doubling is **3.708 W/m²**. The ramp option, *Up 1 % a year, then held*, is exactly the TCR experiment, because forcing is logarithmic in concentration and therefore linear in time while CO₂ rises exponentially.

**Step 1: the one thing a TCR fixes.** A TCR of 1.81 K for 3.708 W/m² is **0.488 K per W/m²**, and the quantity it pins is the sum of the feedback parameter and the ocean heat uptake efficiency: **λ + κ = 2.049 W/m²/K**. The paper's very likely range 1.28 to 2.33 K is **2.90 down to 1.59 W/m²/K**, and AR6's 1.2 to 2.4 K is 3.09 down to 1.55. That single number is what a TCR constraint buys you.

**Step 2: the degeneracy, drawn.** In a one-box model you can reproduce 1.81 K with a stiff feedback and a thin ocean layer, or a weak feedback and a thick one. Solving for λ at each depth: **50 m needs λ = 1.95** (equilibrium sensitivity 1.90 K), **100 m needs 1.84** (2.02 K), **200 m needs 1.56** (2.38 K), **300 m needs 1.19** (3.11 K) and **400 m needs 0.75** (4.97 K). Figure 1 runs three of these out to 300 years with the forcing held after doubling: identical at year 70, between 1.96 and 4.85 K by year 300.

**Step 3: how far the degeneracy goes.** It is not unlimited. With no feedback at all, the ramp can only warm a layer by (F/2) × 70 years ÷ C, so a one-box ocean deeper than **541 m** cannot reach 1.81 K however weak its feedback. Going the other way, the implied ECS reaches AR6's lower bound of **2.0 K at an effective depth of 93 m**, the familiar best estimate of **3 K at 289 m**, and AR6's upper bound of **5.0 K at 401 m** (Figure 2). So the paper's TCR is consistent with the whole AR6 ECS range, as long as the heat reaches roughly 90 to 400 m of water — which is what ocean heat content observations show. The transient constraint and the equilibrium range do not fight.

**Step 4: the realised warming fraction.** TCR ÷ ECS falls from **0.95** at 50 m to **0.36** at 400 m. The textbook value of about 0.6 corresponds to the 3 K best estimate: with ECS 3 K and TCR 1.81 K the fraction is **0.60**, the effective layer is 289 m and the response time C ÷ λ is **31 years**. Anyone quoting a realised fraction is implicitly quoting an ocean depth, and the tool makes that visible in one field.

**Step 5: what the aerosol uncertainty is worth.** The paper gives a present-day aerosol forcing of −1.6 to −0.6 W/m², a spread of 1.0 W/m². At this TCR's efficiency of 0.488 K per W/m², that spread alone is **0.49 K of warming** — a quarter of the TCR itself, and the reason the aerosol-based ECS inference (about 2.2 K) sits so far below the paleoclimate one (around 4 K).

**Step 6: two sanity checks on the paper's own figures.** 2024's 1.5 K above pre-industrial is **83 %** of the warming this TCR gives for a full doubling of CO₂ — a useful way to feel how little headroom is left. And the paper's late-century SSP5-8.5 estimate of 4.3 K: that scenario is named for roughly 8.5 W/m² of forcing in 2100 (**our assumption**, since the paper does not state a forcing), and 8.5 W/m² at 0.488 K per W/m² is **4.15 K**. Within 4 % of the paper's number, from one multiplication, which is a good sign that their projections are the TCR doing the work rather than anything exotic.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/emergent-constraint-transient-climate-response-1-81-k-feedback-parameter.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `co2Forcing` (the Myhre expression), `heatCap`, `runLinear` (the RK4 integration of C dΔT/dt = F − λΔT with the 1 %-per-year ramp) and `efold` from `src/lib/sci/ebm.ts`, the maths behind the [[energy-balance-model|climate balance model]]. The feedback parameter at each depth is found by bisection on the warming at year 70.

Inputs from the paper's text: the TCR and its range, the AR6 and Sherwood ECS ranges, the aerosol forcing range, the SSP5-8.5 estimate and the 1.5 K figure for 2024. **Our assumptions**: that the TCR experiment is a 70-year 1 %-per-year ramp (the standard definition), that a one-box model with a single effective depth is a fair caricature of ocean heat uptake, and the 8.5 W/m² used in Step 6. A real ocean has two or more time scales, which is why "effective depth" is the right phrase.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/energy-balance-model.mjs`, which pins the model against closed-form and SciPy results:

- The doubling forcing must be 5.35 ln 2 = 3.708337 W/m² to 1 part in 10¹², and zero at no change.
- The RK4 run of the linear model must match the exact step solution ΔT(t) = (F/λ)(1 − e^(−λt/C)) at all 101 sample times to better than 10⁻⁸ K, and the ramp forcing must grow linearly to F and then hold.
- 100 m of water must give 418 MJ/m²/K and an e-folding time of 11.038017 years at λ = 1.2.
- The ice-albedo branch must find three equilibria at S = 1250 W/m² and two turning points at S = 1347.3911 and 1185.9647 W/m², as SciPy's root finder does.
- New with this article: the 70-year 1 %-per-year ramp must give 1.81 K at doubling for λ = 1.90 with 75 m of water, for λ = 1.56 with 200 m and for λ = 1.19 with 300 m.

## Check it yourself

On the [[energy-balance-model|climate balance model]] page:

1. In *CO₂ change*, leave *Starting CO₂ C₀* at **280** ppm and *New CO₂ C* at **560** ppm, and set *CO₂ changes* to **Up 1 % a year, then held**.
2. Set *Years to run* to **70**, *Feedback parameter λ* to **1.9** and *Ocean mixed layer* to **75** m. Untick *Ice makes the planet whiter as it cools* so only the Planck response is left in the other curve.
3. In *Warming over time*, *Linear after 70 years* reads **+1.81 K**, "of **1.95 K** at equilibrium", and *Response time (linear)* **5.23 years**. That is the paper's TCR in a world with a low sensitivity.
4. Change λ to **1.56** and the mixed layer to **200** m. *Linear after 70 years* is **+1.81 K** again, but equilibrium is now **2.38 K** and the response time **17.0 years**.
5. Change λ to **1.19** and the layer to **300** m: still **+1.81 K** at year 70, equilibrium **3.12 K**, response time **33.4 years**. Three different planets, one transient response.
6. Now set *Years to run* to **300** and watch the three apart: the curves separate completely after the ramp ends. Use **Download time series CSV** if you want the numbers.
7. For the best-estimate world, type λ **1.24** and layer **289** m: **+1.81 K** at 70 years of **2.99 K** at equilibrium, a realised fraction of 0.60.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a quoted sensitivity into a feedback parameter** and back: λ = 3.708 ÷ ECS, in one field, before comparing two papers that use different language.
- **Check whether a TCR and an ECS are mutually consistent** with any plausible ocean heat uptake, instead of treating them as independent facts.
- **Convert a forcing uncertainty into a temperature uncertainty** at transient efficiency, which is how aerosol uncertainty propagates into attribution.
- **Teach why committed warming exists**: run the ramp, stop the forcing, and watch the temperature keep climbing for a century.
- **Explore tipping behaviour** with the ice-albedo part of the same tool, which the paper's linear framework deliberately leaves out.

## The fine print

- The paper constrains the TCR with an emergent constraint across climate models; we did not reproduce that constraint, which needs the model ensemble. We take its result as given and ask what it implies.
- A one-box ocean is a caricature. Real heat uptake has a fast mixed layer and a slow deep ocean, so the "effective depth" here is a summary, not a measurement of a real layer.
- λ is taken as constant. Most models show it changing as warming goes on, which is why "effective" sensitivity and equilibrium sensitivity differ in the literature the paper reviews.
- The 8.5 W/m² used in Step 6 is our assumption for the scenario's late-century forcing; the paper states only the warming.
- Nothing here bears on whether 1.5 or 2 °C is reachable. That depends on emissions, not on the model above.
