Grasses of the same woodland can differ fivefold in how many stomata they carry per square millimetre, which is one reason some of them survive grazing and drought better than others. A 2026 paper in *Grasses* measured stomatal density, epidermal cell density and stomatal index on ten grasses of an oak-pine ecosystem in Chihuahua, Mexico. Those three numbers are not independent — the index is fixed by the two densities — so a published table can be checked against itself. This article does that with the [[stomatal-density-counter]], and then asks how precise three microscope fields can be. Measure the leaves themselves with the [[leaf-area-analyser]], and plan how many fields to count with the [[sample-size]].

## The paper in brief

The authors sampled ten grasses over 1938 ha in October and November 2022, took epidermal impressions and read them under a bright-field microscope at **400×**, in optical fields of **0.0946 mm²**, with three optical fields per impression and three impressions per leaf.

Stomatal density ranged from **60.0** stomata mm⁻² on the upper (adaxial) surface of *Setaria parviflora* to **299.8** in *Muhlenbergia arizonica*, and on the lower surface from 38.8 in *Bouteloua hirsuta* to **317.5**, again in *M. arizonica*. Epidermal cell density ran from **243.4** to **1499.1** cells mm⁻² on the upper surface. The stomatal index — stomata as a percentage of all epidermal cells — stayed in a narrow band, **11.2 to 19.9 %** above and 10.9 to 20.1 % below, with *M. arizonica* at 16.7 and 17.2 % and *Panicum bulbosum* at 18 and 16.8 %. The authors read the pattern as drought adaptation: the species with many small cells pack in many stomata without raising the share of the epidermis they occupy.

## Checked with the tools

The stomatal index is Salisbury's 1928 definition, which the counter implements as

    stomatal index = 100 × stomata ÷ (stomata + epidermal cells)

so any two of the three published numbers predict the third. Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's own stated values**; nothing is simulated and no table is reproduced.

**Step 1: the Results table is self-consistent.** For *M. arizonica*, 299.8 stomata and 1499.1 cells mm⁻² give an index of **16.67 %** against the published **16.7**, and 317.5 with 1527.3 gives **17.21 %** against **17.2**. For *S. parviflora*, 60.0 and 243.4 give **19.78 %** against the published **19.9** — close, and an epidermal density of 241.5 instead of 243.4 would land exactly on it. Three independent checks pass, which also confirms that the paper's "epidermal cell density" counts ordinary cells only, with the stomata added back in by the index, as Salisbury intended.

**Step 2: the Discussion disagrees with the Results.** The paper's Discussion quotes **1672.0** and **1661.4** cells mm⁻² for the same species and surfaces. Put those into the same formula and the index becomes **15.20 %** and **16.04 %**, not 16.7 and 17.2 (Figure 1). The two sets of epidermal densities cannot both go with the published index, and it is the Results pair that fits. A reader taking the Discussion's number into a meta-analysis would carry a 9 % error on the cell density with them.

**Step 3: what 0.0946 mm² means in stomata.** Multiplying each density by the field area turns the table back into what the counter actually sees: **28.4 stomata and 141.8 epidermal cells** per field for *M. arizonica*, and only **5.7 and 23.0** for *S. parviflora*. Over three fields that is 85 stomata against 17. The sparsest grass in the study is being measured from a couple of dozen objects.

**Step 4: the precision that follows.** A count of k stomata carries a Poisson standard error of √k, so the relative error on the density is 1 ÷ √k. Three fields give **±10.8 %** for *M. arizonica* and **±24.3 %** for *S. parviflora*; the nine fields that make up a leaf (three impressions × three fields) give ±6.3 % and ±14.0 %. Reaching ±10 % on the sparsest species needs about **18 fields** (Figure 2). For the index itself, which is a proportion of all the cells counted, the Wilson score interval on three fields runs from **13.7 to 20.1 %** for *M. arizonica* (centre 16.6) and from **12.7 to 29.4 %** for *S. parviflora* (centre 19.8). So the index band the paper reports across species, 11 to 20 %, is real, but a difference of two or three percentage points between two species is inside the counting error of a single impression.

**Step 5: the field is a frame, not an eyepiece field.** A round field of 0.0946 mm² is **0.347 mm** across, which at 400× implies an eyepiece field number of **13.9 mm**. Real eyepieces are nearer 18 to 22, so this is a camera frame or a counted sub-square, not the whole field of view — worth knowing for anyone repeating the work, because a field number 22 eyepiece at 40× sees **0.2376 mm²**, **2.5 times** as much. One standard field would hold 71 stomata of *M. arizonica* and give the same precision as two and a half of theirs.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/oak-pine-grass-stomatal-index-density-consistency.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `stomatalIndex`, `fovDiameter`, `circleArea` and `wilson` from `src/lib/sci/plantimg.ts`, the module behind the [[stomatal-density-counter]].

Every input is a value the paper prints in its running text: the field area of 0.0946 mm², the 400× magnification, the three fields per impression and three impressions per leaf, the extreme stomatal and epidermal cell densities, the indices for *M. arizonica*, *S. parviflora* and *P. bulbosum*, and the Discussion's conflicting cell densities. **Our additions** are the choice of a 95 % Wilson interval and the Poisson model of counting error, which assumes stomata are scattered at random within a field.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/stomatal-density-counter.mjs` runs on every change to fibub:

- A field number 22 eyepiece with a 40× objective must give a field 0.55 mm across and 0.2375829 mm² in area, and an extra 1.5× magnifier must shrink it to 4/3 mm.
- 23 stomata among 37 other cells must give an index of 38.33 %, and the made-up example epidermis (480 × 320 px at 2 px per µm, 0.0384 mm²) a density of 598.96 per mm².
- Otsu's threshold and the patch finder must find all 23 drawn stomata at their drawn centres, checked against scikit-image and SciPy's `ndimage.label`.
- New with this article: the paper's own densities must give indices 16.67, 17.21 and 19.78 %, its Discussion densities 15.20 and 16.04 %; its 0.0946 mm² field must be 0.3471 mm across, 2.51 times smaller than a field number 22 eyepiece at 40×; and three such fields of *S. parviflora* must give 17 stomata of 86 cells, a Wilson interval of 12.7 to 29.4 % and a ±24.3 % density error.

## Check it yourself

The counter works from a photo you tap, so the quickest way to see the arithmetic is with its own example (23 stomata and 37 epidermal cells already marked). On the [[stomatal-density-counter]] page:

1. Press **Use the example** in the *Microscope photo* card. The legend reads *Stomata: 23* and *Epidermal cells: 37*.
2. In *Field of view*, set *Area counted* to **I type the area** and type **0.0946** in *Area (mm²)* — the paper's field. The page reads about **243 stomata per mm²** with a stomatal index of **38.3 %**.
3. Switch *Area counted* to **Round field (field number and objective)** with *Field number* **22** and *Objective* **40**: the area becomes 0.2376 mm² and the density falls to about **97 per mm²**. The same 23 stomata, two and a half times fewer per mm² — which is why an area must always be stated with a density.
4. Type **13.9** in *Field number* instead and the density returns to **243 per mm²**: that is the eyepiece the paper's field size implies.
5. Press **Add this field to the list** and repeat with a second area to see the mean and standard deviation across fields, the number that should be reported.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published stomatal table against itself** before citing it: two of density, cell density and index fix the third, and a mismatch shows which number to trust.
- **Report the field area with every density.** The counter makes the area explicit — from a scale line, from the field number, or typed — so a reader can convert.
- **Decide how many fields to count** before going to the microscope: for a sparse species it is nearer 18 fields than three, and the chart above gives the trade-off at a glance.
- **Put an interval on a stomatal index**, not just a mean, with the Wilson interval on the cells actually counted.
- **Compare canopy positions or hybrids** on the same footing, as in our article on [[blog:discoveries/maize-hybrid-stomatal-density-leaf-layers|maize stomatal density down the canopy]].

## The fine print

- We checked the three species whose density, cell density and index are all in the running text. The rest of the ten are in the paper's tables, which we do not reproduce.
- The counting errors are Poisson errors from the objects counted. Real studies also carry leaf-to-leaf and plant-to-plant variation, so these are floors, not estimates of the paper's own uncertainty.
- Stomata are not perfectly random on a grass epidermis: they sit in files between the veins, which makes a field-to-field spread a little wider than Poisson.
- The clash between the paper's Results and Discussion cell densities may be a typesetting slip rather than a measurement error; we report only that the two cannot both match its published index.
