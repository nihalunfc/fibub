Grain is thrown about by every machine that handles it: threshing drums, elevators, cleaning decks, chutes. How hard a kernel bounces decides whether it cracks, where it lands, and whether a simulation of the machine is worth anything. A 2025 paper in *Applied Sciences* measures that bounciness — the **coefficient of restitution** — for wheat kernels of one variety hitting a flat metal plate, across four moisture contents and impact speeds from 1 to 5 m/s, and finds it falls from about **0.60 when dry to 0.40–0.50 when wet**, and falls further as the impact gets faster. A single number like that only becomes useful when you turn it into heights, energies and forces, which is what the [[collision-calculator]] does; for the flight before and after the bounce, use the [[projectile-drag]] tool and the [[video-motion-tracker]] to get speeds out of high-speed footage.

## The paper in brief

The authors built a bench rig: grains are guided onto a flat metal surface, a piezoelectric force sensor under the plate (112.41 mV/N, 44.48 N range) records the impact at **1 MHz**, and a high-speed camera with its own lighting watches the bounce. Grains of the 'Memory' winter wheat variety were conditioned to **7, 10, 13 and 16 %** moisture and fired at the plate at normal (straight-on) velocities of **1.0 to 4.5 m/s**.

The result in the text: the coefficient of restitution is about **0.60 at the driest condition** and drops to **0.40–0.50 at 16 % moisture**, and within each moisture level it **falls as the impact velocity rises** from 1.0 to 5.0 m/s. Wet grain is soft grain: more of the impact goes into squashing the kernel and never comes back.

We could read the paper's text and methods but not its figures, where the value for each moisture-and-velocity combination lives. Everything below therefore works with the **ends of its stated range**, 0.60 and 0.40–0.50, which is enough for every question a bench or a simulation asks.

## Checked with the tools

A coefficient of restitution *e* is defined by the speeds along the line of impact: the separation speed divided by the approach speed. Against a plate heavy enough not to move, that means the grain leaves at *e* times the speed it arrived with, so three useful quantities follow at once — and all three come out of the collision calculator's own 1D solver, with the plate as a very heavy second body.

**1. Heights.** Free fall ties velocity to height, v = √(2gh). The paper's 1.0 to 5.0 m/s range needs drops of **5.1 cm to 1.28 m**. The drop-height rigs it cites, 10–25 cm and 155–300 mm, only reach **1.40 to 2.43 m/s** — the bottom third of the range. That is worth knowing before copying an earlier method: past about 2.5 m/s you need either a tall tower or a launcher, which is presumably why this rig guides the grains instead of simply dropping them. On the rebound side, a dry grain (e = 0.60) returns to 36 % of its drop height and a wet one (e = 0.45) to only 20 % (Figure 1).

**2. Energy.** The share of kinetic energy that does not survive the bounce is 1 − e², so the paper's range covers a loss of **64 % at e = 0.60** rising to **84 % at e = 0.40**. Going from dry to wet grain therefore raises the energy dumped into the kernel by a third, which is the mechanism behind moisture-dependent grain damage. There is a trap here that the tool makes obvious: the same *e* destroys a **different share** of the total energy depending on what the grain hits. Against a heavy plate it is 1 − e²; against an identical kernel sitting still, only **half** that — 32 % at e = 0.60 (Figure 2). A discrete-element model that uses a plate-measured *e* for grain-on-grain contacts is not wrong, but the energy book looks completely different.

**3. Forces, and why the rig is built the way it is.** For a kernel of **38 mg** (**our assumption**, a typical wheat grain) at 4.5 m/s with e = 0.5, the calculator gives an impulse of **274 µN·s** and an energy loss of **246 µJ**. Divide the impulse by the contact time and you get the force the plate must take. The paper's sensor tops out at 44.48 N, which would only be reached if the contact lasted about **6 microseconds**; at a more realistic few tens of microseconds the mean force is a few newtons, comfortably in range (Figure 3). It also shows why the amplifier runs at 1 MHz: a contact of 20–50 µs is only 20–50 samples long, and anything slower would miss the peak entirely.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/wheat-grain-restitution-coefficient-impact-velocity-energy-loss.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `collide1D(m1, u1, m2, u2, e)` and `fromMeasured(...)` from `src/lib/sci/phcollision.ts`, the maths behind the [[collision-calculator]]: `collide1D` returns both final velocities, the momentum and energy before and after, the energy lost and the impulse, and `fromMeasured` returns *e* from measured speeds. The plate is modelled as a second body of 10⁹ kg, which is the tool's "ball on a wall" case.

From the paper: the restitution range 0.40 to 0.60, the velocity range 1.0 to 5.0 m/s, the four moisture levels, the 44.48 N sensor range, the 1 MHz sampling and the drop heights it cites. **Our assumptions:** a kernel mass of 38 mg, a contact time swept from 5 to 100 µs because the paper's text does not state one, and free fall with no air resistance (for a 38 mg grain over a metre, drag is a small correction; the [[projectile-drag]] tool will put a number on it). Nothing here is a fit to the paper's data — it is a conversion of its stated restitution values into the quantities an experiment or a simulation needs.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/collision-calculator.mjs` on every change to fibub, pinning the same functions against SymPy solutions of momentum conservation plus the restitution law:

- A 2 kg body at 3 m/s hitting a 1 kg body at −1 m/s with e = 0.6 must give 0.86667 and 3.26667 m/s, and the energy lost must equal the closed form (1 − e²)·μ·Δu²/2 = 3.41333 J.
- Equal masses with e = 1 must swap speeds exactly; a perfectly inelastic collision of 3 kg into 1 kg must lose exactly a quarter of the energy; a ball on a wall must come back at exactly e times its speed.
- In 2D, two equal elastic spheres must leave at 90° to each other, and an oblique impact with e = 0.6 at 20° must match SymPy to 12 figures, with the impulse along the line of centres.
- New with this article: a 38 mg grain at 4.5 m/s on a 1000 kg plate with e = 0.6 must rebound at 2.7 m/s, lose 64.000 % of its kinetic energy and deliver an impulse of 273.60 µN·s; the same grain hitting an identical still grain with e = 0.5 must send them off at 1.125 and 3.375 m/s, losing 37.5 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[collision-calculator]] page:

1. Press **In a line (1D)**. Set *Mass 1 (kg)* to **0.000038** (the grain), *Velocity 1 before (m/s)* to **4.5**, *Mass 2 (kg)* to **1000** (the plate) and *Velocity 2 before (m/s)* to **0**.
2. Put **0.6** in *Coefficient of restitution e*. You should read *Body 1 after* **−2.7 m/s** ("bounces back"), *Kinetic energy* **0.0003848 J → 0.0001385 J** with **64 %** turned into heat and deformation, and *Impulse on body 2* **0.0002736 N·s**.
3. Change *Coefficient of restitution e* to **0.45**: the rebound falls to **−2.03 m/s** and the loss rises to **79.8 %**. That single change is the whole dry-to-wet effect the paper measures.
4. Now make it grain on grain: set *Mass 2 (kg)* to **0.000038** and *Coefficient of restitution e* to **0.5**. The two leave at **1.13 m/s** and **3.38 m/s**, and only **37.5 %** of the energy is lost — half the plate figure, with the same *e*.
5. Press **Find e from a lab run** to work backwards from a bounce you filmed. For a 50 cm drop and an 18 cm rebound, type *Velocity 1 before (m/s)* **3.132**, *Velocity 1 after, measured (m/s)* **−1.879**, with *Mass 2 (kg)* **1000** and both of body 2's velocities **0**: *Coefficient of restitution* reads **0.6**. Ignore the momentum-change line in this mode — against a fixed plate the momentum goes into the bench, so a large change there is expected, not an error.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Design a drop rig.** Work out the height each target velocity needs, and check whether the rebound is tall enough to film.
- **Turn a published *e* into a damage estimate.** The energy a kernel absorbs, in microjoules, is a better predictor of cracking than *e* itself.
- **Set up a discrete-element model honestly.** Grain-on-wall and grain-on-grain restitution are different inputs, and the energy they remove differs by a factor of two even when *e* is the same.
- **Size a force sensor and a sampling rate** from the impulse and an assumed contact time, as in Figure 3.
- **Recover *e* from high-speed video** by reading speeds off the frames with the [[video-motion-tracker]] and feeding them into the "find e from a lab run" mode.

## The fine print

- The paper's per-condition values are in its figures, which we could not read; we use only the range its text gives (0.60 down to 0.40–0.50). We do not reproduce or redraw any of its data.
- The 38 mg kernel mass and the 5–100 µs contact time are **ours**. Every force in Figure 3 scales with the mass and inversely with the contact time.
- Free fall ignores air drag, and a guided grain also rubs against its guide, so bench velocities are a little below the ideal height calculation.
- Real grains are not spheres and rarely hit flat-on; an oblique impact also spins the kernel, which the 2D mode of the tool covers but the paper's normal-impact measurement does not.
- Restitution is a property of the **pair** of surfaces and of the impact speed, never of the grain alone; a value measured on steel does not carry over to rubber or to another grain.
