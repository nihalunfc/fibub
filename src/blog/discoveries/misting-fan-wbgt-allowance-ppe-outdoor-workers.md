Protective clothing saves people from chemicals, dust and heat radiation, and then quietly cooks them. A plastic coverall stops sweat evaporating, and an outdoor worker in one can be in danger on a day that feels merely warm to everyone else. A 2025 paper in *Clean Technologies* put a heated mannequin in work clothes and PPE in front of an outdoor **misting fan** and measured how much extra heat it carried away. The number is small in watts but useful in the language safety rules actually use, the **wet-bulb globe temperature** (WBGT), and this article shows how the [[heat-stress]] calculator turns one into the other, and why the quick estimate from temperature and humidity read 4.8 °C too high on their test day. For the neighbouring jobs, try [[wind-chill-heat-index]] for feels-like numbers and [[psychrometric-calculator]] for the moist-air properties behind them.

## The paper in brief

The authors stood a mannequin with heat-flux sensors at eight body positions in front of a commercial misting fan in Osaka. The fan pushes air at about 0.5 m s⁻¹ as far as 10 m and sprays about 19 L h⁻¹ of water through eight nozzles at 6 MPa, making a fine mist with a Sauter mean droplet diameter of 25 µm; fan and pump together draw about 400 W. The mannequin wore work clothes that were either dry or wetted to stand in for sweat, with and without a vapour-impermeable PPE layer over the top.

Their headline result: under PPE, the misting fan removed more heat than still-air convection by **0.31 met (18.3 W m⁻²)** with wet work clothes and **0.35 met (20.3 W m⁻²)** with dry ones. Because roughly **30 W m⁻² of metabolic rate costs 1 °C of acceptable WBGT** in ISO 7243's work-rest tables, they conclude the fan is worth **more than 0.6 °C** of acceptable WBGT. They also note that the impermeable layer itself is worth 10 to 12 °C of *effective* WBGT in the wrong direction, so the fan claws back only a small part of what the PPE costs.

One sample day is given in full: 13 September 2022, 4 m from the fan, with air at 32.4 °C, 48 % relative humidity and a natural wet bulb of 24.3 °C. Across every trial the globe thermometer sat on average 0.3 °C *below* air temperature (root-mean-square difference 2.1 K), because the tests were in shade with the fan blowing.

Only the paper's abstract, introduction and discussion could be read from here; its results tables are not in the page text we could reach. Everything below uses the values listed in `sources:`.

## Checked with the tools

Three things can be checked without the tables, and all three matter to anyone planning hot-weather work.

**Step 1: the met-to-watts conversion.** ISO 8996 puts 1 met at 58.15 W m⁻² of body-surface heat. So 0.31 met is 18.0 W m⁻² and 0.35 met is 20.4 W m⁻², against the paper's 18.3 and 20.3. The two ways of stating the result agree to about 2 %, which is reassuring: the figures are internally consistent, and the small gap is the rounding in "0.31" and "0.35".

**Step 2: the WBGT allowance.** Dividing by 30 W m⁻² per °C gives **0.61 °C** for wet work clothes and **0.68 °C** for dry ones. The paper's "more than 0.6 °C" is exactly this, and now we have the range rather than the bound.

**Step 3: what 0.6 °C of WBGT is worth in plain air temperature.** This is the question a site supervisor asks, and the tool answers it. Holding humidity fixed and raising air temperature until the tool's estimate climbs by the allowance gives **0.56 to 0.62 °C** of extra air temperature at 48 % humidity, but only **0.41 to 0.46 °C** at 85 % (Figure 2). The reason is in the formula: WBGT rises with both temperature and vapour pressure, and vapour pressure itself climbs steeply with temperature, so in muggy air each degree of air temperature buys more WBGT and the fan's fixed allowance covers less of it. A fan rated in degrees of WBGT is therefore worth less in a monsoon than in a dry summer.

**Step 4: the warning in the paper's own sample day.** Feed the three readings into the tool in *Measured* mode and the outdoor-in-sun weighting gives

> WBGT = 0.7 × 24.3 + 0.2 × 32.1 + 0.1 × 32.4 = **26.7 °C**, a **white** flag,

and the shade weighting gives 26.6 °C, also white: with the globe thermometer sitting at air temperature, the two weightings barely differ. Now switch to *Estimate* and type the same air temperature and humidity: the tool reads **31.5 °C**, a **red** flag. That is **4.8 °C** too high, and three flag bands out. The hypothetical Osaka day in the paper (35 °C, 45 %, natural wet bulb about 25 °C) does the same: measured 27.9 °C and green, estimated 33.7 °C and black, a gap of 5.8 °C.

The estimate is not broken. It is the Australian Bureau of Meteorology's approximation, fitted for *moderately high radiation in light wind*: it quietly carries a sun load and assumes still air. The paper's readings came from a shaded, fan-blown spot where the globe was cooler than the air and the natural wet bulb was practically the psychrometric wet bulb (the tool's Stull value for 32.4 °C and 48 % is 24.0 °C, against the measured 24.3 °C). In exactly those conditions the approximation must over-read, and it does. The practical lesson is the paper's, sharpened: **never judge a misting-fan or shade intervention with a temperature-and-humidity estimate**, because the estimate cannot see the thing you changed.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/misting-fan-wbgt-allowance-ppe-outdoor-workers.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `wbgtOutdoor`, `wbgtIndoor`, `wbgtEstimate`, `vapourPressure`, `stullWetBulb` and `flagFor` from `src/lib/sci/wbgt.ts`, the maths behind the [[heat-stress]] tool, so the article and the tool page cannot drift apart.

Where each input comes from:

- Air temperature 32.4 °C, humidity 48 %, natural wet bulb 24.3 °C, and the hypothetical 35 °C / 45 % / 25 °C day: **the paper**.
- Globe temperature: the paper's finding that the globe averaged 0.3 °C below air temperature, so Tg = Ta − 0.3. Treat it as **our reading of the paper's average**, not a measured value for that hour.
- 0.31 and 0.35 met, 18.3 and 20.3 W m⁻², and 30 W m⁻² per °C of WBGT: **the paper**.
- 1 met = 58.15 W m⁻²: **ISO 8996**.
- The flag thresholds (27.8, 29.4, 31.1, 32.2 °C): **US Army TB MED 507**, built into the tool.
- The starting point of 32.4 °C for the headroom chart, and the humidity range 20 to 95 %: **our assumptions**, chosen to cover the paper's climate.

## How we test the maths

Every change to fibub runs `tests/sci.mjs`, which now loads `tests/sci-tools/heat-stress.mjs`. It pins the tool against published worked values:

- The ISO 7243 weightings themselves: a reading of Tnw 25 °C, Tg 40 °C, Ta 32 °C in sun must give 28.7 °C (0.7 × 25 + 0.2 × 40 + 0.1 × 32), and the same wet bulb and globe in shade 29.5 °C.
- The Bureau of Meteorology estimate at 30 °C and 50 % humidity must give a vapour pressure of 21.14 hPa and a WBGT of 29.26 °C, the worked example in the tool's own guide.
- Stull's wet bulb at 20 °C and 50 % humidity must be 13.7 °C, the value in Stull (2011).
- Flags must switch at 27.8, 29.4, 31.1 and 32.2 °C, and a tenth of a degree below each threshold must still give the lower flag.
- New with this article: the paper's sample day (24.3, 32.1, 32.4 °C) must give a measured WBGT of 26.67 °C with a white flag, the same air temperature and humidity must give an estimate of 31.45 °C with a red flag, and the gap between them must be 4.78 °C; and 18.3 and 20.3 W m⁻² divided by 30 must be 0.61 and 0.68 °C.

## Check it yourself

On the [[heat-stress]] page:

1. Leave *Units* on **°C**. Set *Method* to **Measured: wet bulb, globe and air** and *Where* to **Outdoors in sun**.
2. Type *Natural wet bulb (Tnw)* **24.3**, *Globe (Tg)* **32.1**, *Air temperature (Ta)* **32.4**.
3. The page reads **WBGT 26.7 °C: White flag**, and the working line shows `0.7 x 24.3 + 0.2 x 32.1 + 0.1 x 32.4 = 26.7 °C`.
4. Switch *Where* to **Indoors or shade**: it barely moves, to **26.6 °C**, because the globe is at air temperature.
5. Now set *Method* to **Estimate from air temperature and humidity**, leave *Air temperature (Ta)* at **32.4** and type *Relative humidity %* **48**. It jumps to **31.5 °C: Red flag**, with `e = 23.26 hPa` in the working line, and *Wet-bulb (Stull)* shows **24.0 °C**. That 4.8 °C jump, with nothing about the air changed, is the point of this article.
6. Repeat with **35** and **45** for the paper's hypothetical day: the estimate gives **33.7 °C**, a black flag, where the three readings give 27.9 °C and a green flag.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Convert an intervention's watts into a heat-stress allowance.** Any cooling measured in W m⁻² (a fan, a vest, a shade sail) divides by about 30 W m⁻² per °C to become WBGT headroom, which the tool then turns into flags and air temperatures.
- **Decide whether a study needs real instruments.** If your treatment changes radiation or air movement, as a misting fan does, an estimate from temperature and humidity will miss it; the gap in Figure 1 is how badly.
- **Plan a field protocol.** Simulate a day's WBGT at the humidities you expect, see which flag band you will be close to, and site your measurements where the result still decides something.
- **Sanity-check a reported WBGT.** Put the paper's three readings in and see whether the published number matches the ISO weighting at all; mismatches usually mean the indoor weighting was used outdoors, or °F and °C were mixed.
- **Compare climates fairly.** Run the headroom calculation for a dry site and a humid one before promising the same intervention will work in both; it buys about a third less air temperature at 85 % humidity than at 48 %.
- **Read a weather feed into the same maths** with the [[station-data-viewer]], then screen the hot hours before deciding where to put a globe thermometer.

## The fine print

- We could not read the paper's results tables from here, so nothing above is a recomputation of its heat-flux data. We check its stated conclusions, its unit conversions and the WBGT arithmetic around them.
- The globe temperature we use is the paper's average offset of −0.3 °C from air temperature, not a reading from that hour. The paper reports a root-mean-square difference of 2.1 K, so a single hour could easily be 2 °C away, which would move the measured WBGT by about 0.4 °C.
- The 30 W m⁻² per °C rule is a local slope of ISO 7243's tables, not an exact law. It changes with work rate and clothing, so the 0.61 to 0.68 °C allowance is an order-of-magnitude figure, not a limit to design to.
- The tool's heat flags follow US Army TB MED 507. Other countries set other thresholds and other clothing adjustments; check the rules that bind your site.
- The tool describes its temperature-and-humidity method as a shade estimate. The underlying Bureau of Meteorology approximation is fitted for moderately high radiation and light wind, so in genuine shade with moving air it over-reads, as this paper's day shows. Use measured readings for any safety decision.
- General education, not advice: heat illness is a medical matter, and work-rest rules are set by your regulator and employer.
