Satellite soil moisture products are checked against people with probes in a field, and the first question any such campaign faces is how many probe readings one pixel needs. Too few and the "ground truth" is noisier than the satellite; too many and the field dries out before you finish. A 2023 paper in *Atmosphere* measured soil moisture at six nested scales, from a 2 m square up to 160 m, and published a **reasonable sample number** for each. This article rechecks every one of those numbers with the [[field-sampling-planner]], which holds the same classical sample-size formula, and finds the agreement is excellent at small scales and breaks down at large ones. For the steps either side, size a survey of any variable with the [[sample-size]] and handle readings that fall below an instrument's detection limit with the [[non-detects]].

## The paper in brief

The authors worked a flat field in Hengshui, Hebei, on 20 and 21 July 2018, measuring volumetric soil moisture in the top 20 cm on a nested grid: a basic unit of 0.4 m, 25 of those in a 2 m cell, then 10, 20, 40, 80 and 160 m blocks. From the readings they worked out the **coefficient of variation** (CV, the standard deviation divided by the mean) at each scale: 0.074 at 2 m, 0.109 at 10 m, 0.110 at 20 m, 0.197 at 40 m, 0.350 at 80 m and 0.778 at 160 m. Soil moisture is far patchier over a hundred metres than over two, which is the physical heart of the paper.

They then asked how many samples each scale needs for the mean to be accurate, and reported **2, 5, 5, 8, 20 and 42** samples at 95 % confidence and 10 % relative error. They fitted two rules to those numbers: Rn = 55.10 × CV from the variability, and Rn = 0.265 × √A from the area, each with R² 0.98, and validated the area rule at two further blocks (14 samples at 60 m, 33 at 120 m). Their own comparison table shows that at the 2 m scale a "traditional" statistical calculation asks for 13 samples at 95 % confidence and 10 % error, while their stratified method asks for 7.

## Checked with the tools

The field sampling planner's *Estimating the average* mode solves the textbook relation

    n = ( t(1 − α/2, n − 1) × CV ÷ relative error )²

by iteration, because the Student-*t* multiplier itself depends on n. Nothing here is simulated: we fed the paper's own published CV for each scale into that function, with its own 10 % margin and 95 % confidence.

**Step 1: the small scales agree.** At 2 m (CV 0.074) the planner asks for **3** samples where the paper takes 2; at 10 m and 20 m (CV 0.109 and 0.110) it asks for **7** where the paper takes 5. Those are the same ballpark, and the paper's stratified scheme is meant to beat simple random sampling, so coming in a little lower is exactly what it claims.

**Step 2: the large scales do not.** At 40 m the planner asks for **18** against the paper's 8; at 80 m, **50** against 20; at 160 m, **235** against 42. The reason is visible in Figure 1: the classical requirement grows with the **square** of the CV, while the paper's fitted rule Rn = 55.10 × CV grows in proportion to it. The two agree near CV 0.11 — which is where four of the six scales sit — and separate quickly above it.

**Step 3: what the published numbers actually buy.** Turning the formula around is more useful than arguing about it. With the paper's own CV and its own sample number, the margin of error is t(0.975, n − 1) × CV ÷ √n. Figure 2 shows the result: **±13.5 %** at 10 m, **±16.5 %** at 40 m, **±16.4 %** at 80 m and **±24.2 %** at 160 m. Every scale is wider than the ±10 % the numbers are labelled with, and the 2 m case is a trap: with n = 2 the *t* multiplier is 12.71, so two samples give **±66 %** whatever the CV. Three is the smallest number that can honestly be called a 10 % estimate here.

**Step 4: the layout side.** The planner also turns a sample number into a grid. Spreading 42 points evenly over a 160 m square needs a **24.7 m** square grid; the 235 the classical formula asks for would need **10.4 m**. The paper's own measurement grid was 55 m square at 5 m spacing, which is about 144 nodes, far more than any of these numbers — so at the field scale they measured, nothing was undersampled. The gap only matters for the recommendation the paper hands to the next campaign.

**Step 5: patches, not averages.** If the goal is to catch a wet or dry patch rather than to average the field, the planner's hot-spot mode answers a different question: a 5 m square grid has a **95.1 %** chance of putting a node inside any round patch 3 m in radius, and is certain to catch one of 4 m. Going the other way, catching a 10 m patch with 95 % certainty allows a spacing of up to **16.7 m**.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/soil-moisture-sampling-number-scale-cv-relative-error.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `nForMean`, `spacingForN`, `hotspotP` and `spacingFor` from `src/lib/sci/sampling.ts`, the maths behind the [[field-sampling-planner]], and `tInv` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the margin-of-error step.

Every input is a value the paper states in its text or tables: the six scales, the six coefficients of variation, the six sample numbers, the 10 % margin, the 95 % confidence level, the fitted rule Rn = 55.10 × CV and the 55 m × 55 m measurement grid. The hot-spot radii in Step 5 are **our choice** of illustration; nothing is simulated, and no figure or table from the paper is reproduced.

## How we test the maths

Until this article the planner's maths was pinned only inside `tests/sci.mjs`; it now has its own file, `tests/sci-tools/field-sampling-planner.mjs`, which runs on every change to fibub:

- A coefficient of variation of 0.30 with a ±10 % margin at 95 % confidence must give **37** samples, and ±5 % must give **141** — the iterated *t* solution, not the one-shot normal answer.
- The paper's six coefficients of variation must give **3, 7, 7, 18, 50 and 235** samples, and the margin implied by its own 2, 5, 5, 8, 20 and 42 samples must be **66.5, 13.5, 13.7, 16.5, 16.4 and 24.2 %**.
- A square grid at a spacing equal to the patch radius must be certain to hit the patch (probability 1), and `spacingForN` must put 42 nodes on a 160 m square at 24.69 m.
- The hot-spot geometry itself is checked elsewhere in `tests/sci.mjs` against the area of a union of discs computed with Shapely, and the *t* quantiles against SciPy.

## Check it yourself

On the [[field-sampling-planner]] page:

1. In the *How many samples* card, set *Set the number by* to **Estimating the average**.
2. Type **77.8** in *Expected variability, CV (%)* (the paper's 160 m scale), **10** in *Margin of error (± % of the average)* and **95** in *Confidence (%)*.
3. It shows **235** samples. Change the CV to **11** (the 20 m scale) and it falls to **7**; to **7.4** (the 2 m scale) and it falls to **3**.
4. Keep CV at **19.7** (the 40 m scale) and switch the margin to **5 %**: the number jumps to **63**. Halving the margin quadruples the work.
5. Draw a 160 m square in *Site boundary, one corner per row (x, y)* — `0, 0`, `160, 0`, `160, 160`, `0, 160` — set *Set the number by* to **Giving it myself** with **42** samples and *Layout* to **Square grid**. The plan comes out near a 25 m spacing; download it as GPX for a field GPS.
6. For the patch question, set *Set the number by* to **Finding a hot spot**, *Hot spot radius* to **10** m and *Chance of hitting it* to **95 %**: the widest grid spacing is about **16.7 m**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Size a validation campaign** for a satellite pixel: put your own CV from a pilot transect into the planner and read the sample number, with the margin it really gives.
- **Audit a published sampling rule** the way we did here: ask what margin its sample number buys at its own CV, before adopting it for a different field.
- **Plan the grid, not just the count**: `spacingForN` turns a sample number into a spacing you can walk, and the GPX download goes straight to a handheld GPS.
- **Switch goals cleanly**: comparing a mean with a regulatory action level needs the power-based mode (*Comparing with an action level*), not the margin-of-error mode, and the planner keeps them apart.
- **Teach why variability costs quadratically**: Figure 1 is the whole lesson, and students can redraw it for their own crop or contaminant in a minute.

## The fine print

- The planner's formula assumes simple random sampling from a roughly normal population. The paper's stratified and nested schemes should need fewer samples than that, which explains part — but not all — of the gap at 40 m and above.
- Its CV values are averages over replicates (for example 0.073, 0.070, 0.075 and 0.076 at 2 m), so the sample numbers inherit that spread; at 40 m the two replicate CVs of 0.178 and 0.216 give 15 and 21 samples.
- A 10 % *relative* margin is not the same as an absolute margin of 0.02 cm³ cm⁻³. At the paper's mean of about 0.23 cm³ cm⁻³ they happen to be close, but on a drier day they would not be.
- We did not re-derive the paper's Rn values, because its raw readings are not published; we only compared them with the classical requirement at its own stated CV.
