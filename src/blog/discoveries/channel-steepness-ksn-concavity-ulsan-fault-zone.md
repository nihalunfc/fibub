Rivers record tectonics in their shape. Where rock is pushed up faster, channels steepen, and geomorphologists compress that into one number per reach: **ksn**, the normalised channel steepness, the slope a channel would have at a standard drainage area. A 2024 paper in *Earth Surface Dynamics* mapped ksn, χ (chi) and denudation rates across the Ulsan Fault Zone in South Korea and used them to argue the fault is segmented, with the eastern side of the main divide up to 200 % steeper than the western side along the first 60 km. ksn is not measured, it is fitted, and the fit depends on choices: a reference concavity, a reference area, how many points the profile has. The [[stream-profile]] does the same fits in a browser, so those choices can be tested; this article rebuilds the paper's numbers as profiles and shows what each choice does. The tools next door are the [[dem-terrain]] viewer for the elevation model itself and the [[watershed-delineation]] tool for the catchment the channel drains.

## The paper in brief

The authors combined topographic metrics from a digital elevation model, cosmogenic ¹⁰Be denudation rates and a landscape evolution model. Their ksn values run from **0 to 238 m⁰·⁹** with a regional mean of **24** and a standard deviation of **16**, computed at a reference concavity **θref = 0.45** with a reference area A₀ = 1 and channels starting where the drainage area reaches 10⁵ m². Measured concavity indices across the area are **0.36 to 0.47**, so not all channels match the reference value.

The contrasts are the result: eastern ksn up to 200 % higher than western over the first 60 km of the divide, western up to 137 % higher from 60 to 90 km, and χ 127 % higher on the west over the same first stretch, all pointing to a divide that is migrating and a fault that moves in segments. Denudation rates span **7.4 to 155 mm/kyr** between paired basins, and a strath terrace gives a west-side incision rate of **1395 mm/kyr** against **215** on the east, a ratio near 6.5.

## Checked with the tools

Nothing here is the paper's topography. We built **our own catchment** (Hack's law, drainage area ∝ length¹·⁶⁷, a 20 km channel with an outlet area of 49.8 km² cut off where the area falls to the paper's 10⁵ m² channel head: **our assumption**) and put the paper's own metric values into it, then recovered them with the tool's maths. That is the honest way to test a metric: you know the answer before you fit.

**Step 1: the paper's ksn numbers as topography.** With θref 0.45 and A₀ = 1 our catchment has a total χ of 16.69 m. Since elevation is ksn × χ in a steady channel, the regional mean ksn of 24 means **400.6 m** of relief along the channel, one standard deviation up (ksn 40) means **667.7 m**, and the paper's maximum of 238 would mean **3973 m**: more than five times the highest peak in the study area (Toham, 745 m). So ksn 238 cannot describe a whole 20 km channel; it has to be a short, local reach, which is exactly how such maxima arise next to a fault. Figure 1 draws the three cases.

**Step 2: the fit gives back what we put in.** Feeding the uniform ksn 24 profile to the tool's maths: χ against elevation is a straight line of slope **24.00** with R² = 1, the best-fit concavity scan returns **0.45** exactly, and the log-binned slope-area fit (the older method, which differentiates the profile first) gives **θ 0.449 and ks 23.50** over 27 bins with R² 0.9998. The 2 % low bias in the slope-area ks is the price of differentiating before fitting; the χ method does not pay it. That alone is a reason to prefer the χ plot for comparing reaches, as the paper does.

**Step 3: finding a knickpoint of known size.** We then trebled ksn above a point 14.5 km up the channel (24 below, 72 above, the paper's east-west contrast of 200 %) and added a 2 m elevation scatter to stand in for DEM noise (**our assumption**). The tool's segmented χ fit, choosing the number of breaks by BIC, puts a single knickpoint at **χ = 7.64 m** where the truth is 7.51, that is 14.55 km upstream, and reads **ksn 24.01 below and 72.08 above**. It recovers both steepnesses to better than 0.5 %.

**Step 4: a knickpoint pretends to be concavity.** Run the best-concavity scan on that same stepped profile and it reports **θ = 0.75**, nowhere near the 0.45 the channel was built with. A reach with a step in steepness looks, to a single straight-line fit, like a very concave channel. Anyone reporting a basin-wide best-fit θ should first check the χ plot for kinks, which is why the paper fixes θref and looks at ksn instead.

**Step 5: what a mismatched reference concavity costs.** Take a channel whose real concavity is **0.36**, the bottom of the paper's measured range, and read it at θref 0.45. In our 49.8 km² catchment the fitted value is **ksn 99.3** (R² 0.9957); put the identical channel law in a catchment ten times larger and it reads **119.3** (R² 0.9934), **20 % higher**, with no change in tectonics or erodibility. Two things follow. First, ksn at a fixed θref is only comparable between basins of similar size when the real concavity differs from θref, so the paper's own 0.36-to-0.47 spread sets a floor on how small a ksn contrast is worth interpreting. Second, the slightly-below-1 R² is the warning sign: a well-chosen θref gives a χ plot that is straight.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/channel-steepness-ksn-concavity-ulsan-fault-zone.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `synthetic` (a steady-state profile from Hack's law and a stream-power law), `chi`, `lsq`, `bestTheta`, `slopeArea` and `segments` from `src/lib/sci/streamprof.ts`, the maths behind the [[stream-profile]].

From the paper: ksn 24, 40 (mean plus one standard deviation) and 238, θref 0.45, A₀ = 1, the 10⁵ m² channel head, the concavity range 0.36 to 0.47, the 200 % steepness contrast and the peak elevations. **Our assumptions:** the catchment geometry (Hack's exponent 1.67, a 20 km channel, 49.8 km² at the outlet), the knickpoint position, the 2 m elevation scatter and its seed. The profiles are **simulated**; they are not the paper's rivers, and no figure or table was copied.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/stream-profile.mjs`, which pins the whole chain against independent NumPy and SciPy code:

- On eight random elevation models, the priority-flood pit filling, the D8 receivers and the drainage areas match a separate Python implementation (areas to 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²).
- On four Hack's-law profiles, χ equals `scipy.integrate.cumulative_trapezoid`, the best θ by linearity equals NumPy's scan and the true value for θ from 0.35 to 0.6, and the log-binned slope-area fit equals `np.polyfit`.
- On six noisy χ-elevation series with 0, 1 and 2 kinks, the segment sums of squares and the BIC choice of break count equal a brute-force NumPy search.
- A made-up steady state (`synthetic(400, 20 km, ks 50, θ 0.45)`) must plot as a line of slope exactly 50 through the origin in the χ plot.
- New with this article: the ksn 24 profile used here must return a χ-fit slope of 24 with R² = 1 and a best θ of 0.45; the stepped profile must return one BIC-chosen break with segment steepnesses within 0.2 of 24 and 72.

## Check it yourself

On the [[stream-profile]] page, in the *Channel* card with *Paste a profile* selected:

1. Set *Distance in* to **km** and leave *Area in* on **m²**.
2. Replace the text in *Distance along the channel, elevation and drainage area, one point per line* with our 21-point channel (one per line): **0, 0, 49804415**; **0.976, 8.2, 45813276**; **1.952, 16.7, 41956999**; **2.927, 25.6, 38237947**; **3.903, 34.9, 34658663**; **4.879, 44.6, 31221891**; **5.854, 54.8, 27930603**; **6.83, 65.5, 24788042**; **7.806, 76.8, 21797761**; **8.782, 88.9, 18963683**; **9.758, 101.7, 16290174**; **10.733, 115.5, 13782138**; **11.709, 130.5, 11445145**; **12.685, 146.8, 9285611**; **13.661, 164.9, 7311048**; **14.636, 185.2, 5530446**; **15.612, 208.5, 3954877**; **16.588, 236.1, 2598519**; **17.564, 270.5, 1480634**; **18.539, 317.5, 630071**; **19.515, 400.6, 99939**.
3. Leave *Reference concavity θref* on **0.45** and *Reference area A₀ (m²)* on **1**, as the paper uses, and set *Knickpoint hints* to **None**.
4. The headline reads about **ksn 23.6 at θref 0.45; best θ 0.43**, with *Relief* **400.6 m** and *Drainage area* 99 939 m² to 49.8 km². The true value is 24 at θ 0.45: with only 21 points the χ integral is computed on coarse steps, which pulls both numbers slightly low. Paste a profile with more points and they converge (41 points give ksn 23.9 and θ 0.45).
5. Change *Reference concavity θref* to **0.36** and watch ksn drop to about **5.8**. The number is not wrong; ksn at one θref simply cannot be compared with ksn at another, because its units (m^2θ) change with θ.
6. Switch *Knickpoint hints* back to **Up to 2**: a hint appears even in this smooth channel, because the coarse χ steps bend the line slightly. Real work needs the noise level in mind before a kink is believed.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published ksn** before building on it: rebuild a profile with the stated θref and A₀ and see whether the quoted steepness and relief can live in the same catchment.
- **Decide how small a ksn contrast is worth interpreting** for your own basins, by fitting the same channel at the ends of your measured concavity range, as in step 5.
- **Test a knickpoint extraction** against a profile where you placed the knickpoint yourself, including the noise level, before trusting it on real DEMs.
- **Compare the χ and slope-area methods** on the same data, which makes the bias of differentiating before fitting visible.
- **Teach the metric**: a χ plot where steepness is a slope and a knickpoint is a kink is the clearest route into fluvial geomorphology, and the [[stream-profile]] draws it from a pasted table or from a DEM traced with the [[dem-terrain]] viewer.

## The fine print

- Our catchment and profiles are **made up** from the paper's metric values. The paper's rivers, denudation rates and fault interpretation are its own and are not rechecked here.
- ksn depends on θref **and** on A₀. The paper states A₀ = 1, which matches the tool's default, so the values are comparable; a paper using A₀ = 1 km² would report numbers larger by a factor of (10⁶)^θref, about 500 at θref 0.45.
- The relief figures in step 1 assume a steady channel with the same ksn from the outlet to the head, which real channels rarely are.
- The 2 % slope-area bias is a property of this profile and sampling, not a universal number.
- We did not touch the paper's landscape evolution model, its ¹⁰Be ages or its focal mechanisms; those need their own tools.
