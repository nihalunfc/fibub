Planets do not simply disappear, but their shadows can. A paper published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters* in November 2026 describes TOI-2535 b, a giant planet that the TESS space telescope saw crossing its star in 2019, then saw crossing less and less, until in early 2026 it stopped crossing at all. The team traces the cause to a second, unseen planet that slowly tilts the first one's orbit. The paper is open access under CC BY 4.0. Below we explain the finding and then rebuild the fading transits with the same maths the [[transit-light-curve-fitter]] uses, with the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]] for the orbit's size.

## What they found

TOI-2535 b is a hot Jupiter: a gas giant close to its star, going round once every 8.24 days. The star is an orange K dwarf about 1,000 light-years away. When TESS first looked, in 2019, the planet passed close to the middle of the star's face. In each later visit the path had moved further towards the edge, so the dips in starlight got shorter. By early 2026 the planet missed the star altogether.

The team measured how fast the path moves. Astronomers describe the path with the **impact parameter**, written *b*: how far from the centre of the star the planet passes, in units of the star's radius. A central crossing has *b* = 0; a crossing that just touches the edge has *b* close to 1. For TOI-2535 b, *b* grows by about 0.16 every year, the fastest change seen so far for a planet around a single star.

The transit times also wander, by up to about six hours early or late compared with a steady clock. Putting the timing, the changing transit length and radial-velocity measurements from the NEID spectrograph together, the team concludes that a second planet, at least as massive as Saturn and probably locked in a simple ratio of orbital periods with TOI-2535 b, is pulling on it from an orbit tilted by at least about 5 degrees.

## Why it matters

A planet's orbit around a star is not fixed in space if another planet is nearby on a tilted orbit. The two orbits pull on each other and slowly swing around, like a spinning top that wobbles. This is called **nodal precession**. From Earth, that swing changes the angle we see the orbit at, so a planet that transits today may not transit in twenty years.

That has two consequences. First, some planets we see transiting are only visible for part of the time, so counts of how common planets are need a small correction. Second, a changing transit is a way to find planets that never transit themselves: the hidden companion here was found by its effect, not by its own shadow.

## How they did it

The team used seven years of TESS light curves, ground-based transit observations and NEID velocities. For each TESS visit they fitted the transit shape to get its length, depth and timing. A change in length at constant planet size points to a change in *b*. They then ran models of two planets pulling on each other to find which companions could produce both the timing shifts and the drift in *b*.

## Checked with the tools

We do not have the paper's light curves here, so we did three checks that anyone can repeat with fibub's maths, and we say which numbers are the paper's and which are ours. The paper's numbers we use are the period (8.24 days), the rate (*b* grows by 0.16 a year), the 2019 start and the early-2026 end. For the star and planet we assumed round values for a typical early K dwarf and hot Jupiter: 0.80 solar masses, 0.80 solar radii, 1.0 Jupiter radius and 1.0 Jupiter mass. These are **our assumptions, not the paper's values**, so the exact years below shift a little if the real sizes differ.

**Step 1: the size of the orbit.** Kepler's third law (the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]] does it for you) puts a planet with an 8.24-day period around a 0.80 solar-mass star at about 0.074 astronomical units. That is about 19.9 times the star's radius, so *a*/R★ ≈ 19.9. The same numbers give a star density of about 2.2 g/cm³, normal for a K dwarf, and a Jupiter-mass planet would make the star wobble by about 117 m/s, easily within reach of NEID.

**Step 2: rebuild the fade.** We drew the transit for each year with *b* starting near 0.1 in 2019 and growing by 0.16 a year, using the transit model inside the [[transit-light-curve-fitter]] (a limb-darkened star crossed by a dark disc). One thing the fitter also showed: when the planet crosses near the centre, *b* on its own is poorly pinned down, because a central crossing of a smaller orbit looks much like an off-centre crossing of a larger one. That is why we worked from the transit length, which the fitter measures well, and why the paper's own fit holds the star's density fixed.

**Step 4: how long until it comes back?** If the orbit swings to and fro by some angle either side of edge-on, and crosses the star's edge at the measured rate, a simple swing model gives the years without transits. Figure 6 shows the answer for swings of up to 15 degrees. A swing of 5 degrees gives a gap of about 14 years; the 30 to 50 years quoted by the team need a swing of roughly 7 to 10 degrees. That fits with their lower limit of about 5 degrees on the tilt between the two orbits. This is a toy model, not the team's full calculation.

## The fine print

- The star and planet sizes in our checks are **assumed round values**, not the paper's measured ones, so our dates are approximate. For the measured values and their uncertainties, read the paper.
- Our light curves are **simulated**, not the TESS data. They show that the method works at this signal size, not that the real data say so.
- The companion is inferred from its pull; it has not been seen directly. Its mass is a lower limit.
- The swing model in Figure 6 treats the change in *b* as a simple to-and-fro. Real precession around two planets' combined orbit is more complicated.
- This is one system. It does not tell us on its own how often other transiting planets come and go.
