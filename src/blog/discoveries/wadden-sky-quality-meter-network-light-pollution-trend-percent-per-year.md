A **sky quality meter** (SQM) is a small light meter pointed straight up that reports the night sky's brightness in **magnitudes per square arcsecond**. The scale runs backwards: a bigger number is a darker sky, and every 1.0 less is 2.5 times brighter. The volunteer network *Was het donker?* ("Was it dark?") runs dozens of these meters across the northern Netherlands and the German Wadden coast, recording all night, every night. A 2025 paper in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* by Farhan Shah and colleagues cleaned four years of these readings, 2020 to 2023, of cloudy hours using weather-satellite cloud masks. They found the **darkest skies in the network brightening by 2.78 to 6.68 % a year**. This article decodes those percentages with the [[light-pollution-calculator]] and asks what they mean for the artificial light behind them. To plan dark nights, use the [[dark-sky-calendar]]; for imagers, the [[sub-exposure-calculator]] shows what a brighter sky costs.

## The paper in brief

Clouds are the main problem with SQM data. Over a city they reflect light and brighten the sky; over a dark site they can block stars and darken it. Either way they swamp a slow trend. The team marked an hour as cloudy if any pixel in a small box of the EUMETSAT satellite cloud mask around the meter was cloudy. They also kept only hours with the Sun more than 18° below the horizon and the Moon down. Then they fitted a straight line, plus a yearly wave for the seasons, to the clear-sky readings at 27 sites.

They report each site's starting brightness, its change in mag/arcsec² per year and a "% per year". Negative values mean a brightening sky. Most sites brighten and a few darken. The authors link the steepest brightening (De Held, Groningen, at −0.223 mag/arcsec² a year) to nearby construction. They also show that bright sites vary less from night to night than dark ones, because steady artificial light swamps the natural changes. Density plots reveal greenhouse lighting and the moment streetlights switch off.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: what "% per year" means.** The paper does not say how it turns a slope in magnitudes into a percentage. Dividing the slope by the starting reading does not work (0.070 ÷ 21.77 is 0.32 %, not 6.68 %). The light pollution calculator converts a reading to **luminance**, the physical brightness in candela per square metre: L = 108,000 × 10^(−0.4 m) cd/m². The change in luminance over one year, L(m + Δm) ÷ L(m) − 1, reproduces every percentage we checked to within **0.04 percentage points** (Figure 1). For Hornhuizen, −0.070 mag/arcsec² a year is a luminance rise of **6.66 %** (the paper's 6.68 % comes from the unrounded slope). The percentages are simply the yearly change in light.

**Step 2: the 2.78 %.** The abstract's upper value, 6.68 %, is Hornhuizen, the darkest site. We could not find where the lower value, 2.78 %, comes from. Among the seven sites that started darker than 21.4, the plain average slope is −0.0249 mag/arcsec² a year (**2.32 %** brighter a year) and the average weighted by each slope's uncertainty is −0.0257 (**2.39 %**). The paper's conclusions give a looser "1 to 7 %". Since several slopes are within two standard errors of zero (Lauwersoog's −0.027 ± 0.017, for one), "about 2 to 7 % a year at the darkest sites" is a fair reading.

**Step 3: the artificial light grows faster than the sky.** A meter sees the natural sky (airglow, starlight, zodiacal light) plus artificial skyglow. The calculator compares a reading with a natural sky of 0.174 mcd/m². Hornhuizen's 21.77 is **0.212 mcd/m²**, 1.22 natural skies: artificial light adds **22 %** to the natural glow. If the natural part stays fixed (**our assumption**), the whole sky's 6.66 % yearly rise means the artificial part grows by **37.5 %** a year. After three years at that rate the reading would be about 21.56, with artificial light adding **48 %**. Figure 2 shows the general rule: the darker the site, the faster the artificial light must grow to move the meter by a given percentage.

That last step leans hard on a fixed natural sky, and the natural sky was not fixed. From 2020 to 2023 the Sun climbed from the minimum to near the maximum of its 11-year cycle, and airglow brightens with solar activity. Part of the brightening at the darkest sites may be natural, which would make the artificial growth smaller than our 37.5 %. The paper's method gives the total; splitting it needs a model of the natural sky.

## How we made the graphs

The table and charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/wadden-sky-quality-meter-network-light-pollution-trend-percent-per-year.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `sqmToMcd` and `skyglow` from `src/lib/sci/skyglow.ts`, the maths behind the [[light-pollution-calculator]], with its natural sky of 0.174 mcd/m².

The nine sites' starting readings, slopes, uncertainties and percentages come from the paper's trend table. The fixed natural sky in Step 3 and Figure 2, and the 5 % example rate in Figure 2, are **our assumptions**. Nothing here is a fit to the raw meter data, which the network publishes.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` checks the calculator: 22 mag/arcsec² must be 0.1712 mcd/m², a reading must survive a round trip through luminance, and a sky 2.5 magnitudes brighter must need exactly 10 times the exposure.

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/light-pollution-calculator.mjs` pins the paper's percentages: Hornhuizen's −0.070 mag/arcsec² a year must give 6.68 % within 0.05, De Held's −0.223 must give 22.83 %, Nes's +0.081 must give 7.20 % darker, and 21.77 mag/arcsec² must be 1.216 natural skies with artificial light adding 22 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[light-pollution-calculator]], in the *Your sky* card:

1. Leave *I know* on **A sky quality meter reading (mag/arcsec²)** and type **21.77**, Hornhuizen in January 2020. It reads **Bortle class 3**, *Luminance* **0.212 mcd/m²** and *Brighter than a natural sky* **1.22 times**: artificial light adds **22 %**.
2. Now type **21.70**, one year at Hornhuizen's trend, and put **21.77** in *Compare with (optional)*. It reads **1.07 times brighter here**: the paper's 6.68 % a year.
3. Type **21.56** (three years on) and clear *Compare with*: artificial light now adds **48 %**.
4. Try De Held: **19.695**, then **19.472** with *Compare with* **19.695**. It reads **1.23 times brighter here**, the paper's 22.83 % in one year.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Read a trend table:** turn slopes in mag/arcsec² into percentages of light, and back, with the [[light-pollution-calculator]].
- **Compare sites fairly:** report luminance and the ratio to a natural sky, not only magnitudes, so a change at a dark site and at a city site can be put side by side.
- **Estimate the artificial share:** see how much of a reading is artificial and how fast it must grow to explain a trend, then think about what the natural sky was doing.
- **Plan observing and imaging:** pick moonless windows with the [[dark-sky-calendar]], and see how many times longer faint targets take under a brighter sky with the [[sub-exposure-calculator]].
- **Teach magnitudes:** a 0.07 change on a scale near 21.8 looks tiny, but it is nearly 7 % more light.

## The fine print

- We used nine of the paper's 27 sites and its rounded slopes, so our percentages differ from the paper's in the second decimal.
- The tool's natural sky (0.174 mcd/m²) is a world-atlas value. The real natural sky over the Wadden Sea changes with airglow, the season, the Milky Way and the solar cycle.
- An SQM sees mostly blue-green light. LED lighting with more blue can change a reading differently from the light a person sees.
- Four years is short for a trend, and several dark-site slopes are within two standard errors of zero.
