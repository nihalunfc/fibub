A sheet that stops radio waves can work two ways: it can bounce them off, or it can soak them up. Bouncing is easy with metal, but it only moves the problem somewhere else, so shielding research keeps looking for **absorbing** materials. A 2026 paper in *Scientific Reports* reports a polymer composite loaded with magnetic and conducting particles that gets **22.5 dB of its shielding from absorption** in a 5 mm sheet, with reflection falling to 3.6 dB as the loading rises. Those are the right numbers to be proud of, and they can be checked: absorption in a slab is set by one length, the **skin depth**. This article recomputes it with the [[skin-depth-calculator]]. For the frequencies and the guides the measurement happens in, see the [[waveguide-modes|waveguide mode calculator]] and the [[antenna-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors made poly(di-allyl phthalate) composites filled at 10, 20, 30 and 40 weight per cent with a hybrid filler that is both magnetic and conducting, and measured them across the **X band** (8 to 12 GHz) in sheets about **5 mm** thick. The DC conductivity rises from about **1 × 10⁻² S/m** at the lowest loading to **2.5 S/m** at the highest. The shielding splits as follows (X-band averages, total / absorption / reflection, in dB): 18.9 / 10.7 / 9.9, then 31.5 / 20.1 / 11.4, then 28.4 / 21.3 / 6.7, then 26.3 / 22.5 / 3.6.

The trend is the paper's point and it is a real one: **absorption more than doubles** across the series while **reflection falls by a factor of nearly three**. Normally more conductivity means more reflection, so something else is at work; the authors attribute it to the magnetic part of the filler, and at 10 GHz they report ε′ ≈ 12.0 with ε″ ≈ 4.0 and μ′ ≈ 1.30 with μ″ ≈ 0.30. Magnetic loading raises the material's wave impedance towards that of air, which lowers reflection and lets more energy in to be absorbed. They also quote a **skin depth of about 3 mm** and an **attenuation constant of about 225 Np/m**.

Those last two numbers do not agree with each other: 1 ÷ 225 Np/m is **4.4 mm**, not 3 mm. That is worth a closer look, because the skin depth is where the whole absorption story lives.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the paper's own stated values: conductivity, thickness, ε′, μ′ and ε″. Nothing here is a fit to the paper's data, and no figure of theirs is reproduced.

**Step 1: this composite is not a good conductor.** The familiar skin-depth formula δ = √(2ρ/ωμ) assumes the conduction current swamps the displacement current, that is σ ≫ ωε. Here, at 10 GHz with ε′ = 12.0 and σ = 2.5 S/m, the ratio goes the *other* way: **ωε/σ = 2.67**, so the displacement current is nearly three times the conduction current. The simple formula does not apply, and the tool says so on the page.

Run the exact plane-wave formula instead and the three answers are very different. At 10 GHz, for the 2.5 S/m composite with ε_r 12.0 and μ_r 1.30:

| How it is worked out | Skin depth δ | α | Absorption in 5 mm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact, conduction only (σ = 2.5 S/m) | **6.56 mm** | 152 Np/m | 6.6 dB |
| Exact, folding in ε″ = 4.0 as extra current (σ_eff = 4.73 S/m) | **3.60 mm** | 278 Np/m | 12.1 dB |
| Good-conductor formula √(2ρ/ωμ), not valid here | **2.79 mm** | 358 Np/m | 15.6 dB |
| The paper's quoted values | about 3 mm / 4.4 mm | about 225 Np/m | 14.5 dB (from 3 mm) |

The paper's 3 mm sits right next to the good-conductor answer, and far from the exact one. That is the likely origin of the number: the standard textbook formula, used outside its range. Figure 1 shows the three curves across the microwave band; they do not converge anywhere near 10 GHz.

**Step 2: what the measured absorption actually demands.** Absorption loss through a slab is 8.686 dB per skin depth. Turn it round: 22.5 dB in 5 mm needs δ = **1.93 mm** (α = 518 Np/m), which in turn needs an effective conductivity of about **9.9 S/m**, four times the measured DC value, or equivalently a total loss factor ε″ of about **17.7** rather than 4.0. The same exercise on the other three samples needs 4.2, 8.6 and 9.2 S/m against DC values of 0.01, 0.8 and 1.8 S/m. Figure 2 shows the gap at 5 mm.

So the paper's absorption is real but its stated ε″ and σ do not account for all of it. Two things fill most of the gap, and they are worth naming because they are the physics the paper is actually reporting:

- **Magnetic loss.** The tool's skin depth takes a real permeability, so μ″ = 0.30 is left out. Working the full complex calculation by hand beside the tool, with ε_r = 12.0 − j8.49 (the stated ε″ plus the DC conduction) and μ_r = 1.30 − j0.30, gives α = **380 Np/m**, δ = **2.63 mm** and **16.5 dB** over 5 mm. Magnetic loss alone adds 4.4 dB.
- **The measurement definition.** Absorption shielding effectiveness from a two-port measurement is −10 log₁₀(T/(1 − R)): it counts everything that goes in and does not come back out, including energy that bounces around inside the slab before dying. A single plane-wave pass, which is what 8.686 t/δ describes, is always the smaller number.

Between them, 16.5 dB of single-pass absorption and a few dB of internal re-reflection account for the measured 22.5 dB without needing any extra conductivity. The conclusion is not that the paper is wrong about absorption; it is that **the quoted 3 mm skin depth is not the quantity doing the work**, and anyone reusing it for a different thickness will get the wrong answer.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/emi-shielding-composite-skin-depth-absorption-loss.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `skinDepth`, `skinDepthGood`, `absorptionDb` and `sheetR` from `src/lib/sci/ph-skin.ts`, the maths behind the [[skin-depth-calculator]]. `skinDepth` uses the exact attenuation constant of a plane wave in a lossy medium, α = ω√(με/2)·√(√(1 + (σ/ωε)²) − 1), written so it keeps its precision when σ/ωε is small (as it is here) or huge (as in metal); `skinDepthGood` is the textbook limit, kept in the library so the page can show both; `absorptionDb` is 8.686 t/δ.

Where the numbers come from: the four conductivities, the 5 mm thickness, the shielding effectiveness values, ε′, ε″, μ′, μ″ and the quoted 3 mm and 225 Np/m are **from the paper's text**. Treating ε″ as an extra conduction current (σ_eff = σ + ωε₀ε″) is the standard equivalence, and the complex-permeability calculation in step 2 is **our own hand calculation outside the tool**, written out in the same script, because the tool takes a real μ_r only.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/skin-depth-calculator.mjs`, which pins the library against SciPy (complex Bessel functions for the round wire, the complex propagation constant for lossy media) and against the textbook limits:

- Copper at 50 Hz must give δ = 9.334678 mm from both the exact formula and the good-conductor limit, since copper at mains frequency is as good a conductor as there is.
- For the deliberately poor conductors ρ = 0.2 Ω m with ε_r = 80 at 1 GHz, and ρ = 1000 Ω m with ε_r = 4 at 100 MHz, the exact δ must be 10.62624 mm and 10.620353 m, matching SciPy's complex propagation constant: these are the cases where the simple formula fails, which is exactly this article's case.
- Absorption loss must be 8.685889638 dB per skin depth, and a sheet much thinner than δ must tend to ρ/t per square while a thick one tends to ρ/δ.
- New with this article: the composite at 10 GHz with ρ = 0.4 Ω m, μ_r = 1.30 and ε_r = 12.0 must give δ = 6.5602 mm against 2.7918 mm from the good-conductor formula (a factor of 2.35), and 6.6201 dB of absorption through 5 mm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[skin-depth-calculator]], in the *Material and frequency* card:

1. Leave *Material* on its first entry and type over the numbers: *Resistivity ρ (Ω m)* **0.4** (that is 1 ÷ 2.5 S/m), *Relative permeability μr* **1.3**, *Relative permittivity εr* **12**.
2. Set *Frequency* **10** and *Unit* **GHz**.
3. In the second card, set *Sheet or shield thickness (µm)* to **5000** (5 mm).
4. Read the results: *Skin depth δ* **6.56 mm**, and beside it *Good-conductor formula √(2ρ/ωμ)* **2.79 mm** with the note that the two differ because the material behaves partly as a dielectric, **ωε/σ = 2.67**. *Depth for 99 % of the field to die away* is **30.2 mm**.
5. Lower down, *Thickness in skin depths* reads **0.762** and *Absorption loss through it* **6.62 dB**. That is all the absorption the DC conductivity can explain.
6. Now change *Resistivity* to **0.2116** (σ_eff = 4.73 S/m, the conductivity plus the stated dielectric loss). δ falls to **3.6 mm**, *Thickness in skin depths* rises to **1.39** and the absorption loss to **12.1 dB**: still short of the measured 22.5 dB.
7. For the number the measurement implies, type *Resistivity* **0.1014** (9.9 S/m): δ **1.93 mm** and an absorption loss of **22.5 dB**, matching the paper's figure.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a shielding paper's skin depth in one minute**: if ωε/σ is not far below 1, the √(2ρ/ωμ) formula is the wrong tool, and the page says so in words.
- **Convert a measured absorption into an effective loss**: read the thickness in skin depths off the page, and the conductivity your material must really have.
- **Size a shield for a target**: absorption is 8.686 dB per skin depth, so doubling the thickness doubles the decibels; the chart of δ against frequency shows where that becomes cheap.
- **Separate conduction from dielectric loss** when reporting a composite: give σ_DC and ε″ at the measurement frequency, because at 10 GHz the second often matters more than the first.
- **Sanity-check a shielded enclosure's seams and apertures** with the [[waveguide-modes|waveguide mode calculator]]: a slot that passes a mode beats any amount of absorption in the wall.

## The fine print

- Everything here is recomputed from values stated in the paper's text. We did not use its figures, and we did not refit its data.
- The tool's skin depth takes a real relative permeability, so magnetic loss (μ″ = 0.30) is outside it. The complex calculation in step 2 is ours, done in the article's script, not by the tool.
- Absorption shielding effectiveness as measured on a two-port network analyser is not the same quantity as a single-pass plane-wave absorption loss. The first is always larger; comparing them is a consistency check, not an identity.
- A plane wave in a uniform medium is an idealisation for a filled composite. At 10 GHz the wavelength inside the material is about 8 mm, so particles and clusters much smaller than that are fairly described by an effective ε and μ, which is what we have used.
- The paper's own text is inconsistent about the filler loadings in one place, and its skin depth and attenuation constant do not match each other. We took the stated conductivities, permittivity and permeability as the primary numbers.
- ε″ and μ″ are quoted only at 10 GHz, so our frequency sweep in Figure 1 holds them fixed; in reality both change across the X band.
