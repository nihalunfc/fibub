Nearly every nanoparticle paper proves what it made the same way: a powder pattern, a lattice-fringe spacing from a high-resolution electron micrograph, and a ring pattern (SAED) with the planes written on it. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Materials* makes zinc chromite, ZnCr₂O₄, with a plant-extract route and identifies it as cubic spinel from exactly those three things. None of them is quoted as a cell constant, which is a pity, because the cell constant is what ties them together. This article works it out three ways with the [[electron-diffraction-indexer]] and the [[xrd-peak-calculator]], and shows where a published ring list stops proving anything. For the plane arithmetic behind it, see the [[miller-index-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors boil *Crateva religiosa* leaves, add the extract to zinc and chromium nitrates, and calcine at 600 °C for two hours. The product is reported as cubic spinel ZnCr₂O₄ (space group Fd-3m, matching reference pattern ICDD 22-1107), with powder peaks between **2θ = 18.64° and 87.58°** indexed as (111), (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), (440), (531), (620) and (533), the strongest being (311), and a Scherrer crystallite size of **15.33 nm** (K = 0.9, Cu Kα 1.5406 Å). High-resolution imaging shows lattice fringes **0.17 nm** apart, assigned to **(422)**, and the SAED pattern's rings are assigned to **(111), (220), (311), (400), (422), (440)** and **(620)**. The particles average 15.71 nm across. The rest of the paper is biology and sensing: an IC₅₀ of 72.99 µg/mL against a breast-cancer line, a minimum inhibitory concentration of 6.25 µg/mL against *M. tuberculosis* H37Rv, and copper-ion sensing. We only touch the structural identification.

## Checked with the tools

For a cubic crystal, the spacing of a plane is d = a / √(h² + k² + l²), so any one indexed spacing fixes the cell constant a. That is the whole trick, and it lets each of the paper's three structural numbers be turned into an a and compared.

**Route 1: the lattice fringe.** For (422), h² + k² + l² = 24, so a = 0.17 nm × √24 = **8.328 Å**. This is the paper's only measured spacing, so it is the one route that rests on a measurement rather than on reading a peak position off a figure. Everything below tests it.

**Route 2: the first powder peak.** With Cu Kα, 2θ = 18.64° gives d = 4.7565 Å. Read as (111) that implies a = **8.238 Å**, **1.1 % smaller** than the fringe's value. Run the other way, the fringe cell puts (111) at **18.44°**, two tenths of a degree below the quoted figure. A tenth of a degree is a lot at low angle: the first spinel line is the one most sensitive to a zero-point error, and 0.2° there is worth 1 % in a.

**Route 3: the far end of the pattern.** 2θ = 87.58° gives d = 1.1131 Å. For the fringe cell, (a/d)² = **55.98**, which is 56: the (642) line, predicted at **87.60°**, four hundredths of a degree from the quoted value. For the 8.238 Å cell the same arithmetic gives 54.8, and no cubic line has h² + k² + l² = 54.8. So the high-angle end of the pattern agrees with the fringe cell and not with the first peak.

There is a second, smaller puzzle in the same numbers: (642) is not in the paper's plane list. The highest plane it does list, (533), sits at **74.68°** for a = 8.328 Å, so the quoted range cannot end on a listed line. Most likely the list names the labelled peaks in the figure and the range names the scan's last peak. The tool cannot tell which, but it can tell you the two statements do not meet.

**What a ring list proves, and what it does not.** An indexer works from the *ratios* of ring radii, because R² is proportional to h² + k² + l². Feeding the tool the seven d-spacings the paper's own ring assignment implies, the best fit is **diamond cubic**, a = 8.328 Å, with three allowed lines missing; face-centred cubic fits the same rings equally well but has to leave seven of its lines unaccounted for, so it ranks second. The seven reported rings simply do not contain the evidence that picks a spinel.

Add the two rings the paper's own powder pattern shows but its ring list leaves out, **(222)** and **(511)**, and the ranking flips: **face-centred cubic, a = 8.328 Å**, now first, with only the systematically weak (200), (331) and (420) unseen, and diamond cubic pushed to a nonsense cell of 19.19 Å. Two extra rings settle it. Figure 2 shows the ladder of radius ratios with and without them. The lesson for anyone publishing a SAED figure: index the weak rings too, or the pattern supports less than you think.

**What the pattern would look like on the microscope.** At 200 kV (the usual setting for the instrument named; the paper does not say, so this is **our assumption**) the relativistic electron wavelength is **0.02508 Å**, against 0.02742 Å if you forget the relativistic correction, an 8.5 % error that would land straight in the cell constant. With a 1000 mm camera length the camera constant λL is **25.08 Å·mm**, and the seven rings would sit at radii of 5.22, 8.52, 9.99, 12.05, 14.75, 17.03 and 19.05 mm. The small-angle rule Rd = λL is good to 10⁻⁵ Å here, so the exact geometry is not worth the trouble.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/zinc-chromite-spinel-cell-from-lattice-fringe-saed.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `electronWavelength`, `classicalWavelength`, `dFromR`, `dFromRExact` and `indexRings` from `src/lib/sci/ediff.ts`, the maths behind the [[electron-diffraction-indexer]], and `dFromTwoTheta`, `twoThetaFromD` and `cubicLines` from `src/lib/sci/xrd.ts`, which power the [[xrd-peak-calculator]]. `indexRings` ranks the four cubic lattices by how well the ring radii ratios match their allowed lines, through `rankCubic`.

From the paper: the two end 2θ values, the wavelength, the plane lists, the fringe spacing and its assignment. **Ours:** the 200 kV accelerating voltage, the 1000 mm camera length used for the worked radii, and the decision to test (222) and (511) as the extra rings. Every d-spacing shown is computed from a cell constant; none is a measurement from the paper, which publishes only the 0.17 nm fringe.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/electron-diffraction-indexer.mjs`, which pins the indexer against published constants and worked patterns:

- the relativistic electron wavelength is 0.0370, 0.02508 and 0.01969 Å at 100, 200 and 300 kV, matching the standard table, and the non-relativistic formula is 9 % out at 200 kV;
- a gold-like face-centred cubic standard (a = 4.08 Å) indexes as (111), (200), (220), (311), (222), (400), (331) and calibrates to λL = 25.0920 ± 0.0806 Å·mm, within 0.1 % of the nominal 25.079;
- the same pattern's sample rings then index as body-centred cubic with a = 2.87190 Å, iron's cell, with no line missing, while face-centred cubic leaves lines unaccounted for;
- two spots at 45° give the zone axis [001], and 111 with 220 at 90° gives the [112] family.
- New with this article: the seven rings of this paper give diamond cubic first with a = 8.3283 Å, and with (222) and (511) added the ranking flips to face-centred cubic with the same a; the (422) fringe of 0.17 nm gives a = 8.3283 Å; and the quoted 87.58° peak is (642) for that cell.

## Check it yourself

On the [[electron-diffraction-indexer]] page, in the *Microscope* card:

1. Set *Accelerating voltage (kV)* to **200**. *Nominal camera constant λL* reads **25.079 Å·mm** with *Camera length L* at **1000** mm, from a wavelength of 0.02508 Å.
2. Set *Distances are in* to **1/nm (calibrated image)**, and *Camera constant from* to **voltage and camera length**. In this mode each value you type is a ring radius in nm⁻¹ and d = 1/R, so no calibration is needed.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published phase assignment** before you build on it: convert the quoted planes to ratios and see whether the lattice type really follows, as we did here.
- **Index your own ring pattern** with no calibration at all, from radii in pixels or mm, and get the lattice type plus a relative cell constant.
- **Calibrate a camera length** with a gold or aluminium standard measured under the same lens settings, then carry the camera constant to your sample: the nominal value on the microscope is often a few per cent off.
- **Decide which rings to measure**: run your candidate lattices through the indexer first and look for the rings that separate them, so the weak ones get measured too.
- **Cross-check powder and electron data** in one place: the same cell should reproduce both your 2θ list (with the [[xrd-peak-calculator]]) and your ring radii, and a 1 % disagreement at low angle usually means a zero-point error, not a different phase.

## The fine print

- The paper quotes **no cell constant and only one measured spacing**, so every d-spacing here is computed from a cell constant, not taken from its data. The two end 2θ values and the fringe spacing are the only measurements used.
- The 1.1 % disagreement between the first-peak cell and the fringe cell is what the paper's own quoted numbers give. It is well within what a zero-point or figure-reading error explains, and it is not a claim that the material is different from what the authors say.
- Reading the quoted 87.58° as (642) assumes the pattern's last peak is a spinel line of the fringe cell; the paper's plane list does not include it.
- The 200 kV voltage and the 1000 mm camera length are **our assumptions**, used only to turn the d-spacings into radii. The lattice type and the cell constant do not depend on either.
- We checked only the structural identification. The size, optical, biological and sensing results are outside what a diffraction tool can test, and one band-gap value is quoted inconsistently in the paper, which is for its authors to resolve.
