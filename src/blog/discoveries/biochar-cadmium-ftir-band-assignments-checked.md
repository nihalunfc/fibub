Almost every paper on a new adsorbent ends the same way: a few infrared peaks move by a handful of wavenumbers after the metal is taken up, and the authors name the surface groups responsible. The reasoning is sound, but it stands or falls on the **band assignment**, the step where a number in cm⁻¹ becomes the name of a bond. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Carbon* makes bamboo biochar from *Melocanna baccifera*, activates it with potassium hydroxide, and credits its cadmium uptake to surface complexation at carboxyl and C–O groups, on the evidence of small shifts in its FTIR spectrum. This article runs every peak the paper reports through the [[ir-band-finder]], which holds the standard ranges for each kind of bond, and reports where the tool agrees and where it does not. For the rest of the same paper, fit its uptake curves with the [[reaction-kinetics-fitter]] and turn its absorbance readings into concentrations with the [[beer-lambert-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors pyrolysed bamboo at 500 °C, then activated part of it with potassium hydroxide at 800 °C. Activation raised the BET surface area from **322 to 432 m² g⁻¹** and the Langmuir capacity for cadmium from **15.15 to 24.39 mg g⁻¹**, with up to 97.89 % removal from a 10 mg L⁻¹ solution at pH 5. Uptake follows pseudo-second-order kinetics (R² = 0.999) and the Langmuir isotherm fits better than Freundlich.

To explain *why* cadmium sticks, they compare FTIR spectra before and after loading. Three things change: the broad band near **3395 cm⁻¹** weakens, the band at **1685 cm⁻¹** moves by one or two wavenumbers, and the band at **1572 cm⁻¹** moves up by **11 cm⁻¹** on the pristine biochar and **17 cm⁻¹** on the activated one. They read all of this as surface complexation, with the metal binding carboxyl, carbonyl and phenolic oxygen; and they explicitly note that the peak near 2359 cm⁻¹ is carbon dioxide in the beam, not a surface group.

## Checked with the tools

This is **not** a reanalysis of a spectrum: the paper's spectra are figures, so we have only the peak positions printed in its text. What the tool can do is exactly what a referee does by hand, but completely and in one pass: for each wavenumber, list every bond whose standard range covers it, within the tool's default tolerance of ±10 cm⁻¹.

**The two assignments the tool confirms.** For the broad band at 3395 cm⁻¹ it returns O–H (hydrogen-bonded, 3200 to 3550 cm⁻¹) and N–H (3300 to 3500), which is the paper's own reading. For 2359 cm⁻¹ it returns exactly one entry, O=C=O, flagged in the band table as "carbon dioxide in the beam (background)" — the same caution the authors give.

**The 1685 cm⁻¹ band is a carbonyl, but a conjugated one.** The tool's first candidate is **C=O (conjugated), 1660 to 1700 cm⁻¹**, described as a conjugated or aryl ketone, aldehyde or acid. The plain carboxylic-acid C=O window starts at **1700 cm⁻¹**. So "carboxyl C=O" is defensible only in its conjugated form, which is what you expect on an aromatic char surface, and three other bonds (amide I, alkene C=C, imine C=N) fall in the same window.

**The 1572 to 1589 cm⁻¹ band is not a C=O band.** The lowest C=O range in the whole table is amide I at **1630 cm⁻¹**; with the ±10 cm⁻¹ tolerance nothing carbonyl reaches below 1620. The tool's candidates for 1572, 1583 and 1589 cm⁻¹ are **aromatic ring C=C (1580 to 1620)** and the amide II N–H bend. The paper calls these peaks "C=O stretching", and that label cannot be supported by position alone. It matters, because the largest shift in the paper (+17 cm⁻¹) is this band: the evidence points to the aromatic ring system, or to carboxylate, rather than to a free carboxyl C=O.

**The fingerprint pair is C–O, not C–H.** For 1016 and 1114 cm⁻¹ the tool offers C–O (alcohol, 1000 to 1260), C–O–C (ether, 1050 to 1150), C–N and, as a reminder for ash-rich chars, Si–O–Si (1000 to 1100). **What the whole peak list adds up to.** Fed all ten positions, the tool's group test calls *alcohol or phenol* likely (broad O–H plus C–O) and, on position alone, *amide* likely as well, which is a warning rather than a finding: the amide call rests on sharing windows with C=O and N–H, and a bamboo char has little nitrogen. **Carboxylic acid scores only 1 of 2**, because the very broad acid O–H dimer band from 2500 to 3300 cm⁻¹ is not among the reported peaks. In other words, the paper's carboxyl claim rests on a single band at 1685 cm⁻¹ with no second line of evidence in the peak list.

| Peak (cm⁻¹) | Paper says | Band finder's windows | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3395, broad | O–H (and N–H) | O–H H-bonded 3200–3550; N–H 3300–3500 | agrees |
| 2359 | carbon dioxide in the beam | O=C=O 2330–2370 (background) | agrees |
| 1685 → 1687 / 1684 | carboxyl C=O | C=O conjugated 1660–1700; amide I; C=C; C=N | carbonyl yes, but conjugated, and not unique |
| 1572 → 1583 / 1589 | C=O | aromatic ring C=C 1580–1620; amide II N–H bend | no C=O window reaches here |
| 1016 and 1114 | C–H bending and C–O | C–O 1000–1260; C–O–C 1050–1150; C–N; Si–O–Si | C–O yes, C–H bending no |

None of this touches the paper's main adsorption result, which is a capacity measurement. It sharpens the mechanism: the shifting bands say oxygen-containing and aromatic surface chemistry is involved, which supports surface complexation, while naming "carboxyl" specifically needs more than these positions.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/biochar-cadmium-ftir-band-assignments-checked.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `assign`, `bandsAt`, `parseIR`, `BANDS` and `toMicron` from `src/lib/sci/irbands.ts`, the maths and band table behind the [[ir-band-finder]].

The ten peak positions and every assignment labelled "paper says" come from the paper's text. The band ranges, the ±10 cm⁻¹ tolerance and the choice of which windows to draw are the tool's and **ours**. The spectra themselves were not available to us, so no peak picking was done here: the tool's peak-picking side (paste two columns of wavenumber and %T or absorbance) needs the raw spectrum.

## How we test the maths

Band assignment has no "reference answer" to fit, so the checks pin the table's internal rules instead. The new `tests/sci-tools/ir-band-finder.mjs`, run by `tests/sci.mjs` on every change, holds:

- Every band in the table must have lo < hi, inside 400 to 4000 cm⁻¹, and every band id named by a group rule must exist.
- 1715 cm⁻¹ must return the ketone and carboxylic-acid windows (and the aldehyde one, within tolerance), while 1685 cm⁻¹ must return the conjugated C=O window and not the plain acid one.
- The unit conversions must invert each other: 1685 cm⁻¹ is 5.9347 µm, and 20 %T is an absorbance of 0.69897.
- A spectrum typed as "3300-2500 br, 1710, 1240" must be read as one broad band at 2900 cm⁻¹ and come out a carboxylic acid; a sharp O–H at 3400 with C–O at 1100 must come out an alcohol instead, with no acid offered at all.
- New with this article: the paper's ten peaks must give exactly one candidate for 2359 cm⁻¹ (background carbon dioxide), four for 1685 cm⁻¹ including the conjugated C=O, and for 1572, 1583 and 1589 cm⁻¹ no C=O window at all; and the lowest C=O window in the table must be amide I at 1630 cm⁻¹.

## Check it yourself

On the [[ir-band-finder]], in the *Your peaks* card:

1. Clear the box (*Peak positions in cm⁻¹, one per line*) and type the paper's unloaded-biochar peaks, marking the first as broad: **3395 (br)**, **2359**, **1685**, **1572**, **1016**, **1114**.
2. Leave *Tolerance (± cm⁻¹)* at **10**.
3. Under *Each peak*, 1685 lists **C=O (conjugated)** first, with the range 1660 to 1700, and 1572 lists only **N–H bend (amide II)** and **C=C (ring)** — no carbonyl.
4. Under *Functional groups*, **Alcohol or phenol** and **Amide** come out as likely, and **Carboxylic acid** shows 1 of 2 bands met: the broad acid O–H is missing.
5. Change 1572 to **1589**, the loaded-MAB position, and watch it move into the middle of the aromatic ring window while staying outside every C=O window.
6. Use *cm⁻¹ to µm* with **1685** to get **5.935 µm**, if you need the micron scale older instruments print.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check your own assignments before submission**, peak by peak, with the alternatives listed rather than remembered.
- **Referee a mechanism claim**: if a paper's key shifted band falls outside every window for the bond it names, say so, as above.
- **Decide what extra evidence you need**: here, a second carboxyl band (the broad acid O–H, or the carboxylate pair near 1570 and 1400) would settle the question.
- **Convert between spectra from different decades**, wavenumber to microns and %T to absorbance, before comparing peak heights.
- **Teach the fingerprint region** with a real published list instead of a textbook ideal spectrum.

## The fine print

- We never saw the spectra. Everything here follows from the positions printed in the paper, and a band that is a shoulder on a figure may be listed at a position the eye would not pick.
- Band ranges are general and shift with hydrogen bonding, conjugation, metal binding and sample state; a peak a few wavenumbers outside a window is not a refutation.
- Carboxylate (the deprotonated form, which is what binds a metal at pH 5) has its own pair of bands near 1570 and 1400 cm⁻¹. That, not C=O, is the most likely reading of the paper's 1572 cm⁻¹ band, and it still supports its mechanism.
- The tool's group test works on position only; it has no access to intensities, widths or the rest of the spectrum.
- Cadmium compounds are toxic. The paper's work is laboratory adsorption, not a treatment recommendation.
