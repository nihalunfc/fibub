Volcanoes hum. Mt. Etna produces a near-continuous **tremor** and short **long-period (LP) events**, and both are weak, slow wobbles of the ground that sit on top of louder ocean noise. A 2026 paper in *Solid Earth* tracked where that signal comes from by pairing a small seismic array with a rotational sensor, and it chose very narrow frequency bands to do it. This article asks a simple question that any seismologist faces: what does such a band actually keep, and what does it throw away? To play with the same filters yourself, open the [[seismogram-viewer]], design a filter in the [[digital-filter-designer]], or turn a speed and a frequency into a wavelength in the [[wave-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ran a seven-station broadband array and a co-located rotational sensor (together a "6C" station: three ground-motion and three rotation components) on Etna for 25 days in August and September 2019, a period that included the start of an eruption on 4 September. They worked out the **back azimuth**, the compass direction a wave comes from, for tremor and for 371 LP events, once with the array and once with the 6C station, and compared both with locations from the Italian volcano observatory's network.

Some facts from the text: the stations were 60 to 90 m apart; data were recorded at 200 samples per second and tremor data were resampled to 50. Tremor back azimuths from the observatory network moved from about 210° to 190° early on and to about 230° to 235° at the peak. Both methods show LP events shifted southwards, more strongly for the 6C method, which the authors link to local heterogeneity. From slowness and rotation data they conclude that the waves are mostly surface waves, dominated by horizontally polarised (SH-type, Love-wave) motion, with a phase velocity of about 0.35 to 0.45 km/s for tremor. The array slowness for tremor is about 1.2 s/km, rising to about 1.7 s/km at the peak.

For us the interesting detail is the filtering. Every result is made after a **two-pole zero-phase Butterworth bandpass**: 0.5 to 10 Hz for tremor on the seismometer, 0.5 to 2.5 Hz for tremor on the rotation sensor, 0.6 to 10 Hz for LP events on the array, and 0.6 to 1.4 Hz for LP events on the rotation sensor. The rotation channels use the narrower bands; the paper notes that, according to the literature, this type of rotational sensor has a low-frequency limit of about 4 Hz.

## Checked with the tools

We did **not** have the paper's seismograms, so there is no real Etna data here. What we can check is the filter itself, and what it does to a made-up record. Reading "two-pole" as order 2 per corner with zero phase (**our assumption**), we built each band with the same Butterworth code as the Seismogram viewer.

**Step 1: the bands.** A zero-phase filter runs once forwards and once backwards, so its gain is the one-pass gain squared and every corner sits at exactly 0.5 (about −6 dB, not the −3 dB of a one-pass filter). Figure 1 shows the four bands. The narrowest, 0.6 to 1.4 Hz, spans only about 1.2 octaves, so its gain reaches 1 just near 0.92 Hz and a short LP event loses much of its shape.

**Step 2: what the filters remove.** We simulated 300 s of tremor at 50 samples per second (**our assumption**, not the paper's data): noise shaped to 1 to 5 Hz with an RMS of 1, a strong 0.2 Hz ocean microseism with an RMS of 4, and weak 12 to 20 Hz noise with an RMS of 0.5. The raw record has an RMS of 4.14, almost all of it the microseism. After the 0.5 to 10 Hz band the RMS is 1.00; after 0.5 to 2.5 Hz it is 0.58, because the band cuts the upper part of the tremor too; after 0.6 to 1.4 Hz it is only 0.25. The microseism is cut by factors of about 46 (0.5 to 10 Hz) and 84 (0.5 to 2.5 Hz). Figure 2 shows the spectra.

**Step 3: the cost of a narrow band.** In the Seismogram viewer's own made-up example (a small local earthquake, vertical component, 100 samples per second), the P wave is a 6 Hz burst and the S wave a 2.5 Hz burst. We reproduced the page's example and processing and read the **dominant frequency** (the strongest spectral peak) in a window around each wave:

| Window and band | Dominant frequency |
|---|---|
| P wave (12.5 to 18 s), no filter | 6.00 Hz |
| P wave, 1 to 15 Hz | 6.00 Hz |
| P wave, 0.5 to 2.5 Hz | 1.09 Hz |
| S wave (20 to 32 s), 1 to 15 Hz | 2.50 Hz |
| S wave, 0.5 to 2.5 Hz | 2.50 Hz |
| S wave, 0.6 to 1.4 Hz | 1.11 Hz |

The 1.09 and 1.11 Hz values are not the earthquake: they are a weak 1.1 Hz background wiggle in the example that survives the filter after the burst has been removed. A narrow band can leave you looking at a different signal altogether, so always compare the filtered and unfiltered spectra.

**Step 4: a rule of thumb for the array.** A plane wave is only sampled without ambiguity when two stations are less than half a wavelength apart, so the highest clean frequency is about v ÷ (2 d), with v the apparent speed and d the spacing. The paper's slowness of 1.2 to 1.7 s/km means an apparent speed of 0.83 to 0.59 km/s, and with spacings of 60 to 90 m this gives 3.3 Hz (slowest wave, widest spacing) to 6.9 Hz (fastest wave, closest spacing). This is a rough guide of ours, not a claim about the paper's results; the paper's own processing is more careful than a single rule. It does show why most of the LP and tremor energy, at 0.5 to 5 Hz, is where the array can work.

| Quantity | Paper says | Our tool or arithmetic |
|---|---|---|
| Filter | two-pole zero-phase Butterworth | gain 0.5 at each corner |
| Rotation tremor band | 0.5 to 2.5 Hz | microseism at 0.2 Hz cut about 84 times |
| Wavelength at 1 Hz | not given | 350 to 450 m for 0.35 to 0.45 km/s |
| Alias-free limit | not given | 3.3 to 6.9 Hz for 60 to 90 m spacing |

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/etna-tremor-bandpass-zero-phase-seismogram-viewer.mjs`. It calls `butter`, `sosGain`, `sosfiltfilt`, `detrend`, `taper`, `amplitudeSpectrum` and `dominant` from `src/lib/sci/dsp.ts`, the maths of the [[seismogram-viewer]], and rebuilds that page's made-up example with the page's own formulas.

The bands, sampling rates, spacings, slowness and phase velocities come from the paper's text. The synthetic tremor, the microseism and noise levels, the 300 s length and the reading of "two-pole" as order 2 are **our assumptions**. Nothing here is the paper's measured data.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/seismogram-viewer.mjs`. It checks the filters against SciPy: a one-pass and a zero-phase Butterworth bandpass, the amplitude spectrum and the spectrogram, all matched to SciPy and NumPy results. For this article we added a check that the zero-phase order-2 band of 0.5 to 2.5 Hz at 50 samples per second has a gain of 0.5 at both corners, 0.0119 at 0.2 Hz and below 0.0001 at 15 Hz, and that the 0.6 to 1.4 Hz band has gain 0.0016 at 0.2 Hz.

## Check it yourself

On the [[seismogram-viewer]]:

1. Press **Use the example**. Under *Processing* leave *Trend* on **Remove a straight line** and *Taper* on **5 % each end**.
2. Set *Filter* to **Bandpass**, *Low corner (Hz)* to **1** and *High corner (Hz)* to **15**, *Order* **2**, and keep **Zero phase** ticked.
3. Under *Traces*, type **12.5** in *From (s)* and **18** in *To (s)*. In *Spectrum*, choose the vertical trace (HHZ). *Dominant frequency* reads about **6.00 Hz**.
4. Change *Low corner* to **0.5** and *High corner* to **2.5**. The dominant frequency jumps to about **1.09 Hz**: the burst is gone.
5. Now set *From* to **20** and *To* to **32**. With 0.5 to 2.5 Hz it reads **2.50 Hz**; with *Low corner* 0.6 and *High corner* 1.4 it reads about **1.11 Hz**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Choosing a band before you process:** open a short record, try the paper's style of narrow band and check in the spectrum what survives.
- **Sanity-checking someone else's processing:** a zero-phase band has gain 0.5 at its corners; a one-pass filter has 0.707. Know which one a methods section means.
- **Teaching filtering:** show students that a narrow band can leave only noise and still produce a confident "dominant frequency".
- **Planning an array:** use the half-wavelength rule with your station spacing to see which frequencies the array can resolve, then use the [[wave-calculator]] for wavelengths.
- **Checking a field sensor:** record with the [[phone-seismometer]] on a table and compare broad and narrow bands on the same recording.

## The fine print

- Our tremor, microseism and noise are **simulated**; the paper's seismograms are in its figures and data repositories, which we did not use.
- Reading "two-pole" as order 2 per corner is an assumption. If the paper meant order 1 per corner, the corners would still sit at 0.5 but the slopes would be gentler.
- The 6C and array back-azimuth methods themselves are not recomputed here; only the filtering and the simple geometry are.
- The alias-free limit is a plane-wave rule of thumb, not part of the paper's analysis.
