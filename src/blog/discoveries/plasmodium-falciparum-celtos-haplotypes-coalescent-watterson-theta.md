A malaria vaccine works only if the protein it teaches the immune system to recognise looks the same in the parasites a person actually meets. So before anyone builds a vaccine around an antigen, someone counts how variable that antigen is in the field. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology* did that for **CelTOS**, a *Plasmodium falciparum* protein the parasite uses to cross cell membranes, in 202 sequences from Jazan Province in south-west Saudi Arabia. Its summary numbers are the standard ones: 27 haplotypes, 18 variable sites, nucleotide diversity 0.0059 per site, Watterson's theta 0.0035 per site and a Tajima's D of 1.77. This article shows how the [[coalescent-simulator]] rebuilds those numbers from scratch, and what 20,000 simulated genealogies say about them. For the steps either side, count alleles and heterozygosity with the [[allele-frequency-calculator]], watch drift act on one of those haplotypes with the [[wright-fisher-simulator]], and date a split with the [[molecular-clock]].

## The paper in brief

Blood spots came from patients with microscopically confirmed *P. falciparum* malaria at public clinics across ten regions of Jazan, on the Yemeni border. The team amplified CelTOS by nested PCR, sequenced it by the Sanger method, and analysed **726 ungapped nucleotide sites** (242 codons) in 202 sequences from 201 patients.

Their headline numbers: **27 haplotypes**, haplotype diversity **0.921**, **18 segregating sites** (all parsimony-informative), nucleotide diversity **0.0059 per site** and Watterson's theta **0.0035 per site**. The neutrality tests are all positive except one: Tajima's **D = 1.77** (0.10 > p > 0.05), Fu and Li's **D\* = 1.43** and **F\* = 1.76**, and Fu's **Fs = -3.36**. Gene-wide dN/dS is about **0.36**, so most of the gene is under purifying selection, with a few codons showing episodic diversifying selection in exposed regions. Their conclusion for vaccine work: CelTOS is variable, but moderately so, and the variation sits where antibodies would bind.

Two of those numbers are pure arithmetic, and one is a probability statement about a tree. That is exactly what a coalescent tool is for.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: Watterson's theta is a one-line calculation, and it does not match.** Watterson's estimator is theta = S / a, where S is the number of segregating sites and a is the sum 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... + 1/(n-1). The tool's `harmonic` gives **a = 5.883** for n = 202, so from S = 18:

- theta = 18 / 5.883 = **3.060 per locus**, which over 726 sites is **0.00421 per site**.

The paper reports 0.0035. The gap is not rounding: 0.0035 per site over 726 sites needs **S = 15**, not 18 (15 / 5.883 / 726 = 0.00351). Either three of the 18 variable sites were left out of the diversity calculation (gapped or ambiguous columns are often dropped), or theta was divided by a longer alignment of about 874 sites. Both happen in practice; the paper does not say which.

**Step 2: the same three sites explain Tajima's D.** Tajima's D compares the two ways of estimating theta: from nucleotide diversity (pi x 726 = 4.283 per locus here) and from S. Using the published variance formula (Tajima 1989, worked out in our script, not in the tool):

| Segregating sites used | Watterson's theta per site | Tajima's D |
|---|---|---|
| 18 (the paper's stated S) | 0.00421 | 1.05 |
| 15 (implied by the paper's theta) | 0.00351 | 1.73 |

The paper's **D = 1.77** sits with the 15-site version, not the 18-site one. So the paper's theta and its D agree with each other; what disagrees is the sentence that says 18 sites. This matters for anyone reusing the numbers: a reader who recomputes D from "S = 18, pi = 0.0059" will get about 1.05 and wonder what went wrong. Neither value is significant (D of 1.05 and 1.73 both sit inside the usual -2 to +2 band), so the paper's biological conclusion is unchanged.

**Step 3: how unusual is 18 variable sites for this much diversity?** This is the part a formula cannot answer well, and a simulator can. We ran **20,000 Kingman genealogies** of n = 200 sampled gene copies (the tool's ceiling; a is 5.873 there instead of 5.883, a 0.2 % difference) with mutations falling at theta = 4.283 per locus, the value implied by the paper's own nucleotide diversity. Under strict neutrality those trees give:

- segregating sites **25.1 on average, standard deviation 7.5** (the exact expectation is theta x a = 25.2);
- only **18 % of runs produced 18 or fewer** segregating sites.

So the sample has fewer variable sites than its diversity implies, but not startlingly fewer: about one neutral locus in five would look like this. That is the simulation version of "0.10 > p > 0.05" and it agrees with the paper's cautious wording.

**Step 4: one gene cannot pin theta down.** Running the same 20,000 genealogies with theta set to 3.06 per locus (the S = 18 value) and reading back Watterson's estimate from each simulated sample gives a **95 % range of 0.0019 to 0.0073 per site**. The paper's 0.0035 and our 0.0042 are both deep inside it. A single 726 bp locus simply cannot tell those two apart, whatever the arithmetic, which is worth remembering before building an argument on one antigen's theta.

## How we made the graphs

Everything above comes from `scripts/blog-figures/plasmodium-falciparum-celtos-haplotypes-coalescent-watterson-theta.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `harmonic`, `expected` and `replicate` from `src/lib/sci/coalescent.ts`, the maths behind the [[coalescent-simulator]]: `replicate` builds each genealogy by drawing exponential waiting times for the next merger and dropping Poisson mutations on the branches, then returns that run's TMRCA, total branch length, segregating sites and Watterson estimate.

Inputs from the paper: n = 202 sequences, 726 ungapped sites, S = 18, nucleotide diversity 0.0059 per site. **Our choices:** n = 200 inside the simulator (its maximum), 20,000 replicate genealogies, the seed 2024, and the Tajima (1989) variance formula written out in the script because the tool does not compute D. Nothing here is the paper's own sequence data; we only ever use its published summary numbers.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/coalescent-simulator.mjs`, which pins the simulator against textbook results and against `msprime`:

- for n = 10, the expected time to the most recent common ancestor must be exactly 1.8 units of 2N generations, a must be 7129/2520, and the variance of the TMRCA 1.15814;
- 20,000 simulated genealogies must reproduce those means and variances within four standard errors, and Watterson's estimator must come back unbiased;
- a one-off cross-check recorded in the tool's guide compared 20,000 genealogies of n = 10 from this code with `msprime` 1.4.5, giving two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov p-values of 0.20 (TMRCA), 0.31 (total branch length) and 0.99 (segregating sites);
- new with this article: for n = 202, a must be 5.88301, Watterson's theta from S = 18 must be 3.0597 per locus (0.0042144 per site over 726 sites), and the expected number of segregating sites at theta = 4.2834 must be 25.199 with a standard deviation of 7.4356.

## Check it yourself

On the [[coalescent-simulator]] page, in the *Sample and mutation rate* card:

1. Set *Sample size n (gene copies)* to **200** (the page's maximum; the paper has 202) and *θ = 4Nμ (per locus)* to **4.2834**, the paper's nucleotide diversity times its 726 sites.
2. Set *Genealogies to average* to **20000** and *Seed* to **2024**, then read the *Over many genealogies* table.
3. The row *Segregating sites S* shows an expected **25.16** against a simulated mean of about **25.1**, with a standard deviation near **7.5**. The paper's 18 sites sit about one standard deviation below that.
4. The row *Watterson's θ* shows the estimator averaging back to 4.28, with a standard deviation near **1.27** per locus: divide by 726 and you have +/- 0.0018 per site on a single gene.
5. Now set *θ = 4Nμ (per locus)* to **3.0597**, the value Watterson's formula gives for S = 18. The expected *Segregating sites S* becomes **18.00**, which is the arithmetic of step 1 run backwards.
6. For one tree at a time, switch to *One genealogy*: it prints that run's S, its Watterson's θ and its TMRCA, and *Next tree* draws the next sample. Switch *Show times in* to **Generations** with *Effective size N (diploid)* to put the TMRCA in generations instead of 2N units.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recompute a published theta before you reuse it.** S, n and the alignment length are usually all in the paper; the tool's harmonic sum turns them into theta in one step, and a mismatch tells you the authors used a different site count.
- **Put a spread on a single-locus estimate.** Simulate at your estimated theta and read the standard deviation; it is usually large enough to end an argument about a 20 % difference between two genes.
- **Plan how much sequence you need.** The expected number of segregating sites grows as theta times the harmonic sum, so doubling the sample adds very little: the tool shows how quickly the gain from more individuals runs out compared with more sites.
- **Teach why positive Tajima's D means "too few variable sites for this much diversity"** by running the simulation at theta from pi and counting how often S falls as low as the data.
- **Sanity-check a neutrality test** by simulating under neutrality and reading the proportion of runs more extreme than the data, instead of trusting a tabulated p-value band.

## The fine print

- Everything here uses the paper's **published summary numbers**, not its sequences. We did not re-align or re-count anything.
- Tajima's D is computed in our script from the 1989 formula; the coalescent simulator itself reports S, Watterson's theta, the TMRCA and branch lengths, not D.
- The simulator is the **standard neutral coalescent**: one panmictic population of constant size, no recombination, no selection, infinite sites. CelTOS is under purifying selection with some recombination, so the simulation is a neutral yardstick, not a model of the parasite.
- The tool caps n at 200, so our simulations use 200 where the paper has 202. The harmonic sum differs by 0.2 %.
- We cannot tell from the text which three sites were dropped from the paper's theta, or whether a longer alignment was used instead; both would give the reported 0.0035.
