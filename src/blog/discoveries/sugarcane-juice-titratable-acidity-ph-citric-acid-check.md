Two numbers describe how sour a juice is, and they are not the same number. **pH** is how many free hydrogen ions are in the liquid right now; **titratable acidity** is how much alkali it takes to neutralise all the acid, free and bound. A 2025 survey in *Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems* measured both on 100 cups of fresh sugarcane juice bought from street vendors in Beni Mellal, Morocco, and reported pH **5.23 ± 0.09** with a titratable acidity of **0.16 ± 0.02 %** as citric acid. Those two values are a strong constraint on each other, and the [[titration-curve]] can say whether they agree, and what they imply about how acidic the juice really is. For the arithmetic either side, the [[solution-calculator]] handles the titrant and the [[concentration-converter]] moves between per cent, grams per litre and millimoles.

## The paper in brief

The study is a food-safety survey, not a chemistry paper: 25 samples from each of four vending sites over five months, each measured for pH, titratable acidity and total soluble solids, then plated for aerobic flora, coliforms, staphylococci, yeasts and moulds, *Escherichia coli*, *Salmonella* and *Listeria monocytogenes*.

The microbiology is the headline and it is not reassuring: 5.79 ± 0.36 log CFU mL⁻¹ of total aerobic mesophilic flora, *E. coli* in 77 of the 100 samples, and no *Salmonella* or *Listeria*. The physico-chemical block is short: pH 5.23 ± 0.09 (range 4.98 to 5.54), titratable acidity 0.16 ± 0.02 % as citric acid equivalents by AOAC 942.15 with 0.1 M sodium hydroxide and the standard 0.070 factor, and 18.48 ± 0.69 °Brix of soluble solids. The authors' point about the pH is well taken: at above 4.5 the juice offers no acid barrier at all, which is part of why the counts are what they are.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from the paper's own two numbers**, using the tool's exact charge-balance curve for citric acid at 25 °C with no activity corrections. One modelling choice is ours: we treat the juice's acidity as a single citric acid system, which is exactly what reporting acidity "as citric acid equivalents" assumes. Sugarcane juice in fact carries aconitic, malic and citric acids together.

**Step 1: what 0.16 % actually means in moles.** The AOAC 0.070 factor is grams of citric acid *monohydrate* (210.14 g mol⁻¹, three protons, so 70.05 mg per milliequivalent) per millilitre of 1 N alkali. So 0.16 g per 100 mL is 0.16 / 0.070 = 2.286 meq per 100 mL, or **22.86 meq of alkali per litre** of juice. Read as free citric acid that is 7.62 mmol L⁻¹.

**Step 2: free acid would be far more sour than this.** Put 7.62 mmol L⁻¹ of citric acid in the tool and it reports a starting pH of **2.69**. The juice is measured at **5.23**, two and a half units higher. The acid in the juice is therefore mostly present as its salts: the potassium and other cations in the cane sap have already taken the protons off. This is the single most useful thing the tool says here, because it is easy to assume that a low titratable acidity and a high pH are two ways of saying the same thing, and they are not.

**Step 3: both numbers together give the total acid.** The acidity test counts the protons that come off between the juice's own pH and the end point, so with the species fractions in hand the sum works the other way round. At pH 5.23 a citrate ion has already lost **1.804** of its three protons; at the AOAC end point of pH 8.1 it has lost **2.980**. The titration therefore removes 1.177 protons per citrate, and 22.86 meq L⁻¹ implies a total citrate of

**22.86 ÷ 1.177 = 19.4 mmol L⁻¹**,

that is about **4.1 g of citric acid monohydrate per litre**, of which **60.1 %** is already neutralised. Running the model forward confirms it closes: for 10 mL of 19.4 mmol L⁻¹ citric acid (a 10 mL sample is **our choice**; the result in meq per litre does not depend on it), the tool's curve passes pH 5.23 at 3.50 mL of 0.1 M NaOH and pH 8.1 at 5.78 mL. The difference, 2.28 mL, is 22.84 meq L⁻¹, or **0.160 %** as citric acid: the paper's figure to three decimals.

**Step 4: the end point is slightly early, and that is fine.** For the model juice the tool puts the three equivalence points at 1.94, 3.88 and 5.82 mL, with only the third showing a usable jump (pH 3.96, 5.58 and 9.24; the first two steps are flagged as having no clear jump, because citric acid's pKa values are too close together to separate). Phenolphthalein, whose colour change straddles pH 8.2 to 10.0, comes out as the right indicator with an error of −0.03 %. Stopping at pH 8.1 instead of at the true equivalence biases the acidity low by **0.65 %** relative, which on 0.16 % is 0.001 % absolute: a hundred times smaller than the ± 0.02 % spread between vendors. The half-way points land at pH 3.16, 4.76 and 6.43, within 0.03 of the three pKa values, which is the quickest way to check that a curve has been computed properly.

**Step 5: how much acid would make this juice safe to keep?** Since the model reproduces both reported numbers, it can be pushed a step further. Holding the juice's cations fixed and adding citric acid, the tool's species fractions give **1.48 g L⁻¹** of citric acid monohydrate to reach pH 4.5 and **3.08 g L⁻¹** to reach pH 4.0 (Figure 2). That is a realistic amount of lime or lemon: it is why acidified cane juice keeps, and it quantifies the paper's own observation about the missing acid barrier. It is a model, not a recommendation, and acidification is no substitute for clean water, clean cane and clean equipment.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/sugarcane-juice-titratable-acidity-ph-citric-acid-check.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `alphas`, `pHAt`, `curve`, `equivalenceVolumes`, `indicatorsFor`, `endpoints` and the citric acid preset from `src/lib/sci/titration.ts`, the maths behind the [[titration-curve]].

Inputs from the paper: pH 5.23, titratable acidity 0.16 % as citric acid equivalents, the AOAC 942.15 method with 0.1 M NaOH and the 0.070 factor. **Our inputs:** the citric acid pKa values from the tool's preset (3.13, 4.76, 6.40 at 25 °C), the 10 mL sample volume, the pH 8.1 end point, the single-acid model of the juice, and in Figure 2 the assumption that the neutralising cations stay put while acid is added.

## How we test the maths

The titration maths is pinned in `tests/sci.mjs` against standard worked results, on every change to fibub:

- 25 mL of 0.1 M acetic acid titrated with 0.1 M NaOH must start at pH 2.88, pass through pH 4.76 (its pKa) at 12.5 mL and reach pH 8.72 at equivalence; phenolphthalein must be the first indicator offered.
- The strong-acid case must be exact: 0.1 M HCl starts at pH 1.000 and its equivalence point is pH 7.000.
- Phosphoric acid must give pH 4.70 at its first equivalence and pH 7.20, its second pKa, half way to the second.
- The derivative end-point finder must locate those steps on a curve sampled every 1 mL.

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/titration-curve.mjs` pins this paper's case: a citrate ion must have lost 1.804 protons at pH 5.23 and 2.980 at pH 8.1; the resulting total citrate must be 19.42 mmol L⁻¹; 7.62 mmol L⁻¹ of free citric acid must sit at pH 2.69 while 19.4 mmol L⁻¹ partly neutralised sits at 5.23; and the model must give back 22.84 meq L⁻¹, or 0.160 % as citric acid, between pH 5.23 and pH 8.1.

## Check it yourself

On the [[titration-curve]] page, in the *Simulate a titration* card:

1. Set *In the flask* to **Citric acid**. The *pKa values* box fills with **3.13, 4.76, 6.4**.
2. Type **0.0194** into *Its concentration (mol/L)* and **10** into *Its volume (mL)*, and leave *NaOH in the burette (mol/L)* at **0.100**.
3. The headline reads *Equivalence at 5.82 mL, pH 9.24*, and *Starting pH* is **2.46**. Under *Steps with no clear jump* you should see **1.94 mL, 3.88 mL**: citric acid's first two protons never give a visible step.
4. Check the three half-way lines: pH **3.16** at 0.97 mL, **4.76** at 2.91 mL and **6.43** at 4.85 mL, each within 0.03 of a pKa.
5. The indicator table should offer **Phenolphthalein** (changes at pH 8.2–10.0, error −0.03 %) and **Thymolphthalein** (+0.21 %).
6. Press **Curve (CSV)** and look up the volumes where the pH passes 5.23 and 8.1: **3.50 mL** and **5.78 mL**. The 2.28 mL between them is the paper's titratable acidity, 22.8 meq per litre.
7. Change the concentration to **0.00762**, the same acidity read as free acid, and watch *Starting pH* fall to **2.69**, nowhere near a real juice.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Cross-check a reported pH against a reported acidity** before building anything on either, with the [[titration-curve]]; a mismatch means the acid is buffered, which is normal in juices, dairy and soil extracts, but it changes what the numbers mean.
- **Convert an acidity convention properly.** "As citric acid" can mean the anhydrous acid (0.064) or the monohydrate (0.070); say which, and use the [[concentration-converter]] to move between % w/v, g L⁻¹ and mmol L⁻¹.
- **Choose and justify an end point.** The tool gives the error of every indicator against the true equivalence, so a method can state its bias instead of assuming it is zero.
- **Plan an acidification step** for a juice, sauce or ferment, and see how much acid a given pH target really needs.
- **Carry the uncertainty through.** The ± 0.02 % on the acidity and ± 0.09 on the pH propagate into the derived total acid; the [[uncertainty-calculator]] does that arithmetic.

## The fine print

- Sugarcane juice's acids are mainly aconitic, malic and citric. Treating the lot as citric acid is the paper's own reporting convention and ours; a different acid set would shift the derived total by a few per cent.
- The juice contains other buffers (amino acids, phosphate, bicarbonate) which we have ignored. The derived 19.4 mmol L⁻¹ is therefore an upper reading of the citrate-equivalent acid, not a measured concentration.
- The paper does not state its sample volume or its end-point pH; we used 10 mL and pH 8.1, the AOAC 942.15 value. Neither choice affects the result in milliequivalents per litre.
- All pH values are calculated at 25 °C with activity coefficients of 1. At the ionic strength of a real juice the true pH values sit a few hundredths lower.
- The microbiological findings are the paper's main result and are quoted here only as context; nothing in this article checks them.
