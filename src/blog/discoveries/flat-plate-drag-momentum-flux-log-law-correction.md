How hard does a flat plate rub on the air sliding along it? Everyone agrees on the answer at laboratory speeds, but not on the limit: what the friction does as the Reynolds number goes to infinity. One widely used scaling says the momentum in a turbulent boundary layer grows like U&tau;²&delta;, the friction velocity squared times the layer thickness. A 2026 paper in *Fluids* integrates the logarithmic velocity law across the layer and shows that this scaling hides a slowly growing factor, and that putting the factor back changes the fitted drag exponent from about &minus;0.56 to &minus;0.51, which finally agrees with the asymptotic &minus;1/2. This article reproduces the paper's factor and asks what it is worth on an ordinary plate, using the [[boundary-layer]] calculator. For the Reynolds number itself see the [[dimensionless-numbers|dimensionless numbers calculator]], and for a whole wing section the [[airfoil-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

Inside a turbulent boundary layer the mean speed follows the **log law**: U/U&tau; = (1/&kappa;) ln(y U&tau;/&nu;) + A, with the von Karman constant &kappa; and an additive constant A. The paper takes the quantity M that the usual scaling treats as proportional to U&tau;²&delta;, integrates the log law across the whole layer, and gets

M / (U&tau;² &delta;) = f(Re&tau;) = 2/&kappa;² &minus; 2A/&kappa; + A² + ln(Re&tau;)(2A/&kappa; &minus; 2/&kappa;²) + ln²(Re&tau;)/&kappa;²,

where Re&tau; = &delta;U&tau;/&nu; is the **friction Reynolds number**. So the scaling is not a constant times U&tau;²&delta; at all: there is a factor that grows roughly like the square of the logarithm.

Fitting published data with the factor restored, the author finds the drag exponent moves from &minus;0.557 and &minus;0.543 (uncorrected) to &minus;0.506 and &minus;0.510, with bootstrap 95 % intervals that now include the asymptotic &minus;1/2; the uncorrected fit does not. The paper is explicit that this is a leading-order amendment, since the log law is integrated beyond the range where it strictly holds.

## Checked with the tools

Two things can be checked without the paper's data: whether the closed form really is the integral it claims to be, and whether the factor matches what an independent description of the same boundary layer gives. We did both. Everything is **computed from the formulas**; no data are fitted and nothing is simulated.

**Step 1: the closed form is the mean square of the log-law profile.** Writing &eta; = y/&delta;, the mean of [(1/&kappa;)ln(&eta; Re&tau;) + A]² over 0 &lt; &eta; &lt; 1 gives the paper's f(Re&tau;) exactly, using the two integrals the paper quotes, &int;ln&eta; d&eta; = &minus;1 and &int;ln²&eta; d&eta; = 2 (our 400,000-step sums give &minus;1.00000 and 1.99997). Evaluating both the closed form and a brute-force 200,000-step average at eight friction Reynolds numbers from 397 to 13,245 agrees to seven figures: at Re&tau; = 1,225 both give **405.300**. So the quantity being corrected is the **momentum flux** through the layer, &int;U² dy, not the momentum &int;U dy, and the paper's algebra holds.

**Step 2: the same factor from a completely different description.** The [[boundary-layer]] tool does not use the log law. Its turbulent layer is the classic 1/7 power law, U/U&infin; = (y/&delta;)^(1/7), with &delta; = 0.37x Re&#8339;^&minus;0.2 and c_f = 0.0592 Re&#8339;^&minus;0.2. For that profile the mean square across the layer is 7/9 of U&infin;², so

M / (U&tau;² &delta;) = (7/9)(U&infin;/U&tau;)² = (7/9)(2/c_f) = 14/(9 c_f),

which the tool's skin friction gives at any station. We ran air at 20 °C and 20 m/s along a tripped plate from x = 0.2 m to x = 30 m, which covers Re&tau; from 397 to 13,245, and compared.

With the paper's **fitted** constants (&kappa; 0.39, A 5.7) the two independent routes agree within 8 % from Re&tau; = 400 to 4,000, and cross over near Re&tau; = 4,000; with its reference constants (A 4.3) the log-law factor is 7 to 27 % lower throughout. Both drift apart at the top end, where the 1/7 power law is itself out of its range (it is usually quoted up to Re&#8339; of about 10⁷, which is x = 7.6 m here). For a correction term derived by integrating the log law outside its strict validity, agreeing within 8 % with an unrelated profile over a decade of Re&tau; is a good result.

**Step 3: how big is the correction, and in which direction?** Over our plate the factor grows from 319 to 869, a factor of 2.7, so it is not a detail. Fitted as a power law, the tool gives f &prop; Re&tau;^**0.286** and the paper's closed form gives Re&tau;^**0.236**: both say the "constant" in the scaling creeps up like roughly the quarter power of Re&tau;. That is exactly why an uncorrected fit returns too steep an exponent, and the size matches: the paper's own exponent moves by 0.051 when the correction is applied.

**Step 4: the asymptotic limit is a long way off.** The paper's corrected fit approaches U&tau;/U&infin; &prop; &delta;&#771;^&minus;1/2 with &delta;&#771; = &delta;U&infin;/&nu;. On our plate the tool gives U&tau;/U&infin; &prop; &delta;&#771;^**&minus;0.125**, four times shallower, because the asymptotic bookkeeping assumes &delta;&#771; &prop; Re&tau;², while a real flat plate at these speeds has &delta;&#771; &prop; Re&tau;^**1.14**. The same mismatch explains the one apparent disagreement we found: the paper's &radic;f &prop; &delta;&#771;^0.05 becomes &radic;f &prop; &delta;&#771;^0.103 (closed form) or ^0.125 (tool) once the real relation between &delta;&#771; and Re&tau; is used. So the correction is real and worth applying, but the &minus;1/2 limit it restores belongs to Reynolds numbers no wind tunnel reaches.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/flat-plate-drag-momentum-flux-log-law-correction.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `local` (the local boundary layer: thickness, displacement and momentum thickness, skin friction), `plateCD` (the plate's average drag coefficient) and `blasiusCached` (the laminar solution) from `src/lib/sci/phbl.ts`, the maths behind the [[boundary-layer]] calculator, and codes the paper's closed form beside a brute-force integration of the log law.

The log-law constants (&kappa; 0.39, A 4.3 and the fitted 5.7), the closed form and the fitted exponents are from the paper. The flow conditions (air at 20 °C, 20 m/s, a tripped plate from 0.2 to 30 m) are **ours**, chosen to cover the Reynolds numbers the paper plots. The identification of M with &int;U² dy is **our reading** of the paper's formula, confirmed in step 1.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/boundary-layer.mjs`, which pins this tool against SciPy and NumPy:

- the Blasius solution: f''(0) = 0.332057 against SciPy's `solve_bvp` value 0.33205734, &eta; at 99 % of the free-stream speed 4.90999, and the displacement and momentum integrals 1.72079 and 0.66411 from `quad`;
- the turbulent layer at x = 1 m in 10 m/s air: &delta; = 25.37 mm and c_f = 0.00406 from the 1/7 power law, to 1 part in 10⁸;
- the mixed plate drag coefficient 0.00309658 with the textbook A = 1742, and the Prandtl-Schlichting value 0.00211219 at Re = 10⁸.
- New with this article: in 20 m/s air the tripped layer at x = 1 m must give &delta; = 22.09 mm, c_f = 0.003534 and a friction Reynolds number of 1,225, so that the momentum-flux factor 14/(9c_f) is 440.2 and lies within 5 % of the paper's closed form with its fitted constants.

## Check it yourself

On the [[boundary-layer]] page, in the *Plate and flow* card:

1. Leave *Fluid* on **Air, 20 °C, 1 atm** (density 1.204 kg/m³, kinematic viscosity 1.516 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s).
2. Type *Free-stream speed U (m/s)* **20**, *Plate length L (m)* **2**, *Plate width (m)* **1** and *Look at distance x from the leading edge (m)* **1**.
3. Set *Boundary layer* to **Turbulent from the leading edge (tripped)**. The tool reads a layer **22.1 mm** thick, a displacement thickness of 2.76 mm, a local skin friction coefficient of **0.003534** and a wall shear stress of about **0.85 Pa**.
4. Work out the friction velocity from the skin friction: U&tau;/U&infin; = &radic;(c_f/2) = **0.0420**, so U&tau; = 0.84 m/s and Re&tau; = &delta;U&tau;/&nu; = **1,225**.
5. The momentum-flux factor is then 14/(9 c_f) = **440**, against **463** from the paper's closed form with &kappa; = 0.39 and A = 5.7, a 5 % difference.
6. Switch *Boundary layer* back to **Laminar, then turbulent past transition** and the same station is still turbulent (transition is at x = 0.379 m); the plate's average drag coefficient reads **0.003185**, so one side of the 2 m × 1 m plate feels **1.53 N**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a scaling before fitting it.** A factor that grows like ln² is easy to absorb into a fitted exponent; computing it first tells you how much of your exponent is physics.
- **Convert between friction quantities** in one place: c_f to U&tau;, U&tau; to Re&tau;, Re&tau; to &delta;&#771;, which is where published boundary-layer tables usually disagree.
- **Judge whether a laboratory Reynolds number can see an asymptotic claim at all**: on this plate the exponent is &minus;0.125, not &minus;0.5.
- **Design a measuring station**: pick the x that gives the Re&tau; you need, and see the layer thickness your probe must resolve.
- **Teach the two classic descriptions side by side.** The 1/7 power law and the log law give the same momentum flux within 8 % over a decade, and part company where each is known to fail.

## The fine print

- We could not refit the paper's data: the measurements behind its exponents are in its figures, so steps 3 and 4 compare **formulas**, not data.
- The identification of M with the momentum flux &int;U² dy is our inference from the closed form, which it reproduces exactly; the paper writes M symbolically.
- The tool's turbulent layer is the 1/7 power law with a fixed &delta;* = &delta;/8 and &theta; = 7&delta;/72. It is a smooth-plate, zero-pressure-gradient model and it is normally trusted only to Re&#8339; ~ 10⁷.
- Our air properties are rounded values at 20 °C, and the plate is taken as tripped at the leading edge, so there is no laminar run.
- The paper's own page states CC BY 3.0 in its rights metadata while its footer and the Crossref record say CC BY 4.0; we used the Crossref record.
