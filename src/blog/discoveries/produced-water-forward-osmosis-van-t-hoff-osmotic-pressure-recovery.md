Oil and gas wells produce far more salty water than oil, and cleaning it is a standing problem. A 2024 paper in *Membranes* tests a two-stage answer: **forward osmosis** pulls water out of the salty feed into a strong magnesium chloride solution, then **nanofiltration** takes the water back out of that draw solution so the salt can be reused. The whole design rests on one quantity, the osmotic pressure of each solution, and that is exactly what fibub's [[colligative-properties]] calculator works out. This article reproduces the paper's 16.3 bar feed, backs out the concentrations behind its draw solutions, and shows that the recoveries it reports sit neatly between two limits the ideal law predicts. For the unit conversions along the way, use the [[concentration-converter]]; to summarise a water analysis, the [[water-quality-index]].

## The paper in brief

The authors made a synthetic water that mimics pretreated produced water, with a salinity of about **20 g/L**, and state its osmotic pressure as **16.3 bar**, worked out with the commercial OLI Studio package. Against it they ran **magnesium chloride draw solutions** of 30, 40, 60, 80 and 120 bar, using 2.5 L of feed and 2.5, 4 or 5.5 L of draw (volume ratios of 1, 1.6 and 2.2), all at **21 °C**.

Forward osmosis fluxes were **4 to 10 litres per square metre per hour**, and each run was stopped when the flux fell to 2. Water recovery ranged from about **25 %** in the weakest case to about **73 %** in the strongest. The nanofiltration stage reached a best rejection of **96.6 %** at an average flux of 21 L m⁻² h⁻¹, and the authors estimate that 5 to 10 % of the draw salt has to be replaced each cycle.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated salinities, pressures and volumes with the ideal (van 't Hoff) law, π = i·c·R·T. Nothing is taken from the paper's figures.

**Step 1: the feed.** Treat the 20 g/L as sodium chloride, which is what dominates produced water. The tool's chain is: molality = 20 g ÷ 58.44 g/mol ÷ 1 kg = **0.342 mol/kg**, then for water c ≈ 0.997 m = **0.341 mol/L**, then π = 2 × 0.341 × 0.0831 × 294.15 K = **16.69 bar**. The paper's own value, from a commercial thermodynamic package, is **16.3 bar**: the ideal law is **2.4 % high**, which at this dilution is about what you would expect from the osmotic coefficient of sodium chloride being a little below 1. Read backwards, 16.3 bar corresponds to **19.5 g/L**, matching the paper's "approximately 20 g/L".

The same solution would freeze **1.27 K** below pure water and reads **684 mOsm/kg**, two numbers any laboratory can measure with an osmometer to confirm a feed salinity in minutes.

**Step 2: the draw solutions.** The paper quotes its draw strengths as osmotic pressures rather than concentrations. With three ions per magnesium chloride unit, the ideal law needs:

| Draw osmotic pressure | Ideal MgCl₂ concentration | As a mass |
|---|---|---|
| 30 bar | 0.409 mol/L | 38.9 g/L |
| 40 bar | 0.545 mol/L | 51.9 g/L |
| 60 bar | 0.818 mol/L | 77.9 g/L |
| 80 bar | 1.090 mol/L | 103.8 g/L |
| 120 bar | 1.636 mol/L | 155.7 g/L |

These are **upper bounds**. Magnesium chloride is a strongly non-ideal salt: above roughly 0.3 mol/kg its osmotic coefficient climbs well above 1, so the real solution reaches a given pressure at a lower concentration than the ideal line says. The direction of the error is worth knowing when you order salt.

**Step 3: why the runs stopped where they did.** Forward osmosis moves water only while the draw is stronger than the feed, and every litre that crosses makes the feed saltier and the draw weaker. If no salt crosses the membrane, the feed's osmotic pressure at a recovery r is 16.69/(1 − r), while a draw of initial pressure π₀ and volume V_d falls to π₀·V_d/(V_d + 2.5r) for a 2.5 L feed. Setting the two equal gives the **recovery at which osmosis stops**, whatever the membrane:

| Draw | 1× volume (2.5 L) | 1.6× (4 L) | 2.2× (5.5 L) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 bar | 28.5 % | 32.9 % | 35.4 % |
| 40 bar | 41.1 % | 46.2 % | 49.0 % |
| 60 bar | 56.5 % | 61.5 % | 64.1 % |
| 80 bar | 65.5 % | 70.0 % | 72.3 % |
| 120 bar | 75.6 % | 79.2 % | **81.0 %** |

The paper's measured range, **25 % to 73 %**, sits just inside the span of these ceilings, **28.5 % to 81.0 %**. At 73 % recovery the feed has concentrated to **61.8 bar** while the strongest draw has fallen to **90.1 bar**: a difference of 28 bar still remains, which is why the authors stop at a flux of 2 L m⁻² h⁻¹ rather than at zero driving force. The last few per cent of recovery are slow because the gap is closing, not because the membrane has failed.

## How we made the graphs

Both figures and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/produced-water-forward-osmosis-van-t-hoff-osmotic-pressure-recovery.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `molality`, `osmotic`, `deltaT` and the solvent table from `src/lib/sci/colligative.ts`, the maths behind the [[colligative-properties]] calculator, including the same c ≈ 0.997 m step the tool uses for water.

From the paper: the 20 g/L salinity, the 16.3 bar feed, the five draw pressures, the three volume ratios, the 21 °C and the 25 to 73 % recoveries. **Our assumptions**: that the feed behaves as sodium chloride and the draw as fully dissociated magnesium chloride, that no salt crosses the membrane, and that both solutions stay well mixed. The recovery ceilings are a thermodynamic limit, not a prediction of flux.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the colligative module against textbook results:

- 10 g of glucose in 200 g of water must give 0.2776 mol/kg, a freezing point depression of 0.5162 K and a boiling point elevation of 0.1421 K;
- 1 g of an unknown in 50 g of benzene that lowers the freezing point by 0.40 K must come out as 256 g/mol, the molar mass of S₈;
- 10 g/L of a protein with an osmotic pressure of 3.73 mbar at 25 °C must give 66,460 g/mol, about right for albumin;
- physiological saline, 9 g/L of NaCl at 37 °C, must give 7.943 bar.

New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/colligative-properties.mjs`: 20 g/L of NaCl at 21 °C must give 0.342231 mol/kg, an osmotic pressure of 16.6897 bar and 684.46 mOsm/kg; the MgCl₂ concentration needed for 120 bar must be 1.635524 mol/L; and the recovery ceiling for a 120 bar draw at 2.2 times the feed volume must be 80.97 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[colligative-properties]] page, in the first card:

1. Leave *Solvent* on **Water**. Type *Solute formula or molar mass* **NaCl**, *Solute mass (g)* **20**, *Solvent mass (g)* **1000**.
2. Set *Particles per formula unit, i* to **NaCl, KCl, KNO₃ (2 ions)** and *Temperature, for vapour and osmotic pressure (°C)* to **21**.
3. Read *Osmotic pressure* **16.69 bar**, and in the same line **684 mOsm/kg**. The freezing point shows **−1.27 °C**.
4. Now the draw solution: change the formula to **MgCl2**, the mass to **155.7**, and *i* to **CaCl₂, MgCl₂, Na₂SO₄ (3 ions)**. The osmotic pressure reads about **120 bar**, the paper's strongest draw.
5. For the feed halfway through a run, type **40** g instead of 20 with i back on 2 ions: **33.4 bar**, the feed at about 50 % recovery. Compare that with your draw and you can see the driving force disappear.
6. The second card goes the other way: choose *You measured* **osmotic pressure**, type **16.3** bar with *i* **2** at **21 °C** and 20 g in 1000 g, and it returns the molar mass that would explain it, a quick check on an assumed salt.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a quoted osmotic pressure into a concentration** (or the reverse) before ordering salt or comparing two papers that report different quantities.
- **Find the thermodynamic recovery ceiling** of any forward osmosis or osmotically assisted run from the feed salinity and the draw strength, before blaming a membrane for a low yield.
- **Check a feed salinity by cryoscopy**: the same page gives the freezing point depression and the osmolality that an osmometer reads.
- **Size a draw loop**: the ceilings table shows that doubling the draw volume buys only a few per cent of recovery, while raising the draw strength buys a lot.
- **Teach non-ideality**: compare the ideal line with a commercial package's number, as we do here, and the 2 % gap at 0.34 mol/L becomes a concrete lesson in osmotic coefficients.

## The fine print

- The van 't Hoff law is the **ideal limit**. It is good to a few per cent for the dilute feed, and increasingly optimistic in the wrong direction for concentrated magnesium chloride, where real osmotic coefficients exceed 1.
- We treat the feed as sodium chloride. Real produced water also carries calcium, magnesium, sulfate and organics, which change the number of particles a little.
- The recovery ceilings assume a perfectly selective membrane and perfect mixing. Salt that leaks across, and the concentration polarisation layer the paper discusses, both lower them.
- The paper's own 16.3 bar comes from a commercial thermodynamic model, not from a measurement, so the 2.4 % difference is between two calculations.
- Nanofiltration rejection, flux and draw-salt replacement are quoted from the paper and not recomputed here.
