How good are people at telling an AI-generated picture from a photograph or artwork made by a person? A 2025 study in the *Journal of Imaging* showed 161 viewers 32 images and asked each time: AI or human-made? It reports the answers as percentages correct for each kind of image. Percentages mix two different things: how well people **see** the difference, and how readily they **say** "AI". **Signal detection theory** separates the two, and this article does that with the [[signal-detection-calculator]] using only numbers stated in the paper. To score a machine classifier on the same task, use the [[confusion-matrix]]; for the same sums framed as a screening test, the [[diagnostic-test-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors recruited 161 adults in Spain: 71 visual professionals (people who work with images) and 90 others. Everyone saw the same 32 images: 24 made with three AI generators and 8 human-made stock images, in four categories (portraits, landscapes, everyday scenes and detailed objects), each with 6 AI and 2 human-made images. For every image they said whether it was AI-generated or human-made and rated how realistic and how credible it looked, on scales from 1 to 5.

The headline: people called AI images "AI" only **61.08 %** of the time but recognised human-made images **78.03 %** of the time, even though three in four images were AI. Visual professionals did a little better on AI images (62.09 % against 60.28 %) and clearly better on human-made ones (82.57 % against 74.86 %). AI images were also rated less realistic and less credible (means of 3.58 and 3.53 against 4.22 and 4.20).

## Checked with the tools

In signal detection terms, an AI image is a **signal** and answering "AI" is saying **yes**. A **hit** is an AI image called AI; a **false alarm** is a human-made image called AI. From the hit rate H and false-alarm rate F, the calculator works out **d′ (d-prime)** = z(H) − z(F), how far apart the two kinds of image are in the viewer's mind, in standard deviations (0 is guessing, 1 is moderate), and the **criterion c** = −(z(H) + z(F))/2, the lean of the answers (above 0 means a lean towards "human-made"). Here z is the inverse of the normal curve.

**Step 1: rebuild the counts.** The paper gives percentages, and the number of trials follows from the design: each professional judged 24 AI and 8 human-made images, so the group had 1704 and 568 trials. The stated percentages then give whole numbers: 1058 hits (62.09 %) and 469 correct rejections (82.57 %) for professionals, 1302 of 2160 (60.28 %) and 539 of 720 (74.86 %) for the others. Every count reproduces the paper's percentage to two decimals. The category counts work the same way (966 AI and 322 human-made trials per category). This is a **recomputation** from the text; we pool all answers in a group, which is **our choice** (see the fine print).

**Step 2: sensitivity and bias.** For all 161 people together, the calculator gives **d′ = 1.054** (95 % interval 0.968 to 1.141) and **c = 0.246**. The positive c is the paper's main pattern in one number: people lean towards answering "human-made". As a check on the rebuilt counts, they imply 51.3 % "AI" answers overall; the paper says 51.24 %.

**Step 3: professionals see better; they do not just guess "AI" more.** Their d′ is 0.314 higher (1.245 against 0.931). On pooled counts that difference is 3.5 standard errors, but pooled counts ignore differences between people and images, so treat it as a sign, not a test. The gain comes almost entirely from fewer false alarms (17.4 % against 25.1 %), not more hits, and professionals are also *more* cautious (c 0.315 against 0.205). That matches the paper's finding that the professional edge shows most on human-made images.

**Step 4: categories differ in kind, not just in score.** Portraits and landscapes are hardest (d′ 0.94 and 0.88), detailed objects easiest (1.30). But landscapes are hard in a different way: people say "AI" freely there (c 0.04, near neutral) and so wrongly call a third of real landscapes AI, while portraits draw the strongest lean towards "human" (c 0.36), so nearly half of AI portraits pass as human-made.

**Step 5: the lean costs accuracy.** An ideal viewer with the same d′ of 1.054 who knew that three images in four are AI would set its criterion at c = ln(1/3)/1.054 = **−1.042**, leaning towards "AI". It would then catch 94.2 % of AI images, wrongly flag 69.7 % of human-made ones, and be right **78.2 %** of the time, against **65.3 %** for the real viewers. Their eyes were not the main problem; their lean was. This is **our calculation** from standard signal detection theory, not a claim made in the paper.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ai-generated-images-detection-signal-detection-d-prime-visual-professionals.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `yesNo` (d′, c, β, A′ and the standard error of d′) and `isoROC` (the equal-sensitivity curves) from `src/lib/sci/sdt.ts`, the maths behind the [[signal-detection-calculator]], and `normCdf` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the ideal observer.

Group sizes, image numbers and percentages come from the paper's text. The whole-number counts are rebuilt from them (Step 1). Pooling answers across people, and the ideal-observer comparison, are **our choices**. No correction for rates of 0 or 1 was needed.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/signal-detection-calculator.mjs`, which pins the calculator against SciPy:

- 42 hits, 8 misses, 12 false alarms and 38 correct rejections must give d′ 1.700760, c −0.144078 and β 0.782672, as `scipy.stats.norm` does, with a standard error of d′ of 0.288362 (Gourevitch and Galanter).
- The log-linear correction on the same table must give d′ 1.657419, and the 1/(2N) rule with a perfect hit rate must give 3.607899.
- A six-category rating ROC must fit Az 0.813825 by maximum likelihood, matching `scipy.optimize`.
- New with this article: the visual professionals' table (1058 hits of 1704, 99 false alarms of 568) must give d′ 1.245149, c 0.314750 and a standard error of 0.069187; Python's `statistics.NormalDist` gives the same d′.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Re-express any "percent correct" study** of real-versus-fake judgements (images, voices, text, news) as sensitivity and bias with the [[signal-detection-calculator]].
- **Compare groups fairly** when they differ in how often they say "fake": d′ is not fooled by a lean.
- **Analyse each person** with the calculator's *Many people* mode, then test the group difference on the per-person d′ values with the [[hypothesis-tests]] tool, which is the right way to do what Step 3 only sketches.
- **Score an AI detector on the same images** with the [[confusion-matrix]] and set it beside the human d′.
- **Plan the base rate**: as Step 5 shows, a 3-to-1 mix of AI and human images changes the best criterion; say it in the instructions or balance the set.

## The fine print

- We pooled every answer in a group. Pooled d′ is not the same as the average of each person's d′, and pooled standard errors are too small because they ignore differences between people and between images. The paper itself used mixed models on the realism and credibility ratings.
- The paper's overall percentages for human-made images (78.03 % correct, 21.74 % wrong) do not add to 100 %, and the group counts give 1008 correct rejections where the overall rate gives 1005; we used the overall rate for "everyone" and the group rates for the groups. The differences change d′ by less than 0.01.
- With only 8 human-made images, the false-alarm rates rest on few pictures; one unusual image can move a category's d′.
- Equal-variance signal detection is an assumption; confidence ratings would be needed to test it (the calculator's ROC mode does that).
