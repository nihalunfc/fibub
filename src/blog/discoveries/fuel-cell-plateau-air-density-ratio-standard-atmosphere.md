A hydrogen fuel cell breathes. It needs a steady stream of air for its oxygen, and a second stream through a radiator to carry its waste heat away. Take the same system up onto a plateau and both streams thin out, so the stack loses power and the cooling fans have to work far harder. A 2026 paper in *Energies* measured exactly that: the same 80 kW fuel cell system tested in Wuhan, near sea level, and in Kunming, on the Yunnan plateau. Almost every altitude number in the paper can be rebuilt from first principles with the [[standard-atmosphere|standard atmosphere calculator]], and that is what this article does: the site densities, the density ratios the paper quotes, and the cooling and fan scaling it derives from them. For the aviation version of the same arithmetic see the [[density-altitude|density altitude calculator]], and for the radiator itself the [[heat-exchanger-sizing|heat exchanger sizing tool]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ran a high-power proton-exchange-membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) system with liquid cooling for the stack and an air-cooled radiator, first in Wuhan (ambient pressure 101.3 kPa, taken at a representative 17 °C) and then in Kunming, whose test site sits at about 1,800 m and is labelled as the "2,000 m" condition (80.1 kPa, a representative 15 °C). Peak recorded stack power fell from **93.98 kW to 84.0 kW**, maximum net power from 82 to 72 kW, and the time to load up from 10 to 90 % stretched from 24 to 32 seconds. The share of stack power eaten by the system's own pumps and fans rose to 18.2 % at the 80 kW point.

To design a control strategy that adapts to altitude, the paper leans on the **standard atmosphere**: an agreed table of how temperature, pressure and density change with height on an average day. It quotes a density ratio of **0.822 at 2,000 m** and **0.669 at 4,000 m**, a pressure of **61.6 kPa at 4,000 m**, an idealised radiator air-side heat-transfer coefficient falling to **92.5, 85.4, 78.8 and 72.5 %** of its sea-level value at 1,000 to 4,000 m, and a fan power penalty of about **1.5 times at 2,000 m and 2.2 times at 4,000 m** for the same air-side duty.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the pressures and temperatures the paper states, using the 1976 standard atmosphere as fibub implements it. No measured curve from the paper is reproduced, and nothing is fitted.

**The two site densities come out right.** Air density is pressure divided by the gas constant for air times temperature. At Wuhan's 101.3 kPa and 17 °C the tool gives **1.2163 kg/m³** (the paper says about 1.22); at Kunming's 80.1 kPa and 15 °C it gives **0.9684 kg/m³** (the paper says about 0.97). Their ratio is **0.7962**, which is the paper's "about 0.80". The paper also notes a sensitivity case with Wuhan at 25 °C: the tool then gives **0.8182**, matching the 0.82 the paper reports for it. Against the sea-level standard density of 1.225 kg/m³, Kunming's air is at **79.05 %**, the paper's "about 0.79".

**The paper's standard-atmosphere ratios are exact.** The tool's 1976 standard gives a density ratio σ of **0.8217 at 2,000 m** and **0.6689 at 4,000 m**, against the paper's 0.822 and 0.669; its pressure at 4,000 m is **61.66 kPa**, against the paper's 61.6. The paper's idealised compressor pressure ratio for 4,000 m, 198.9 kPa ÷ 61.6 kPa = 3.23, becomes **3.226** with the tool's pressure. Figure 1 puts both test sites on those curves.

**But the plateau site is not a standard day, and that matters.** Feed the measured 80.1 kPa into the tool's *Altitude from a pressure* card and it answers a pressure altitude of **1,939 m**, which is why labelling the campaign "2,000 m" is fair on pressure. The standard temperature at that height is **2.4 °C**, while the site was about 15 °C: an ISA deviation of **+12.6 °C**. Warm air is thin air, so the real density, 0.9684 kg/m³, is **4.4 % below** the 1.0127 kg/m³ the standard would give at the same pressure altitude. In aviation terms the tool reports a **density altitude of 2,382 m**: for anything that breathes air, the Kunming test was a 2,380 m day, not a 1,940 m one. A control law that interpolates on geometric altitude alone, as the paper's sweep does, will under-correct on a warm plateau afternoon by roughly that 4 %.

**Where the cooling numbers come from.** The paper's four idealised radiator percentages and two fan-power multipliers are given without their formula. The tool's density ratio settles both at once (Figure 2):

| Altitude | Paper's air-side coefficient | σ^0.8 from the tool | Paper's fan power | σ⁻² from the tool |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 m | 92.5 % | 92.53 % | – | 1.214 |
| 2,000 m | 85.4 % | 85.46 % | 1.5 × | 1.481 |
| 3,000 m | 78.8 % | 78.78 % | – | 1.815 |
| 4,000 m | 72.5 % | 72.49 % | 2.2 × | 2.235 |

So the air-side coefficient is simply the **standard density ratio to the power 0.8**, the classic turbulent convection exponent, and the fan penalty is the **density ratio to the power −2**. Both match to within a tenth of a point, from nothing but the altitude. Anyone extending the paper to another height can now do it in one line: at 3,000 m the air-side coefficient is 78.8 % of sea level and the fan needs 1.81 times the sea-level power for the same duty.

**One stated number only makes sense in "normal" litres.** The paper converts an air-flow reading of 5,600 L/min to 0.121 kg/s "at assumed standard density". At the 1976 standard's 15 °C that would be **0.1143 kg/s**; at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, the normal-litre convention used in flow meters, it is **0.1206 kg/s**. The paper's 0.121 is the second, so its flow readings are normal litres. At the site's own 0.9684 kg/m³ the same volume flow is only **0.0904 kg/s**, a quarter less, which is the reading that matters for stoichiometry.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/fuel-cell-plateau-air-density-ratio-standard-atmosphere.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `atmosphere`, `pressureAltitude`, `densityAltitude` and `stdTP` from `src/lib/sci/eng-atmos.ts`, the maths behind the [[standard-atmosphere|standard atmosphere calculator]], which builds the 1976 standard from its defining layer temperatures and lapse rates rather than from a printed table.

From the paper: the two ambient pressures, the two representative temperatures, the quoted density ratios, the 4,000 m pressure, the radiator percentages, the fan multipliers and the 5,600 L/min flow. **Our own additions:** the exponents 0.8 and −2 (we fitted nothing, we simply tried the two standard exponents and they land on the paper's points), and treating both sites' stated temperatures as the air temperature at the measurement.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/standard-atmosphere.mjs` on every change to fibub. It pins the atmosphere against an independent Python script that integrates the hydrostatic equation with SciPy from the same defining constants:

- Sea level must give exactly 288.15 K and 101,325 Pa, a density of 1.225 kg/m³, a speed of sound of 340.294 m/s and a viscosity of 1.78938 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s.
- Every layer boundary of the standard must come out: 22,632.06 Pa at 11 km, 5,474.889 Pa at 20 km, 868.0187 Pa at 32 km, down to 3.956420 Pa at 71 km.
- The SciPy integration must be matched to 2 parts in 100,000 at eight heights from −2,000 m to 71,000 m, for example 54,048.3 Pa and 0.736428 kg/m³ at 5,000 m.
- Pressure altitude must invert the pressure in every layer (70,000 Pa gives 3,012.18 m), and a density of 1.0 kg/m³ must give a density altitude of 2,064.29 m.
- New with this article: the plateau case. A pressure of 80,100 Pa must give a pressure altitude of 1,938.9 m; 80.1 kPa at 15 °C must give 0.96840 kg/m³ and a density altitude of 2,382.1 m; and the standard density ratios at 2,000 m and 4,000 m must be 0.82168 and 0.66885, so that σ^0.8 reproduces the paper's 85.46 % and 72.49 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[standard-atmosphere|standard atmosphere calculator]]:

1. In the *Altitude* card, type **2000**, leave *Height type* on **Geometric (tape-measure height)** and *ISA deviation ΔT* on **0**. It reads **275.15 K (2 °C), 79,501 Pa, 1.007 kg/m³**, and under *Ratios to sea level*, **σ = 0.8217** with δ = 0.7846. Those are the paper's 0.822 and its 2,000 m condition.
2. Change *ISA deviation ΔT* to **12.6** (the plateau's warm day). Temperature becomes **287.75 K (14.6 °C)**, the pressure does not move, and density falls to **0.9625 kg/m³** with a *Density altitude* of **2,442 m**.
3. In *Altitude from a pressure*, type **80.1** and set *Pressure unit* to **kPa**. It answers **Pressure altitude 1,939 m**, and notes the standard temperature there as 275.55 K.
4. In *Density altitude at an airfield*, set the field elevation to **1800** m, *Altimeter setting QNH* to **995.9** hPa (the setting that gives 80.1 kPa at that elevation) and *Outside air temperature* to **15** °C. It reads **Density altitude 2,382 m**, with a pressure altitude of 1,939 m, an *ISA deviation* of **+12.6 °C** and an *Air density* of **0.9684 kg/m³**, **79.1 % of sea-level standard**: the paper's 0.97 kg/m³ and 0.79.
5. For the cooling numbers, read σ at 1,000, 2,000, 3,000 and 4,000 m (0.9075, 0.8217, 0.7422, 0.6689) and raise each to the power 0.8 for the air-side coefficient, or to the power −2 for the fan power.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Convert a measured ambient pressure into an altitude** that other people can compare with, instead of a nominal site label: 80.1 kPa is a pressure altitude of 1,939 m anywhere in the world.
- **Separate the pressure effect from the temperature effect** on any air-breathing machine. The ISA deviation box shows how much of a density loss is height and how much is a hot day; here it is 4 % of extra loss that a height-only lookup misses.
- **Correct volumetric flow readings** to a mass flow at the real site state, and work out which reference state someone else's "standard litres" used.
- **Scale a radiator or fan to another altitude** with the density ratio and the right exponent, then size the real exchanger with the [[heat-exchanger-sizing|heat exchanger sizing tool]].
- **Plan a high-altitude test campaign**: tabulate pressure, density, viscosity and speed of sound every 500 m, so compressor maps and Reynolds numbers can be corrected before the trucks leave.

## The fine print

- We use the 1976 standard atmosphere. The paper does not name its standard, but its quoted ratios match it to three decimals.
- The paper gives representative temperatures, not logged ones, and says so. Every density here therefore carries that uncertainty; 1 K changes a density by about 0.35 %.
- The exponents 0.8 and −2 are our reading of the paper's idealised cooling numbers, not values it states. They fit all six of its points to within 0.1 point, but a different pair of assumptions could come close.
- Nothing here checks the paper's fuel cell measurements, its control strategy or its thermal model. Only the atmosphere and the scaling that follows from it are checked.
- Humidity is ignored, as in the paper. Moist air is slightly less dense than dry air at the same pressure and temperature.
