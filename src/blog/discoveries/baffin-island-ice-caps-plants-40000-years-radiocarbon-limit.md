This autumn a study in *Science* argued that the last remnant of the great North American ice sheet, the Barnes Ice Cap on Baffin Island, had started to grow again about 2,000 years ago, the first step towards the next ice age, until 20th-century warming turned it back around 1960. The evidence behind that kind of claim is unusual: dead plants that the ice killed when it advanced, now melting out at its edge, and dated with radiocarbon. An earlier, openly licensed paper by many of the same researchers used the same method on 30 small ice caps nearby and found plants that had been buried for **more than 40,000 years**. That is close to the limit of radiocarbon dating, and the [[radiocarbon-calibration]] tool and the half-life solver in the [[decay-calculator]] show exactly how close. For carrying the measurement errors through, see [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

When an ice cap advances over tundra, it kills the moss and small plants in its path and can preserve them, rooted where they grew, for tens of thousands of years. When the ice retreats, the plants appear at its edge. Their radiocarbon age is the time since they died, which is the last time that spot was free of ice. The authors call it a kill date.

The team collected **48 plants** within a metre of the ice edge at **30 ice caps** on eastern Baffin Island, most of them only 1 to 2 km² and all inside a region of 170 by 70 km, mostly at 1,380 to 1,600 m above sea level. Every one dated to **40,000 to more than 50,000 radiocarbon years**, at or beyond the range of the method. To test whether the ground had been briefly uncovered at some point since, they also measured carbon-14 made inside the rock by cosmic rays (in situ ¹⁴C) at nine sites, and modelled how much of it would build up during any ice-free spell. Holocene exposure, in the last 11,700 years, turned out unlikely at all but one site.

Their conclusion: these spots have been under ice since before the last ice age reached its peak, so the summer warmth now uncovering them is greater than any century in about **115,000 years**, back to the last interglacial.

## Checked with the tools

Two things decide how far such a claim can be trusted: how much carbon-14 is left to measure, and what a radiocarbon age means in calendar years. Both are recomputations with the tools' maths. The laboratory background and the example errors below are **our assumptions**, chosen to be typical; the paper's own sample-by-sample errors are in its table, which we did not copy.

**Step 1: there is almost nothing left to count.** Radiocarbon ages are, by convention, worked out with the Libby half-life of 5,568 years. With it, the half-life solver says a plant 40,000 radiocarbon years old keeps **0.688 %** of its original carbon-14, after 7.18 half-lives; at 45,000 years **0.369 %**, and at 50,000 years **0.198 %**, after nearly 9 half-lives. A careful laboratory's background, carbon-14 that creeps in from chemistry and the instrument itself, is often around 0.1 to 0.2 % of modern carbon. At our assumed 0.2 %, the solver puts the limit at **49,921 years**; at 0.1 %, **55,489 years**. So "more than 50,000" means the sample was indistinguishable from the blank, which is exactly why the paper reports those dates as limits, and why it is careful with pretreatment: early, less thoroughly cleaned samples from two sites had given ages under 40,000 years, and more cleaning pushed them back above it.

**Step 2: calendar years are older still.** A radiocarbon age is not a calendar age, because the amount of carbon-14 in the air has changed over time. The [[radiocarbon-calibration]] tool converts with the IntCal20 curve, which reaches back to 55,000 calendar years. An example date of 40,000 ± 500 radiocarbon years calibrates to **44,172 to 42,633 cal BP** (95.4 %), median **43,264**; 45,000 ± 800 becomes **48,967 to 45,649 cal BP**, median **47,285** (cal BP means calendar years before 1950). In this part of the curve, calendar ages run 2,000 to 3,500 years older than radiocarbon ages, so the plants are, if anything, older than the headline number.

**Step 3: why a short ice-free spell would still show.** Carbon-14 made in the rock decays with the true half-life, which the paper gives as 5,700 years. The solver shows that after 4,500 years under ice, a common estimate for when Baffin's ice caps regrew in the Holocene, **57.9 %** of any carbon-14 built up during a warm spell is still there; after 12,000 years, **23.2 %**. A site that had been ice-free in the early Holocene would therefore still carry a strong signal, and only one of the nine sites does. After 40,000 years under ice, only **0.77 %** remains, which is why the long burial shows up as such low values.

**Step 4: the plants really are fresh out of the ice.** At the paper's retreat rate of about 10 m a year, a plant found 200 m from the edge has been exposed for only about **20 years**, and the team sampled within 1 m. Fresh plants have no time to be contaminated by new growth, which the paper notes can arrive within 1 to 3 years.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/baffin-island-ice-caps-plants-40000-years-radiocarbon-limit.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `halfLifeSolve()` from `src/lib/sci/decay.ts`, the half-life solver in the [[decay-calculator]], for the carbon-14 left and the background limit, and `calibrate()` from `src/lib/sci/radiocarbon.ts` with the IntCal20 curve in `src/data/intcal20.txt`, the maths behind the [[radiocarbon-calibration]] tool, for the calendar ranges.

The 40 to more than 50 ka range, the 5,700-year half-life, the 10 m a year retreat and the 200 m survival distance come from the paper. The 5,568-year Libby half-life is the radiocarbon convention. The 0.2 % background and the ±500 and ±800 year errors are **our assumptions** for illustration.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks both pieces:

- The IntCal20 calibration is pinned against OxCal 4.4: 3000 ± 30 BP must give two 95 % ranges, the main one 1304 to 1125 BC holding 86 % of the probability, with a median of 1239 BC; the probabilities must add up to 1, and a date near the 55,000-year end of the curve (54,500 ± 300) must be flagged.
- The decay maths is pinned at published half-lives, for example 8 hours leaving 0.914220 of copper-67 and 0.646234 of copper-64.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/radiocarbon-calibration.mjs`: 40,000 ± 500 BP must calibrate to a single 95.4 % range of 44,172 to 42,633 cal BP with a median of 43,264, and the half-life solver must give 0.6877 % left at 40,000 years and reach a 0.2 % background at 49,921 years with the Libby half-life.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Decide whether a sample is worth dating**: the [[decay-calculator]] shows how much carbon-14 remains at the age you expect, against your laboratory's background.
- **Turn kill dates into calendar years**: the [[radiocarbon-calibration]] tool calibrates a list of dates at once and plots each one, so a set of ice-margin plants becomes a timeline of advances.
- **Report limits honestly**: when the solver says the age at your background is below your sample's apparent age, report it as "more than", as this paper does.
- **Propagate errors** from the laboratory value to the age with the [[uncertainty-calculator]].
- **Teach how glaciers keep records**: one page shows why 40,000 years is close to the edge of the method, and why calendar ages are older still.

## The fine print

- We worked from the article text; the paper's table of individual dates and errors was not used, and our example errors are assumptions, not the paper's values.
- Laboratory backgrounds vary with the instrument and the cleaning; 0.1 to 0.2 % of modern carbon is a typical range, not this team's stated value.
- IntCal20 ends at 55,000 cal BP. Dates near the radiocarbon limit calibrate to wide ranges, and the tool says so when a date runs off the end of the curve.
- The in situ ¹⁴C numbers here only show how fast a signal decays under ice; the paper's own model also accounts for the production rate at each site and for partial shielding by thin ice.
- The 2026 *Science* study on the Barnes Ice Cap is not openly licensed; we used only facts stated in its abstract, as news context.
