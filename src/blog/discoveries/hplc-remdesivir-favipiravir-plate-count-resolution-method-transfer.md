Two antiviral drugs, remdesivir and favipiravir, are sometimes packed into the same capsule, and a quality control laboratory needs one assay that measures both. A 2025 paper in *PLOS ONE* develops and validates such an assay: a plain isocratic reversed-phase HPLC method on a 250 mm column, with favipiravir leaving at 11.5 minutes and remdesivir at 20.95 minutes. The numbers in its system suitability table are exactly the kind that fibub's [[chromatography-calculator]] exists to check, and they turn out to describe a separation with enormous room to spare. For the steps on either side, turn peak areas into concentrations with the [[calibration-curve]] tool and judge the validation figures with [[method-validation]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ran a C18 column, 250 x 4.6 mm with 5 µm particles, at 1.0 mL/min with a mixture of acetonitrile, methanol and water (50:350:100 by volume) acidified with phosphoric acid, and watched the detector at 300 nm. Injecting 10 µL of a solution containing 0.08 mg/mL favipiravir and 0.04 mg/mL remdesivir gave two peaks, at **11.5 and 20.95 minutes**.

Their system suitability limits are the usual pharmacopoeial ones: **more than 4,000 theoretical plates** for each peak, **resolution above 2.0**, a **tailing factor no greater than 1.5** and a repeatability of 2 % or better (they report 0.38 % and 0.33 %). The validation is thorough: linear from 0.02 to 0.06 mg/mL for favipiravir and 0.022 to 0.066 mg/mL for remdesivir, mean recoveries of 99.76 % and 99.92 %, and forced degradation that destroys 23.6 % of the drug at 70 °C and 17.9 % in acid. The paper lists one weakness of its own method: the run takes a long time.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the numbers in the paper's text. Nothing is read off its figures.

**Step 1: the dead time and the retention factors.** The paper does not report the dead time t₀, so we estimate it from the column: an interstitial plus pore porosity of 0.68 (**our assumption**, the usual value for a packed C18 column) over a 250 x 4.6 mm tube gives 2.83 mL of mobile phase in the column, which at 1.0 mL/min is **t₀ = 2.83 min**. From there the tool's retention factors are **k₁ = 3.07** for favipiravir and **k₂ = 6.42** for remdesivir, so the **selectivity α = 2.09**. That is a very large selectivity: the column holds remdesivir more than twice as long as favipiravir.

**Step 2: how much resolution 4,000 plates buys.** The paper reports resolution only as "more than 2.0", so we work out what its own efficiency floor implies. Turning N = 4,000 back into a half-height width, W½ = tR√(5.545/N), gives **0.43 min** for the favipiravir peak and **0.78 min** for the remdesivir peak. The tool's European Pharmacopoeia resolution from those widths is **Rs = 9.2**; the more conservative Purnell equation, which assumes both peaks are as wide as the later one, gives **Rs = 7.1**. Either way the pair is separated four to six times more than the method asks for.

**Step 3: the plates this pair actually needs.** Run the Purnell equation backwards for a target resolution of 2.0 at this selectivity and retention factor, and the answer is **315 plates**. A 250 mm column with 5 µm particles will deliver that in its first two centimetres. The method's 4,000-plate limit corresponds to a plate height of **62.5 µm**, or 12.5 particle diameters, where a healthy modern column runs at 2 to 3: in other words, even a badly damaged column would pass this test, and pass it for good reason, because the separation does not depend on efficiency at all.

**Step 4: the long run time, fixed.** Because the separation is so easy, the method's only real cost is time and solvent. The tool's **method transfer** does the geometric scaling of USP <621> from the paper's 250 x 4.6 mm, 5 µm column to a modern 100 x 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm one:

- flow **0.579 mL/min** instead of 1.0, from the diameter squared and the particle ratio;
- injection **0.83 µL** instead of 10 µL, in proportion to column volume;
- every time multiplied by **0.144**, so a 25-minute run (**our assumption** for the run length, since the paper gives only the 20.95 min retention time) becomes **3.6 minutes**;
- solvent per injection **2.08 mL** instead of 25 mL, that is **8.3 %**;
- the length-to-particle-size ratio goes from 50,000 to 55,600, a change of **+11.1 %**, inside the −25 % to +50 % window USP <621> allows without revalidation of the separation.

The resolution does not suffer: the new column has more plates than the old one, and the pair needed 315.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/hplc-remdesivir-favipiravir-plate-count-resolution-method-transfer.mjs` in fibub's public code. It imports `retentionFactor`, `plates`, `resolution`, `purnell`, `platesFor` and `transfer` from `src/lib/sci/chrom.ts`, the maths behind the [[chromatography-calculator]], and prints the values the article quotes.

Inputs from the paper: the column dimensions, the flow, the two retention times, the 4,000-plate limit and the 10 µL injection. **Our assumptions**, clearly separated: the column porosity of 0.68 that sets the dead time, and a 25-minute run length. The new column size (100 x 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm) is our choice of target, not the paper's.

## How we test the maths

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a system suitability table** before you submit it: if the plates needed for your target resolution are a few hundred and your limit is thousands, the limit is not protecting the separation and something else should be.
- **Shorten a validated method honestly.** The transfer card gives the flow, injection and gradient times that keep the chromatogram the same shape, plus the USP L/dp check that says whether the change counts as an adjustment.
- **Estimate what you were not given.** Retention factors need a dead time; the column volume gives a usable one, and the tool shows how much your conclusions move if the porosity is 0.6 or 0.7 instead.
- **Decide where to spend effort.** Figure 2 shows that for a pair with α = 1.05 you need more than 100,000 plates for the same resolution; changing the mobile phase or the column chemistry is then the only sensible move.
- **Teach it.** The same three fields show why a long column is rarely the answer: resolution grows only with the square root of the plates.

## The fine print

- The dead time, and so every retention factor and selectivity here, rests on our porosity assumption. The paper reports no t₀ or peak widths.
- Resolution and plate height come from the method's **minimum** plate count, not from measured widths, so they are the floor of what the method guarantees, not what the instrument printed.
- The Purnell equation assumes both peaks have the same plate count and uses the width of the later one, which is why it gives a lower resolution than the width-based formula.
- A transfer that changes the column chemistry, not just its size, is a change to the method and needs revalidation, whatever the geometry says.
- The forced degradation and recovery figures are quoted as the paper reports them; we did not recompute them.
