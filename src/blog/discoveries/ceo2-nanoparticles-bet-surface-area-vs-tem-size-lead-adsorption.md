Lead in drinking water harms the brain and kidneys, and cheap solids that grab lead ions out of water are an active field. A 2026 paper in *Separations* makes **cerium dioxide (ceria, CeO₂) nanoparticles** by a simple precipitation and shows they take up lead well. Two of its numbers seem to disagree: electron microscopy shows particles of about 100 nm, while gas adsorption gives a surface area of 118.96 m² per gram, far more than 100 nm particles could have. This article shows how the [[nanoparticle-calculator]] settles that in seconds, and what the surface area means for the lead uptake. To check the crystal structure from the same paper, use the [[xrd-peak-calculator]]; to measure sizes from your own microscope images, the [[particle-size-image|particle size from images tool]].

## The paper in brief

The authors precipitated cerium hydroxide from cerium nitrate with ammonia, dried it and heated it at 500 °C for three hours to make ceria. X-ray diffraction (XRD) shows the six main lines of the cubic **fluorite** structure that CeO₂ is known for. Electron micrographs give an average particle size of about 100 nm. Nitrogen adsorption gives a **BET surface area** (the area per gram found from how much nitrogen gas sticks to the powder at low temperature) of 118.96 m²/g and a pore volume of about 0.40 cm³/g, with pores a few nanometres across.

In lead tests, 0.01 g of the powder in 50 mL of solution removed up to 80 % of the lead at pH 6, the best pH. The highest uptake they measured was **192 mg of lead per gram**. The uptake over time followed a pseudo-second-order rate law, which the authors read as chemical binding to the surface, and a Langmuir isotherm (a model for a single layer of adsorbed ions) fitted to their data gives a theoretical capacity of 714 mg/g.

## Checked with the tools

These are **recomputations** from values stated in the paper's text, with one input of our own: the bulk density of ceria, **7.22 g/cm³ (our assumption, a handbook value)**, because the paper does not give one.

**Step 1: what size does the surface area mean?** Smooth, solid spheres of diameter d and density ρ have a specific surface area of 6/(ρd). Turned round, 118.96 m²/g at 7.22 g/cm³ is the surface of spheres just **6.99 nm** across. Separate 100 nm spheres would have only **8.31 m²/g**, about fourteen times less (Figure 1). So the 100 nm objects in the micrographs cannot be solid single grains. The simplest picture that fits both numbers is that each 100 nm particle is a porous cluster of grains about 7 nm across, with gas reaching most of their surface.

**Step 2: does the structure check out?** The XRD peak calculator's cubic indexer, given the paper's six rounded peak positions as a face-centred cubic list (with a 2 % tolerance, because the positions are rounded to the nearest half degree), indexes all six as (111), (200), (220), (311), (222) and (400) and returns a lattice parameter of **5.441 Å**, 0.55 % above the reference 5.411 Å for ceria. That is as close as peaks rounded to 0.5° allow, so the phase is fluorite CeO₂ as the authors say.

**Step 3: how much of the oxide is surface?** The calculator's sphere model counts formula units (one Ce and two O) and those within one unit diameter (0.42 nm) of the surface. A 7 nm grain holds about 4,500 units, **32 %** of them at the surface; a solid 100 nm particle would hold 13 million, only **2.5 %** at the surface (Figure 2). By volume, one 100 nm cluster could hold up to about 2,900 grains of 7 nm.

**Step 4: the pores and the lead.** Two quick checks of our own on the paper's numbers, done in the article's script:

- If the pore volume (0.3962 cm³/g) sat in straight cylindrical pores whose walls carry all 118.96 m²/g, their diameter would be 4V/S = **13.3 nm**, a radius of 6.7 nm, close to the paper's DFT mean pore radius of 6.28 nm. The surface area and pore numbers hang together. The same pore volume means the clusters are about **74 % empty space** at bulk density.
- Spread over 118.96 m²/g, the highest measured uptake, 192 mg/g, is **4.7 lead ions per nm²**. A flat ceria (111) face has about 7.9 cerium atoms per nm², so the measured uptake is a plausible single layer. The Langmuir capacity of 714 mg/g would need **17.5 ions per nm²**, more than twice the number of surface metal sites, so it is best read as a fitting extrapolation, as the authors themselves note, not as a capacity to design with.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/ceo2-nanoparticles-bet-surface-area-vs-tem-size-lead-adsorption.mjs` imports `specificSurface`, `betDiameter`, `sphereAtoms` and `atomVolume` from `src/lib/sci/nano.ts`, the maths behind the Nanoparticle calculator, and `indexCubic` and `dFromTwoTheta` from `src/lib/sci/xrd.ts`, the maths behind the XRD peak calculator. It draws Figure 1 from 6/(ρd) and Figure 2 from the sphere surface-shell model, and prints every number in this article.

Inputs from the paper: the BET area (118.96 m²/g), the ~100 nm microscopy size, the pore volume (0.3962 cm³/g), the six XRD peak positions, and the lead uptakes (192 and 714.28 mg/g). Our assumptions: bulk density 7.22 g/cm³, molar mass 172.11 g/mol, reference lattice parameter 5.411 Å, and smooth spheres and straight cylindrical pores as the shapes. Real nanoceria can be slightly less dense than bulk; at 6.5 g/cm³ the equivalent size would be 7.76 nm, which changes nothing in the story.

## How we test the maths

The Nanoparticle calculator's maths is pinned in `tests/sci-tools/nanoparticle-calculator.mjs`, run on every change with `npm test`. Among its checks:

- The atom count of a 20 nm gold sphere (19.3 g/cm³) matches an independent NumPy calculation, 247,174.86 atoms, to 13 digits.
- The specific surface of 20 nm gold spheres is 15.544 m²/g, and `betDiameter` turns any specific surface back into the diameter it came from (37 nm silica round trip).
- Magic-number clusters (13, 55, 147, 309, 561 atoms) match clusters built atom by atom in NumPy.
- **New with this article:** 118.96 m²/g at 7.22 g/cm³ gives 6.9858 nm, and 100 nm ceria spheres give 8.3102 m²/g with 2.52 % of units at the surface.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[nanoparticle-calculator|nanoparticle surface area calculator]].
2. In section 1, set **Material preset** to "My own numbers", then type **Density ρ (g/cm³)** 7.22 and **Molar mass M (g/mol)** 172.11. Leave **Atom diameter** blank.
3. Type **Particle diameter d (nm)** 100. The headline reads **8.310 m²/g, 2.52 % of atoms on the surface**, and **Atoms per particle** shows 1.323 × 10⁷.
4. Go to section 4, **BET-equivalent diameter**, and type **Measured specific surface area (m²/g)** 118.96. You should see a **BET-equivalent diameter** of **6.986 nm** and **dBET ÷ d above** of 0.0699, flagged as "more surface than smooth spheres of size d".
5. Back in section 1, change the diameter to 7: the specific surface becomes 118.7 m²/g, almost exactly the paper's BET value.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-checking a characterisation table.** Put the BET area and the microscope size side by side in the Nanoparticle calculator: a ratio far from 1 tells you about aggregation, porosity or roughness before a reviewer does.
- **Estimating grain size when there is no Scherrer analysis.** The BET-equivalent diameter is a quick cross-check on crystallite sizes from peak widths in the XRD peak calculator.
- **Normalising adsorption per surface site.** Dividing an uptake in mg/g by the surface area gives ions per nm², which can be compared with the number of metal sites on a crystal face to judge whether a claimed capacity is physically possible.
- **Planning doses.** The calculator's concentration section turns mg/mL of powder into particles per mL and total surface per litre, useful when comparing adsorbents or catalysts at equal surface rather than equal mass.
- **Teaching.** Sliding the diameter from 1 to 1000 nm shows students why nanomaterials are surface materials.

## The fine print

- The paper's sizes are approximate (the microscopy average is read from a histogram), and BET areas carry a few per cent uncertainty. The 7 nm result is an equivalent size for smooth spheres, not a direct measurement of grains; rough or porous grains would be somewhat larger.
- A small inconsistency for readers to check in the source: removing 80 % of 45 mg/L lead from 50 mL with 0.01 g of powder corresponds to 180 mg/g, while the pH section reports 60 mg/g for that condition. Our checks above use only the 192 mg/g maximum.
- We did not refit the isotherm or the kinetics: fibub has no adsorption isotherm fitter yet, and the paper's raw uptake data are in figures.
- Lead removal results come from lab solutions. They say nothing about any treatment product for drinking water; for that, check your local water authority.
