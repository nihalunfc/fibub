Farm robots, lawn mowers and delivery carts often follow a list of GPS points. Classic path followers such as Pure Pursuit and the Stanley method work, but their gains are usually tuned by trial and error. A 2026 paper in *Sensors* instead picks the gains of a simple proportional controller by **pole placement**: choose where the closed loop's poles (the roots that set how fast and how smoothly it responds) should sit, then solve for the gains that put them there. The paper prints its model, its poles and its gain, so the [[pole-placement]] tool can redo the design in seconds and show the response those numbers imply. For the same loop as a PID, try the [[pid-simulator]]; to smooth noisy GPS fixes first, try the [[kalman-filter-playground]].

## The paper in brief

The authors built a small wheeled robot with a centimetre-grade GPS receiver (real-time kinematic, RTK) and a 100 Hz control loop. It drives at a steady **0.2 m/s** along waypoints **6 m** apart and slows its turning at sharp corners. They identified a simple linear model from driving data and wrote it in state-space form, x′ = Ax + Bu, with **A = [−0.0010, 0; 1, 0]** and **B = [1; 0]**: the input drives a rate, and the second state is the integral of the first, like turning rate and heading. With state feedback u = −Kx they placed the poles at **−0.7505 ± 0.9679j** and report the gain **K = [1.5, 1.5]**.

On the real robot the path stayed within about **3 cm** (root-mean-square error) of the planned route, against about 8 cm at the turn for an earlier state-flow method; the Stanley controller oscillated at corners. The paper does not give a damping ratio, overshoot or settling time for its design.

## Checked with the tools

We put the paper's A, B and poles into the Pole placement tool's maths. This is a **recomputation** from the paper's printed numbers; for the step response we also need an output, and we took the second state (C = [0, 1]) as the output, **our assumption**, since the paper does not print C.

**Step 1: can the poles be placed at all?** The open-loop poles are **0** and **−0.001**, one pure integrator and one barely damped mode, so the robot alone does not hold a heading. The controllability matrix [B, AB] has **rank 2 of 2**, so a single input can move both poles anywhere.

**Step 2: the gain.** Ackermann's formula with the paper's poles gives **K = [1.5000, 1.5001]**, matching the paper's [1.5, 1.5] to four figures. Going the other way, K = [1.5, 1.5] gives the characteristic polynomial s² + 1.501 s + 1.5, whose roots are **−0.7505 ± 0.96786j**: the paper's poles, rounded. The design is self-consistent.

**Step 3: what the poles mean.** That polynomial has a natural frequency of **1.225 rad/s** and a damping ratio of **0.613**, a well-damped choice (0.7 is a common target). The tool's step response (Figure 1) overshoots by **8.75 %**, rises from 10 to 90 % in **1.54 s** and settles within 2 % after **4.9 s**. The textbook formula for overshoot, exp(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)), gives the same **8.75 %**, because this loop is an exact second-order system.

**Step 4: is it fast enough for the course?** At 0.2 m/s the robot moves only about **0.98 m** while the heading settles, and it takes **30 s** to drive between waypoints 6 m apart, six times the settling time. The 100 Hz loop is also more than **500 times** faster than the poles' natural frequency (in rad/s), so designing in continuous time, as the paper does, is safe.

**Step 5: why 1.5?** With the same gain k on both states the closed loop is s² + (k + 0.001)s + k. Figure 2 traces the poles as k rises: they stay a complex pair until k is about 4, moving left and becoming better damped. Smaller gains overshoot more and settle slower (k = 0.5: **30.4 %** overshoot, **15.5 s** to settle; k = 1: **16.3 %**, **8.1 s**), larger ones settle faster (k = 2: **4.3 %**, **4.3 s**; k = 3: **0.4 %**, **2.6 s**) but ask more of the motors and amplify GPS noise. k = 1.5 sits in the middle.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/gps-waypoint-following-robot-pole-placement-state-feedback-gains.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `acker`, `eig`, `charPoly`, `ctrb`, `rank`, `dcGain`, `step` and `stepInfo` from `src/lib/sci/eng-statespace.ts`, the maths behind the [[pole-placement]] tool.

A, B, the poles, the gain, the 100 Hz loop, the 0.2 m/s speed and the 6 m spacing come from the paper's text. The output matrix C = [0, 1], the reference scaling (so the output settles at 1) and the range of other gains in the figures are **our choices**.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/pole-placement.mjs`, which pins the tool against python-control, NumPy and SciPy:

- For the tool's three-state example with poles −2 ± 2j and −10, Ackermann's gain must be **K = [20, 9.8125, 1.375]**, as python-control's `acker` and `place` give, and the closed-loop eigenvalues must come back as the chosen poles.
- The same example's step response must match SciPy at 0, 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 s, with an overshoot of **4.10 %** as `step_info` reports.
- Four random systems of 2 to 5 states must match python-control's gains and observer gains to about one part in a million.
- New with this article: the robot model with the paper's poles must give K = [1.5, 1.50008] and, with K = [1.5, 1.5], an overshoot of 8.75 % and a settling time of 4.9 s.

## Check it yourself

On the [[pole-placement]] page:

1. In *A (n × n)* type two rows: **-0.001 0** and **1 0**. In *B (n × inputs)* type **1** and **0** on two rows. In *C (outputs × n)* type **0 1**, and leave *D* empty.
2. The *Analysis* card shows *Open-loop eigenvalues* **0, −0.001**, *Controllable* **yes** (rank 2 of 2) and *Observable* **yes**.
3. In *Closed-loop poles for A − BK* type **-0.7505±0.9679j**.
4. The *Pole placement* card shows **K = [1.5, 1.5001]**, the paper's gain, and *Reference gain N* **1.5001**.
5. Below the step response read *Overshoot* **8.75 %**, *Rise time (10–90 %)* **1.54 s** and *Settling time (2 %)* **4.9 s**.
6. Try **-1.5±0.9679j** in the poles box: the gain rises to K = [2.999, 3.1868], the overshoot drops to 0.77 % and the settling time to 2.3 s. Faster poles always cost bigger gains.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recheck a published design:** put a paper's A, B and poles into the [[pole-placement]] tool and see if its gains follow, as here.
- **Turn specs into poles:** pick an overshoot and settling time, work out ζ and the natural frequency, and let the tool give the gains; compare with a PID tuned in the [[pid-simulator]].
- **Plan an estimator:** the same page gives an observer gain L for estimating states you cannot measure; for noisy GPS, try a filter in the [[kalman-filter-playground]].
- **Plan the route:** waypoints for a robot like this one can come from a grid search in the [[path-planner]].
- **Teach state feedback:** a two-state model with printed numbers is an ideal classroom example of controllability, Ackermann's formula and second-order response.

## The fine print

- We recomputed the controller from the paper's printed model; we cannot check the measured 3 cm path error, which depends on the robot, the GPS and the ground.
- The output matrix C is our assumption. It does not change K or the poles, only which state the step response shows.
- The paper's model is linear and continuous; the real robot turns at corners with a separate rule, which we did not model.
- The gain is for the authors' identified model and units; another robot needs its own model.
