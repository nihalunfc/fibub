The stars of the **S-cluster** swing around the four-million-solar-mass black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, and the closest of them move fast enough that Newton's gravity is not good enough. Each time such a star passes its closest point, the whole ellipse turns a little: the **pericentre advance**, the same effect that shifts Mercury's orbit by 43 arcseconds a century, but millions of times stronger here. A 2026 paper in *Universe* uses these orbits as test cases for relativistic trajectory work and tabulates the advance for several of them. This article rebuilds those numbers with the [[schwarzschild-orbits|Schwarzschild orbit plotter]], and finds they pin the black hole's mass more tightly than the paper itself says. For the rest of the strong-gravity toolbox, try the [[gw-chirp|gravitational wave chirp]], the [[cosmology-calculator]] and the small, everyday version of the same physics in the [[gps-relativity|GPS relativity explainer]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ask an unusual question: if you could fly a probe from one S-star to another, how badly would a Newtonian plan miss? They integrate timelike geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric with a high-order Runge-Kutta scheme (DOP853) and compare them with classical two-body (Lambert) transfers. Their headline number is a **relativistic miss distance** of at least about 1.4 × 10⁷ km for a transfer between S62 and S4714, with most of the solution space far worse, and a relativistic-minus-Newtonian speed difference peaking at 1231 m/s. They also report accumulated time dilation of 5.81 hours over a roughly 2000-day transfer, and check the whole machinery on an Earth-to-Mars control case, where the integration error stays under 1.5 × 10⁵ km.

Underneath that sits the relativistic bookkeeping of the orbits themselves. The paper lists the orbit sizes it uses (for example S62 at a semi-major axis of 740.08 au with eccentricity 0.976, and S4714 at 841.35 au with eccentricity 0.985) and tabulates the pericentre advance per orbit: about **75 arcminutes for S62**, **104 arcminutes for S4714** and **12 arcminutes for S2**, the star whose precession was actually measured by the GRAVITY instrument in 2020. Those three numbers are what a reader can check, and they are what we check here.

## Checked with the tools

The orbit plotter works in geometric units: lengths in **M = GM/c²**, which is half the Schwarzschild radius and 1.4766 km per solar mass. Give it a closest distance r_p and a farthest distance r_a in those units and it returns the exact advance from one pericentre to the next, worked out in closed form from the complete elliptic integral K, together with the orbit's energy and angular momentum. So the recipe is simple: turn each star's semi-major axis and eccentricity into r_p and r_a for an assumed black hole mass, and read off the advance.

**Step 1: the mass matters, and the paper does not state it.** The readable text of the paper gives the orbits and the advances but never the black hole mass used. That is a problem, because the advance is very nearly proportional to the mass. With the GRAVITY Collaboration's 4.297 × 10⁶ M☉ the tool gives **78.8′ for S62, 110.6′ for S4714 and 12.4′ for S2**: every one of them about 5 % above the paper. Figure 1 shows why. Each star's advance rises almost linearly with the assumed mass, and the three published values are crossed at almost the same place.

**Step 2: the three values agree on one mass.** Solving each one separately, S2's 12′ wants 4.16 × 10⁶ M☉, S62's 75′ wants 4.09 × 10⁶ and S4714's 104′ wants 4.04 × 10⁶. The spread is only what you would expect from numbers rounded to whole arcminutes, and the middle of it is **4.1 × 10⁶ M☉**. That is a real check, not a coincidence: the three stars differ by a factor of nine in their advance and by a factor of nine in how close they come, so a single mass reproducing all three means the paper's relativistic bookkeeping is self-consistent. At 4.1 × 10⁶ M☉ the tool gives 11.84′, 75.13′ and 105.49′.

The pericentres in the last column are worth a glance on their own: the paper describes S62 and S4714 as coming in to about 2 × 10² Schwarzschild radii, and the tool makes that 219 and 156. The two wide stars in the paper's "outer cluster", S22 and S91, turn their ellipses by less than a third of an arcminute per orbit, which is why they make a fair Newtonian test case and the close pair does not.

**Step 3: the textbook formula is good enough here, and the tool says by how much.** The weak-field formula taught in every relativity course, 6πM/p with p the semi-latus rectum, is an approximation. The plotter prints it next to the exact value. For S4714, the star that comes closest, the exact result is only **0.77 % larger**; for S62 0.55 %, and for S2 0.086 %. Figure 2 shows the gap as a function of how close the orbit comes: it stays under a per cent outside about 100 Schwarzschild radii and only becomes serious inside about 20. In other words, nothing in this paper's precession table needed the exact geodesic, but a star coming ten times closer would.

**Step 4: the rest of the orbit.** For S4714 at 4.1 × 10⁶ M☉ the tool also returns an energy per unit mass of E = 0.999976 (a binding energy of 0.0024 % of the rest mass), an angular momentum of L = 24.96 Mc, and an angle from one pericentre to the next of 361.76° rather than 360°. S4714's pericentre, at 12.6 au, is about 52 times further out than that innermost orbit.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/s-cluster-stars-schwarzschild-pericentre-precession-sgr-a.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `bound`, `circular` and `lightRay` from `src/lib/sci/phgr.ts`, the maths behind the [[schwarzschild-orbits|Schwarzschild orbit plotter]], and nothing else: no curve is typed by hand.

The semi-major axes and eccentricities of S62, S4714, S22 and S91, and the three published advances, come from the paper's text and appendix tables. S2's orbit (1020 au, eccentricity 0.885) and the 4.297 × 10⁶ M☉ comparison mass come from the GRAVITY Collaboration's 2022 paper and are **our addition**, because the paper under discussion gives neither. The 4.1 × 10⁶ M☉ figure is **our result**, read off Figure 1. Converting au to M uses GM☉/c² = 1476.625 m and 1 au = 1.495978707 × 10¹¹ m.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/schwarzschild-orbits.mjs`, which pins this maths against independent integrations:

- For an orbit between 10M and 30M, the closed form must give E = 0.9763152613, L = 4.375949745 Mc and an advance of 1.8472766562 radians, matching a SciPy `solve_ivp` integration of the orbit equation at a relative tolerance of 10⁻¹².
- At the innermost stable circular orbit the energy must be exactly √(8/9) and the angular momentum √12 Mc, and a circular orbit at 5.9M must be flagged unstable.
- A light ray with impact parameter 6M must turn around at 4.453363194M and bend by 1.719388310 radians, as SciPy's `quad` gives, and any ray inside 3√3 M must be captured.
- New with this article: S4714's orbit around a 4.1 × 10⁶ M☉ black hole, r_p = 311.845M and r_a = 41 267.5M, must give an advance of 105.49 arcminutes, a weak-field estimate of 104.68 arcminutes, E = 0.99997595 and L = 24.9601 Mc.

## Check it yourself

On the [[schwarzschild-orbits|Schwarzschild orbit plotter]], in the *Black hole and orbit* card:

1. Type **4100000** in *Mass (solar masses)* and leave *Follow* on **A massive particle (orbit)**.
2. For S4714, type **311.845** in *Closest distance r_p (in M)* and **41267.5** in *Farthest distance r_a (in M)*. Set *Turns to draw* to **3**.
3. The headline reads **Periapsis advances 1.758° per orbit**, which is 105.5 arcminutes, and *Advance per orbit* adds that one pericentre to the next is 361.76° while the weak-field formula 6πM/(a(1 − e²)) gives 1.745°.
4. *Shape* shows **e = 0.985** with a semi-latus rectum of 619M, and *Closest and farthest* reads **12.62 au and 1670 au**: the paper's own orbit, back again.
5. For S2, change r_p to **2907** and r_a to **47502**. The advance falls to **0.1973°**, or 11.8 arcminutes.
6. Scroll to *Key radii for this mass*: the ISCO line reads 3.632 × 10⁷ km with a period of about 31 minutes. Now put **4297000** in the mass box and watch every advance rise by about 5 %.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published precession value** in one minute, and work backwards to the mass or the orbit the authors must have used, as we did here.
- **Decide whether the weak-field formula is enough** for your orbit, instead of assuming: the tool prints the exact value and 6πM/p side by side.
- **Convert between observers' units and geometric units.** Giving a mass in solar masses turns every radius into kilometres and au, which is where most sign-of-the-times errors creep in.
- **Teach the effect.** Draw the same orbit with three and then ten turns and the rosette appears; the effective-potential panel shows why the turning points sit where they do.
- **Sanity-check a numerical integrator** before trusting it on a hard problem, by running it against the closed-form advance for a few test orbits, which is exactly what fibub's own tests do.

## The fine print

- We check the paper's **orbital and precession bookkeeping**, not its transfer-trajectory results. The miss distances, the 1231 m/s speed difference and the time-dilation figures come from the authors' own integrations and are outside what any fibub tool can redo.
- The black hole mass of 4.1 × 10⁶ M☉ is **our inference** from the paper's three advance values, not a statement in the paper.
- S2's orbital elements are not in the paper's readable text; we used published GRAVITY values, so the 11.84′ for S2 is a comparison with the literature rather than a reproduction of the paper.
- The Schwarzschild metric ignores the black hole's spin. For Sgr A* the spin's effect on the pericentre advance of these stars is far smaller than the mass uncertainty, but it is not zero.
- The paper's appendix tables were only partly readable through our reader, so we used the advance values quoted in its text and summary tables and did not attempt its per-orbit integrated rates.
