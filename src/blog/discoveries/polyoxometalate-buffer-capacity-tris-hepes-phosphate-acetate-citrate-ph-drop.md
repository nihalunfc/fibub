A buffer is a chemical shock absorber: it keeps the pH steady when small amounts of acid or base arrive. When you are studying something that releases acid as it dissolves, the shock absorber has to be big enough. A 2024 paper in *Frontiers in Chemical Biology* studied how two polyoxometalates (large metal-oxide cluster anions, here tungsten-based) break down at different concentrations and in different buffers, and it reports pH drops of up to 2.5 and up to 5 units in buffers that were supposed to hold the pH. The [[buffer-recipe-calculator]] works out how much capacity a buffer really has at a given pH, so we check the paper's choices and its drops. To trace what happens as acid goes in, see the [[titration-curve]] tool; for stock solutions and dilutions the [[solution-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors prepared 0.1 M acetate, citrate, phosphate, Tris-HCl and HEPES buffers from pH 2 to 8, dissolved a Keggin-type polyoxotungstate (PW12) and a Wells-Dawson-type one (P2W18) at 3 mM and 15 mM, and followed their breakdown by phosphorus NMR, after 24 hours at 37 °C. They find that higher concentrations are more stable near neutral and alkaline pH, and that buffer choice and added sodium nitrate also matter. They note that phosphate does not buffer between pH 3.5 and 5.5 (it was tested there only to compare with earlier work), that Tris at pH 7 to 8 has poor capacity, and that dissolving the larger amounts makes the solutions more acidic than the starting buffer: by up to 2.5 pH units for 15 mM P2W18 from pH 8 and up to 5 units for PW12 in Tris-HCl.

## Checked with the tools

**Buffer capacity at the paper's pH values.** Buffer capacity β is how many millimoles of strong acid or base shift one litre of the buffer by one pH unit. A single acid/base pair has its maximum, 57.6 mM per pH unit at 0.1 M, exactly at its pKa′, and falls fast beyond one unit either side. The tool gives these shares of that maximum at 25 °C:

| Buffer (0.1 M) | pH | Capacity, share of one pair's maximum |
|---|---|---|
| Phosphate | 3.5 / 4.5 / 5.5 | 15 % / 3 % / 16 % (almost none) |
| Phosphate | 7 / 8 | 96 % / 22 % |
| Tris | 7 / 7.5 / 8 | 25 % / 60 % / 97 % |
| HEPES | 7 / 7.5 / 8 | 79 % / 99 % / 64 % |
| Acetate | 4 / 5 / 5.5 | 56 % / 86 % / 43 % |
| Citrate | 3 / 4 / 5 / 6 / 6.5 | 117 % / 125 % / 118 % / 98 % / 52 % (three overlapping pKa values) |

This matches the paper's text: phosphate at pH 4.5 has 3 % of the capacity of a good buffer, and Tris at pH 7 has a quarter, which is what "poor capacity" means numerically. The paper cites a phosphate pKa of 6.86; the tool starts from 7.2 at zero ionic strength and gets an apparent pKa′ of **6.81** at 0.1 M, close to the paper's value. At 0.1 M the ionic strength itself shifts the pKa, which is why a recipe from a 0.01 M table does not carry over.

**Why Tris breaks first.** We took each buffer at pH 8.0 and added strong acid, to represent acid released when an acidic polyoxometalate dissolves. A 0.1 M Tris buffer is half protonated already, so it has only about 43 mM of base to absorb acid. The acid needed to reach pH 3 is **43.3 mM for Tris**, 81.2 mM for HEPES and 105 mM for phosphate. Our assumption: PW12 was added as the free acid H₃PW12O40, which carries three protons per formula unit (the text we saw does not say). Then 15 mM PW12 releases 45 mM of protons, just above Tris's 43 mM, and the pH falls to **2.63**, a drop of 5.4 units, in line with the reported "up to 5 units". The same 45 mM leaves HEPES at pH 7.13 and phosphate at 6.79. For 2.5 units in Tris (pH 5.5) about 41.8 mM of acid is needed, which fits the order of magnitude of the other reported drop, though we cannot tell how many protons the P2W18 salt released.

**Temperature.** Samples were measured at 25 °C but kept at 37 °C for a day. A Tris buffer set to pH 8.0 at 25 °C sits at **7.64** at 37 °C, HEPES at 7.82 and phosphate at 7.96, so Tris-buffered samples were more acidic during incubation than the pH on the label says.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/polyoxometalate-buffer-capacity-tris-hepes-phosphate-acetate-citrate-ph-drop.mjs` imports `BUFFERS`, `oneForm`, `pHOf`, `pHAtOtherT`, `pKaApparent` and `betaMaxSingle` from `src/lib/sci/buffer.ts`, the maths of the Buffer recipe calculator. The buffers, concentration (0.1 M) and pH values come from the paper. The pKa values are the tool's own (they differ slightly from the paper's cited ones, which we list above). The amount of acid released by the polyoxometalate is **our assumption**: pH after acid is found by removing that much base equivalent from the buffer's charge balance (with chloride added as counter-ion) and solving for the pH with `pHOf`.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/buffer-recipe-calculator.mjs` is new with this article. It pins the tool to the numbers above: Tris at 0.1 M and pH 8.0 has a capacity of 56.1 mM per pH unit and needs 58.0 mL of 1 M HCl per litre with 12.11 g of Tris base, moves to pH 7.64 at 37 °C, falls to pH 2.63 with 45 mM of added acid and 3.0 at 43.3 mM; phosphate at 0.1 M has pKa′ of 6.81, and at pH 4.5 only 3 % of the maximum capacity. The older `tests/sci.mjs` checks that every buffer form has a molar mass. Both run on every change with `node tests/sci.mjs`.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[buffer-recipe-calculator]] and choose **Buffer** Tris. Set **Target pH** to 8.0, **Concentration (mM)** to 100, **Temperature when you set the pH (°C)** to 25 and **Temperature you will use it at (°C)** to 37.
2. Choose "Weigh one form, then add strong acid or base" and **Form to weigh** "Tris base". You should read about 12.11 g of Tris base and about 58 mL of 1 M HCl per litre.
3. Read the pH at 37 °C: about 7.64. The **Buffer capacity β** reads about 56.1 mM per pH unit, 97 % of the most a 100 mM buffer gives.
4. Now choose **Buffer** Phosphate (sodium), **Target pH** 4.5, and read the warning that the pH is more than one unit from the nearest pKa′ (6.88); the capacity is only about 1.8 mM per pH unit.

## What researchers can use it for

- Choosing a buffer for a sample that releases acid: compare the capacity at your pH with the acid you expect, before running the experiment.
- Reporting the pH properly: set it at the temperature of use, as the tool's second temperature box shows.
- Sanity-checking "stability at pH X" claims: a pH that drifts by units is a sign that the buffer was swamped, not that the chemistry changed.
- Planning a titration: see how the buffer's own capacity competes with your sample using the [[titration-curve]] tool.
- Teaching: how far from the pKa a buffer still works, with real chemical numbers.

## The fine print

This checks buffer capacity and the pH response to added acid from the paper's printed buffer conditions. It does not test the NMR speciation results, which depend on the chemistry of the polyoxometalates. The amount of acid each polyoxometalate released is our assumption; the paper's own explanation of its pH drops may differ (the drops also depend on hydrolysis and on which salts were used). The paper itself points out an inconsistency in the pH of the sodium nitrate experiments with Tris and HEPES (pH 7 in the text, pH 8 in two tables). Education, not a lab recipe: always check the final pH with a calibrated meter.
