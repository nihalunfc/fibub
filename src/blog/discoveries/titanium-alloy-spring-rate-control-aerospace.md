Replacing a steel spring with a titanium one looks like a straight swap: same job, half the weight. It is not, because titanium's shear modulus is about half steel's, so every coil has to be redrawn — and then the stiffness has to land on a number the aircraft was designed around. A 2024 paper in *Aerospace* does that job for a large spring, 616 mm long, and then does something unusual: it tunes the finished spring's **rate** by changing the ageing heat treatment. Every step is closed-form spring arithmetic, so the [[spring-designer|Spring designer]] can follow all of it. For the material side, see [[stress-strain-analyser]] and [[fatigue-sn-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The spring being replaced is steel: a **12 mm** wire wound to a **66 mm** mean coil diameter, **26 active** and 28 total coils, **616 mm** free length, a rate of **28.0 to 28.8 N/mm** and a mass of about **5.1 kg**. It works compressed to a **341 mm** length.

The titanium replacement has to match the middle of that rate band, **28.4 N/mm**, with a shear modulus of only **40 GPa** against steel's 80. The authors trade coils for wire: **18 active coils** instead of 26, and a **13.0 mm** wire instead of 12, with the mean diameter set by D = 78 − d so the spring still fits its housing. That gives a shear stress of **780 MPa** at the working length against an 800 MPa limit, and a mass of about **2.6 kg**, over 49 % lighter.

Then comes the interesting part. Titanium's modulus depends on its ageing treatment, so the finished rate does too: aged at **540 °C for 10 h** the spring measured **25.2 N/mm**, 11 % under target and with permanent set; aged at **480 °C for 12 h** it measured **28.32 N/mm**, on target, with no set after 48 h held at the working length.

## Checked with the tools

The Spring designer takes the wire diameter, mean coil diameter, active coils, end type, free length, shear modulus and a load or deflection, and returns the rate, the Wahl stress factor, the working and solid stresses, the solid height, the surge frequency, the mass and a buckling check. We typed both springs in, with the end type **squared and ground** (which matches the paper's "tightened and ground flat over 3/4 coil" and gives 2 extra end coils) and a deflection of **275 mm** (616 − 341).

| Check | Paper | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Steel rate | 28.0 to 28.8 N/mm | 27.50 (G = 79.3 GPa) to 28.68 (G = 82.7 GPa) |
| Steel total coils, mass | 28 coils, about 5.1 kg | 28 coils, **5.15 kg** |
| Titanium rate | 28.4 N/mm (target) | **28.89 N/mm** at G = 40 GPa |
| Titanium total coils, solid height | 20 coils | 20 coils, 260 mm |
| Stress at the 341 mm working length | 780 MPa | **784 MPa** (771 MPa at the paper's own 28.4 N/mm) |
| Titanium mass | about 2.6 kg | **2.61 kg** (4.08 m of wire) |
| Weight saved against steel | over 49 % | **49.3 %** |
| Rate change per 0.1 mm of wire | about 0.9 N/mm | **0.90 N/mm** (28.89 to 29.79) |

Eight independent quantities agree, several to the last digit the paper prints. The one visible gap is the rate itself: the tool's 28.89 N/mm is 1.7 % above the paper's 28.4 N/mm target for the same geometry, which means the paper's design arithmetic used a shear modulus of **39.3 GPa** rather than the 40 GPa it quotes, or a mean coil diameter a fraction larger than 78 − d. Either way it is a rounding, not a disagreement.

**The fourth power is the whole story.** The rate goes as d⁴, so the paper's warning that 0.1 mm of wire is worth 0.9 N/mm — 3 % of the rate — falls straight out of the formula (Figure 1). That is why the paper cannot hit 28.4 N/mm by machining alone: ordinary spring wire tolerance is of that order.

**So they tune the modulus instead, and the numbers close.** The rate is strictly proportional to G, so each measured rate tells you the modulus of the finished spring. Run the tool backwards: **25.2 N/mm implies G = 34.9 GPa** and **28.32 N/mm implies G = 39.2 GPa**. The paper separately measured Young's modulus on bars after the same treatments: about **95 GPa** after 440 °C and **105.5 GPa** at 480 °C, which with a Poisson ratio of 0.33 (**our assumption**) are **35.7** and **39.7 GPa**. Two quite different measurements — a spring's stiffness and a bar's modulus — agree within 2 %, which is strong evidence that the ageing really is working through the elastic modulus and not through set or geometry. Figure 2 puts both on the same line.

**Two things the tool flags that the paper does not discuss.** First, at the **solid** height of 260 mm the spring would see **1015 MPa**, well past the 800 MPa limit, so it must never be compressed solid: the working stress has a margin of only 1.02 on the allowable. Second, the buckling check: with flat parallel ends the usual stability limit is 2.63 D/α, here **342 mm**, and the free length is 616 mm. A spring this slender will buckle unless it is guided over a rod or inside a tube. Neither is a mistake in the paper — an aircraft actuator spring is guided — but both are the sort of thing worth seeing before metal is cut.

**The small test spring.** The paper's high-temperature coupon (6 total coils, 4 active, 144 mm long, 78 mm outside diameter) measured 156.8 N/mm but its wire diameter is not stated. Inverting the rate formula gives **13.54 mm**, so it was wound from the same 13-ish mm stock, as you would expect. Its rate fell 14.4 % at 380 °C while the bar modulus fell 19 %, so the two do not track quite as neatly up there.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/titanium-alloy-spring-rate-control-aerospace.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `compression` (the whole compression-spring result), `rate`, `shear`, `wahl` and `index` from `src/lib/sci/eng-spring.ts`, the maths behind the [[spring-designer|Spring designer]].

From the paper: both springs' wire and coil diameters, coil counts, free length, working length, shear modulus, target rate, stress, masses and the two measured rates after ageing, plus the measured Young's moduli. **Our assumptions:** the squared-and-ground end type, the Poisson ratio of 0.33 used to turn E into G, the 4820 kg/m³ density of Ti-13V-11Cr-3Al and 7850 kg/m³ for the steel, the 800 MPa allowable applied to both, and the flat-plate end condition (α = 0.5) in the buckling check. Nothing is simulated: every figure is closed-form arithmetic on the paper's own numbers.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/spring-designer.mjs`, which pins the formulas:

- The four end types must give the right total coils and solid heights, and a free length below the solid height must be refused.
- A worked compression spring must give the Wahl stress τ = Kw 8FD/πd³ to one part in 10¹⁰, the force and stress at solid, the buckling limit 2.63 D/α and the surge frequency NumPy computes (223.133 Hz).
- The extension and torsion panels must match Castigliano's result for a torsion spring with legs (496.157 N·mm per radian) and the hook bending factor (4C² − C − 1)/4C(C − 1).
- New with this article: the paper's titanium spring (13 mm wire, 65 mm mean coil, 18 active coils, G = 40 GPa, 616 mm free, 275 mm of compression, squared and ground) must give a rate of 28.89 N/mm, 20 total coils, a 260 mm solid height, 784 MPa working stress, 1015 MPa at solid, a 2.61 kg mass at 4820 kg/m³ and a buckling limit of 341.9 mm; and the steel spring it replaces must give 5.15 kg and 28.26 N/mm at G = 81.5 GPa.

## Check it yourself

On the [[spring-designer|Spring designer]] page, with the **Compression** tab chosen:

1. Set *Wire diameter d (mm)* to **13**, *Mean coil diameter D (mm)* to **65**, *Body coils* to **18** and *Ends* to **Squared and ground**.
2. Set *Free length L0 (mm)* to **616**, *Load given as* to **Deflection (mm)** and *Deflection (mm)* to **275**.
3. Set *Shear modulus G (GPa)* to **40**, *Allowed shear stress (MPa)* to **800**, *Density (kg/m³)* to **4820** and *Ends on* to **Flat parallel plates (fixed)**.
4. You should read a rate of about **28.89 N/mm**, a working force of **7944 N**, a shear stress of **784 MPa**, **20** total coils, a **260 mm** solid height, **1015 MPa** at solid and a mass of **2.61 kg** — and a warning that the free length is past the **342 mm** buckling limit.
5. Change *Shear modulus G (GPa)* to **34.9**: the rate falls to **25.2 N/mm**, the spring the paper got from the 540 °C ageing. Put **39.2** and it reads **28.32 N/mm**, the one that passed.
6. For the steel original, set d **12**, D **66**, body coils **26**, G **81.5** and density **7850**: 28.26 N/mm, 28 coils and **5.15 kg**, against the titanium spring's 2.61 kg.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a measured spring rate into a modulus.** Because the rate is exactly proportional to G, a load-deflection test on a finished spring is a modulus measurement — handy when a coupon test is not possible.
- **Check a published spring design** in a minute: rate, stress, solid height, mass and buckling all follow from six numbers, and a mass that matches is a good sign the geometry has been read correctly.
- **Set a wire tolerance from a rate tolerance.** The d⁴ law turns "±0.5 N/mm" into "±0.06 mm of wire", which is the number a drawing needs.
- **Plan a material substitution**: hold the rate and the housing, then see what coil count and wire diameter the new modulus demands before any metal is ordered.
- **Teach the Wahl factor** with a real case: here C = 5, Kw = 1.31, so the inside of the coil carries 31 % more stress than the simple torsion formula suggests.

## The fine print

- Every geometry number is the paper's; the stresses, masses, buckling limit and implied moduli are the tool's arithmetic on them, not the paper's own claims, except where the table says so.
- The Poisson ratio of 0.33 used to convert E to G is our assumption. At 0.31 the two moduli would be about 1.5 % higher and the agreement in Figure 2 slightly worse.
- The paper's design value of 28.4 N/mm cannot be reproduced exactly from the stated geometry and G = 40 GPa; 39.3 GPa reproduces it. We report both.
- The tool treats the spring as a perfect helix with constant pitch and no end-coil effects, no residual stress from setting or shot peening and no creep, all of which matter for a spring held compressed at temperature.
- The paper's stress-relaxation and high-temperature results are measurements we cannot recompute; only their link to the rate through the modulus is checked here.
