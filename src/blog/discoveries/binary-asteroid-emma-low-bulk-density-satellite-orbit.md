A moon is the only cheap way to weigh an asteroid. Watch the satellite go round, measure how wide the orbit is and how long one circuit takes, and Kepler's third law hands you the mass of whatever it is going round — no spacecraft needed. A 2025 paper in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* did exactly that for two large main-belt binaries, (283) Emma and (762) Pulcova, using adaptive-optics images stretching back two decades, and found Emma's bulk density to be **0.9 g cm⁻³**: lighter than water. This article shows that the same two numbers from the paper's abstract are enough for anyone to get the mass and the density back, using the maths behind the [[planet-data-explorer]]. The same law in a different costume powers the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]] and the [[double-star-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

At least 15 % of asteroids wider than 140 km carry a satellite, and the usual story is that an impact shattered the parent and the pieces fell back together as a loose pile of rubble. The authors re-reduced archival high-resolution images of Emma and Pulcova, measured new astrometry of each satellite, and fitted orbits with a model that allows for the primary's lumpy gravity as well as a plain Keplerian ellipse.

For **(283) Emma** they get a satellite orbit of **588 km with a period of 3.41 days** and an eccentricity of 0.11, giving a primary mass of **1.4 ± 0.2 × 10¹⁸ kg**. The primary's equivalent radius is 71 km, so the bulk density comes out at **0.9 ± 0.3 g cm⁻³**. Their orbital **J2** (the measure of how much the gravity field is flattened) is 0.11, clearly below the ≈0.14 the shape model predicts for a uniform interior — their evidence that the inside is not uniform.

For **(762) Pulcova** the new orbit is **738 km and 4.14 days**, against an earlier solution of 703 km and 4.44 days. That change matters: the mass rises to **1.865 ± 0.019 × 10¹⁸ kg** while the measured size shrinks from 149 to **136 km** across, and the density nearly doubles from the previously published 0.9 to **1.4 g cm⁻³**. Across the whole population of large binaries they find a dichotomy, with one group showing a strong correlation between how elongated the primary is and how eccentric its satellite's orbit is.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the values printed in the paper**. No image, catalogue or fit of ours is involved: we put the published semi-major axis and period into `parentMassFromOrbit`, the Kepler's-third-law function behind the Planet data explorer's orbit plot, and the resulting mass and the published size into `derived`, the same function that fills the tool's density, gravity and escape-speed columns.

**Step 1: the masses.** Kepler's third law in the form M = 4π²a³/(G P²) gives **1.385 × 10¹⁸ kg** for Emma and **1.858 × 10¹⁸ kg** for Pulcova. The paper's own values are 1.4 ± 0.2 and 1.865 ± 0.019 × 10¹⁸ kg: Pulcova agrees to **0.4 %**, which is well inside its own quoted 1 % error.

**Step 2: the densities.** Divide by the volume of a sphere of the published equivalent radius and Emma comes out at **924 kg m⁻³**, that is **0.92 g cm⁻³**, against the paper's 0.9 ± 0.3. Take the paper's alternative shape model instead, 133 km across, and the same mass gives **1.13 g cm⁻³** against their 1.1 ± 0.1. Pulcova at 136 km across gives **1.41 g cm⁻³** against their 1.4 ± 0.2. All three match to the digits the paper reports, which is a useful thing to know: a reader can audit a published asteroid density with two numbers and a pocket calculator.

**Step 3: why Pulcova's density moved.** Figure 1 plots the Kepler mass against the assumed period at the paper's semi-major axes. Because M goes as a³/P², shortening Pulcova's period from 4.44 to 4.14 days at a slightly wider orbit lifts the mass from **1.40 to 1.86 × 10¹⁸ kg**, a third more, while the smaller measured diameter cuts the volume by 24 %. The two effects push the same way, which is why a density can move by three-quarters between two papers on the same object without either being careless.

**Step 4: how strange 0.92 g cm⁻³ really is.** Figure 2 drops the two primaries onto the density-against-radius plot that the Planet data explorer draws for the planets, Pluto and the large moons. Emma lands **below the water line** and below every solid world in that table: the least dense of them, Enceladus, is 1610 kg m⁻³, and the only thing lower anywhere in the table is Saturn's 687, which is mostly hydrogen. ## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/binary-asteroid-emma-low-bulk-density-satellite-orbit.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `parentMassFromOrbit`, `derived` and the `BODIES` table from `src/lib/sci/planets.ts`, the maths behind the [[planet-data-explorer]]. `parentMassFromOrbit` is Kepler's third law with G = 6.6743 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²; `derived` works out density as mass over the volume of a sphere, gravity as GM/R² and escape speed as √(2GM/R).

Every input for Emma and Pulcova — the semi-major axes, the periods, the equivalent radii and the published masses and densities we compare against — is a value printed in the paper's text. The planets and moons in Figure 2 are the tool's own built-in data (NASA and JPL). **Our assumptions:** that the equivalent radius can be treated as the radius of a sphere of the same volume (which is what "volume-equivalent" means, and what the paper does), and that the satellite's mass is negligible compared with the primary's — it is about 14 km across against 136 to 142 km, so under 0.2 % of the volume.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` pins this maths against the published Solar System numbers:

- `derived` must give Earth's density as 5514 kg m⁻³ and Saturn's as 687 (NASA's fact-sheet values) within 1, the Moon's surface gravity as 1.62 m s⁻² within 0.01, and `weightOn` must put 70 kg at 26.43 kg on Mars.
- `parentMassFromOrbit` must return Jupiter's mass from Io's orbit to within 0.3 %, and the Earth–Moon system mass from the Moon's orbit (384 400 km, 27.322 days) to within 0.5 % — the check that the law, not the data, is being tested.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/planet-data-explorer.mjs`: the paper's two orbits must give 1.3853 × 10¹⁸ kg and 1.8582 × 10¹⁸ kg, inside the paper's own error bars, and the published radii must turn those into 924, 1125 and 1411 kg m⁻³, with Emma's surface gravity 0.0183 m s⁻² and escape speed 51.0 m s⁻¹.

## Check it yourself

You cannot type an asteroid into the [[planet-data-explorer]] — its table is the Solar System's own worlds — but you can see both halves of the argument on the page in a minute:

1. Open the tool and press **All** so moons and planets are both in the table.
2. In *Plot any two quantities*, set **Across** to *Orbit size (semi-major axis)* and **Up** to *Orbital period*. The note under the chart names it: Kepler's third law, each family of orbits on its own straight line of slope 3/2. That offset between the lines is the parent's mass, and it is the whole measurement in this paper.
3. Now set **Across** to *Radius* and **Up** to *Density*. Every solid world sits between about 1600 and 5500 kg/m³; the only point under 1000 is Saturn. Emma's 924 would plot below all of them.
4. In *Compare two worlds*, choose **Phobos** and **Enceladus**. Phobos reads a surface gravity of **0.0058 m/s²** and an escape speed of **0.0113 km/s**: Emma's 0.0183 and 0.051 are the same order of magnitude, so a 140 km rubble pile behaves like a big moonlet, not a planet.
5. Type **70** into *What you weigh on Earth* and look down the list. On Phobos the scale would read **0.0414 kg**; on Emma, whose gravity is 3.2 times higher, roughly 0.13 kg.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published density** in one line: the mass follows from the satellite's a and P, the density from the mass and the equivalent diameter. If the three numbers in a table do not close, something is wrong in the table.
- **Propagate the orbit's error into the density** before trusting a porosity estimate: Figure 1 is the derivative you need, since M goes as a³/P² and ρ as M/R³.
- **Sanity-check a new binary detection**: an orbit that implies a density under 0.5 or over 4 g cm⁻³ for a rocky body is usually a wrong period, not a new kind of asteroid.
- **Teach Kepler's third law with real stakes** using the tool's orbit plot, then step to a case where the answer is a surprise.
- **Scale an impact**: feed a bulk density and size into the [[impact-crater-calculator]] to see what a rubble pile this loose would do, or compare the binary's separation with the [[double-star-calculator]]'s geometry.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation of published values**, not a reanalysis. We did not touch an image, an astrometric measurement or an orbit fit.
- The paper's real result is the **J2** comparison — a flattening of the gravity field lower than the shape predicts, which is why they argue the interior is uneven. No fibub tool computes J2 from a shape model, so that part is not checked here.
- Our masses are system masses with the satellite's mass folded in, as the paper's are. The satellite is under 0.2 % of the volume, so the distinction is below the quoted errors.
- Equivalent radii carry their own uncertainty (Pulcova's is ±10 km, or 7 %, which alone is 22 % in density). A density that matches to three digits does not mean a density known to three digits.
- The two bulk densities quoted for Emma come from two different shape models in the same paper, not from two different masses.
