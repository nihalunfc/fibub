A direction in the sky has no single name. The same point can be written as right ascension and declination, as ecliptic longitude and latitude, or as Galactic longitude and latitude, and a paper often switches between them. A 2025 letter in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* on the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS prints one such point, the **radiant** of its orbit (the direction the comet is projected to have come from), in all three systems. That makes it a neat test: type one, and the [[sky-coordinates|RA/Dec converter]] should print the others. It does, to about an arcsecond. The same tool then shows how little of the Galactic map the comet's path covers. For the orbit itself see the [[two-body-orbit|Two-body orbit sandbox]], and for the clocks behind "of date" positions see the [[astro-time|astronomy time converter]].

## The paper in brief

3I/ATLAS was reported on 1 July 2025 and quickly shown to be only the third object seen entering the Solar System from interstellar space. The authors observed it with the 10.4 m Gran Telescopio Canarias and the 2 m Twin Telescope in the Canary Islands in early July 2025 (photometry on 2, 3 and 5 July), when it was 3.44 au from Earth. They found a faint coma about 26,000 km across, a surface brightness at its edge of 25.4 magnitudes per square arcsecond, a rotation period of about 16.8 hours from the brightness variations, and a reddish reflectance slope (about 18 % per 1000 Å between 3800 and 9200 Å), steeper than the values the paper quotes for the first two interstellar objects, 'Oumuamua and Borisov. No gas emission showed up in the visible spectrum.

They also asked where it came from. The orbit is hyperbolic, with an eccentricity above 6. Projected back, it points to a **radiant** in the constellation Sagittarius. The paper gives that point as **19h 40m 10.2s, −19° 04′ 13.4″** (J2000), or **ecliptic λ = 293.603°, β = +2.340°**, or **Galactic l = 20.726°, b = −19.057°**. Using the comet's speed through the Galaxy (components U, V, W of −51.233, −19.456 and +18.930 km/s), they argue from the motion alone that it belongs to the Milky Way's thin disc, the flat layer where the Sun lives, not the older, thicker disc or the halo.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's printed numbers**. We did not touch any image, photometry or orbit solution. We used the frame rotations of the [[sky-coordinates|Sky coordinate converter]] (the same IAU matrices from astronomy-engine that its page applies) and its of-date (JNow) conversion.

**Step 1: equatorial to Galactic and ecliptic.** Typing the paper's right ascension and declination gives Galactic **l = 20.724°, b = −19.057°** and ecliptic **λ = 293.603°, β = +2.340°**. The paper says 20.726°, −19.057°, 293.603° and +2.340°. The ecliptic numbers match to the last digit; the Galactic longitude differs by 0.002°, or about 8 arcseconds, which is the rounding of the paper's right ascension (its own error bars are +0.003 and −0.004° in RA).

**Step 2: the other way round.** Typing the paper's Galactic l and b into the converter gives **19h 40m 10.5s, −19° 04′ 06.8″**, within 7 arcseconds of the paper's equatorial position. From the ecliptic numbers: **19h 40m 10.3s, −19° 04′ 14.7″**, within 1.5 arcseconds. Both are limited by how many decimals the paper prints. Three systems, one point.

**Step 3: where is it on the map?** Figure 1 puts the radiant on a Galactic map with the Galactic plane (the horizontal line), the Galactic centre and the ecliptic, the line along which the planets' orbits lie, converted point by point. The radiant sits **27.9° from the Galactic centre** and **19.1° below the Galactic plane**, but only **2.3° from the ecliptic**. The ecliptic crosses the Galactic plane at about l = 6°, not far from the direction of the centre of the Milky Way.

**Step 4: does the velocity agree?** The three velocity components give a speed of **57.980 km/s**, the same figure the paper quotes for the backward-integrated incoming speed. The direction the velocity points back to is Galactic **l = 20.79°, b = −19.056°** by simple trigonometry, which matches the radiant's b to 0.001° and its l to 0.07°. That small difference in l is larger than the rounding of U and V explains, so the radiant likely comes from the paper's own orbit solution rather than from the rounded components: we flag it and do not resolve it.

**Step 5: why "J2000" matters.** Telescope mounts usually want the position "of date", because Earth's axis has wobbled since the year 2000 (precession, about 50 arcseconds a year). Figure 2 shows the shift of this radiant: by 2025 it is **21.9′ in right ascension and 3.4′ in declination** away from the J2000 numbers, and by 2050 **43.9′ and 7.3′**. On 2 July 2025, the day of the paper's stacked image, the radiant's position of date is **19h 41m 39.5s, −19° 00′ 42.8″**. Pointing a mount with J2000 figures and no conversion would miss by about 21 arcminutes on the sky, more than a third of a degree, which is a large share of a typical field of view. (The radiant is a direction in the sky, not where the comet was on that night. It was a long way from there.)

## How we made the graphs

The graphs and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/interstellar-comet-3i-atlas-radiant-galactic-ecliptic-coordinates-conversion.mjs` in fibub's public code. It applies `Rotation_EQJ_GAL`, `Rotation_EQJ_ECL` and their inverses from astronomy-engine to unit vectors, exactly as `src/tools/sky-coordinates/Tool.astro` does, and calls `toJNow`, `constellation` and `separation` from `src/lib/astro/sky.ts`, the helpers behind the converter's of-date, constellation and distance outputs. The ecliptic line in Figure 1 is 720 points of ecliptic longitude at zero latitude, each converted to Galactic coordinates the same way.

Every input is printed in the paper (the radiant in three systems, the velocity components and the quoted speed). **Our assumptions:** the Galactic centre position (Sgr A* at 266.405°, −28.936°) is a standard value; a point-like direction is treated as exact; and the ecliptic is the J2000 ecliptic, as the converter uses.

## How we test the maths

Every change runs `tests/sci-tools/sky-coordinates.mjs`, new with this article. It pins the converter to the paper: the radiant's Galactic l = 20.724° and b = −19.057° (within 0.005° of the paper's printed 20.726°), ecliptic λ = 293.603°, β = +2.340°, the constellation, the 27.87° distance from the Galactic centre, the way back from Galactic l, b to within 10 arcseconds, the speed and direction from (U, V, W), and the position of date on 2 July 2025. A round trip from J2000 to of-date and back must return the starting point to 10⁻⁹ degrees.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[sky-coordinates|RA/Dec converter]]. Leave **Coordinates are** on *RA/Dec J2000 (ICRS)*.
2. Type **19h 40m 10.2s** under *Right ascension* and **−19° 04′ 13.4″** under *Declination*.
3. Read **Galactic l, b** as **20.7237°, −19.0566°** and **Ecliptic λ, β (J2000)** as **293.6028°, 2.3404°**. The *Constellation* row says **Sagittarius (Sgr)**.
4. Now change **Coordinates are** to *Galactic l, b* and type **20.726** and **−19.057**. The *RA, Dec J2000* row returns **19h 40m 10.5s −19° 04′ 06.8″**, close to what you started with.
5. Set **At** to 2 July 2025 and read *RA, Dec of date (JNow)*: about **19h 41m 39.5s −19° 00′ 42.8″**. The altitude row depends on the place you set at the top.
6. Paste a list in **A whole list**, such as `radiant, 19 40 10.2, -19 04 13.4`, press **Convert the list** and download the CSV to keep all systems side by side.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a paper's coordinates** before pointing a telescope: if the printed equatorial, ecliptic and Galactic values disagree by more than the rounding, one of them has a typo.
- **Convert a whole target list** between J2000, JNow, Galactic and ecliptic with the list box and download the result for an observing run.
- **Judge whether a direction is Galactic or Solar-System in origin**: its latitude from the ecliptic and from the Galactic plane (here 2.3° and 19.1°) tells which plane a path follows.
- **Teach coordinate systems** with a real, newsworthy object: one point, three names.
- **Plan follow-up**: pair the converter with the [[two-body-orbit|Two-body orbit sandbox]] for the hyperbolic path or the [[astro-time|astronomy time converter]] for Julian dates of the observations.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation**, not new astronomy. The paper's main results are the comet's colour, activity and rotation. No fibub tool measures those, so they are described but not checked here.
- The **radiant** is a direction on the sky from the orbit solution, not the comet's position on any night. We used it only to test coordinate conversion.
- The paper does not define "radiant" beyond saying the orbit is projected toward it. We took it as the direction the object came from, which the check against the velocity components supports (its b agrees to 0.001°).
- A 0.07° gap in longitude between the radiant and the direction of (U, V, W) is not explained by rounding. We did not chase it.
- The paper's thin-disc argument uses a Toomre diagram and Gaia-based comparison stars. None of that is reproduced here.
