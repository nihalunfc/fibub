Hospital planning needs two numbers that are easy to confuse: how many patients are **in** the hospital, and how many cases the staff are **working on**. A 2025 study in *PLOS ONE* forecasts the monthly workload of two elephant hospitals in Thailand and reports both their case counts and their physical capacity, which makes the difference measurable. Using the [[queueing-calculator]] and one line of queueing theory, we show that most of the reported workload cannot be sitting in a stall, and work out the longest average stay each hospital can actually sustain. The forecasting side of the paper belongs to the [[time-series-analyser]], and staff rosters to the [[shift-scheduler]].

## The paper in brief

The authors assembled the monthly workload of two elephant hospitals in northern Thailand: a non-governmental hospital (NEI) with **467 cases over 60 months** from 2020 to 2024, and a government hospital (DLD) with **198 elephants over 108 months** from 2016 to 2024. Average monthly workload was **30.43 cases** at NEI (range 28 to 31) and **18.68** at DLD (range 0 to 31). They tested several forecasting models and chose exponential smoothing for NEI, which predicts about 30.44 cases a month in 2025, and a neural-network model for DLD, predicting about 13.71. NEI's caseload is seasonal (skin problems peak in December and January, digestive problems in April to May and July to August); DLD's is not.

The paper also states the physical limits: NEI can take **up to 20 elephants at a time**, DLD **no more than three**, and both "frequently operate near full capacity".

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's own printed numbers**: the case totals, the months covered, the workload averages and the stated capacities. The monthly workload series itself is not published, so we do not refit the forecasts.

**Step 1: the arrival rate.** 467 cases in 60 months is **7.78 new cases a month** at NEI; 198 in 108 months is **1.83 a month** at DLD. Those are the flows that any capacity calculation has to start from.

**Step 2: Little's law, run backwards.** For any stable system, the average number in it equals the arrival rate times the average time each one stays (L = λW). Treat the reported workload as the number of cases open at one time and the average stay follows:

- NEI: 30.43 / 7.78 = **3.9 months** per case.
- DLD: 18.68 / 1.83 = **10.2 months** per case.

**Step 3: the capacity bound, which those stays break.** A hospital with 20 places cannot hold more than 20 elephants, so with 7.78 arrivals a month it cannot touch more than about **20 + 7.78 = 27.8** elephants in a month even if every place is full all month and every arrival is admitted. The reported 30.43 is above that. For DLD the bound is **3 + 1.83 = 4.8**, and the reported figure is 18.68, almost four times higher. Figures 1 and 2 show where the lines flatten.

The conclusion is not that the paper is wrong; it is that its workload series counts **open cases, not occupied places**. At NEI, at least 10 of the 30 cases in an average month must belong to elephants that are not in a stall: follow-ups, village visits, chronic cases under supervision. At DLD, at most about 5 of its 18.7 cases can be in-hospital, so three-quarters of the work is out in the field. That matters for the paper's own use of the forecast: 30 cases a month is a staffing and drug-supply number, not a bed number.

**Step 4: the longest stay each hospital can keep up with.** This is the question the capacity figures can answer. A c-place hospital keeps up only while the offered load λW stays under c, so the average stay must be under c / λ: **2.57 months** at NEI and **1.64 months** at DLD. Past that the hospital is permanently full and elephants are turned away or wait outside.

**Step 5: the price of running close to the edge.** With no waiting room, the share of arrivals that find every place busy is the Erlang B formula, which the [[queueing-calculator]] gives directly. At NEI (20 places, 7.78 arrivals a month):

| Average stay | Offered load (erlangs) | Elephants present | Arrivals with no place free |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 month | 7.8 | 7.8 | 0.01 % |
| 1.5 months | 11.7 | 11.6 | 0.8 % |
| 2.0 months | 15.6 | 14.7 | 5.6 % |
| 2.5 months | 19.5 | 16.6 | 14.5 % |
| 3.9 months (the figure Little's law implies) | 30.4 | 18.6 | 38.8 % |

At DLD (three places, 1.83 arrivals a month) the squeeze comes much sooner: a one-month average stay already turns away **18.5 %** of arrivals, and the tool's M/M/c version, where elephants can wait their turn, puts the average wait at about 0.32 months (ten days) with 37 % of arrivals waiting at all.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/elephant-hospital-monthly-workload-queueing-capacity-mmck.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `solveQueue` (for the `erlangb` and `mmc` models) and `erlangB` from `src/lib/sci/queue.ts`, the maths behind the [[queueing-calculator]].

Inputs from the paper: 467 cases in 60 months, 198 in 108 months, the two workload averages, and the stated capacities of 20 and three elephants. **Our assumptions**, needed to turn those into a queue: that arrivals are roughly Poisson and stays roughly exponential (the usual first approximation, which the paper's own seasonality result shows is only an approximation at NEI), and that a case needs one place for its whole length. The curves sweep the average stay from 0.25 to 6 months because the paper does not publish length-of-stay figures in its text.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/queueing-calculator.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the tool against exact rational arithmetic in Python (the `fractions` module):

- 60 random models across all six kinds (M/M/1, M/M/c, M/M/c/K, Erlang B, M/M/infinity, M/G/1) must match the exact values to one part in a billion, with the state probabilities summing to 1.
- Erlang B with 12 servers and the Erlang C help-desk example must match their textbook values, and 10 erlangs at a 1 % loss target must need 18 lines.
- A fuel station modelled as M/M/2/8 must lose 11.76 % of arrivals, and M/M/1/3 at a load of 0.9 must give the blocking and queue values 0.0729 and 0.3439.
- The built-in discrete-event simulation is checked against the formulas for every model, and must repeat exactly with the same seed.
- Unstable systems (offered load at or above the servers) and a capacity K below the server count are refused with a plain message, which is what stops the kind of silent nonsense this article is about.
- New with this article: 20 places with 0.256 arrivals a day and a 61-day average stay must give 14.7285 elephants present with 5.6836 % of arrivals lost under Erlang B, and 16.3841 in the system with an average wait of 3.0004 days and 21.563 % waiting under M/M/c.

## Check it yourself

The tool works in seconds, minutes, hours or days, so convert months to days (a month is about 30.44 days). On the [[queueing-calculator]] page:

1. Set *Model* to **Erlang B (M/M/c/c): no queue, busy means lost**.
2. *Arrivals* **0.256** per **day** (7.78 a month), *Average service time* **61** **days** (two months), *Servers c* **20**.
3. The *Results* card shows an offered load of **15.6 erlangs**, **14.73** elephants in the hospital on average, **5.68 %** of arrivals turned away and the places busy **73.6 %** of the time.
4. Change *Model* to **M/M/c: several servers (Erlang C)**, which lets elephants wait for a place: now **16.38** are in the system, **21.6 %** have to wait and the average wait is **3.0 days**.
5. For the smaller hospital, set *Servers c* to **3**, *Arrivals* to **0.06** per **day** and *Average service time* to **30** **days**: Erlang B turns away **18.0 %** of arrivals, and the M/M/c version makes **35.5 %** of them wait, for **8.9 days** on average.
6. Push *Average service time* up to **50** days with three servers and the M/M/c model refuses to answer: the offered load has passed the number of places, which is exactly the point of step 4 above.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a workload statistic.** Divide the total cases by the months to get the arrival rate, then use Little's law: if the implied stay or the implied occupancy exceeds the building, the statistic counts something else.
- **Turn capacity into a policy number.** "How long can an average patient stay before we are permanently full?" is c / λ, and the [[queueing-calculator]] then prices the last few percent of occupancy.
- **Plan referrals and overflow.** The Erlang B loss at the planned stay length is the share of animals that will need another hospital, a field treatment or a wait.
- **Separate forecasting from capacity.** Use the [[time-series-analyser]] to project the caseload and seasonality, and the queueing model to say what that caseload does to the places and the staff.
- **Size the roster.** Once the number of concurrent cases is known, the [[shift-scheduler]] turns it into people on duty, and the staffing table in the queueing tool answers "how many servers for this target wait?".

## The fine print

- The paper's monthly workload series is not published in its text, so we work from its averages, totals and capacity statements. Our reinterpretation of workload as open cases rather than occupied places is an inference from those numbers, not a statement by the authors.
- Poisson arrivals and exponential stays are approximations. NEI's caseload is seasonal, so arrivals are not constant through the year, and veterinary stays are probably less variable than exponential, which makes our loss figures slightly pessimistic.
- A real hospital is not a pure loss system: sick elephants can be held, treated in the village, or sent elsewhere. Erlang B is the clean bound, not the whole story.
- The 467 NEI figure counts cases, and the 198 DLD figure counts individual elephants, so the two arrival rates are not quite the same quantity; an elephant with two problems can be two cases.
- Nothing here is veterinary advice, and none of it should be read as a judgement on either hospital's care.
