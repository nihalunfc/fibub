Two people can say the same thing with the same intonation and still show completely different numbers in hertz, because one voice simply sits higher than the other. Anyone comparing pitch across speakers therefore has to **normalise**: pick an anchor for each voice and measure everything from it. A 2026 methods paper in *Frontiers in Communication* argues that the anchor should be the speaker's **median** F0, and the unit should be semitones. We checked its numbers, and reproduced its central robustness claim, with the maths behind the [[pitch-contour-tracker]]. For the neighbouring jobs, [[vowel-formant-chart]] handles vowel quality and [[audio-spectrum-analyser]] the rest of the spectrum.

## The paper in brief

The author measured F0 across a corpus of conversational speech: 29 female and 16 male speakers. Female voices had an average median of 217 Hz, with average extremes of 140 and 584 Hz; male voices 117 Hz, with extremes of 77 and 315 Hz. Expressed in semitones, the voices spanned 18 to 36 semitones, about 24 on average — but half of all the frames fell inside an interquartile range of only **4.2 semitones**. In other words, a speaking voice spends most of its time in a narrow band and visits its extremes rarely.

That asymmetry is the argument. The minimum and maximum are set by a handful of frames, so they move a long way if a few frames are wrong: the paper reports that trimming just 1 % off the top moves the maximum by nearly 6 semitones. The mean is pulled by the same outliers. The median barely moves, and it sits inside the band where the voice actually lives. The paper then works through a two-speaker extract, expressing each turn's peak as semitones above that speaker's own median, so that "high for Beth" and "high for Dan" become comparable numbers.

## Checked with the tools

The [[pitch-contour-tracker]] measures F0 frame by frame from a recording, then reports the mean, median, extremes, range and spread, either in hertz or in semitones relative to 100 Hz or to the speaker's own median. Its `semitones` and `pitchStats` functions are exactly the operations the paper describes, so we can check its printed columns directly.

**Step 1: the paper's semitone column.** The paper prints four turn peaks and each speaker's median. Putting those pairs through the tool's semitone conversion, 12·log₂(f / reference), reproduces every printed value:

| Turn | Peak F0 | Speaker's median | Paper says | Tool gives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dan, line 3 ("Wow") | 164.3 Hz | 130.2 Hz | 4 | 4.03 st |
| Dan, line 6 | 234.3 Hz | 130.2 Hz | 10.2 | 10.17 st |
| Beth, line 4 | 367.3 Hz | 213.7 Hz | 9.4 | 9.38 st |
| Beth, line 8 | 474.3 Hz | 213.7 Hz | 13.8 | 13.80 st |

The paper never states the formula it used, so this is worth doing: the column is the plain semitone conversion against each speaker's median, with no reference to 100 Hz and no rounding tricks.

**Step 2: the voice ranges follow from the extremes.** The paper reports ranges of 18 to 36 semitones with a mean of 24 ±1. From the average minima and maxima it prints, the tool gives **24.73 semitones** for the female speakers (140 to 584 Hz) and **24.39** for the male speakers (77 to 315 Hz). The two sexes differ by nearly an octave in hertz and by a third of a semitone in range — which is the whole point of working in semitones. The case-study speakers come out at 23.00 (Dan) and 20.94 (Beth) semitones.

**Step 3: a contour on the median scale.** To see the shape the paper describes, we invented a five-phrase conversational contour, synthesised it as a voice, and tracked it with the tool. The tracked contour has a median of 141 Hz, extremes of 82 and 287 Hz, a full range of **21.6 semitones** and an interquartile range of **4.8 semitones** — close to the 4.2 the paper reports as the average across its 45 real speakers, and nothing like the full range. Figure 1 shows it on the median scale: the quartile band is a narrow strip, and the peaks are rare excursions above it.

**Step 4: the robustness claim, reproduced.** The paper's strongest practical claim is that F0 extraction errors move the mean about four times as far as the median (0.4 against 0.1 semitones on average). The usual error is an **octave error**: the tracker locks onto half or twice the true frequency for a few frames. We took the tracked contour above, halved or doubled a random share of its frames, and measured how far each statistic moved, averaged over 200 random draws:

| Share of frames with an octave error | Mean moves by | Median moves by |
|---|---|---|
| 4 % | 0.218 st | 0.046 st |
| 8 % | 0.378 st | 0.066 st |
| 12 % | 0.513 st | 0.072 st |
| 20 % | 0.809 st | 0.101 st |

The paper's pair of numbers, 0.4 and 0.1 semitones, lands around 8 to 20 % of frames being wrong, and the ratio between the two statistics is 5 to 8 times across the whole sweep (Figure 2). Note also what *does not* change: the median is still wrong by a tenth of a semitone, so a median is robust, not immune.

**Step 5: the same thing with real tracker errors.** The experiment above injects errors by hand. We can also provoke genuine ones. Analysing the tool's own built-in example with its default pitch range of 75 to 500 Hz gives a median of 155.4 Hz, a mean of 158.7 Hz, a maximum of 217 Hz and a range of 10.1 semitones. Raising *Highest pitch* to 1000 Hz lets the tracker double some frames: the maximum jumps to **609 Hz** and the range to **27.9 semitones**, the mean moves **0.56 semitones** — and the median moves **0.09**. That is the paper's claim, reproduced inside the tool with two field changes and no simulation at all.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number here come from `scripts/blog-figures/pitch-normalisation-median-semitones-conversation.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `semitones`, `synthVoice`, `pitchTrack` and `pitchStats` from `src/lib/sci/pitch.ts`, the maths behind the [[pitch-contour-tracker]]: `semitones` for steps 1, 2 and 5, and the full synthesise-and-track chain for steps 3 and 4.

The F0 values, medians, extremes, interquartile range and error shifts attributed to the paper are stated values from its text. The five-phrase contour, the 16 kHz synthesis, the 5-frame median smoothing, the random seed, the share of frames corrupted and the 200 draws are **our assumptions**; the contour is **invented**, not a recording from the paper's corpus.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/pitch-contour-tracker.mjs`, which pins the tracker against known inputs:

- the YIN medians on four pure tones must match an independent numpy implementation of de Cheveigné and Kawahara (2002) to 10⁻⁹ Hz, and every frame of a 120-to-240 Hz glide must be within 0.5 % of the true pitch, with no octave errors;
- on a made-up voice, every frame must be within 2 % with no missed or invented voiced frames, and noise-free silence must give no pitch at all;
- the running median must stay inside each voiced stretch, so a pause never smears pitch across it; `semitones` must give exactly 12 for a doubling and 1 for a 2^(1/12) step; and `pitchStats` must report a 24-semitone range and a 12-semitone mean on a fixed four-frame array.
- New with this article: the four turn peaks above must give 4.03, 10.17, 9.38 and 13.80 semitones over their speakers' medians, and the printed sex averages must give ranges of 24.73 and 24.39 semitones.

## Check it yourself

On the [[pitch-contour-tracker]]:

1. Press **Load the made-up example** (a synthetic voice: a falling statement, a pause, then a rising question, 2.15 s).
2. Set *Semitones relative to* to **the median pitch**. Leave *Method* on **YIN**, *Lowest pitch (Hz)* **75**, *Highest pitch (Hz)* **500**, *Voicing threshold* **0.15** and *Smoothing* **5 frames**.
3. The headline reads **Median pitch 155 Hz, range 10.1 semitones**, with *Mean* **158.7 Hz** (SD 24.3 Hz), *Lowest, highest* **121 to 217 Hz**, *Mean in semitones* **0.17 st** (SD 2.59 st, re 155.4 Hz) and *Voiced* **79 %** (168 of 213 frames).
4. Now change *Highest pitch (Hz)* to **1000**. The range jumps to **27.9 semitones** and the highest pitch to **609 Hz**: those frames are octave errors, not speech. The mean rises to **164.0 Hz** while the median moves only from 155.4 to **156.2 Hz**.
5. Put *Highest pitch* back to **500** and set *Semitones relative to* to **100 Hz** instead: the shape of the contour is identical, only the zero line moves. That is why a shared reference is fine for one speaker and useless for comparing two.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Normalise before you compare speakers.** Take each speaker's median from the [[pitch-contour-tracker]] and report every measurement as semitones above or below it; the tool will do the conversion for you.
- **Report the interquartile range, not just the range.** Two voices with the same 24-semitone span can differ completely in where they spend their time.
- **Catch octave errors before they reach your statistics.** A range above about 20 semitones for one conversational turn, or a maximum near twice the median, almost always means the pitch ceiling is set too high.
- **Prefer the median for any summary that feeds a comparison**, and say which statistic you used: on these numbers the choice is worth about half a semitone, which is the size of many published effects.
- **Teach the scale.** A semitone is a 5.9 % change in frequency; an octave doubling is 12 of them. Running the example twice with the two reference settings makes that concrete in a minute.

## The fine print

- We never had the paper's recordings. Steps 1, 2 and 5 use numbers printed in its text; steps 3 and 4 use an **invented contour** synthesised by the tool.
- Our interquartile range of 4.8 semitones is one made-up contour, not a sample of speakers; the paper's 4.2 ±0.1 is an average over 45 real ones.
- The paper's 0.4-against-0.1-semitone comparison comes from its own error analysis on real tracks. Ours comes from injecting octave errors at a known rate, so the two agree in direction and in ratio, not by construction.
- The tracker's own accuracy depends on the pitch range, the voicing threshold and smoothing. Change them and the extremes change; the median changes very little, which is the paper's point.
- Semitones are a ratio scale, so a difference in semitones is meaningful but their arithmetic mean is not a frequency. Convert back only through the reference you started from.
