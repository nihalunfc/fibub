When a satellite catches a flash of X-rays from the far sky, the race is on to look at the same spot with every telescope available. The paper that follows such a hunt is a list of times: when the flash began, and how many hours or days after it each telescope looked. A 2026 paper in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* on the fast X-ray transient EP250905a does exactly that for sixteen observing epochs, from a first X-ray look 405 seconds after the flash to an optical template image 152 days later. This article rebuilds those delays from the start times alone with fibub's [[astro-time|astronomy time converter]], then asks the question observers ask next: could each telescope really see the target at that moment? The position can be double-checked in the [[sky-coordinates|RA/Dec converter]], and the delays can be turned into the source's own clock with the [[redshift-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The Einstein Probe satellite's wide-field X-ray telescope flagged a new source at 11:07:08 UTC on 5 September 2025. The flash lasted at least 195 seconds (the observation ended before it did), and at its brightest delivered about 10⁻⁹ erg per square centimetre per second at 0.5 to 4 keV. Over the next five months the authors followed it with X-ray satellites (the Einstein Probe's own follow-up telescope and Swift), optical telescopes on La Palma, Tenerife, Chile and China, an infrared camera and the MeerKAT radio array.

A faint optical counterpart showed up at magnitude i = 23.23 about 0.6 days after the trigger, and faded below detection in later epochs. Two galaxies lie nearby. The authors favour the fainter, more distant one at redshift **z = 2.714**, only 0.27 arcseconds from the counterpart, over a closer one at z = 0.374, and conclude that the data are best explained as the afterglow of a mildly off-axis jet. Radio, infrared, Swift and gamma-ray searches found nothing, which set upper limits (for example below 30 µJy/beam at 3 GHz).

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from times printed in the paper**: no image, spectrum or light curve of ours is involved. We used the Julian Date, sidereal-time and light-time maths of the [[astro-time|Astronomy time converter]], and for altitudes the same position helper that powers the [[sky-coordinates|RA/Dec converter]].

**Step 1: the clock.** The trigger, 2025-09-05 11:07:08 UTC, is **JD 2460923.96329** and **MJD 60923.46329**. A Julian Date is just a running day count from a reference noon, so the delay between any two times is a subtraction.

**Step 2: sixteen delays.** We converted each printed UTC start time to a Julian Date and subtracted the trigger. Figure 1 plots our delay against the paper's, on logarithmic axes spanning five orders of magnitude, from 405 seconds to 152 days. Fourteen of the sixteen agree to the digits the paper prints: for example the first Gran Telescopio Canarias (GTC) image 0.6212 days after (paper: 0.62), the first spectrum 19.7165 days (paper: 19.72), the last template image 152.39 days (paper: 152.4), and the X-ray look 405 s after the trigger. Two differ a little: the TRT telescope is 0.034 days after the trigger by its start time, where the paper prints 0.04, and the Las Cumbres 1 m telescope 0.478 days where the paper prints 0.49. Both gaps (8 and 18 minutes) are about what you would expect if the paper quotes the middle of the exposure and we used its start, but the paper does not say, so we leave it as an open point.

**Step 3: could the telescopes see it?** The target sits at 00h 17m 48s, +37° 28′. From La Palma in September that point is highest in the early morning hours. The converter's sidereal time says that at the first GTC image (02:01:39 UT on 6 September) the local sidereal time was **23h 51m 47s**, only half an hour before the target crosses the meridian, so it stood **79.5° high** (airmass 1.02: almost straight overhead). Figure 2 follows its altitude through two nights. The later GTC epochs were also well placed: 58° high at the second imaging epoch, 53° at the third, 75.7° at the last spectrum. Only the template image in February was lower, at 39° (airmass 1.59), which is fine for a deep image of a faint, stationary host.

The satellite epochs and the telescopes at other sites are not at La Palma, so this altitude test does not apply to them. For the eight La Palma epochs (seven with GTC and one with the Nordic Optical Telescope) the target stood between 39° and 80° high every time, which is what a sensible schedule looks like.

**Step 4: a sidereal clock check.** The converter's local sidereal time gains **236.56 seconds of sidereal time per solar day** between two identical UTC times, the textbook 3 m 56.555 s. That is why the same star crosses the meridian about four minutes earlier each night. Five months after the trigger, at the template image's 20:35 UT start on 4 February 2026, the target had already passed the meridian by about four hours.

**Step 5: the light-time correction.** If you want an event's time on the Solar System's own clock, as is standard when comparing telescopes on different planets or years, you add the light-travel time to the barycentre, the centre of mass of the Solar System. For the counterpart on the trigger date that correction is **+333.5 seconds** (and +335.8 seconds to the Sun's centre). The target lies 32.3° from the ecliptic, so the correction can reach at most 422 seconds (499 s × cos 32.3°) over a year, as Figure 3 shows. For a transient as long-lived as this one, six minutes is invisible; for a pulsar or an eclipsing binary it would matter hugely.

**Step 6: the source's own clock.** If the redshift z = 2.714 is right, time in the source's frame runs slower by a factor of 1 + z = 3.714 as seen from Earth. By our arithmetic the 195-second flash was about **53 s** long in its own frame, the first optical image was **4.0 hours** after the burst in the source frame (0.62 days observed), and the template image 152.4 days later is **41 days** in the source frame. We use the paper's favoured redshift; the alternative at z = 0.374 would shrink these by only 1.374.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/ep250905a-transient-follow-up-delay-times-julian-date-sidereal-time-la-palma.mjs` in fibub's public code. For Julian Dates it uses `MakeTime` from astronomy-engine exactly as `src/tools/astro-time/Tool.astro` does (`ut + 2451545`); for sidereal time, `lst`, and for altitude, `altAzJ2000`, `observer` and `airmass`, all from `src/lib/astro/sky.ts`; and for the barycentric correction the tool's own recipe, `BaryState` and `HelioVector` of the Earth dotted with the direction to the target and multiplied by 499.004783836 light-seconds per astronomical unit.

Every input is a value printed in the paper: the trigger time, the position of the counterpart, the sixteen UTC start times and the delays. **Our assumptions:** the observatory coordinates for La Palma (a public value), that all La Palma epochs can be treated as that one site, and that a start time is what the paper means by an epoch's time. The altitudes use the converter's refraction-free geometry plus the airmass formula of the sky helper.

## How we test the maths

Every change runs `tests/sci-tools/astro-time.mjs`, new with this article. It pins: the trigger as JD 2460923.96329 (and equal to unix time ÷ 86400 + 2440587.5), ten printed delays recovered from Julian Dates to the paper's rounding, the 405 second first look, the gain of 236.555 s of sidereal time per day, Greenwich sidereal time 01h 03m 21s at the first GTC image, the 79.5° altitude and airmass 1.02, and the +333.5 s and +335.8 s light-time corrections, which must stay under 499 s × cos β.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a follow-up table**: recompute every delay from the start times and flag any that disagree with the printed delay by more than rounding.
- **Plan the next response**: enter the target position and the telescope's site to see whether the next three nights put it high enough, using the local sidereal time and the [[sky-coordinates|RA/Dec converter]]'s altitude.
- **Put events from different instruments on one clock**: convert UTC start times to JD or MJD, and add the light-time correction when comparing different years.
- **Express timescales in the source frame** for a given redshift, as in step 6, with the [[redshift-calculator]] for the distance.
- **Teach time scales**: JD, MJD, sidereal time and UTC in one real, recent set of observations.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation of printed times**. The paper's science (the afterglow model, the host association, the upper limits) is described here but not checked: no fibub tool fits a jet model.
- The two disagreements in delay (TRT and Las Cumbres) are open: we cannot tell from the paper whether it quotes start or mid-exposure times.
- Writing this article turned up a real error in the converter: it took TT from a predicted-ΔT polynomial, which read 74.9 s for September 2025 where the true TT − UTC offset is 69.184 s, making the page’s **BJD (TDB)** about 5.7 seconds too late. Terrestrial Time now comes from the leap-second table (TT = TAI + 32.184 s), so it is exact, and the page shows **TT − UTC** rather than an estimated ΔT. Julian Dates, delays, sidereal time and light-time corrections were never affected.
- Altitudes assume the standard site position and the converter's geometry. A real schedule also has the Moon, dome limits and weather.
- The favoured redshift z = 2.714 is the paper's judgment, not a certainty; the paper also discusses z = 0.374 and gravitational lensing.
