Hospitals, labs and nuclear sites need windows that stop gamma rays while people can still see through them. Lead glass does the job, but lead is toxic, so researchers look for heavy, clear glasses without it. A 2025 study in *Scientific Reports* makes six barium zinc borate glasses, each with a small amount of a different sixth oxide, and measures how well they stop gamma rays. This article shows how the [[gamma-shielding]] calculator checks the paper's numbers from the glass recipe alone, and where its built-in formulas stop being reliable. To work out how strong a source will be on the day you shield it, the [[decay-calculator]] follows its activity over time.

## The paper in brief

All six glasses share a recipe of 45% boron oxide, 20% zinc oxide and 30% barium oxide (by moles); the last 5% is calcium, magnesium, aluminium, titanium, copper or iron oxide. Their densities run from **3.93 to 4.03 g/cm³**, about one and a half times that of concrete. Thin samples (3.4 to 4 mm) sat between a collimated gamma source and a high-purity germanium detector, which counts the gamma rays that get through. Three sources gave energies from 0.06 MeV (americium-241) through 0.662 MeV (caesium-137) to 1.17 and 1.33 MeV (cobalt-60).

The measured attenuation agreed closely with the Phy-X computer program. The sixth oxide matters little: at 0.662 MeV the **half-value layer** (the thickness that halves a narrow beam) runs from 2.31 cm for the calcium glass to 2.26 cm for the iron glass. At 0.06 MeV every glass stops almost everything within a millimetre, thanks to the barium.

## Checked with the tools

The [[gamma-shielding]] calculator works out the **mass attenuation coefficient** (μ/ρ, the fraction of a beam removed per gram per square centimetre of material) from physics formulas, element by element, rather than from a data table. We gave it each glass's recipe: moles of each oxide turned into a weight fraction of each element with the tool's own `fromFormula` and `mix` functions. Then we compared its answer with the paper's measurements. This is a **recomputation from the recipe**, independent of the paper's data.

**Step 1: the middle energies.** At 0.662 and 1.33 MeV, where gamma rays mostly bounce off electrons (Compton scattering), the tool lands **within 1 to 4.5%** of the measured values:

**Step 2: the low energy, where formulas fail.** At 0.06 MeV the paper measures about **15.4 per cm**; the tool's formulas give only about **3.5 per cm**, more than four times too low. The reason is the barium **K-edge** at 37.4 keV: just above it, barium's inner electrons absorb gamma rays very strongly, and the tool's photoelectric formula is a smooth scaling law with no edges. That is why the tool only offers its own estimates from 0.1 MeV up and asks for a typed value from a table or a measurement for real low-energy work. The glass's strength at low energies is real; the formula just cannot show it.

**Step 3: the half-value layer and a shield's thickness.** Typing the measured coefficient into the tool reproduces the paper's figures for the calcium glass: a half-value layer of **2.31 cm** and **74.1%** transmitted through 1 cm. Cutting a narrow 0.662 MeV beam to 1% takes about **15.3 cm** of this glass. Per centimetre it beats concrete (half-value layer about 3.9 cm with the tool's simple concrete) but not lead (Figure 2).

One small inconsistency is worth knowing: for the titanium glass the paper's half-value layer (2.295 cm) matches its Phy-X coefficient (0.302 per cm), while its measured 0.292 per cm gives 2.37 cm. The difference is about 3%, and the tool makes such checks quick.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and the table come from `scripts/blog-figures/barium-zinc-borate-glass-gamma-shielding.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fromFormula`, `mix`, `parts`, `massAtten`, `hvl` and `transmission` from `src/lib/sci/phgamma.ts`, the maths behind the [[gamma-shielding]] calculator. The glass recipes, densities and measured coefficients come from the paper; the molar masses come from standard atomic weights. The concrete and lead curves use the tool's built-in materials and formulas (its concrete is a simple stand-in mix of 2.30 g/cm³). For lead the formulas give a half-value layer of 0.65 cm at 0.662 MeV, while reference tables give about 0.56 cm: the tool says its built-in values for heavy elements can read low.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci-tools/gamma-shielding.mjs` (run by `tests/sci.mjs`) checks the tool:

- The Compton part must match a numerical integration of the Klein-Nishina formula done with SciPy, to 12 digits, at energies from 0.05 to 100 MeV (2.5614 × 10⁻²⁵ cm² per electron at 0.662 MeV).
- With μ = 1.2618 per cm, the half-value layer must be **0.5493 cm**, the tenth-value layer **1.8249 cm**, and 2 cm must let through **8.018%**.
- New with this article: the Fe₂O₃ glass, built from its recipe, must come within 6% of the paper's measured **0.307 and 0.208 per cm** at 0.662 and 1.333 MeV; and a typed μ/ρ of 0.0763 cm²/g at 3.932 g/cm³ must give **2.31 cm** and **74.1%** through 1 cm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[gamma-shielding]] calculator:

1. Leave *Gamma energy* on **Cs-137 (0.662 MeV)**.
2. Type **0.0763** in *μ/ρ, mass attenuation (cm²/g)* (the CaO glass: 0.300 per cm divided by 3.932) and **3.932** in *Density (g/cm³)*.
3. Type **1** in *Shield thickness* with *Unit* on **cm**, leave *Buildup* on **none (narrow beam)**, and type **1** in *Wanted transmission (%)*.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a shielding table**: turn every linear coefficient into a half-value layer and a transmission, and spot entries that do not agree with each other.
- **Screen a new composition** before making it: the tool's formulas, given a recipe, are good to a few per cent in the Compton range (0.5 to 2 MeV) for glasses like these.
- **Size a window or wall**: the thickness for a wanted transmission, with or without a simple buildup factor for broad beams.
- **Teach** why heavy elements matter at low energies (photoelectric absorption and edges) but density matters most in the middle range, where every material removes about the same amount per gram.

## The fine print

- The paper's values come from thin samples in a narrow, collimated beam. A real shield also sees scattered gamma rays (buildup), which the tool can add roughly with its *Buildup* setting.
- The tool's own formulas are approximate, as it says: fine in the Compton range, poor near absorption edges and for heavy elements at low energy. For design work, type in measured or reference-table values.
- The paper's measured values are read from its text; its full tables and figures hold more.
- Making the glass, its clarity under long exposure to radiation, and its cost are outside what these numbers show.
