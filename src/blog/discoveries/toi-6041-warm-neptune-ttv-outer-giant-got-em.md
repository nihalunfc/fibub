TOI-6041 is a bright, Sun-like star (a G7 dwarf) 72 parsecs away. The TESS satellite saw a planet the size of Neptune pass in front of it every 26 days, but not like clockwork: its transits come up to about an hour early or late. Those **transit-timing variations** (TTVs) are the fingerprint of another planet tugging on it. A new study from the GOT'EM survey, which follows long-period transiting planets, finds that second planet with years of velocity measurements: a Saturn-mass giant on an 88-day orbit. This article shows how the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]], the [[habitable-zone-calculator]] and the [[transit-light-curve-fitter]] let anyone rebuild the system's key numbers.

## The paper in brief

- **TOI-6041 b** is a **warm Neptune**: 4.55 Earth radii, on a 26.05-day orbit. Its wobble on the star is too small to measure; the paper sets an upper limit of 7 m/s, so its mass is below about 29 Earth masses.
- **TOI-6041 c** shows up in the star's wobble with an 88-day period and a minimum mass of about **0.25 Jupiter masses**, close to Saturn's 0.30. Whether it also transits is not known, because the timing of its orbit is uncertain by about 12 days.
- The inner planet's transit times swing by about an hour. A first timing model needs planet c's orbit to be somewhat elongated (eccentricity about 0.3); the authors keep a circular orbit in their main fit, as the velocities cannot yet tell.

Warm Neptunes with timing variations are rare, and the system is a good target for measuring planet b's mass from its timings alone.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated values, plus **simulated** curves for illustration. We assumed circular orbits, an albedo of 0.3 and a transit across the middle of the star.

**Orbits and starlight.** Kepler's third law with a star of 0.885 Suns puts planet b at **0.165 au** and planet c at **0.372 au**, both well inside Mercury's distance from the Sun. With the star's 0.532 solar luminosities, b gets **19.5 times** and c **3.85 times** the sunlight Earth gets. Their equilibrium temperatures, with albedo 0.3 and heat spread round the planet, are about **535 K** and **357 K**. The star's conservative habitable zone runs from 0.71 to 1.26 au, so neither planet is in it.

**Planet b's mass limit.** Typing the 7 m/s limit, the 26.05-day period and the star mass into the mass maths gives **29.9 Earth masses**, close to the paper's 28.9. The small gap is expected: 7 m/s is a rounded limit, and the paper's mass limit comes from its full statistical fit. At that limit b's density would be at most about **1.75 g/cm³**, so it cannot be much denser than Neptune (1.64).

**Planet c's wobble.** A 0.245 Jupiter-mass planet on an 88-day orbit swings the star by **12.1 m/s** (12.7 m/s if the eccentricity is 0.3). Figure 1 compares it with what planet b could do. If b had Neptune's mass (17 Earths) its wobble would be only **4.0 m/s**, small next to c's signal, which is why b's mass is still open.

**The transit.** Planet b is 4.55 Earth radii across a star of 0.879 solar radii, so it blocks **0.225 %** of the light. It orbits **40 star radii** out, and a central crossing lasts about **5.2 hours** (Figure 2). A dip of 0.2 % on a 10th-magnitude star is within reach of good amateur and small university telescopes, which is why timing campaigns can keep following it.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/toi-6041-warm-neptune-ttv-outer-giant-got-em.mjs`. It calls:

- `minMass`, `semiAmplitude` and `density` from `src/lib/sci/rvmass.ts` (the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]]);
- `auForPeriod`, `insolation`, `tEq` and `habitableZone` from `src/lib/sci/habitable.ts` (the [[habitable-zone-calculator]]);
- `fluxAt` and `durations` from `src/lib/sci/transit.ts` (the [[transit-light-curve-fitter]]).

The star and planet values are from the paper. The albedo (0.3), the central transit, the limb darkening (the fitter's "Sun-like star, visible light" setting, 0.40 and 0.25) and the circular orbits are our assumptions. Both figures are **simulated** illustrations, not the TESS or velocity data.

## How we test the maths

Every change to fibub runs `tests/sci.mjs`, which checks these functions against known answers:

- 51 Pegasi b's minimum mass must come out at **0.477 Jupiter masses** from its 55.94 m/s wobble, and Jupiter's pull on the Sun at **12.47 m/s**.
- The Sun's conservative habitable zone must be **0.950 to 1.676 au** (Kopparapu and colleagues, 2014), and Earth's equilibrium temperature with albedo 0.306 must be **254 K**.
- The transit model must block exactly **1 %** of a uniform star for a planet one tenth its size at the centre, and the duration formula must give HD 209458 b's transit as **3.07 hours** (Winn 2010).

## Check it yourself

**Planet b's mass limit, on the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]]:**

1. Keep **Mass from the wobble**.
2. Type **Star mass (Suns)** 0.885, **Orbital period (days)** 26.04945, **Eccentricity** 0 and **Velocity semi-amplitude K (m/s)** 7.
3. The mass reads at least **29.9 Earth masses** (0.094 Jupiters).
4. Add **Transit depth (%)** 0.225 and **Star radius (Suns)** 0.879: the **Planet radius** reads **4.55 Earth radii**.

**Planet c's wobble:** press **Wobble from the mass**, type **Orbital period (days)** 88, **Planet mass** 0.245 **Jupiters**: the **Velocity semi-amplitude** reads about **12.1 m/s**. Set **Eccentricity** to 0.3 and it rises to about 12.7 m/s.

**The starlight, on the [[habitable-zone-calculator]]:** type **Temperature (K)** 5445, **Luminosity (Suns)** 0.532 and **Mass (Suns)** 0.885, then 26.04945 in **or orbital period (days)** and **Albedo (0 to 1)** 0.3. **Starlight at the planet** reads about **19.5 times Earth's** and the **Equilibrium temperature** about **535 K**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turning a velocity upper limit into a mass limit**, and back, with the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]], before writing a proposal for more spectrograph time.
- **Planning timing campaigns:** the [[transit-light-curve-fitter]] models a 0.2 % dip of about 5 hours, and the [[transit-planner]] finds when the next transits are visible from a given site.
- **Placing planets against the habitable zone** and estimating their temperatures with the [[habitable-zone-calculator]].
- **Teaching Kepler's third law:** the 26-day and 88-day orbits at 0.165 and 0.372 au are a real-world check of T² ∝ a³, which students can explore with [[kepler-orbits]].
