A muon is a heavy copy of the electron, about 207 times heavier, and it can take an electron's place in an atom. In helium the muon sits so close to the nucleus that it behaves almost like a hydrogen-like ion of its own, with the remaining electron orbiting far outside. A 2019 workshop paper computes the energies of this "muonic helium" atom with a **variational method** (a computer search for the lowest-energy wave function made of many Gaussians). That is heavy machinery, but the headline number, the ground-state energy, turns out to be reproducible with the hydrogen-like formula plus one rescaling. We did it with the [[hydrogen-orbitals]] viewer, taking the constants from the [[physical-constants]] page.

## The paper in brief

The authors treat the atom as three particles: a helium-3 or helium-4 nucleus, a muon and an electron. They solve the Schrödinger equation with a large set of trial Gaussian functions, then add small corrections one at a time: the finite size of the nucleus, relativity, and **vacuum polarisation** (the way the Coulomb field is slightly altered by virtual electron-positron pairs, which matters because the muon is so close in). They list two ground-state energies, **−10858.496122 eV** and **−10956.319047 eV** (we read the first column as helium-3 and the second as helium-4, because the more massive nucleus binds the muon more tightly), and totals after corrections of −10873.702742 and −10973.028228 eV. They also estimate the 2P-2S splitting of the electron, about 10 GHz, and give hyperfine-structure numbers that we did not check.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation** from standard constants. No data were fitted.

**Step 1: treat the muon as a hydrogen-like ion.** The tool's formula for a one-electron atom is E = −13.6057 Z²/n² eV for a nucleus of infinite mass. For helium (Z = 2, n = 1) it reads **−54.4228 eV**. That is the electron's value. For the muon two things change. The muon is 207 times heavier, and because the energy scales with the **reduced mass** μ = m·M/(m + M), the energy grows by the ratio of the muon's reduced mass to the electron mass: **201.0687** for helium-4 and **199.2712** for helium-3 (the nucleus recoils a little less when it is heavier). Multiplying gives the muon's binding energy: **−10942.715 eV** for helium-4 and **−10844.893 eV** for helium-3.

**Step 2: add the electron.** The muon and nucleus together act like a core with charge +1, around which the electron orbits as in hydrogen: **−13.6039 eV** (helium-4) and **−13.6033 eV** (helium-3), the hydrogen value rescaled by the electron's own reduced mass, which differs from 1 by about 1 part in 7500. The sums are:

| | Hydrogen-like estimate | Paper, variational | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Helium-4 | −10956.318804 eV | −10956.319047 eV | 0.000243 eV (2.2 × 10⁻⁸) |
| Helium-3 | −10858.495866 eV | −10858.496122 eV | 0.000256 eV (2.4 × 10⁻⁸) |

The variational calculation and the plain hydrogen-like picture agree to better than 3 parts in 100 million, even though the variational code treats the electron, muon and nucleus all together. The small leftover, about a quarter of a milli-electronvolt, is the interaction between the electron and the muon's cloud that the simple picture ignores. A nice check on the paper's mapping of columns: swapping them would put the heavy-nucleus value on the wrong atom and break the agreement.

**Step 3: how small is the muon's orbit?** The tool also gives mean radius ⟨r⟩ = 0.75 a₀ for Z = 2, and dividing by the muon's reduced mass puts the muon at **197.4 fm** from a helium-4 nucleus (199.2 fm for helium-3), with its most likely distance at about 132 fm. The nucleus itself is only about 1.68 fm across, so the muon lives about 117 nuclear radii away: close enough to feel the nucleus's size, far enough to be hydrogen-like. The electron's mean radius is **79.4 pm**, about 400 times farther out than the muon.

**Step 4: the small corrections.** The paper's three correction rows are −0.5977, +2.3663 and −18.4777 eV for helium-4 (−0.5946, +3.1587 and −17.7806 eV for helium-3). The extraction we could read does not name the rows, so we identified two by recomputing them.

- The **negative first row** looks like the relativistic correction. The leading Dirac result for a 1s level, −(Zα)²|E|/4, is **−0.5827 eV** (helium-4) and **−0.5775 eV** (helium-3), 2.5 % and 2.9 % smaller than the table's entries. The remaining few percent is recoil and spin physics beyond the leading term.
- The **positive second row** looks like the nuclear-size correction. To first order it is (2/3) Z (Zμ)³ r² in atomic units, which uses the muon's wave function at the nucleus, the same function the viewer plots. With rounded radii of 1.68 fm and 1.97 fm (**our assumption**) it gives **+2.378 eV** and **+3.183 eV**, within 0.5 % and 1 % of the paper's +2.3663 and +3.1587 eV. Run backwards, the paper's two rows ask for radii of **1.676 fm** and **1.963 fm**, about where the published nuclear radii lie. The ratio of the two rows, 1.335, is also reproduced (1.339) by the radius and mass dependence alone.
- That leaves the vacuum-polarisation row, about −18 eV: a 0.17 % correction to the muon's binding, larger than the finite-size term because the muon's orbit (about 197 fm) is smaller than the 386 fm reach of the electron-positron cloud (the electron's reduced Compton wavelength). We did not recompute this row.

One more finding, and one for the authors to check. Adding the printed corrections to the helium-4 ground-state energy gives −10973.028194 eV, within 0.00004 eV of the printed total. For helium-3 the same sum gives −10873.7126 eV, which is **0.0099 eV away** from the printed total of −10873.7027. If the relativity or size row were 3.1686 instead of 3.1587 the sum would balance. We read the numbers from a machine-extracted PDF, so this may be an extraction slip rather than a paper error; check the original table before relying on that entry.

## How we made the graphs

The script is `scripts/blog-figures/muonic-helium-ground-state-energy-hydrogen-like-reduced-mass.mjs`. It calls `properties` (energy, mean radius), `radial` (the 1s radial function) and the constants `RYDBERG_EV` and `BOHR_PM` from `src/lib/sci/phhydro.ts`, the maths behind the [[hydrogen-orbitals]] viewer. The nuclear charge Z = 2 and the paper's table entries are the paper's. The particle mass ratios and the fine-structure constant are CODATA values and **our input**; the rounded nuclear radii are **our assumption**. The rescaling by reduced mass and the two correction estimates are our own formulas applied to the tool's output; no data are fitted.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/hydrogen-orbitals.mjs`, which checks the tool against SciPy and closed forms:

- the radial function R_nl against SciPy's Laguerre polynomials at several points (for example R₃₁ for Z = 2 at r = 2 to 1 part in 10¹³);
- the spherical harmonics against `scipy.special.sph_harm_y`, and the real harmonics against their complex combinations;
- the expectation values ⟨r⟩, ⟨r²⟩, ⟨1/r⟩ against the closed forms and SciPy integration.
- New with this article: the tool's Z = 2 ground state, rescaled by the muon's reduced mass for helium-4 and helium-3 and with the electron added, must land within 3 × 10⁻⁸ of the paper's −10956.319047 and −10858.496122 eV.

## Check it yourself

On the [[hydrogen-orbitals]] page:

1. Set *n (shell)* to **1**, *l (subshell)* to **0** (the s orbital) and *Nuclear charge Z* to **2**.
2. The page reads **E = −54.4228 eV** and *Mean radius ⟨r⟩* **0.75 a₀** (39.688 pm).
3. Multiply the energy by **201.0687** (the muon's reduced mass in electron masses for helium-4): −10942.7 eV. Divide the radius by the same number: 0.197 pm, which is 197 fm.
4. Set *Nuclear charge Z* to **1**: the energy −13.6057 eV, times 0.99987 for the heavy core, gives the electron's −13.6039 eV. Add the two: −10956.32 eV, the paper's ground state.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a three-body variational result** with a two-line scaling before trusting a long computation (hydrogen orbital viewer).
- **See why exotic atoms probe the nucleus.** The muon's orbit is hundreds of times smaller than the electron's, so its energy depends on the nuclear radius (hydrogen orbital viewer, constants page).
- **Estimate correction sizes.** Relativity, nuclear size and vacuum polarisation come out as 5 × 10⁻⁵, 2 × 10⁻⁴ and 2 × 10⁻³ of the binding energy: a quick way to decide which terms a calculation needs (hydrogen orbital viewer).
- **Teach reduced mass.** Compare hydrogen, deuterium, positronium-like and muonic atoms by changing one number (physical constants).

## The fine print

- The paper's contribution is the variational calculation and its corrections. The hydrogen-like picture checks only the leading energy; it says nothing about the 2P-2S splitting or the hyperfine numbers.
- Our reading of which column is which isotope and which row is which correction is inferred. The paper's text, as extracted, does not label them; the match of the leading energy to 2 × 10⁻⁸ and of the size row to the nuclear radii supports it but does not prove it.
- The nuclear radii are our rounded assumption. The first-order size formula uses a point-Coulomb muon, which is slightly too crude at the percent level.
- The tool assumes an infinitely heavy nucleus. The reduced-mass scaling is exact for a two-body atom, not for the full three-body problem.
