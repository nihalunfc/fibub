Rain carries a chemical fingerprint in its water molecules: the share of heavy oxygen and hydrogen atoms. Four numbers summarise it: δ¹⁸O, δ²H, **d-excess** and a very small quantity called Δ′¹⁷O (the "triple-oxygen excess"), written in **per meg**, which is one thousandth of a per mil. A 2026 paper in *Earth System Science Data* presents five years of such numbers for rain in Seoul and uses Δ′¹⁷O and d-excess to tell what happened to the air before it rained. Δ′¹⁷O is a tiny difference of two large numbers, so the choice of constants matters a great deal. The [[isotope-delta-converter]] does the sums, so we recompute the paper's headline values and test how the reference slope moves them. To unmix sources with such numbers, use the [[mixing-model-calculator]]; for straight-line fits such as the meteoric water line, the [[calibration-curve]] tool.

## The paper in brief

The authors collected every precipitation event at one site in Seoul from 2016 to 2020 and measured δ²H, δ¹⁸O and δ¹⁷O. They built a **local meteoric water line** (δ²H = 7.79 δ¹⁸O + 10.2), and monthly precipitation-weighted means. Winter rain is lightest and has a high d-excess; the triple-oxygen excess is highest in spring and lowest in summer. They give the definition Δ′¹⁷O = ln(δ¹⁷O + 1) − 0.528 ln(δ¹⁸O + 1), with δ values as plain fractions and the result scaled to per meg, and compare the Seoul regression of δ′¹⁷O on δ′¹⁸O with the one from a nearby station in the IAEA network (Cheongju).

## Checked with the tools

We used the paper's precipitation-weighted monthly means (δ¹⁷O −3.74 ‰ and δ¹⁸O −7.11 ‰) in the tool's triple-oxygen box. The tool's linearised values are δ′¹⁷O = −3.7470 ‰ and δ′¹⁸O = −7.1354 ‰, and with λ = 0.528 the excess is **20.5 per meg** (20.48 before rounding). The paper prints a mean of 18 per meg, but a mean of monthly excesses is not the excess of the mean values, and the printed δ values are rounded to 0.01 ‰: changing δ¹⁷O by only 0.005 ‰ moves the excess by 5 per meg. So 20.5 and 18 agree to within what the rounding allows, and 20.8 per meg, the intercept of the paper's own Seoul line, is in the same place. The d-excess of the means, −45.2 − 8 × (−7.11), is **11.68 ‰**, which matches the printed 11.7.

**The reference slope matters.** The Seoul regression has slope 0.528 and the Cheongju one 0.5283, almost the same, but each line is a straight line in δ′ space, so how far apart they sit in Δ′¹⁷O depends on δ¹⁸O. Worked out with the same λ = 0.528, the two lines give 19.5 (Cheongju) and 20.8 (Seoul) per meg at the mean δ¹⁸O of −7.11 ‰, but 16.1 and 20.8 at the lightest sample, δ¹⁸O = −18.21 ‰. A change of λ from 0.528 to 0.5283 alone raises the excess by 2.1 per meg at the mean and by 5.5 per meg at −18.21 ‰ (and lowers it 0.3 at +1.15 ‰). Every 0.001 in λ is worth about 7 per meg at the mean and 18 per meg at the lightest samples, compared with the paper's analytical reproducibility of 9 per meg. That is why the paper states λ, and why anyone comparing Δ′¹⁷O between datasets must check that they used the same slope.

**A note on the errors.** If the 1σ of δ¹⁷O (0.01 ‰) and δ¹⁸O (0.07 ‰) were independent, the error of Δ′¹⁷O would be about 38 per meg (0.528 × 0.07 ‰ alone is 37 per meg). The reported one-year reproducibility is 9 per meg, so the errors of the two δ values must be strongly correlated, as expected when both come from the same measurement run. That is **our reading, not a statement in the paper**.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/seoul-precipitation-triple-oxygen-excess-per-meg-lambda-0528-recomputed.mjs` imports `capDelta17` and `deltaPrime` from `src/lib/sci/delta.ts`, the maths of the Isotope delta converter. The monthly means, the two regression lines and the analytical errors come from the paper. Both lines are turned back into δ¹⁷O values (our step, from the line's equation) and sent through `capDelta17` with the chosen λ, from δ¹⁸O = −18.5 to +1.5 ‰.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` pins the converter: δ from a ratio, the VPDB to VSMOW conversion (a VPDB-scale zero is +30.92 ‰ on VSMOW), fractionation, mixing and Δ′¹⁷O. New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/isotope-delta-converter.mjs` checks the Seoul numbers: δ¹⁷O −3.74 ‰ and δ¹⁸O −7.11 ‰ give 20.5 per meg at λ = 0.528 and 22.6 at λ = 0.5283, and a sample on the Seoul regression line gives 20.8 per meg at any δ¹⁸O. It runs on every change with `node tests/sci.mjs`.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[isotope-delta-converter]] and go to the **Triple oxygen: Δ′¹⁷O** box.
2. Type δ¹⁷O = −3.74 and δ¹⁸O = −7.11, and leave the reference slope λ at 0.528.
3. The tool shows Δ′¹⁷O as "+20 per meg" with 0.0205 ‰ beside it (the page rounds per meg to whole numbers; the exact value is 20.48).
4. Change **Reference slope λ** to 0.5283: the tool now shows +23 per meg (0.0226 ‰).
5. Put λ back to 0.528 and change δ¹⁷O to −3.735: the answer rises to +25 per meg (0.0255 ‰), showing how much a 0.005 ‰ rounding is worth.

## What researchers can use it for

- Checking whether two Δ′¹⁷O datasets share a reference slope before comparing them (a λ mismatch of 0.0003 shifts light samples by several per meg).
- Estimating how much rounding the printed δ values contribute to Δ′¹⁷O, before trusting differences of a few per meg.
- Teaching the difference between δ and δ′ and why triple-oxygen work uses logarithms.
- Unmixing rain or vapour sources from δ and d-excess with the [[mixing-model-calculator]].
- Fitting a meteoric water line to your own δ²H and δ¹⁸O with the [[calibration-curve]] tool.

## The fine print

This recomputes a handful of numbers printed in the paper. We did not have the sample-level dataset, so the paper's seasonal results, correlations and uncertainty budget are not tested here. The abstract's sample ranges and the monthly weighted ranges in the text differ and the paper does not explain why, so we use only the monthly means. Our reading of the error correlation is an inference. Education, not a reference value: use the published dataset for any real comparison.
