Dark muscle is the red strip along a tuna's flank, and in canning it is mostly waste. A 2025 paper in *Foods* broke it down with enzymes, chased the fraction that made immune cells multiply, and read out 17 peptides by de novo sequencing, naming five of them in the text. Peptide sequences are the kind of claim anyone can check arithmetic on, so we put those five through the [[protein-calculator|protein parameter calculator]]: their exact masses, their charge in the buffer they were dissolved in, and what the paper's fixed test concentration means in molecules. One result is worth the whole exercise: two of the named peptides are **16 parts per million apart in mass**, inside the study's own 20 ppm search window. For the fragment-ion side of the same job use the [[peptide-mass-calculator|peptide mass calculator]], and for how greasy each peptide is, the [[hydropathy-plot]].

## The paper in brief

The authors hydrolysed yellowfin tuna (*Thunnus albacares*) dark muscle protein, split the hydrolysate on Sephadex G-25 and then by reversed-phase HPLC, and tested each fraction on immune cells. Fraction F1, and within it the RP-HPLC peak R2, raised cell proliferation (about 72 %), phagocytosis (about 79 %) and nitric oxide release (4.48 µM). The active peak went into a Q Exactive Orbitrap, and **PEAKS Studio** de novo sequencing with an ALC score of at least 90 returned **17 peptides of 5 to 12 residues and 616 to 1441 Da**, none of them already in the BIOPEP database. Five were synthesised at over 98 % purity and retested; **HDCDLLR** and **YGSVELDELGK** came out strongest, HDCDLLR ahead on proliferation, phagocytosis and nitric oxide, YGSVELDELGK ahead on interleukin-6 and TNF-α.

The search settings matter for what follows: a **non-specific enzyme** (so any cut is allowed), **carbamidomethyl cysteine as a fixed modification**, a precursor tolerance of **20 ppm** and an MS/MS tolerance of **0.02 Da**.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the masses, and whether they fit the stated range.** The calculator gives each sequence's formula, average mass and monoisotopic mass. All five fall inside the paper's quoted 616 to 1441 Da window, which is a small but real consistency check on a de novo list.

**Step 2: two peptides almost share a mass.** Look at the last two rows. FPPDYLDDALR and QYGDLSTPEALK have different lengths and different compositions, yet their monoisotopic masses differ by **0.0211 Da, which is 16.0 ppm**, and their average masses agree to 0.003 Da. The paper searched with a precursor tolerance of **20 ppm**, so both sequences fit the same precursor peak. Nothing is wrong with that: de novo sequencing decides between them on the fragment ions, not the precursor, and the MS/MS tolerance of 0.02 Da is tight enough to do so. But it means a reader cannot treat these two as independently measured masses, and anyone targeting them later needs a method that separates them in time or by fragments, not by precursor mass.

**Step 3: the same concentration is not the same dose.** The paper tested peptides at **200 µg/mL**. Because the peptides differ in mass by up to 50 %, that fixed mass is a different number of molecules each time (Figure 2): **229.6 µM** for the small HDCDLLR against **165.4 µM** for YGSVELDELGK, which is **39 % more molecules**. HDCDLLR is reported as the more active of the two on three of five readouts. Part of that gap is simply the extra molecules, and a molar comparison would settle how much. Equal-mass dosing is normal in food-peptide work; the fix is to report µM alongside µg/mL, which the calculator does in one step.

**Step 4: charge in the buffer, and what it says about handling.** All five peptides are acidic, with isoelectric points between 3.70 and 5.27, so in phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.4 they all carry a net negative charge (Figure 1), from −1.03 to −2.03 proton units. That is useful twice over: an acidic peptide is unlikely to be a membrane-disrupting cationic antimicrobial, which fits the paper's immune-signalling story, and it tells you to expect these peptides to come off an anion exchanger rather than a cation exchanger. HDCDLLR, the most active, is the least charged (−1.04) and has the highest pI (5.27), so it is also the one nearest to precipitating if a sample drifts acidic.

**Step 5: how to measure what you weighed out.** The 280 nm extinction coefficient comes from tryptophan and tyrosine only. Four of the five peptides contain one Tyr each, so ε₂₈₀ is 1490 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ and a 1 mg/mL solution reads **1.13 to 1.23** absorbance units: a quick A280 check works. **HDCDLLR has neither Trp nor Tyr**, so its ε₂₈₀ is **0** and A280 cannot see it at all. That is consistent with the paper monitoring its columns at 214 and 220 nm, where the peptide bond itself absorbs, and it is the single most useful warning for anyone reproducing this work with the most active peptide.

**Step 6: the cysteine.** HDCDLLR is the only one of the five with a cysteine, and the search treated cysteines as **carbamidomethylated**, which adds 57.02146 Da. The sequence as searched therefore weighs **927.4232 Da** monoisotopic, while the synthesised peptide with a free cysteine weighs **870.4018 Da**. If you order this peptide and check it by mass spectrometry, that 57 Da is the difference between agreeing with the paper's table and thinking the synthesis failed. A free cysteine also oxidises: expect a dimer near 1738.79 Da in an old tube.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/tuna-immunomodulatory-peptide-mass-and-charge.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `analyse` (formula, average and monoisotopic mass, pI, charge at a chosen pH, extinction coefficient and the A280 of a 1 mg/mL solution), `chargeCurve` (charge against pH) and `uMFromMgMl` from `src/lib/sci/protparam.ts`, the maths behind the [[protein-calculator|protein parameter calculator]].

From the paper: the five sequences, the 200 µg/mL test concentration, the PBS solvent, the 20 ppm and 0.02 Da tolerances, the carbamidomethyl setting and the 616 to 1441 Da range. **Our own**: nothing but the choice of pH 7.4 for PBS and the decision to compare the five named peptides. No data table, figure or peak list from the paper was used; the paper's per-peptide masses are in a table we did not read, so the masses here are computed from the sequences alone.

## How we test the maths

`npm test` runs `tests/sci.mjs`, which pins the protein parameters against **ExPASy ProtParam** and **Biopython** on the tool's example sequence and random sequences:

- The made-up example protein of 120 residues must come out as C626H932N176O183S9, with a monoisotopic mass of 14130.6520 Da as Biopython gives it and an average mass of 14139.93 Da.
- Its extinction coefficient must be 36440 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ reduced and 36690 with its two cystines, again as Biopython gives it, and the A280 of a 1 mg/mL solution 2.577.
- Its pI must be 7.3814 and its charge at pH 7 +0.6804, and the charge curve must be exactly zero at the pI.
- Setting two disulfides must take four hydrogens (4.032 Da) off the mass and raise ε by 250.
- New with this article: `tests/sci-tools/protein-calculator.mjs` pins the five peptides above to their formulas, average and monoisotopic masses, pI values, charges at pH 7.4 and extinction coefficients, checks that FPPDYLDDALR and QYGDLSTPEALK are 16.0 ppm apart, and checks the 229.6 µM that 200 µg/mL of HDCDLLR comes to.

## Check it yourself

On the [[protein-calculator|protein parameter calculator]] page:

1. Clear the sequence box and paste **HDCDLLR**. The results show length 7, formula **C₃₅H₅₈N₁₂O₁₂S**, average mass **870.98** and monoisotopic **870.4018** Da.
2. The isoelectric point reads **5.27**. Set the pH box to **7.4** and the net charge reads **−1.04**.
3. The extinction coefficient at 280 nm reads **0**, and the A280 of a 1 mg/mL solution **0.000**: this peptide is invisible at 280 nm.
4. Paste **QYGDLSTPEALK** instead: average **1321.45**, monoisotopic **1320.6561** Da, pI **4.11**, ε₂₈₀ **1490**, A280 of 1 mg/mL **1.128**.
5. Paste **FPPDYLDDALR**: the average mass is the same to two decimals, but the monoisotopic mass is **1320.6350** Da, 0.0211 Da lower. That gap is 16 ppm.
6. In the concentration card, enter **0.2** mg/mL for HDCDLLR (mass 870.98) and it reports **229.6 µM**; the same 0.2 mg/mL of YGSVELDELGK is **165.4 µM**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Convert µg/mL to µM** before comparing peptides in an activity table, so a ranking is not an artefact of molecular weight.
- **Check whether A280 can measure your peptide** at all: no Trp or Tyr means no absorbance, and a 214 nm reading or amino acid analysis instead.
- **Spot near-isobaric pairs** in a de novo peptide list before you build a targeted assay around them.
- **Account for fixed modifications**: compare the searched mass (with carbamidomethyl) against the synthetic peptide's mass before deciding a synthesis went wrong.
- **Plan a purification**: the pI and the charge curve say which ion exchanger will hold a peptide and at what pH it will come off, and the [[hydropathy-plot]] says how it will behave on a reversed-phase column.

## The fine print

- Every value here is computed **from the sequences the paper names**, with standard residue masses and pKa values. It is not a reanalysis: the paper's own per-peptide mass table, spectra and activity table were not used.
- Only five of the 17 peptides are named in the paper's text, so the near-isobaric pair we found may not be the only one in the full list.
- Isoelectric points and charges from side-chain pKa values are estimates; measured values differ by a few tenths of a unit, more so for very short peptides.
- A peptide that makes immune cells multiply in a dish is not a treatment, a supplement claim or dietary advice. This is general science education.
- The dimer mass we quote for an oxidised HDCDLLR assumes one disulfide between two copies and no other change.
