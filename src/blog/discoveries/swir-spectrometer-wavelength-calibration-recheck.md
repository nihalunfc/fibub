Methane absorbs light in a narrow band near 1.65 µm, and a spectrometer that measures it from the air has to know which wavelength each of its pixels sees, to better than a twentieth of a nanometre. A 2025 paper in *Remote Sensing* reports that calibration for a short-wave infrared grating spectrometer: a straight pixel-to-wavelength line, a measured instrument line width, and an uncertainty budget. Everything in it can be rechecked from the numbers printed in its text, and this article does that with the [[spectrometer-calibration|spectrometer calibration tool]], the [[uncertainty-calculator]] and the [[spectrum-peak-fitter]].

## The paper in brief

The instrument covers **1592.4 to 1677.6 nm**, the window that holds the methane band used for satellite and airborne monitoring. The authors illuminated it with a xenon calibration lamp, fitted a Gaussian to each emission line to get its pixel position, and fitted a straight line through the pairs:

λ = **1677.63199 − 0.13341 × pixel** nanometres, with R² = 0.99999.

They then measured the **spectral response function** (the shape the instrument gives a perfectly sharp line) with a tunable source, getting a Gaussian of **0.1867 nm FWHM**, and checked the calibration against a **1653 nm** laser: the largest deviation was **0.022 nm**, inside the **0.05 nm** the mission requires. Their uncertainty budget lists three contributions: **0.005 nm** from lamp instability, **0.012 nm** from the Gaussian fit and peak identification, and **0.016 nm** from the regression. They do not give a combined figure.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the stated coefficients and uncertainties, not a reanalysis of their spectra, which we do not have. Three things come out of it.

**Step 1: the calibration line, the array and a typo.** Feed seven pixel-and-wavelength pairs built from the paper's own line into the tool's fit and it returns **λ = 1677.63200 − 0.13341 × pixel** with a dispersion of **−0.13341 nm per pixel** and residuals at the 10⁻¹³ nm level, as it should: a straight line through points on a straight line. The useful part is what the coefficients imply. Pixel 0 is 1677.632 nm, and the line reaches **1592.38 nm at pixel 639**. In other words the detector is a **640-pixel array**, the standard size for an InGaAs line sensor, and the stated range 1592.4 to 1677.6 nm is exactly its two ends. The abstract's 1677.2 nm is a typo for 1677.6.

The same line puts the 1653 nm verification laser at **pixel 184.63**, and the 1605.328 nm line used for the response function at **pixel 541.97**. A deviation of 0.022 nm is **0.165 of a pixel**; the 0.05 nm requirement is **0.375 of a pixel**. Stated that way, the achievement is clearer: they located a line to a sixth of a pixel.

**Step 2: the budget, combined.** The paper lists its three uncertainty components and stops. Running them through the uncertainty tool's maths (the GUM first-order rule on λ = a + b + c, each component normal) gives a **combined standard uncertainty of 0.0206 nm** and, at 95 % coverage with k = 2.00, an **expanded uncertainty of 0.0412 nm**. That matters: the requirement is 0.05 nm, so at 95 % confidence the margin is only **18 %**. The regression term carries **60 %** of the variance, the peak fitting **34 %** and the lamp **6 %**, so more lamp lines, not a better lamp, is where the next improvement lies.

| Calibration line | λ = 1677.63199 − 0.13341 × pixel | recovered as 1677.63200 − 0.13341 × pixel |

**Step 3: why they had to fit Gaussians.** Divide the line width by the pixel spacing: 0.1867 ÷ 0.13341 = **1.40 pixels per FWHM**. Sampling theory asks for at least two, so this instrument is **undersampled**: a line lands on effectively three pixels, one tall and two small (Figure 1).

That has a consequence for how you find the line. The quick method, and the one fibub's own calibration page uses, is a **parabola through the three highest pixels**. Walk a noise-free line of this width across one pixel and ask the tool's peak finder where it is, and the answer is systematically wrong by up to **0.017 nm**, simply because a Gaussian this narrow is not a parabola (Figure 2). At two pixels per FWHM the worst error falls to **0.0087 nm** and at three pixels to **0.0039 nm**.

So at this instrument's sampling a three-point peak finder would spend more than the paper's **entire 0.012 nm** peak-identification budget on bias alone, before any noise. Fitting the full Gaussian, as the authors did, is not a refinement here; it is the only way to meet the requirement. That is a transferable lesson: **check pixels per FWHM before choosing a peak-finding method**, and if it is below about two, fit the line shape.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/swir-spectrometer-wavelength-calibration-recheck.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `calibrate`, `polyval`, `plainCoeffs` and `findPeaks` from `src/lib/sci/phspec.ts`, the maths behind the [[spectrometer-calibration|spectrometer calibration tool]], and `propagate` from `src/lib/sci/uncertainty.ts`, the maths behind the [[uncertainty-calculator]]. `calibrate` fits the pixel-to-wavelength polynomial with the pixel axis centred and scaled for numerical stability, then `plainCoeffs` expands it back into plain powers of the pixel; `findPeaks` refines each local maximum with a parabola through its three highest points; `propagate` applies the GUM first-order rule and reports each input's share of the variance.

Where the numbers come from: the calibration coefficients, the spectral range, the response-function width, the 0.022 nm deviation, the 0.05 nm requirement and the three uncertainty components are **from the paper's text**. The seven pixel positions we fit, the Gaussian line shape used in Figures 1 and 2 and the 2.00 and 3.00 pixels per FWHM comparisons are **ours**.

- The tool's made-up example spectrum must return its known pixel-to-wavelength map, 380 + 0.32x + 2 × 10⁻⁵x², from the peaks alone.

## Check it yourself

Two short runs. First, on the [[spectrometer-calibration|spectrometer calibration tool]], the calibration itself needs a spectrum photo, so use the paper's line through fibub's general fitter instead — on the [[spectrum-peak-fitter]], in *Energy calibration (optional)*:

1. Paste any spectrum (or press **Use the example**) so the page has data, then in *Pairs, one per line: position and energy* type **20 1674.9638**, **220 1648.2818**, **420 1621.5998** and **620 1594.9178** — four points on the paper's line.
2. Set *Calibration* to **straight line** and *Energy unit* to **nm**. The page prints **E = 1677.63 + −0.133410 × x**, the paper's coefficients.
3. Change *Calibration* to **quadratic** and the extra term comes out at −1.4 × 10⁻¹⁷, that is zero: the paper's linear fit is justified.

Second, the uncertainty budget, on the [[uncertainty-calculator]]:

4. In *What you calculate*, type **e = a + b + c**.
5. Add three measurements, each with *Kind* **± standard uncertainty** and *Value* **0**: *a* ± **0.005** (lamp), *b* ± **0.012** (peak fitting), *c* ± **0.016** (regression).
6. Set *Coverage* to **95 % (2 sigma)**. The result reads **0 ± 0.0412** with a combined standard uncertainty of **0.0206**, and the table shows the regression contributing **60 %** of the variance.
7. Change *c* to **0.008**, as two or three times as many lamp lines would roughly give, and the expanded uncertainty falls to **0.0305 nm**: a real margin against the 0.05 nm requirement.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recheck a published calibration line** against the stated spectral range and pixel count: if the two ends do not land on pixel 0 and pixel N − 1, something in the paper is mistyped.
- **Convert a wavelength error into pixels** before judging it. A sixth of a pixel is a strong result; 0.022 nm on its own means nothing without the dispersion.
- **Finish an uncertainty budget** that a paper leaves as a list, and see which term to attack: here it is the regression, which means more calibration lines.
- **Choose a peak-finding method from pixels per FWHM**: below about two, fit the line shape; above three, a parabola is fine, and the [[spectrum-peak-fitter]] will fit Gaussians with uncertainties either way.
- **Plan a laboratory calibration**: simulate your own line at your own sampling, run both methods, and see what bias you would be accepting.

## The fine print

- We recompute from values stated in the paper's text. Its spectra and fitted figures are not reproduced or digitised here.
- The 640-pixel array is our deduction from the coefficients and the stated range, not something the paper says.
- Our peak-finder bias is noise-free and assumes the pixels sample a Gaussian at their centres. A real pixel integrates over its width, which smooths the line slightly and reduces the bias a little.
- The paper fits Gaussians to its lamp lines, which is the right method; our bias figure applies to the quick three-point estimator, including the one fibub's own calibration page uses.
- The expanded uncertainty assumes the three components are independent and normal, as the paper's presentation implies. If the regression and peak-fitting terms are correlated, the true combined value differs.
- The response-function width is quoted at one wavelength (1605.328 nm). In a grating spectrometer it changes across the array, so pixels per FWHM is not the same at both ends.
