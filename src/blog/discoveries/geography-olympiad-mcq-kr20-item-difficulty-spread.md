A reliability coefficient is not a free-standing fact. Once you know how many questions a test has, what the average score was and how widely scores were spread, KR-20 is **already decided** by one remaining quantity: how much the questions differ in difficulty. A 2024 paper in *Cogent Social Sciences* analysed 100 multiple-choice questions from Indonesia's National Geography Olympiad, answered by 259 students, and reports all four of those numbers. We use the [[item-analysis|item analysis tool]]'s own maths to work backwards to the item-difficulty spread the paper's figures require, then build a whole answer sheet with that spread and run the tool on it. For the same ideas on questionnaires rather than exams, see [[reliability-analysis]] and [[irt-item-analysis]], and for before-and-after teaching studies [[effect-size-learning-gain]].

## The paper in brief

The authors took the 2019 National Geography Olympiad paper, 100 multiple-choice questions, and the answer sheets of 259 students, and ran a classical item analysis: **difficulty** (the share of students who answered an item correctly), **discrimination** (how much better the strong students did than the weak ones on that item), distractor quality, and the test's overall reliability.

Their headline numbers: reliability **KR-20 = 0.82**, mean score **33.85 out of 100** with a standard deviation of **9.85**, mean difficulty index **0.33** and mean discrimination index **33 %**. They conclude that many items were moderately difficult with high discriminating power, and recommend writing better distractors. An olympiad paper is meant to be hard, so a mean difficulty of 0.33 is not a fault: it is the point.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the spread of difficulty is not free.** KR-20 is

> KR-20 = (k / (k − 1)) × (1 − Σ pᵢqᵢ / s²)

where *k* is the number of items, *pᵢ* the difficulty of item *i*, *qᵢ* = 1 − *pᵢ*, and *s* the standard deviation of the total scores. Now Σ pᵢqᵢ = Σ pᵢ − Σ pᵢ², and Σ pᵢ is just *k* times the mean difficulty, which the mean score already fixes (33.85 ÷ 100 = 0.3385, matching the paper's stated 0.33). So the only freedom left in Σ pᵢqᵢ is the **variance** of the item difficulties.

Putting the paper's numbers in: *k* = 100, *s*² = 9.85² = 97.02, mean difficulty 0.3385. For KR-20 to be 0.82, Σ pᵢqᵢ must be **18.26**, which needs the 100 difficulties to have a standard deviation of **0.203**. That is a wide but entirely normal spread for an olympiad: a mixture of items almost nobody gets and items most people get. If instead every item had had the same difficulty of 0.3385, the same scores would have given KR-20 = **0.777**; a spread of 0.25 would have given **0.842**. Figure 1 shows the whole relationship, and how little it moves if the score spread is a point either side of 9.85.

**Step 2: build the answer sheet and run the real analysis.** Working backwards from a formula is one thing; we then made a full 259-student, 100-item answer sheet with exactly that difficulty spread (**simulated**, not the paper's data: a normal-ogive model where a student of ability θ answers item *i* right with probability Φ(aθ − bᵢ), with the item difficulties set to mean 0.3385 and standard deviation 0.203, and the single discrimination *a* turned up or down until the total-score standard deviation came out at the paper's 9.85). Then we fed that sheet to the item analysis tool's `analyse` function, the same code the tool page runs.

| Check | Paper | Tool, on our simulated sheet |
|---|---|---|
| Items, students | 100, 259 | 100, 259 |
| Mean score | 33.85 | 34.44 |
| Score standard deviation | 9.85 | 9.86 |
| KR-20 | 0.82 | **0.813** |
| Mean difficulty | 0.33 | 0.344 |
| Mean discrimination index | 33 % | **24.2 %** |
| KR-21 | not reported | 0.775 |
| Standard error of measurement | not reported | 4.27 marks |

Four of the paper's five numbers come back. The fifth does not, and the gap is instructive. **A mean discrimination index of 0.33 across 100 items implies a more reliable test than KR-20 = 0.82.** When we tune the same simulation until the tool reports a mean discrimination index of exactly 0.330, the total-score standard deviation rises to 13.4 marks and KR-20 to **0.904**. The two reported numbers pull in different directions by a long way.

That is not a claim that the paper is wrong. The most likely explanation is that its "33 %" is an average over a selected set of items (the abstract says only 10 % to 30 % of items were in the acceptable-to-fairly-good band), or an average of a differently defined index, or taken over the top-and-bottom groups of a different size. It is a claim that **a reader cannot take a mean discrimination index and a reliability coefficient from the same abstract and treat them as independent facts**: the tool shows they constrain each other, and a 100-item test whose items really average 0.33 on the 27 % index should land near 0.90.

**Step 3: why difficulty caps discrimination.** The upper-lower discrimination index compares the top 27 % of students with the bottom 27 %, so it cannot exceed what the item's own difficulty allows. An item that only 10 % of students answer right cannot separate the groups by more than about 0.37, however well written it is. Figure 2 plots our simulated items against that ceiling. Of the 100 items, our hardest has a difficulty of 0.015 and a discrimination index of 0.014: the paper's recommendation to fix the distractors is exactly right for items like that, because an item nobody can answer discriminates nothing.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/geography-olympiad-mcq-kr20-item-difficulty-spread.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `analyse` and `kr20` from `src/lib/sci/itemanalysis.ts`, the maths behind the [[item-analysis|item analysis tool]], and `normCdf` and `normInv` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the simulated answers.

The inputs taken from the paper are its 100 items, 259 students, mean score 33.85, score standard deviation 9.85 and KR-20 of 0.82. Everything else is **our assumption**: the normal-ogive response model, one shared discrimination parameter per sheet, item difficulties placed at evenly spaced normal quantiles and clipped to the range 0.03 to 0.9 (which is why our mean score lands at 34.4 rather than 33.85), and a fixed random seed. The answer sheet is **simulated**; the paper's own answer sheets are not public, and none of its tables were copied.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/item-analysis.mjs`, which pins this maths against numpy and scipy on a made-up 30-student, 10-item example:

- The ten item difficulties, the 27 % upper-lower index for each item and the group size of 8 must come out exactly as an independent numpy script gives them.
- Each item's point-biserial correlation must match `scipy.stats.pointbiserialr` and the corrected item-total correlation `scipy.stats.pearsonr`, to 1e-10.
- KR-20 must equal Cronbach's alpha for the 0/1 case (0.76112), KR-21 must be 0.69491, and the alpha-if-deleted column must match for all ten items.
- A hand-checkable case is pinned too: the four-student, three-item sheet 111 / 110 / 100 / 000 has KR-20 exactly 0.75.
- New with this article: the 100-item relation above must hold, so that a test with mean difficulty 0.3385, score standard deviation 9.85 and item difficulties of standard deviation 0.2033 gives KR-20 = 0.820, falling to 0.7770 if every item shares the same difficulty.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published reliability figure.** With the number of items, the mean score and the score spread, work out what item-difficulty spread the reported KR-20 needs. If the answer is absurd, something in the paper's numbers does not fit.
- **Report the score standard deviation.** Many item-analysis papers leave it out, and without it a KR-20 cannot be checked or compared at all.
- **Decide how many items you need.** Run your pilot, then use the test-length relation in step 5 above to see how long the final paper must be for the reliability you want.
- **Find the items worth rewriting.** The tool's distractor table, plus difficulty against discrimination as in Figure 2, points straight at the items that are too hard to discriminate.
- **Compare classical and modern analyses** on the same answer sheet: difficulty and discrimination here, then item characteristic curves in [[irt-item-analysis]].

## The fine print

- The answer sheet in Figures 1 to 2 and in "Check it yourself" is **simulated** from the paper's summary numbers. It is not the olympiad's data, and no table from the paper was reproduced.
- The paper reports its mean discrimination index as a percentage (33 %). We read that as 0.33 on the usual 0 to 1 index; if it means something else, the mismatch we describe may disappear.
- Our simulation gives every item the same discrimination parameter. Real tests vary, which raises the mean discrimination index a little at a given reliability, but not by the margin seen here.
- KR-20 assumes one underlying ability and items scored right or wrong. A paper covering several geography topics may be measuring more than one thing, which pushes KR-20 down.
- We worked from the paper's abstract, which is where all the numbers used here appear. We did not read its tables.
