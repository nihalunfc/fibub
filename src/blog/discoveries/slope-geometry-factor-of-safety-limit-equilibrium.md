Cut a slope into soft soil to fit houses onto a hillside and you create a question that two very different methods try to answer: how close is it to sliding? A 2025 paper in *E3S Web of Conferences* measured eleven cut slopes in a housing estate in Pekanbaru, Indonesia, from 2.62 m to 4.8 m high and 49° to 84° steep, and worked out a **factor of safety** for each one twice, once by limit equilibrium (Bishop's method of slices) and once by finite elements. Both of those numbers can be rechecked in a browser: fibub's [[slope-stability]] runs the same Bishop search. Before the stability check, name the soil with the [[soil-classification]]; for a long planar slope with a shallow failure, the [[infinite-slope]] is quicker.

## The paper in brief

The soil comes from cone penetration tests and is weak: three layers of clayey silt and stiff clay with cohesion of 7.2, 12.7 and 27.3 kPa and friction angles of only 7.7°, 8.2° and 10.6°. The stiff clay starts at 3 m depth and goes to nearly 12 m. The eleven "review points" are existing cut slopes of different heights and angles along the estate.

The headline findings are two. First, safety falls as a slope gets steeper and taller: the gentlest 49° slope has a factor of safety of 1.778 by limit equilibrium, the steepest 84° slope only 1.117. Two of the eleven fall below the 1.25 the authors treat as the line between adequate and critical by limit equilibrium, and eight of the eleven by finite elements. Second, the **finite element method always gives the lower number**, on average 11 % lower (0.84 to 0.93 of the limit-equilibrium value), with the biggest gaps on the slopes above 3 m or steeper than 70°. Their recommendation is to treat the finite-element answer as the design value.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated values**: its soil parameters and its slope geometry, with no fitted or invented soil. The one thing the paper does not state is how it handled water in the limit-equilibrium runs, so we ran the slopes **dry with the saturated unit weights (our assumption)** and tested a pore-pressure ratio separately.

The tool searches a grid of slip-circle centres and radii and reports the lowest Bishop factor of safety it finds, so each number below is the critical circle, not a circle we chose. Because the tool takes two layers and the paper has three, we used the middle silt (c′ = 12.72 kPa, φ′ = 8.21°, γ = 16.6 kN/m³) over the stiff clay (27.31 kPa, 10.618°, 16.7), with the boundary 3 m below the crest, as the paper's layer table says.

The two-layer column sits on average **5.5 % above** the paper's limit-equilibrium values (1.009 to 1.118 of them) and correlates with them at r = 0.974. For a browser tool given nothing but a soil table and two geometry numbers, that is a close match to a commercial package's answer, and it supports the paper's first finding without reservation: these slopes lose safety as they steepen, and the steepest of them are close to the line.

**The layering is the real story.** Look again at points 4 to 7 in the paper's own numbers: the slope gets taller (3.12 → 4.36 m) and the factor of safety goes **up**, 1.338 → 1.365 → 1.388, before easing to 1.370. A plain single-soil analysis cannot do that: our soft-silt-only column falls straight down through that range, 1.347 → 1.174 → 1.065 → 0.998, and crosses 1 at point 7, which would mean an already failed slope. With the stiff clay at 3 m the rise reappears: 1.400 → 1.429 → 1.442 → 1.387. The reason is that a taller cut forces the critical circle deeper, and below 3 m it has to cut through clay with four times the cohesion of the silt above. Height hurts; reaching the stiff layer helps; between 3 and 4.4 m the second effect wins.

So the paper's awkward non-monotonic column is not noise. It is the signature of a layered profile, and the tool reproduces it from the paper's own soil table.

How much does that depth matter? Figure 2 moves it and changes nothing else.

For the steepest slope (point 11), assuming the stiff clay starts at 2 m gives 1.429; at 3 m, 1.197; at 4 m, 0.908; at 6 m, 0.786, after which the circle never reaches it. A single 2 m error in the depth to a strong layer swings the answer by half a factor of safety, far more than the 11 % difference between the two methods that the paper is about. That is worth knowing before arguing about methods.

That last line is worth a note for anyone who learned that Bishop's method always gives the higher number. It usually does, but with friction angles under 10° and deep circles the m(α) correction can tip the other way, and the tool reports both so you can see which is which.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/slope-geometry-factor-of-safety-limit-equilibrium.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `search` from `src/lib/sci/slope.ts`, the maths behind the [[slope-stability]]: Bishop's simplified method on circular slip surfaces with 40 slices, iterated to convergence, over a 13 × 13 grid of centres with eight circle depths each and a pattern-search refinement, plus the ordinary (Fellenius) method on the same circles.

The soil parameters, slope heights, slope angles and the two sets of published factors of safety are the paper's stated values. Dry conditions with saturated unit weights, the 3 m boundary measured below the crest, the two-layer simplification of a three-layer profile and the grid extent are **our assumptions**. No slope was tuned to match: the same search settings were used for all eleven.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/slope-stability.mjs`, which pins the method of slices against an independent Python script (slip-mass ends by `scipy.optimize.brentq`, Bishop's equation solved by root-finding rather than iteration):

- Seven circles over four made-up slopes, with no water, with a pore-pressure ratio, with a piezometric line and with two layers, must match Python's Bishop and ordinary factors of safety to 1 × 10⁻¹⁰, and the slip-mass ends to 1 × 10⁻⁹.
- A φ′ = 0 soil must give Bishop exactly equal to the ordinary method, since the correction term vanishes.
- The grid search minimum for one slope with a pore-pressure ratio of 0.25 must land on SciPy's Nelder-Mead minimum of 1.18178.
- New with this article: the paper's soil over stiff clay at 3 m must give a Bishop factor of safety of 1.400 for a 3.12 m slope at 70° and 1.197 for a 4.80 m slope at 84°, and the soft silt alone must give 1.347 and 0.786, so the layering effect stays pinned.

## Check it yourself

On the [[slope-stability]] page, rebuild point 11, the steepest slope:

1. *Slope height H (m)* **4.8**, *Slope angle β (°)* **84**.
2. Set *Soil layers* to **Two soils (horizontal boundary)**. Under *Upper soil*: *Cohesion c′ (kPa)* **12.72**, *Friction angle φ′ (°)* **8.21**, *Unit weight γ (kN/m³)* **16.6**.
3. Under *Lower soil*: *Boundary level (m above the toe; negative is below)* **1.8** (that is 3 m below the crest), *Cohesion c′ (kPa)* **27.31**, *Friction angle φ′ (°)* **10.618**, *Unit weight γ (kN/m³)* **16.7**.
4. Leave *Pore pressure from* on **None (dry)**, set *Find* to **The critical circle (grid search)**, press **Fit the grid to the slope**, and read the result: Bishop about **1.20**, the ordinary method about **1.21**. The paper's value for this slope is 1.117.
5. Change *Soil layers* back to **One soil** and the same slope reads about **0.79**: a failed slope. The stiff clay is doing the work.
6. Switch *Pore pressure from* to **Pore pressure ratio ru** and type **0.2**: the two-layer answer falls to about **1.14**.
7. Repeat with *Slope height H* **3.12** and *Slope angle β* **70** (boundary level **0.12**) for point 4: about **1.40**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published factor of safety** before you cite it, using only the soil table and the geometry. A difference of more than about 10 % usually means water, layering or a different critical circle, not a different method.
- **Separate geometry from soil.** Run the same soil over a range of heights and angles to see which of the two is driving your result, as the paper intended.
- **Test what an uncertain layer depth costs**, as in Figure 2, and use that to decide whether another borehole is worth more than a finer analysis.
- **Compare Bishop with the ordinary method** on the same circles, and report both; the gap is a measure of how much the method itself matters for your slope.
- **Teach the method of slices** with a real case: the tool lists every slice with its weight, base angle and pore pressure, and the slice table downloads as CSV.

## The fine print

- The tool takes two soil layers; the paper has three. We dropped the thin top layer (0 to 0.8 m) into the middle silt, which makes our slopes slightly stronger near the surface.
- The paper does not state the water conditions for its limit-equilibrium runs, and it says the finite-element runs were fully saturated. We ran dry with saturated unit weights, which is conservative in weight and optimistic in pore pressure.
- fibub has no finite-element method, so the paper's second finding (finite elements give 11 % less) cannot be reproduced here, only quantified from its published numbers.
- Factors of safety from circular slip surfaces assume a rotational failure. A slope with a weak seam, a tension crack or fill over a buried channel may fail another way.
- These are real slopes with houses on them. Nothing here is a design check for them; the paper's own measurements and local practice are.
