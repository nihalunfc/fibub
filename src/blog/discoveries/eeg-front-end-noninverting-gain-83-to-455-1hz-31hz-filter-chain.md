Brain signals picked up from the scalp are tiny, a few tens of microvolts, and sit among mains hum and slow drift. Any EEG recorder therefore starts with an amplifier and a chain of filters before the signal reaches a converter. A 2016 conference paper describes a low-cost version built around an Arduino board, and, usefully, prints the design numbers of that analogue front end: the gain range, the filter corners and the pass band. Those are exactly the numbers the [[op-amp-calculator|op-amp circuit calculator]] and the [[filter-designer|analog filter designer]] work with, so this article checks them. Once the stages are chosen, the [[circuit-simulator]] can check them together.

## The paper in brief

The authors describe an analogue chain feeding an Arduino Mega: an op-amp active electrode, a 50 Hz twin-T notch filter whose quality factor can be tuned, a 1 Hz high-pass stage, a gain stage with a potentiometer, and a low-pass stage around 31 Hz. The pass band they aim for is about **1 to 30 Hz**, which covers the delta to beta rhythms. The text states:

- a non-inverting gain stage with a fixed **220 Ω** resistor and a **0 to 1000 Ω** potentiometer, giving a gain from about **83** to about **455**;
- a **10 nF** capacitor in parallel with a **100 kΩ** resistor, which gives a low-pass corner near **160 Hz**;
- a low-pass stage with a gain of **0.71 at 31.23 Hz** and a fall of roughly **100 times by 300 Hz**.

The paper reports design calculations, not a measured frequency response, and does not state the order of its low-pass filter. That gap is what the tools can fill.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the gains are exact.** A non-inverting amplifier has gain `1 + Rf / R1`. With the feedback resistor at 100 kΩ (the value the paper's own sums imply), the potentiometer turned to zero leaves 220 Ω to ground and a gain of **455.5**; turned fully up the resistance is 1220 Ω and the gain is **83.0**. Both are what the paper reports (Figure 2 shows the whole range).

**Step 2: the op-amp itself is not the limit.** An op-amp's closed-loop bandwidth is its gain-bandwidth product divided by the stage gain. With a general-purpose op-amp of 1 MHz (**our assumption**, the paper names no part), the stage keeps its gain up to about **2.2 kHz** at gain 455 and about **12 kHz** at gain 83. The EEG bands of interest stop near 30 Hz, so at 30 Hz the gain error is 0.04 dB or less. The feedback capacitor, not the op-amp, sets the top of the band: `1 / (2π × 100 kΩ × 10 nF)` = **159.2 Hz**, which is the paper's "about 160 Hz".

**Step 3: the 1 Hz corner implies a large capacitor.** The paper's high-pass uses a 100 kΩ resistor, and for a 1 Hz corner `1 / (2π R C)` requires **1.59 µF**. The paper (in the text available to us) states the resistor, not the capacitor, so treat that value as our inference. A first-order high-pass at 1 Hz is 3 dB down at 1 Hz and 20 dB down at 0.1 Hz.

**Step 4: the low-pass must be second order.** The paper gives two points on the low-pass curve: 0.71 (that is minus 3 dB) at 31.23 Hz, and about 100 times down at 300 Hz, a frequency ratio of 9.6. A Butterworth low-pass of order 1 is only 9.7 times down there, order 2 is **92 times** down (39.3 dB) and order 3 is 886 times down. Only order 2 is close to "roughly 100", so we infer that the paper's stage is a second-order Butterworth-type section. (A strict 40 dB requirement at that frequency would need order 3, according to the filter tool's "What order do I need?" panel.) Figure 1 shows the whole chain, a 1 Hz high-pass followed by each low-pass, with the paper's two points as dots.

**Step 5: the pass band.** With the second-order filter the chain is within 0.1 dB of flat at 10 Hz, 3.0 dB down at 1 Hz, 2.7 dB down at 30 Hz and **8.8 dB down at 50 Hz**. So the low-pass alone does not remove mains hum: the twin-T notch the paper includes has to do that job, which is why its Q is made adjustable.

**Step 6: parts for the same filter.** Asked for a second-order Butterworth low-pass at 31.23 Hz built from Sallen-Key stages with a 1 µF capacitor (**our design**, not the paper's), the tool gives two equal resistors of **3.60 kΩ**, a 1 µF capacitor to ground and a 2 µF feedback capacitor, with a quality factor of 0.7071.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/eeg-front-end-noninverting-gain-83-to-455-1hz-31hz-filter-chain.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `gainOf()`, `noiseGain()`, `bandwidth()` and `realGains()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-opamp.ts` (the [[op-amp-calculator|op-amp circuit calculator]]) and `stages()`, `response()`, `minOrder()` and `sallenKey()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-filter.ts` (the [[filter-designer|analog filter designer]]).

The resistor values, capacitor value and the two filter points come from the paper's text. The 1 MHz gain-bandwidth product, the 1.59 µF high-pass capacitor, the Butterworth shape and the Sallen-Key parts are **our assumptions or inferences**, labelled as such above. Nothing here is a measurement of the real circuit.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/op-amp-calculator.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the tool against python-control and NumPy circuit solutions:

- A real 1 MHz op-amp in inverting and non-inverting gain-of-10 circuits must match python-control at six frequencies from 10 Hz to 1 MHz to nine figures, including the phase at 100 kHz.
- Integrator, differentiator, summing and difference circuits must match an independent NumPy node-equation solve; the worst-case common-mode rejection for 1 % resistors at gain 10 must be 48 dB.
- New with this article: the stage with 220 Ω to ground must give gain 455.55 and the one with 1220 Ω gain 82.967, with closed-loop bandwidths of 2195 Hz and 12,053 Hz for a 1 MHz op-amp.

The filter tool has its own checks in `tests/sci-tools/filter-designer.mjs` against SciPy's analogue Butterworth and Chebyshev poles and frequency responses.

## Check it yourself

On the [[op-amp-calculator|op-amp circuit calculator]]:

1. Set *Circuit* to **Non-inverting amplifier**.
2. Type *R1, input resistor (Ω)* **220** (for R1 here it is the resistor to ground) and *Rf, feedback resistor (Ω)* **100k**. The gain reads **455.5**.
3. Change R1 to **1220**. The gain reads **83.0**.
4. Type *Gain-bandwidth product GBW (Hz)* **1M** and read the bandwidth: about **2.2 kHz** at 455 and about **12 kHz** at 83.

Then on the [[filter-designer|analog filter designer]]:

5. Set *Pass* to **Low-pass**, *Build as* to **Active: Sallen-Key op-amp stages**, *Shape* to **Butterworth**, *Order* **2**, *Corner frequency (Hz)* **31.23**, *Capacitor C (F)* **1u** and *Round parts to* **exact values**. You should see two resistors of about **3.6 kΩ** and capacitors of 1 µF and 2 µF.
6. Open *What order do I need?*, type *At least this cut (dB)* **40** and *at frequency (Hz)* **300**. The tool says order **3**, while order 2 reaches 39.3 dB, which is why the paper's "roughly 100 times" fits order 2.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a front-end design on paper** before soldering: gain range, noise gain and bandwidth from the resistor values alone, with [[op-amp-calculator]].
- **Infer an unstated filter order** from two stated points, as done here, and choose the real parts with [[filter-designer]].
- **Plan a passband for a biosignal**: 0.5 to 40 Hz for EEG, 0.05 to 150 Hz for ECG, 10 to 500 Hz for muscle, each with a different corner and order.
- **Teach** why a notch is needed for 50 or 60 Hz mains: a low-pass at 31 Hz is still only 9 dB down at 50 Hz.
- **Simulate the whole board** with the [[circuit-simulator]] once the stages are chosen.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation** of design numbers, not a test of the built circuit; the paper reports no measured response.
- The feedback resistor (100 kΩ) is implied by the paper's gain calculation, not named outright in the text we could read; the 1.59 µF capacitor and the second-order shape are our inferences.
- The paper labels one stage "HPF" in a heading where the context is the 31 Hz low-pass, and does not state the ADC resolution or sampling rate, so nothing about the digital side is checked here.
- The 1 MHz op-amp is our assumption. Real parts differ, and a low-power part with a smaller gain-bandwidth product would lose gain sooner at high gain.
- General education about circuits only. This is not a medical device design: never connect home-built electronics to a person without qualified guidance and proper isolation.
