Electroplating has one law at its heart: the charge you push through the cell decides how much metal lands, and nothing else. Everything that makes plating hard is the gap between that law and reality — the share of the current that goes into hydrogen instead of metal. A 2026 paper in *Coatings* measures that share directly for nickel-phosphorus baths, and watches it **fall from about 55 % to about 1 %** as phosphorous acid is added and the bath turns more acidic. Those efficiencies are exactly what the [[electroplating-calculator]] needs to turn a current into a thickness, so we closed the loop: from their efficiency to their measured film, and back out to the current density the experiment must really have used. Alongside it, the [[nernst-calculator]] gives the potentials that decide whether hydrogen or metal wins, and the [[gas-law-calculator]] turns the wasted charge into litres of gas.

## The paper in brief

The authors plated five coatings, S1 to S5, from a nickel sulfate-chloride bath with 0, then increasing amounts of **phosphorous acid** (H₃PO₃) up to 40 g/L, which is both the phosphorus source and the thing that drives the pH down. Each run lasted 30 minutes at 40 °C with stirring, on a 6 cm² window masked out on stainless steel.

The findings: phosphorus goes into the deposit fast, from about 0.2 at.% with no acid to **20 to 24 at.%** in every acid-containing bath. The **cathodic current efficiency** — the fraction of the charge that becomes coating — collapses: about **55 %** for the acid-free S1, under **15 %** for S2, and **5 % down to 1 %** for S3 to S5. S1 came out about **3.6 µm** thick; the rest are "ultrathin" films whose thicknesses appear only in a figure. Despite that, the middle bath S2 gave the best tribology, a steady friction coefficient of about 0.30 against 0.80 for the pure-nickel S1, and every coating passed adhesion testing at rating 0.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from numbers in the paper's text, using the same Faraday's-law maths the [[electroplating-calculator]] runs in the browser: nickel with two electrons per atom, molar mass 58.693 g/mol and density 8.908 g/cm³. No fitting and no simulation.

**Step 1: the printed current density cannot be right.** The paper states a current density of **0.100 A/m²**. Put that into the tool with 55 % efficiency and 30 minutes, and the answer is **3.4 nanometres** — a thousand times thinner than the 3.6 µm actually measured on S1 (Figure 2). Running the calculation backwards, the current density that gives 3.6 µm in 30 minutes at 55 % efficiency is **0.01065 A/cm², which is 1.065 A/dm², 106.5 A/m², or about 9.9 A per square foot**. That is a thoroughly ordinary Watts-bath value, and a round **1.00 A/dm²** gives 3.38 µm, within 6 % of what was measured. So the experiment almost certainly ran near 1 A/dm² and the printed unit slipped by three decades. Over the 6 cm² window that is a cell current of about **64 mA**, and the metal deposited weighs **19.2 mg**.

**Step 2: the efficiency collapse, in micrometres.** Figure 1 takes that current density and sweeps the efficiency. Because Faraday's law is linear, the paper's four efficiencies map straight onto four thicknesses for the same half-hour run:

| Sample | Efficiency (paper) | Thickness in 30 min (tool) |
|---|---|---|
| S1 (no H₃PO₃) | about 55 % | 3.60 µm — matches the measured 3.6 µm |
| S2 (5 g/L) | under 15 % | 0.98 µm |
| S3 | about 5 % | 0.33 µm |
| S5 (40 g/L) | about 1 % | 0.065 µm (65 nm) |

**Step 3: where the rest of the charge went.** The charge that does not plate metal mostly splits water. Treating the whole remainder as hydrogen (**our assumption**; some of it reduces phosphorous acid to phosphorus, which is the point of the bath), the tool's gas side gives about **6.9 mL of hydrogen at 40 °C** for the S1 run and about **15.2 mL** for S5 — more than twice as much gas from the same current, bubbling at the same small cathode. That is a plain mechanistic reason for the paper's other observations: more bubbles means more local alkalinity, rougher deposits and pitting risk, and it is why high-phosphorus baths are usually run hotter and at higher current density rather than simply more acidic.

**Step 4: how much the density assumption matters.** A deposit with 22 at.% phosphorus is lighter than nickel. Repeating the S2 case with a density of 7.3 g/cm³ (**our assumption**, a typical electroless Ni-P value) and nickel's molar mass gives **1.20 µm** instead of 0.98 µm. So the thicknesses above are good to about 20 % for the phosphorus-rich samples, and essentially exact for S1, whose phosphorus content is 0.2 at.%.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ni-p-electrodeposition-current-efficiency-faraday-thickness-current-density.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `rate` (plating rate in µm/h from current density and efficiency), `mass`, `thickness`, `timeFor`, `moles` and `gasVolume` from `src/lib/sci/faraday.ts`, the maths behind the [[electroplating-calculator]].

Inputs from the paper's text: the 30-minute plating time, the 6 cm² area, the 40 °C bath, the four current efficiencies and S1's 3.6 µm thickness. Nickel's molar mass, density and the Faraday constant are the standard reference values listed in `sources:`. Three things are **our assumptions**: that S1's deposit is close enough to pure nickel to use nickel's density (its phosphorus content is 0.2 at.%), that all the non-plating charge makes hydrogen, and the 7.3 g/cm³ used for the Ni-P density sensitivity.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` and `tests/sci-tools/electroplating-calculator.mjs` pin this maths against textbook electrochemistry:

- Copper at 2 A/dm² must plate at 26.46 µm/h and nickel at 1 A/dm² at 12.29 µm/h, the rates quoted in plating handbooks.
- Silver must obey the classic silver-coulometer figure: 1 A for one hour deposits 4.0247 g.
- Thickness, time and current must round-trip: the time worked out for 25 µm at 2 A over 100 cm² at 95 % efficiency must give back 2 A and 25 µm when fed the other way.
- One hour at 1 A must make 0.01866 mol of hydrogen, which is 0.4181 L at 0 °C and 1 atm.
- New with this article: 1.065 A/dm² for 30 minutes at 55 % efficiency must give 3.60 µm of nickel and 19.24 mg over 6 cm², the printed 0.100 A/m² must give 3.38 nm over the same run, and 1 % efficiency must give 65 nm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[electroplating-calculator]]:

1. Leave the mode on **Thickness**. In *Metal or gas* choose **Nickel (Ni, n = 2)**.
2. Set *Area plated (cm²)* to **6**, *Current (A)* to **0.0639**, *Time (min)* to **30** and *Current efficiency (%)* to **55**.
3. It reads a thickness of about **3.60 µm**, a mass of about **19.2 mg** and a current density of about **1.07 A/dm²** — the paper's measured film, from the paper's own efficiency.
4. Change *Current efficiency (%)* to **15**, then **5**, then **1**: the thickness falls to **0.98 µm**, **0.33 µm** and **0.065 µm**. That is the paper's efficiency collapse in thickness.
5. Now set *Current (A)* to **0.00006** (the printed 0.100 A/m² over 6 cm²) with efficiency back at **55**: the thickness drops to about **0.0034 µm**, which is 3.4 nm. A unit slip of three decades is easy to see this way.
6. Switch *Metal or gas* to **Hydrogen gas**, keep *Current (A)* at **0.0639** and *Time (min)* at **30**, set *Current efficiency (%)* to **45** (the share that did not plate) and *Gas temperature (°C)* to **40**: about **6.9 mL** of hydrogen.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a plating paper** before building on it: a current density, a time and a thickness must agree through Faraday's law, and unit slips show up at once ([[electroplating-calculator]]).
- **Turn a measured current efficiency into the thickness you will actually get**, so a tribology or corrosion result is reported with the film thickness it belongs to.
- **Budget the hydrogen** a low-efficiency bath will make, for ventilation, for stirring and for embrittlement risk, with the tool's gas side and the [[gas-law-calculator]].
- **Set the plating time** for a target thickness on a new bath: measure the efficiency once, then read the time off the tool instead of guessing.
- **Check whether metal or hydrogen should win** at your pH and potential with the [[nernst-calculator]], before blaming the additives.

## The fine print

- The 1.065 A/dm² is **our inference** from the paper's own thickness and efficiency, not a value the paper states. If the real efficiency was not 55 %, the inferred density moves in proportion.
- Thicknesses for S2 to S5 assume nickel's density and molar mass. A real Ni-P deposit at 22 at.% P is less dense, which makes the film thicker for the same charge, by about 20 % at the density we assumed.
- Faraday's law is exact for the charge that plates; everything uncertain here sits in the current efficiency, which the paper measured and we took as given.
- Phosphorus reduction consumes some of the "wasted" charge, so our hydrogen volumes are upper limits.
- The paper's tribology, roughness and wettability results are not checked here; only the charge-to-thickness chain is.
