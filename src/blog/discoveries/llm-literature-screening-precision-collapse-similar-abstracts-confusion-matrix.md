A systematic review starts with thousands of abstracts and ends with a handful of studies. Reading them all is the slowest part, so researchers are testing large language models (LLMs) as first-pass screeners. A 2025 study in *BioMedInformatics* did this with a twist: it sorted candidate abstracts by how **similar** they are to the trials the review actually included, and tested six models on the easy end and the hard end. The headline: the best models kept finding every target trial, but on the most similar abstracts they also let through many false alarms. This article rebuilds the study's confusion matrices with the [[confusion-matrix]] tool and shows why **accuracy** hid the problem. To see how "similar" abstracts cluster, try the [[embedding-visualiser]]; to compare two screeners' hit rates properly, the [[bayesian-ab-test]].

## The paper in brief

The authors took two published systematic reviews (one in periodontology, one on bronchoscopy), gathered thousands of candidate abstracts for each, and turned every abstract into a text **embedding**, a list of numbers that places texts with similar meaning close together. They ranked the candidates by similarity to the review's target trials and split them into four **quartiles**: Q1 least similar, Q4 most similar and hardest to tell apart. From each quartile they sampled 200 abstracts, with the nine target trials of the periodontology review among them.

Six models then screened each set, answering include or exclude: three older or small open models (OpenHermes, Flan T5 and GPT-2) and three commercial ones (Claude 3 Haiku, GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4o), each with a long **verbose** prompt and a short **concise** one. The commercial models kept **recall** (the share of target trials found) at or near 100 % everywhere, but their **precision** (the share of included abstracts that are real targets) fell sharply in Q4. GPT-4o held up best, with one false positive in Q4. The small models struggled to find the targets at all.

## Checked with the tools

The paper gives most results as percentages, not full count tables. With 200 abstracts and 9 targets per set and recall at 100 %, each stated precision fixes the number of **false positives**, and we rebuilt each run's confusion matrix from it. The rebuilt matrices give back the paper's accuracy and precision for every run we used. That the Q2 to Q4 sets also hold 9 targets in 200 is **our assumption**; the paper's own numbers fit it.

**Step 1: the rebuilt matrices.** Every run below found all 9 targets:

| Run (periodontology review) | False positives | Precision (95 % interval) | Accuracy | Balanced accuracy | MCC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claude 3 Haiku, verbose, Q1 | 5 | 64.3 % (38.8 to 83.7) | 97.5 % | 98.7 % | 0.791 |
| Claude 3 Haiku, verbose, Q3 | 7 | 56.3 % (33.2 to 76.9) | 96.5 % | 98.2 % | 0.736 |
| Claude 3 Haiku, verbose, Q4 | 47 | 16.1 % (8.7 to 27.8) | 76.5 % | 87.7 % | 0.348 |
| Claude 3 Haiku, concise, Q4 | 69 | 11.5 % (6.2 to 20.5) | 65.5 % | 81.9 % | 0.271 |
| GPT-3.5 Turbo, verbose, Q4 | 21 | 30.0 % (16.7 to 47.9) | 89.5 % | 94.5 % | 0.517 |
| GPT-4o, concise, Q4 | 3 | 75.0 % (46.8 to 91.1) | 98.5 % | 99.2 % | 0.859 |
| GPT-4o, verbose, Q4 | 1 | 90.0 % (59.6 to 98.2) | 99.5 % | 99.7 % | 0.946 |

The tool's F1 values match the paper's: 46.2 % against the paper's 46.1 % for GPT-3.5 Turbo (the paper rounds down), and 94.7 % and 85.7 % for GPT-4o.

**Step 2: why accuracy misleads.** With only 9 targets in 200, a screener that **rejects everything** scores 95.5 % accuracy while finding nothing: its balanced accuracy is 50 % and its Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC, a correlation between truth and prediction from −1 to 1, with 0 meaning no better than chance) is 0. Figure 1 shows what happens to a screener that keeps all 9 targets as false positives pile up: at Claude 3 Haiku's 47 false alarms, accuracy still reads 76.5 %, but precision is 16.1 % and MCC 0.348. In plain terms, a reviewer would read 56 abstracts to find 9. That is still far fewer than 200, which is why high recall with poor precision can be useful as a first filter, but it is not the "97 % accurate" screener the Q1 numbers suggest.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/llm-literature-screening-precision-collapse-similar-abstracts-confusion-matrix.mjs` in fibub's public code calls `metrics` (accuracy, balanced accuracy, MCC, kappa, Wilson intervals) and `binary` (precision, recall, specificity, F1) from `src/lib/sci/confusion.ts`, the maths behind the [[confusion-matrix]] tool.

The set size of 200, the 9 targets, the stated precisions, accuracies and F1 values, and the full Flan T5 and OpenHermes counts come from the paper's text. The false-positive counts are rebuilt by us from those values. Figure 1's curve is our what-if for a screener that keeps all 9 targets.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/confusion-matrix.mjs`, which checks the tool against scikit-learn and statsmodels:

- The tool's 3-class example (42, 6, 2 / 8, 37, 5 / 1, 4, 25) must give accuracy 0.8, balanced accuracy 0.8044 and MCC 0.6943, with per-class precision, recall and F1 equal to scikit-learn's to 12 digits.
- Cohen's kappa and its standard error must match statsmodels, and every Wilson interval must match `proportion_confint(method='wilson')`.
- A random 4-class set in which one class is never predicted must follow scikit-learn's zero-division rule.
- New with this article: the Claude 3 Haiku Q4 matrix (9, 0, 47, 144) must give precision 16.07 % with a Wilson interval of 8.7 % to 27.8 %, accuracy 76.5 % and MCC 0.348; rejecting everything must give 95.5 % accuracy with MCC 0.

## Check it yourself

On the [[confusion-matrix]] tool:

1. Set *Start from* to **Four counts (two classes)**. Name the classes **Target** and **Other** in *Positive class name* and *Negative class name*.
2. Type **9** in *True positives (TP)*, **0** in *False negatives (FN)*, **47** in *False positives (FP)* and **144** in *True negatives (TN)* (Claude 3 Haiku, verbose prompt, Q4).
3. The big line reads **Accuracy 76.5 % (95 % CI 70.2 to 81.8 %)**; the facts show *Balanced accuracy* **87.7 %** and *Matthews correlation (MCC)* **0.348**.
4. In the two-class table, *Precision (positive predictive value)* reads **16.1 %** with **8.7–27.8 %**, and *Sensitivity (recall, true positive rate)* **100.0 %**.
5. Now type **0**, **9**, **0**, **191** (a screener that rejects everything): accuracy is still **95.5 %**, but MCC drops to **0.000**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report a screener fairly**: give precision, recall and MCC with intervals, not accuracy alone, using the [[confusion-matrix]] tool.
- **Re-check a paper's table**: rebuild the counts from stated percentages and catch slips, like the accuracy and specificity swap here.
- **Plan reading time**: precision tells you how many abstracts a reviewer must read per real hit (56 per 9 here), which matters more than accuracy.
- **Compare two screeners' recall or precision** with the uncertainty included, using the [[bayesian-ab-test]] on the hits and totals.
- **Inspect hard cases**: plot embeddings of included and excluded abstracts in the [[embedding-visualiser]] to see why the most similar quartile is hard.

## The fine print

- We rebuilt counts from the paper's percentages; where several counts fit a percentage (for example GPT-3.5 Turbo's concise Q4 run) we left the run out.
- The 9 targets per 200 in Q2 to Q4 is our assumption; it reproduces every stated accuracy and precision we checked.
- Each set is only 200 abstracts, so all the precision intervals are wide; the paper's own conclusions are about broad patterns, not small gaps.
- The models tested were versions available in 2024; newer models may behave differently, so re-test before relying on any screener.
