An RFID reader antenna has to push as much of its power as possible into the air and as little as possible back into the transmitter. Two numbers describe how well it does that: S11, the fraction of power reflected at its input expressed in decibels, and VSWR, the same thing as a standing-wave ratio. A 2022 conference paper designs a slotted patch antenna for the 2.4 GHz ISM band, then joins eight of them into an array, and reports S11 and VSWR for both. Those pairs of numbers are exactly what the [[smith-chart|Smith chart tool]] converts between, and the feed lines the paper lists can be checked with the [[antenna-calculator|antenna size calculator]] and the [[pcb-trace-calculator|PCB trace calculator]]. How much range the gain buys is a job for the [[link-budget-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors etch a rectangular patch (42.78 × 41.32 mm) with slots on a 1.56 mm Rogers RT/duroid 5880 board (relative permittivity 2.2), fed from a 50 Ω line through a **quarter-wave transformer** 24.05 mm long and 0.72 mm wide. Simulation gives, at 2.4 GHz, an **S11 of −24.57 dB and a VSWR of 1.12** with a peak gain of 7.09 dBi. They then feed eight such patches through a power divider: the array reaches **14.61 dBi** gain, an **S11 of −13.14 dB and VSWR of 1.56**, with VSWR under 2 across about 30 MHz (2.385 to 2.415 GHz). Efficiency stays above 97 % in both. The numbers are simulated; the paper reports no measurements.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: S11 and VSWR are the same fact.** A reflection coefficient is `|Γ| = 10^(S11/20)`. For −24.57 dB that is 0.0591, and VSWR `(1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|)` is **1.126**, the paper's 1.12. For −13.14 dB, `|Γ|` is 0.2203 and VSWR is **1.565**, the paper's 1.56. The Smith chart tool gives the same, as a load of 56.3 Ω (single patch) or 78.3 Ω (array) on a 50 Ω line (we pick a purely resistive load because only |Γ| is known; **our assumption**). **Step 2: what the reflection costs.** The single patch reflects `|Γ|²` = **0.35 %** of the power, a mismatch loss of **0.015 dB**: nothing. The array reflects **4.9 %**, a loss of **0.22 dB**. That is still small, but it is fourteen times worse, which the paper does not mention; the VSWR 2 edge of its 30 MHz bandwidth corresponds to S11 of −9.5 dB, 11 % reflected and 0.51 dB lost.

**Step 3: the quarter-wave transformer.** A line a quarter wavelength long turns a load `ZL` on a line of impedance `Zq` into `Zq²/ZL`. To bring a patch edge resistance down to 50 Ω, the line impedance must be `√(50 × ZL)`. The patch model in the antenna tool, run with the paper's width of 41.32 mm, gives a patch edge resistance of **339 Ω** at 2.4 GHz (a plain patch of that width and a length of 41.5 mm; the paper's slots change its detail). The transformer line then needs `√(50 × 339)` = **130.3 Ω**, and the Smith chart tool confirms that a quarter-wave line of that impedance turns 339 Ω into exactly 50 Ω.

**Step 4: the paper's trace widths agree.** With the paper's substrate and an assumed 35 µm copper (**our assumption**), the PCB trace calculator's microstrip maths gives **127 Ω** for the 0.72 mm line, within 2.5 % of the 130 Ω the transformer needs, and **49.4 Ω** for the 4.84 mm feed line, within 1.2 % of 50 Ω. Solved the other way, 130.3 Ω needs a width of 0.67 mm and 50 Ω needs 4.75 mm (Figure 2). Its quarter-wave length also agrees: the 0.72 mm line has an effective permittivity of 1.71, so a quarter of its wavelength at 2.4 GHz is **23.9 mm**, against the paper's 24.05 mm.

**Step 5: where the paper and the plain patch model differ.** The tool's plain patch of width 41.32 mm needs a length of 41.5 mm to resonate at 2.4 GHz, while the paper's slotted patch is 42.78 mm long, about 3 % more. That is consistent with slots lengthening the current path, but we cannot check it: slotted patches are beyond this model.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/rfid-patch-antenna-array-s11-vswr-mismatch-loss-smith-chart.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `mismatch()` and `lineZin()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-smith.ts` (the [[smith-chart|Smith chart tool]]), `patch()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-antenna.ts` (the [[antenna-calculator|antenna size calculator]]) and `microstrip()` and `widthFor()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-pcb.ts` (the [[pcb-trace-calculator|PCB trace calculator]]).

Frequency, permittivity, board thickness, S11 values and widths come from the paper. The purely resistive load, the 35 µm copper thickness and the plain (unslotted) patch model are **our assumptions**. The array's S11 is read as that of its input port only.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/smith-chart.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the tool against NumPy complex arithmetic and a textbook example:

- Γ, VSWR, return loss, mismatch loss and the lossless-line input impedance must match NumPy for a set of loads and line lengths; every L-network and stub solution must turn the load into exactly Z0.
- Pozar's example 5.1 (200 − j100 Ω to 100 Ω at 500 MHz) must give 0.92 pF with 39.0 nH, or 46.1 nH with 2.61 pF.
- A quarter-wave line of 50 Ω must turn 100 Ω into 25 Ω.
- New with this article: a resistive load at S11 −24.57 dB must give VSWR 1.1256 and mismatch loss 0.0152 dB, and at −13.14 dB 1.5651 and 0.2160 dB; and 339.34 Ω through a quarter-wave 130.26 Ω line must give 50 Ω.

## Check it yourself

On the [[smith-chart|Smith chart tool]]:

1. Type *Line impedance Z0 (Ω)* **50**, *Load resistance R (Ω)* **56.28** and *Load reactance X (Ω)* **0**. You should see VSWR about **1.126**, return loss **24.57 dB** and mismatch loss **0.015 dB**.
2. Change *Load resistance R (Ω)* to **78.25**: VSWR about **1.565**, return loss **13.14 dB**, mismatch loss **0.216 dB**.
3. For the transformer, type *Line impedance Z0 (Ω)* **130.26**, *Load resistance R (Ω)* **339.34** and *Line length (wavelengths)* **0.25**. The impedance after the line should read **50 Ω** with no reactance.

Then on the [[pcb-trace-calculator|PCB trace calculator]]:

4. Choose the microstrip impedance calculation and type *Relative permittivity εr* **2.2**, a board height of **1.56** mm, copper weight 1 oz and trace width **0.72** mm: about **127 Ω**. Width **4.84** mm gives about **49 Ω**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Translate between papers:** one reports S11, another VSWR, a third return loss; the [[smith-chart|Smith chart tool]] converts them and the mismatch loss in power.
- **Sanity-check a feed network:** compare the line widths a paper lists against the impedances they need, as in step 4.
- **Compare a single element with an array** honestly: gain goes up 7.5 dB, but the match gets worse, which the loss column shows.
- **Teach matching:** the transformer, the L-network and the stub on one chart.
- **Estimate range** from gain with the [[link-budget-calculator]] once the antenna is chosen.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation** of numbers from a simulation paper, not a measurement of a built antenna.
- S11 gives only the size of the reflection, not its phase. Our resistive-load picture is one of many loads with the same VSWR.
- The paper's own text has inconsistencies we noticed (the abstract writes the array area as 117 × 475 mm², a unit slip, and its comparison table's citation numbers do not match its reference list). We used only the numbers that were self-consistent.
- The patch edge resistance comes from a textbook patch model; real slotted patches and their feed networks differ. A 3 % gap in length and a few per cent in line impedance are within what simplified models give.
- RF equipment is regulated; this is general education, not advice about transmitting. Check your national rules for the 2.4 GHz band.
