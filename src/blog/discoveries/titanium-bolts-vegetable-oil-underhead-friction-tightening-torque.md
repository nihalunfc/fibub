Titanium screws are light, strong and do not rust, which is why they turn up in aircraft, racing bikes and medical devices. They have one well-known weakness: titanium **galls**, meaning it tends to smear and stick to the surface it slides on, so friction is high and hard to predict. That matters because a screw is tightened by torque, but what holds the joint together is the **preload**, the tension in the screw. A 2025 study measured the friction of titanium M10 screws lubricated with two vegetable oils and a mineral oil, and found the friction under the screw head several times higher than for steel, with castor oil the best of the three. Friction coefficients only become useful once they are turned into torques and preloads, and that is what the [[bolt-torque-calculator]] does. To see how a screw material behaves once it is loaded, follow on with the [[stress-strain-analyser]] and the [[fatigue-sn-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

When you tighten a screw, the torque you apply goes three ways: a small part actually stretches the screw (the **pitch** term, set by the thread's slope), a larger part is lost to **thread friction**, and the rest is lost to **under-head friction**, where the head turns on the part it clamps. The friction coefficient under the head is written μb and the one in the thread μt. ISO 16047 gives a standard way to measure both, with a rig that records the total torque, the thread torque and the preload at once.

The authors tightened M10 × 1.25 titanium socket head cap screws and, for comparison, class 12.9 steel screws of the same size into steel plates, aiming for a preload of 44.5 kN. Each screw was lubricated with castor oil, fractionated coconut oil or a commercial VG46 mineral oil, tightened at 10 or 250 rpm, and re-tightened ten times. Tests stopped at the target load or at 60 N·m, whichever came first.

The thread friction hardly changed between lubricants or between titanium and steel. The under-head friction did. Steel started near 0.15 and settled at about 0.1 with coconut oil or 0.20 with castor oil. Titanium with coconut oil reached 0.40 to 0.60 in final tightening, with VG46 0.38 to 0.48, and with castor oil about 0.35 at first, falling to about 0.20 after a few re-tightenings, close to steel. The authors conclude that castor oil performs well and is worth considering for titanium joints.

## Checked with the tools

The paper reports friction coefficients; we turned them into torques and preloads with the friction split behind the [[bolt-torque-calculator]]. This is a **recomputation from the stated friction values**, with three **assumptions of ours**: a thread friction μt of 0.12 (the paper gives no number, only that it is about the same as steel), a bearing face of 16 mm outside diameter (the head of an ISO 4762 M10 screw) and a 10.5 mm hole.

**Step 1: where the torque goes.** For an M10 × 1.25 thread, the tool works out a pitch diameter of 9.188 mm and a stress area of 61.2 mm², so 44.5 kN is a tensile stress of about 727 MPa in the thread. With steel's μb of 0.15, the **nut factor** K (torque divided by preload times diameter) is 0.183, and 54 % of the torque is lost under the head, 35 % in the thread and only 11 % stretches the screw. With titanium and coconut oil at μb 0.60, K rises to 0.481 and 83 % of the torque is lost under the head; only 4 % does useful work.

**Step 2: the torque the target preload needs.** Figure 1 shows the torque for 44.5 kN as μb rises. Steel at μb 0.15 needs **81 N·m**; titanium needs **155 to 214 N·m** with coconut oil (μb 0.40 to 0.60) and **149 to 179 N·m** with VG46 (μb 0.38 to 0.48). With castor oil the start (μb 0.35) needs 140 N·m, but once μb settles to about 0.20, it needs only **96 N·m**, close to steel. That is the practical meaning of the paper's main finding: the lubricant changes the torque needed by more than a factor of two.

**Step 3: what 60 N·m buys, and a consistency check.** Turned round, a fixed torque gives very different preloads (Figure 2). At 60 N·m, the tool gives 32.8 kN for steel at μb 0.15 but only **12.5 to 17.2 kN** for titanium with coconut oil. The paper reports about **16.6 kN** for titanium with coconut oil in its 10 rpm runs, inside that range. In the 250 rpm runs the torque overshot past 80 N·m and the preload reached about **19.5 kN**; at 80 N·m the tool gives **17.9 to 19.9 kN** for the μb of 0.48 to 0.55 the paper reports for that lubricant in pre-tightening. The friction values and the preloads in the paper agree with each other through the standard torque formula.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/titanium-bolts-vegetable-oil-underhead-friction-tightening-torque.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `torqueFriction` (the ISO 16047 torque split T = F (P/2π + μt d₂ / (2 cos 30°) + μb Dm/2)), `stressArea` and `pitchDia` from `src/lib/sci/eng-bolt.ts`, the maths behind the [[bolt-torque-calculator]]. This is the same equation the paper uses to work out its friction coefficients.

The thread size, target preload, 60 N·m limit, under-head friction ranges and the 16.6 and 19.5 kN preloads come from the paper's text. The thread friction of 0.12, the 16 mm bearing face and the 10.5 mm hole are **our assumptions**.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/bolt-torque-calculator.mjs`, which checks the calculator against NumPy and SciPy:

- The stress area of an M10 × 1.5 thread must be 57.99 mm², the well-known table value, and of a 1/2-13 UNC thread 0.1419 in².
- The long-form torque for 26.09 kN in an M10 × 1.5 screw with μt and μb of 0.12 must be 42.893 N·m (K 0.16441), as an independent NumPy calculation gives.
- New with this article: an M10 × 1.25 screw tightened to 44.5 kN with μt 0.12, μb 0.15 and a 16 mm by 10.5 mm bearing face must need 81.40 N·m (K 0.1829, 54 % under the head), and 60 N·m at μb 0.60 must give 12.47 kN.

## Check it yourself

On the [[bolt-torque-calculator]]:

1. In *Bolt and thread*, keep *Thread system* on **ISO metric**, and type *Nominal diameter d* **10** and *Pitch (mm)* **1.25**.
2. In *Tightening*, set *Find* to **Preload from a torque** and type **60** in *Torque*.
3. Set *Method* to **Thread and under-head friction**, then type *Thread friction μt* **0.12**, *Under-head friction μb* **0.15**, *Bearing face outside diameter* **16** and *Hole diameter* **10.5**.
4. The result reads that 60 N·m gives a preload of about **32.8 kN**, and the *Torque* line shows K about **0.183**, with about 54 % of the torque lost under the head.
5. Change *Under-head friction μb* to **0.6**: the preload drops to about **12.5 kN** and K rises to about **0.481**. Change it to **0.2**, the settled castor-oil value: about **27.8 kN**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn friction measurements into torque specifications**: type measured μt and μb and read the torque for any preload, as in Step 2.
- **Check a friction study for consistency**: compare its preloads and torques with what its own friction values predict, as in Step 3.
- **See where the torque goes**: the pitch, thread and under-head shares show at once whether a lubricant change will help, and where.
- **Plan joints with scattered friction**: the *If K is 25 % off* line shows how far the preload can stray for the same torque.
- **Follow the joint under load**: with the preload known, the *Joint* card gives the bolt's share of an external load and the load that would open the joint.

## The fine print

- The paper gives no thread friction numbers, the titanium grade or the screw's strength class; our μt of 0.12 and the bearing face size are assumptions, and our torques are estimates, not the paper's measurements.
- The paper lists the screws as M10 × 1.25 and the nuts as M10 × 1.5; we used 1.25. With 1.5 the torque for 44.5 kN at μb 0.15 is 82.7 N·m instead of 81.4, a small change.
- Friction values change with each re-tightening and with speed; we used the ranges the paper states, not single measured curves.
- This is a short conference paper; the friction ranges are read from its text, and its figures hold more detail.
