Heat kills bacteria at a steady rate on a log scale, and two numbers describe the whole process: the **D-value**, the minutes at one temperature that kill 90 % of them, and the **z-value**, the extra degrees that make the killing ten times faster. A study of *alheira*, a Portuguese smoked sausage made from a raw meat-and-bread batter, measured D-values for *Salmonella* Typhimurium in that batter at four temperatures and fitted a z-value to them. Those four pairs of numbers are all in the paper's text, which makes the whole result something anybody can rebuild in a browser: this article refits them with the [[thermal-process-calculator]], gets the paper's z-value, reference D and 70 °C cooking time back, and then shows where the fit is weakest. For the canning convention of F₀ use the [[f0-calculator]], and to turn a cooking step into a written control use the [[haccp-plan-builder]].

## The paper in brief

The authors inoculated alheira batter with *Salmonella* Typhimurium at about 7.0 to 7.5 log CFU/g (CFU means colony-forming units, the countable living cells) and heated 10 g bags at **54, 57, 60 and 63 °C**, taking samples over 100 minutes at the coolest temperature and over 5 minutes at the hottest. Survivor counts at each temperature fell along a straight line on a log scale, which is the **first-order** or log-linear model:

- log₁₀ N(t) = log₁₀ N₀ − t ÷ D

Fitting that line at each temperature gave D-values of **26.52, 7.936, 3.663 and 0.348 minutes**. The paper also reports the same thing as an exponential death rate, which is simply 1 ÷ D: 0.038, 0.126, 0.273 and 2.872 log CFU per minute.

The second step is the **Bigelow model**, a straight line through those four D-values on a log scale:

- log₁₀ D = log₁₀ D_ref − (T − T_ref) ÷ z

With a reference temperature of 50 °C the paper reports log₁₀ D_ref = 2.302 (about **200 minutes** per log at 50 °C) and **z = 5.016 °C**, with R² = 0.947. From that line it concludes that a 7-log reduction at 70 °C needs only **9.0 seconds**.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the four D-values printed in the paper**. No data were simulated and nothing was read off a figure.

**Step 1: the Bigelow line.** Typing the four temperature and D-value pairs into the *z-value from D-values* card gives **z = 5.0153 °C** and **R² = 0.9469**, against the paper's 5.016 °C and 0.947. The same fit puts log₁₀ D at 50 °C at **2.3020**, so **D_ref = 200.4 minutes**, against the paper's 2.302 and "200 min". The agreement is exact to the digits the paper prints, which means the paper's fit is the ordinary least-squares line and nothing else (Figure 1).

**Step 2: the 70 °C claim.** Extending that line to 70 °C gives **D = 0.02061 minutes**, which rounds to the paper's 0.021. Seven log reductions therefore need 7 × D = **0.1443 minutes, or 8.66 seconds**. The paper says 9.0 seconds, the same number rounded from its own slightly different z. So the headline cooking figure checks out: at a centre temperature of 70 °C, this *Salmonella* in this batter is gone in under ten seconds.

**Step 3: where the fit is weak.** R² of 0.947 sounds high, but with only four points it hides a real miss. The fitted line puts D at 63 °C at **0.513 minutes**, while the measured value is **0.348**. In practical terms, a 7-log kill at 63 °C takes **3.59 minutes** by the line and **2.44 minutes** by the measured D: a 47 % difference at the hottest temperature tested. The 63 °C D-value also carries the paper's largest standard error (0.763 on an exponential death rate of 2.872, about 27 %), which is what you expect when a whole log-linear curve has to be caught inside five minutes. Figure 2 turns the line into the number a cook or a process engineer wants, the minutes for a 7-log kill at each centre temperature.

**Step 4: a real cook is not a hold.** A sausage centre does not jump to a temperature and sit there, so the lethality has to be added up over the heating curve. This is the **general method**: at every instant the lethal rate is L = 10^((T − T_ref) ÷ z), and F is the area under L against time. We put an **assumed** heating curve through the tool's profile card: a gentle cook in which the centre creeps up to 58.1 °C after 30 minutes and then cools (**our curve**, not measured, and not from the paper). With the paper's z and a 70 °C reference it gives **F = 0.043 minutes**, which is **2.08 log reductions** — far short of seven, even though the sausage spent half an hour in the "nearly cooked" range (Figure 3). A z-value as small as 5 °C means almost all of the killing happens in the last few degrees.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/salmonella-alheira-sausage-batter-bigelow-z-value-recomputed.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `zValue` (the Bigelow least-squares fit, which also returns D at any temperature), `fNeeded` (F = D × log reductions) and `fProfile` (the general method by the trapezoid rule) from `src/lib/sci/thermproc.ts`, the maths behind the [[thermal-process-calculator]].

The four temperatures and four D-values are the paper's, quoted from its running text. The heating curve in Figure 3 is **our assumption**: a first-order rise towards 59 °C read every two minutes, with a linear cool-down. The 70 °C reference temperature in that calculation is our choice, so that F can be read directly as log reductions of this organism.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/thermal-process-calculator.mjs`, which pins the tool against NumPy and SciPy:

- A made-up five-point survivor curve must give D = 2.9559926 min, log₁₀ N₀ = 5.9973723 and R² = 0.99993701, as `numpy.polyfit` does.

## Check it yourself

On the [[thermal-process-calculator|F-value calculator]]:

1. In the *Reference* card, set *Reference temperature, T_ref (°C)* to **50** and leave *z-value (°C)* as it is for now.
2. In *z-value from D-values*, paste the paper's four pairs, one per line: **54, 26.52**; **57, 7.936**; **60, 3.663**; **63, 0.348**.
3. It shows **z = 5.02 °C**, with *D at 50 °C* **200 min**, *R²* **0.9469** and *D-values* **4**.
4. Now change *Reference temperature, T_ref (°C)* to **70**. The same card's *D at 70 °C* fact changes to about **0.0206 min**.
5. Press **Use this z and D above and below**. The *z-value (°C)* box becomes **5.02** and *D at T_ref (min)* fills with **0.021**, the rounded D at 70 °C.
6. In *F needed for a log reduction*, type **10000000** in *Starting count, N₀* and **1** in *Count wanted, N*. It reads **F = 0.147 min (7 log reductions)**, that is 8.8 seconds. Replace the rounded 0.021 with **0.0206** and it reads **F = 0.144 min**, our 8.66 seconds.
7. Change *Count wanted, N* to **1e-3** to ask for ten logs instead: F becomes about **0.206 min**, or 12 seconds.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Refit a published z-value** from the D-values in a table, and see its R² and the D it predicts at the temperature you actually cook at.
- **Check a cooking claim** such as "70 °C for 15 seconds", by comparing it with 7 × D at that temperature from the paper you are relying on.
- **Turn a logger file into lethality**: paste a time-and-temperature profile from a thermocouple into the profile card and get F and the log reductions by the general method, with any reference temperature and z.
- **Teach why z matters**: a z of 5 °C and a z of 10 °C give the same D at the reference temperature but completely different answers five degrees away.
- **Separate the pathogen from the convention**: F₀ (121.1 °C, z = 10 °C) is for spores in cans, not for *Salmonella* in a sausage; the [[f0-calculator]] does the canning convention, this tool does any reference you set.

## The fine print

- The paper is a short conference paper from an electronic Foods conference, with four D-values and no survivor-count table, so we could refit the Bigelow line but not the primary curves behind it.
- Its z-value carries a standard error of 0.839 °C. Put z = 4.2 or 5.9 °C through the same sums and the time for a 7-log kill at 70 °C moves by roughly a factor of two.
- The 54 and 57 °C D-values come from heating that lasted 100 and 50 minutes. Long holds at those temperatures can let heat-shocked cells adapt, which the log-linear model does not allow for.
- The heating curve in Figure 3 and its 70 °C reference are ours. Nothing in it is a measurement of alheira.
- D- and z-values belong to one organism in one food. Do not carry these numbers to another product.
