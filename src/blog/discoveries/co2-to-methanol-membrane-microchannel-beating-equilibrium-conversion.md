Turning carbon dioxide into methanol with hydrogen is an equilibrium reaction that stops early: it makes one water molecule for every methanol molecule, and that water pushes the reaction back. A 2026 paper in *Membranes* attacks the problem directly by coating a copper catalyst straight onto a water-selective zeolite membrane, so the water leaves as it forms. The reactor reaches **82 % carbon dioxide conversion** at 220 °C and 30 bar, which the authors call about three times the equilibrium value. That claim is exactly the kind fibub's [[equilibrium-solver]] can test: this article recomputes the limit at the reactor's own conditions, reproduces the 27 % figure the paper quotes from elsewhere, and works out how much of the water has to disappear. The formation data behind it comes from the [[thermochemistry-calculator]], and the pressures and molar volumes from the [[gas-law-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The reactor is a stainless steel block with five microchannels, 500 µm deep and 60 mm long, holding a flat-sheet **LTA zeolite membrane** (4.1 Å pores, water-loving) on an alumina support, with a 45 µm layer of **Cu-ZnO-Al₂O₃ catalyst** (42 mg of it) spread on top. Nitrogen sweeps the far side of the membrane to keep the water partial pressure there low.

Fed hydrogen and carbon dioxide at **3:1**, at **220 °C and 3.0 MPa** with a weight hourly space velocity of 18,000 h⁻¹, the reactor gives a **carbon dioxide conversion of about 82 %**, a **methanol selectivity of 51.6 %** and so a **methanol yield of 42.3 %** over a three-hour run. The authors explain the result by Le Chatelier's principle: pulling water out shifts the reaction forward. For a comparison they quote an equilibrium conversion of **27 %**, taken from another study and, as they note, measured at **250 °C and 5.0 MPa** rather than at their own conditions.

## Checked with the tools

All of this is a **recomputation** from standard thermodynamic data and the paper's stated conditions. We did not refit anything to the paper's measurements.

**Step 1: the equilibrium constant.** From the formation data listed in the sources, the methanol reaction CO₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ CH₃OH + H₂O has ΔH° = **−49.32 kJ/mol** and ΔG° = **+3.49 kJ/mol** at 25 °C, so **Kp(25 °C) = 0.2447 bar⁻²**. The tool's van 't Hoff step, with the enthalpy held constant, gives **Kp(220 °C) = 9.375 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻²** and **Kp(250 °C) = 4.703 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻²**. The reaction is exothermic, so heating it makes the equilibrium worse, while the catalyst needs heat to work at all: that is the whole difficulty of methanol synthesis.

**Step 2: does our thermodynamics reproduce the paper's quoted 27 %?** Solve the ICE table in partial pressures at 250 °C with a 3:1 feed and squeeze the volume until the total pressure is back to 50 bar, and the answer is **27.28 % conversion**. The paper's quoted 27 % is reproduced to better than half a percentage point, which tells us the method below is sound.

**Step 3: the limit at the reactor's own conditions.** Repeat at 220 °C and 30 bar. In a closed vessel started at 30 bar the tool gives **22.60 %** conversion, with the total pressure falling to **26.61 bar** because four molecules become two. Squeezing the volume to **0.8775** of its original size brings the pressure back to 30 bar and raises the conversion to **24.51 %**, which is the proper constant-pressure equilibrium value.

So the right baseline for this reactor is **24.5 %**, not 27 %. Lower pressure and lower temperature pull in opposite directions here, and at these conditions the pressure wins.

**Step 4: what the 82 % should be compared with.** Carbon dioxide can also take a second route, the reverse water-gas shift, CO₂ + H₂ ⇌ CO + H₂O, and the paper's 51.6 % methanol selectivity says that about half of the converted carbon dioxide went that way. The same tool gives the reverse shift its own equilibrium conversion at 220 °C: **13.08 %** for a 3:1 feed, and because that reaction does not change the number of gas molecules, pressure makes no difference to it. Equilibrium for the two routes together therefore sits somewhere between 24.5 % and the sum of the two, which is still far below 82 %.

The cleanest like-for-like comparison is the methanol one: the reactor's **methanol yield of 42.3 %** against the methanol equilibrium of **24.5 %**, which it exceeds by a factor of **1.73**.

| Check | Paper says | The tool gives |
|---|---|---|
| Kp at 220 °C | not given | 9.375 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻² |
| Equilibrium conversion, 250 °C and 50 bar | 27 % (quoted from another study) | 27.28 % |
| Equilibrium conversion, 220 °C and 30 bar | not given | 24.51 % (closed vessel: 22.60 %) |
| Reverse water-gas shift at 220 °C | not given | 13.08 %, pressure-independent |
| Measured methanol yield against the limit | "about 3 times equilibrium" | 42.3 % is 1.73 × the methanol equilibrium |

**Step 5: how much water must leave.** Now use the equilibrium condition the other way. Hold the conversion at a chosen value, keep the total pressure at 30 bar, and ask what water partial pressure makes the reaction quotient equal Kp:

- for the measured **methanol yield of 42.3 %**, the water partial pressure must be **0.81 bar**, which means **82 %** of the water made has to have left;
- for a conversion of **82 %** by the methanol route alone, it would need **0.024 bar**, that is **99.85 %** of the water gone.

The first is a demanding but believable duty for a 20 µm zeolite membrane with a nitrogen sweep. The second is not, which is another sign that the 82 % figure is the sum of two reactions rather than a methanol equilibrium pushed that far.

## How we made the graphs

Both figures and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/co2-to-methanol-membrane-microchannel-beating-equilibrium-conversion.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `parseReaction`, `solveIce`, `quotient` and `kAtT` from `src/lib/sci/equilibrium.ts`, the maths behind the [[equilibrium-solver]]. The constant-pressure answers come from solving the ICE table and then rescaling the pressures until the equilibrium total is the working pressure, which is what the tool's volume control does; three or four passes are enough to converge.

From the paper: 220 °C, 3.0 MPa, the 3:1 feed, the 82 % conversion, the 51.6 % selectivity, the 42.3 % yield and the 27 % figure it quotes. From the literature: the four standard formation enthalpies and Gibbs energies. **Our assumptions**: that the reaction enthalpy is constant between 25 °C and 250 °C (a van 't Hoff approximation, worth a few per cent in Kp), that the gases are ideal at 30 to 50 bar, and that the water-removal calculation can treat the reaction side as one well-mixed volume at a fixed water partial pressure.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the equilibrium module against textbook problems:

- H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI with 0.5 M each and Kc = 54.3 must give the standard answer, and 0.1 M acetic acid with Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ must give pH 2.875;
- silver chloride with Ksp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ must dissolve to 1.8 × 10⁻⁹ M in 0.10 M chloride;
- the solver must stay exact for K as small as 10⁻³⁰ and as large as 10³⁰, recovering K from its own answer;
- Kp must follow Kc(RT)^Δn, with a round trip at Δn = −2, and the van 't Hoff step must raise K with temperature for an endothermic reaction and lower it for an exothermic one.

New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/equilibrium-solver.mjs`: Kp for methanol synthesis must be 0.2446687 bar⁻² at 25 °C and 9.375186 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻² at 220 °C; the ICE table at 30 bar in a closed vessel must give 22.6035 % conversion with a total pressure of 26.6095 bar; the constant-pressure answers must be 24.50799 % at 220 °C and 30 bar and 27.27806 % at 250 °C and 50 bar; and the water partial pressure that allows 42.3 % conversion must be 0.809929 bar, that is 82.086 % of it removed.

2. Set *K is* to **Kp (pressures in bar)**, type *K* **0.2447** and *Temperature (°C)* **25**.
3. Under *Starting amounts* type **7.5** for CO₂ and **22.5** for H₂ (a 3:1 feed at 30 bar in total), leaving methanol and water at 0.
4. In the Le Chatelier card, type *ΔH° of the reaction (kJ/mol)* **-49.32** and *New temperature (°C)* **220**. The page moves K by the van 't Hoff equation to about **9.38 × 10⁻⁵** and the conversion falls to about **22.6 %**, with the total pressure down at **26.6 bar**.
5. Now type *New volume ÷ old volume* **0.8775**. That squeezes the mixture back to 30 bar and the conversion rises to about **24.5 %**: the paper's real equilibrium baseline.
6. To check the figure the paper quotes, start again with *K* **4.703e-5** at **250 °C**, amounts **12.5** and **37.5** (3:1 at 50 bar) and a volume ratio of **0.8636**. You should read about **27.3 %**.
7. For the other route, type **CO2(g) + H2(g) ⇌ CO(g) + H2O(g)** with *K* **6.865e-3** and amounts **7.5** and **22.5**: about **13.1 %**, and squeezing the volume changes nothing, because the number of gas molecules does not change.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check an "exceeds equilibrium" claim** at the conditions actually used. Quoted equilibrium values usually belong to some other temperature and pressure, and the difference here is 2.8 percentage points.
- **Separate the routes.** Running each reaction's own ICE table shows how much of a conversion figure a side reaction could account for before any catalyst is credited.
- **Size a membrane duty.** Turning a target conversion into the water partial pressure it needs, as in step 5, gives a permeation requirement that can be compared with a measured water flux.
- **Plan an operating window.** Figure 1 shows how little is gained by heating and how much by pressure for this reaction, which is the whole argument for in-situ water removal.
- **Teach Le Chatelier with real numbers**, including the part students miss: at constant pressure the shrinking gas volume is itself part of the shift.

## The fine print

- Our equilibrium constants come from standard formation data with a constant reaction enthalpy. That is good to a few per cent in Kp over this temperature range; the agreement with the paper's quoted 27 % suggests it is adequate.
- We treat the gases as ideal. At 30 to 50 bar and 220 °C the fugacity corrections are small but not zero, and they slightly favour the forward reaction.
- The two-reaction system is not solved simultaneously; we give each route's own limit and compare the methanol yield with the methanol limit.
- The water-removal figures assume one well-mixed reaction volume. In a real membrane microchannel the water partial pressure varies across the catalyst layer, so the true requirement is somewhere between our number and no removal at all.
- Conversion, selectivity, yield and the reactor's geometry are quoted from the paper; the three-hour run says nothing about long-term stability, as the authors note.
