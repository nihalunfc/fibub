A spectrophotometer that measures how much light a sample absorbs is only as good as the filter used to check it. A 2025 paper from the International Metrology Congress characterises three plastic reference filters on a primary spectrophotometric system and, unusually, prints its whole uncertainty budget: five influences, in absorbance units, for each filter. That makes it a rare worked example anyone can rebuild, and a good test of the [[uncertainty-calculator]], which follows the GUM and adds a Monte Carlo check. If you are using such a filter to set up a method, the [[calibration-curve]] and [[beer-lambert-calculator]] are the next two steps, and the [[control-charts]] tool watches the drift this paper is really about.

## The paper in brief

The filters are plastic films used to calibrate nano-volume spectrophotometers, the kind that read a one- or two-microlitre drop. The authors measure their spectral absorbance on a primary system — xenon flash lamp, single monochromator, double split beam, semiconductor detector, 190 to 1100 nm — and then list what limits the value they can certify.

Their budget has five entries per filter: the **spectrophotometric system** itself (dispersion, drift, stray light, resolution, wavelength setting), bundled as 0.00104 absorbance for all three; the **one-year stability** of the filter, 0.0104 to 0.0173; **homogeneity** across the filter's area; the effect of **exposure to light**; and **temperature**. The combined standard uncertainties come out at 0.0106, 0.0115 and 0.0177 absorbance, and the expanded uncertainties at k = 2 are 0.0213, 0.0231 and 0.0356.

## Checked with the tools

The Uncertainty propagation calculator takes a formula and a list of measured quantities with their uncertainties. A budget of this shape is written as the certified value plus one zero-valued correction per influence, so we typed `A = 0.5 + sys + stab + homo + light + temp`, with each correction at zero and the paper's value as its standard uncertainty. The 0.5 is an illustrative certified absorbance (**ours**: the paper's certified values were not in the text we could read). Because every term adds, the sensitivity coefficients are all exactly 1 and the result does not depend on that 0.5 at all.

**The budget checks out.** Combining all five terms in quadrature reproduces each of the paper's combined uncertainties, and doubling them gives its expanded uncertainties:

| Filter | Paper's uᶜ | Tool's uᶜ | Paper's U (k = 2) | Tool's U at 95.45 % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FOL1 | 0.0106 | 0.010614 | 0.0213 | 0.021228 (k = 2.000) |
| FOL2 | 0.0115 | 0.011557 | 0.0231 | 0.023115 (k = 2.000) |
| FOL3 | 0.0177 | 0.017783 | 0.0356 | 0.035566 (k = 2.000) |

Two small things worth saying out loud. First, the paper's uᶜ values are its own numbers rounded **down** rather than to nearest (0.011557 printed as 0.0115), which is why its expanded values are not exactly twice them. Second, the tool's coverage factor comes out as exactly 2.000 at 95.45 % coverage because every input here is a Type B estimate with no degrees of freedom attached; if even one term came from a handful of repeated readings, the Welch-Satterthwaite rule in the tool would push k above 2, and the paper's k = 2 would be slightly optimistic. A Monte Carlo run of 200,000 draws agrees with the GUM result to four figures (standard deviation 0.010607 against 0.010614 for FOL1).

**One term is the budget.** The tool's "Where the uncertainty comes from" table gives each influence's share of the variance:

| Influence | FOL1 | FOL2 | FOL3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-year stability | 96.0 % | 90.6 % | 94.6 % |
| Temperature | 2.6 % | 0.0 % | 0.0 % |
| Spectrophotometric system | 1.0 % | 0.8 % | 0.3 % |
| Light exposure | 0.1 % | 5.9 % | 3.0 % |
| Homogeneity | 0.3 % | 2.7 % | 2.0 % |

**So what would actually improve the standard?** The tool answers that by changing one line. Halving the stability term — in practice, certifying the filter every six months instead of every year, if drift grows with time — cuts uᶜ by **47 %** (FOL1 0.010614 → 0.005615; FOL3 0.017783 → 0.00958). Nothing else comes close: a perfect thermostat, a perfect instrument and a perfectly uniform film together buy under 5 %.

| Check | What the tool gives |
|---|---|
| uᶜ from all five terms, FOL1 / FOL2 / FOL3 | 0.010614 / 0.011557 / 0.017783 |
| uᶜ from the stability term alone | 0.0104 / 0.0110 / 0.0173 |
| uᶜ with the stability term halved | 0.005615 / 0.006544 / 0.009580 |
| Coverage factor at 95.45 %, all Type B | k = 2.000 |
| Monte Carlo standard deviation, FOL1 | 0.010607 (GUM: 0.010614) |
| If the four Type B terms were ± half-widths, not 1-sigma | 0.010472 / 0.011189 / 0.017463 |

That last line matters because the paper does not say which of its entries are standard uncertainties and which are the half-widths of a range. We read all five as standard uncertainties. Treating the four small ones as rectangular half-widths instead (dividing by √3, which the tool does for you) changes uᶜ by 1.3 to 3.2 % — a reassuring answer, and one that only takes a change of the *Kind* dropdown to find.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/spectrophotometer-filter-uncertainty-budget.mjs` in fibub's public code. It uses exactly the pieces the tool page uses: `splitName`, `parse`, `variables` and `compile` from `src/lib/sci/expr.ts` for the formula, then `propagate` (GUM first-order propagation with numerical sensitivity coefficients, Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom and a coverage factor from the t distribution) and `monteCarlo` (JCGM 101:2008, 200,000 draws, fixed seed) from `src/lib/sci/uncertainty.ts`.

Every uncertainty value comes from the paper's budget tables. **Our assumptions**: the certified absorbance of 0.5 (which cancels), reading all five entries as standard uncertainties with no correlation between them, and the sweep in Figure 2 holding the other four terms at the paper's values.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` pins the GUM routine itself, and the new `tests/sci-tools/uncertainty-calculator.mjs` pins this paper's budget:

- Ohm's law, R = V/I with V = 5 ± 0.01 V and I = 0.2 ± 0.0005 A, must give R = 25 ohms with uᶜ = 0.08004 ohms and k = 2.0.
- The pendulum, g = 4 pi² L / T² with L = 1 m ± a 0.001 m half-width and five period readings (2.01, 2.00, 2.02, 1.99, 2.01 s), must give g = 9.81 m/s², uᶜ = 0.050 and U = 0.14 m/s², and the Monte Carlo interval must hold g and match U to within 10 %.
- New with this article: the paper's three five-term budgets must give uᶜ = 0.010614, 0.011557 and 0.017783 absorbance and U = 0.021228, 0.023115 and 0.035566 at k = 2.000, with infinite effective degrees of freedom and every sensitivity coefficient equal to 1; the stability share must be 96.01 %, 90.59 % and 94.64 %; each filter's budget must sit within 5 % of its stability term alone; halving that term must give 0.005615; reading the four small terms as rectangular half-widths must give 0.010472; and a 200,000-draw Monte Carlo run with the tool's fixed seed must give 0.010607.

## Check it yourself

On the [[uncertainty-calculator]] tool page:

1. In *What you calculate*, type **A = 0.5 + sys + stab + homo + light + temp**. Five measurement rows appear.
2. Leave every *Value* at **0** and every *Kind* on **± standard uncertainty**. In the *±* boxes type, for filter FOL1: **0.00104** (sys), **0.0104** (stab), **0.0006** (homo), **0.0004** (light), **0.0017** (temp).
3. With *Coverage* on **95 % (2 sigma)**, the result reads **A = 0.5000 ± 0.0212** with *Standard uncertainty u* **0.010614** and *Coverage factor k* **2.00**.
4. The *Where the uncertainty comes from* table puts **stab** at about **96 %** of the variance and everything else in the noise. The *Monte Carlo check* gives a standard deviation of **0.01061**.
5. Change *stab* to **0.0052** and watch the standard uncertainty fall to **0.005615**: halving one term halves the budget.
6. Put the FOL3 column in instead (**0.00104**, **0.0173**, **0.0025**, **0.0031**, **0.0001**) and you get **0.017783** and an expanded **0.0356**, the paper's value for its worst filter.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Reproduce a published budget** before you rely on its certificate: five numbers and a formula are enough (Uncertainty propagation calculator).
- **Find the term worth spending money on.** Here it is recalibration frequency, not thermostats (Uncertainty propagation calculator).
- **Check a coverage factor.** k = 2 is right only when the degrees of freedom are large; the tool works out the effective value for you.
- **Test how much the unstated distributions matter**, by switching entries between standard uncertainty, rectangular and triangular.
- **Carry the filter's uncertainty into a method**: feed it to the [[calibration-curve]] tool as the standard's uncertainty, and watch the instrument's own drift between checks with the [[control-charts]] tool.

## The fine print

- The uncertainty values are the paper's; the certified absorbance of 0.5 is **ours** and cancels out of everything here.
- The paper does not state distributions or sensitivity coefficients. We read all entries as standard uncertainties with unit sensitivity, which reproduces its combined values, and show above what the alternative reading costs.
- We assume the five influences are independent. A correlation between, say, stability and light exposure would change the combined value, and the GUM has terms for that which this budget does not report.
- "Halving the stability term" is an illustration of the budget's shape, not a claim about how these particular films drift.
- The paper is a conference article from a metrology congress; its budget is as printed and we did not re-measure anything.
