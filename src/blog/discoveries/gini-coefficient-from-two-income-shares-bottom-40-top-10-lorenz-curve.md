The **Gini coefficient** is the most quoted number for inequality: 0 when everyone has the same income, 1 when one person has it all. Working it out properly needs the whole income distribution, but many countries and years publish only a few summary shares, such as how much the poorest 40 % or the richest 10 % receive. A 2025 paper in *PLOS ONE* shows that just **two** such shares, the bottom 40 % and the bottom 90 % (the top 10 % is what is left), are enough to estimate the Gini to within about 0.002. This article reruns the paper's simulations with the maths of the [[gini-lorenz]] tool and shows where the shortcut holds and where it slips. To draw the income distributions themselves, use the [[distribution-explorer]]; to weight survey incomes before you start, the [[survey-weighting]] tool.

## The paper in brief

The **Lorenz curve** plots the share of all income received by the poorest x of the population; the Gini coefficient is twice the area between it and the straight line of perfect equality. The authors fit a simple two-parameter curve, L(x) = x − x^p (1 − x)^q, adapted from a three-parameter form by Kakwani. Two known points fix p and q exactly, through a pair of linear equations in the logarithms, and the Gini then has a closed form, **G = 2B(p + 1, q + 1)**, where B is the Beta function from calculus.

They tried six pairs of population shares. The pair **40 % and 90 %** worked best: on 16 economies from the World Income Inequality Database it missed the published Gini by less than 0.004 in all 16 (by 0.002 or less in 11 of them), and on thousands of survey records it had a root-mean-square error (RMSE, the typical size of the miss) of **0.00180** (World Bank PIP, 2383 records) and **0.00173** (Luxembourg Income Study, 5545 records). In simulations of 1000 samples drawn from a **log-normal** distribution (incomes whose logarithm follows a bell curve), its RMSE was **0.00311** at 1000 incomes and **0.00255** at 10,000. They call an estimate valid when it misses by less than 0.01.

## Checked with the tools

The paper's real survey records are not in its text, so we checked its **simulations**, which it describes fully. We drew our own random samples from the paper's log-normal design, worked out each sample's true Gini and Lorenz curve with the Gini and Lorenz tool's maths, read off the two Lorenz points, and applied the paper's formula. This is a **re-run of the paper's simulation with our own random draws**, not its data.

**Step 1: one large sample.** The theoretical Gini of this log-normal is 2Φ(1.1021/√2) − 1 = **0.5642**, the paper's 0.56420. Our sample of 10,000 incomes has a Gini of **0.5606** from all incomes, with the bottom 40 % holding **8.80 %** of income and the bottom 90 % holding **57.5 %**. Those two numbers give p = 1.025 and q = 0.442, and a two-point Gini of **0.5590**, a miss of 0.0016. Figure 1 shows how closely the fitted curve follows the real one.

**Step 2: 1000 samples at each size.** Repeating the draw 1000 times at each of four sample sizes, the 40/90 pair gives these RMSEs:

| Incomes per sample | Paper's RMSE (40/90) | Our re-run (40/90) |
|---|---|---|
| 500 | 0.00366 | 0.00356 |
| 1000 | 0.00311 | 0.00311 |
| 5000 | 0.00258 | 0.00257 |
| 10,000 | 0.00255 | 0.00253 |

The agreement is close at every size. The error does not shrink towards zero with more data: it settles near 0.0025, because the two-parameter curve is not exactly the log-normal's Lorenz curve. That floor is a **model error**, not sampling noise. In our runs the estimate always came out slightly **below** the true Gini at 10,000 incomes (by 0.0003 to 0.0041), and every one of the 1000 estimates at 1000 incomes or more passed the paper's 0.01 validity test.

**Step 3: why 40 and 90?** We also tried the pairs 30/80 and 50/90, and a second income shape, a **Beta-2** distribution built from the paper's stated parameters (Figure 2). On the log-normal, 50/90 is even better than 40/90 (RMSE 0.0007 at 10,000 incomes), but on the Beta-2 it misses badly (RMSE about 0.0096, and only 65 to 81 % of estimates valid). The 30/80 pair is middling on both. The 40/90 pair is the only one that stays near or below 0.003 on both shapes, which supports the paper's choice: it is the **robust** pair, not the best on every shape.

**Step 4: two shares can beat ten.** If you only have **decile** shares (ten groups of 10 %), the Gini and Lorenz tool's grouped mode joins them with straight lines, which assumes everyone inside a decile earns the same. For our 10,000-income sample that gives **0.5449**, 0.016 below the true 0.5606. The two-point curve, using only two of those same shares, gives **0.5590**. A smooth curve through two points beats straight lines through ten, because the straight lines throw away the inequality inside each group.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/gini-coefficient-from-two-income-shares-bottom-40-top-10-lorenz-curve.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `inequality` (Gini, Lorenz curve, bottom 40 % and top 10 % shares), `lorenzAt` (a point on the Lorenz curve) and `fromGroups` (grouped data) from `src/lib/sci/gini.ts`, the maths behind the [[gini-lorenz]] tool, and `logGamma` and `normCdf` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the Beta function and the theoretical Gini. The paper's two-point formula is written out in the script itself, as it is not part of the tool.

The curve, the formula, the log-normal and Beta-2 parameters and the paper's RMSEs come from the paper's text. The random draws (seeded, so the run repeats exactly) are **ours**. Our Beta-2 samples have a Gini near 0.51, not the paper's 0.474, so the paper's Beta-2 design must be set up in a way its text does not fully state; we use it only as a second income shape, not as a replication.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/gini-lorenz.mjs`, which checks the tool against an independent NumPy calculation:

- An 18-income list must give the Gini of 0.398595 from the pairwise mean absolute difference, with matching quintile shares, top 1 %, Palma ratio, Theil and Atkinson indices.
- Grouped quintile shares of 6.5, 11, 15.5, 22 and 45 % must give the same measures as NumPy's interpolation.
- Edge cases: four equal incomes give 0; one person with everything among four gives 0.75 (1 after the n/(n − 1) correction).

3. It shows **Gini 0.545**, *Bottom 40 % share* **8.8 %** and *Top 10 % share* **42.5 %**.
4. Those two shares are all the paper's method needs: with L(0.4) = 0.088 and L(0.9) = 0.5753 the formula gives 0.559, closer to the 0.561 of all 10,000 incomes than the tool's 0.545 from ten straight-line groups.
5. Press **Incomes** and paste your own list to see the exact Gini, then compare with the grouped answer.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Fill gaps in inequality series**: where a statistics office publishes only the bottom 40 % and top 10 % shares, the paper's formula gives a Gini good to about 0.002; check it against years where the full data exist with the [[gini-lorenz]] tool.
- **Know the bias of grouped Ginis**: grouped data understate the Gini; compare the tool's grouped and full results on a sample like ours to see by how much.
- **Pick reporting shares**: if you design a survey report, publishing the bottom 40 % and top 10 % shares lets readers recover the Gini closely.
- **Teach the Lorenz curve**: simulate log-normal incomes with the [[distribution-explorer]], paste them in and watch the curve and Gini move with σ.
- **Weight first**: survey incomes need their weights; the tool takes a weight column, and the [[survey-weighting]] tool makes the weights.

## The fine print

- We re-ran the paper's simulations with our own random numbers; the paper's results on 16 economies and on thousands of survey records are not checked here, because the shares behind them are not in its text.
- The paper's error is the true Gini minus the estimate; our runs use the estimate minus the true Gini. The RMSE is the same either way.
- Our Beta-2 shape does not reproduce the paper's sample Gini of 0.474, so our Beta-2 numbers are not a check of its Table 3.
- The two-point curve is a fitting device, not a guaranteed Lorenz curve: for unusual pairs of shares it can bend the wrong way, which is one reason the paper sets a validity rule.
