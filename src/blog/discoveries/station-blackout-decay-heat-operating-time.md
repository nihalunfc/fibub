When a reactor loses all electrical power, fission stops within seconds but the fuel keeps making heat, and the clock that matters is how long the water lasts. A 2023 study in *Frontiers in Energy Research* modelled a 3,250 MW four-loop pressurised water reactor through a flood-induced **station blackout** with the system code RELAP5-3D, and found something a reader can check by hand: the time to core damage depends strongly on how long the reactor had been running. One week of operation buys 15.4 hours; 1,350 days buys only 9.7. This article rebuilds that spread with the [[decay-heat|decay heat estimator]], which uses the same Way-Wigner formula the paper does. For the physics before shutdown, see the [[neutron-diffusion]] calculator and the [[point-kinetics|point kinetics simulator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors build a plant model of a generic Westinghouse four-loop PWR (based on Zion Unit 1) and run two blackout scenarios. In the **short-term** case the turbine-driven auxiliary feedwater pump fails at once: the steam generators dry out after 1.13 h, the core starts to uncover at 1.44 h, and the peak cladding temperature reaches the 1,477 K damage limit at 2.49 h. In the **long-term** case that pump runs on battery power for about four hours first, which pushes dryout to 7.92 h and core damage to 9.74 h.

They then ask what difference the reactor's operating history makes, because that history sets how much fission-product inventory has built up. Keeping everything else the same, core damage comes at **15.37 h** after one week of operation, **12.47 h** after one month, **10.71 h** after one year and **9.74 h** for the base case of 1,350 effective full-power days. The decay power itself comes from the Way-Wigner relation, P(t)/P₀ = 0.0622 [t⁻⁰·² − (t₀ + t)⁻⁰·²], which is exactly the formula behind fibub's tool.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from the paper's own stated inputs** (3,250 MW, the four operating histories, the blackout at 100 s) with the tool's version of the formula. No data are fitted and nothing is simulated.

**Step 1: how much heat, and when.** One minute after shutdown of a long-running core the formula gives 2.59 % of full power, about 84 MW. One hour after shutdown the decay heat is **34.4 MW** for the 1,350-day history but only **25.2 MW** for the one-week history: a 37 % difference from the operating time alone, because a week is not long enough to build up the longer-lived fission products. The heat first falls below 1 % of full power after 1.28 h for the long history and after only 26 minutes for the one-week history, and below 0.5 % after 22 h and 3.7 h respectively.

**Step 2: the energy budget, which is what actually ends the accident.** Boiling water away is an energy problem, not a power problem, so we asked the tool for the **energy released** between the blackout at 100 s and each core-damage time the paper reports (the tool uses the exact integral of the formula, so this is not a numerical approximation):

| Operating history | Paper's core-damage time | Decay heat at 1 h | Energy released by then | Water that much heat boils |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 week | 15.37 h | 25.2 MW | 792 GJ | 351 t |
| 1 month | 12.47 h | 28.8 MW | 850 GJ | 377 t |
| 1 year | 10.71 h | 32.9 MW | 923 GJ | 409 t |
| 1,350 days | 9.74 h | 34.4 MW | 910 GJ | 403 t |

The four core-damage times differ by 58 %, but the energy released by then differs by only 15 %, and averages **869 GJ**, enough to boil about **385 tonnes** of water. In other words the accident is governed by a roughly fixed amount of water to be boiled, and the operating history only changes how fast the heat arrives.

**Step 3: predicting the paper's times from that one number.** Turning it round, we asked the tool when each history has released 869 GJ. That single number, with no plant model at all, gives **17.9 h, 12.9 h, 9.9 h and 9.2 h**, against the paper's 15.4, 12.5, 10.7 and 9.7 h: the right order, the right spacing, and within 0.4 to 2.5 hours. The one-week case is the loosest, which makes sense, as it is the case where the fixed-budget picture is worst (less power, so more time for the stored heat and the structures to matter).

**Step 4: the short-term case is a different problem.** Run the same sums on the short-term blackout and the energies are much smaller: 165 GJ by steam generator dryout at 1.13 h and only 313 GJ (about 139 t of water) by core damage at 2.49 h. That is the signature of losing the feedwater at once: the heat is not much higher, but there is far less water to take it, so the same energy budget argument does not carry across scenarios. In the long-term base case, dryout at 7.92 h corresponds to 776 GJ, around 344 t.

One detail worth flagging: the paper labels its 1,350-day base case "infinite". The formula's truly infinite history gives **24.8 MW** at 10 h against **20.0 MW** for 1,350 days, a 24 % difference, so the two are not interchangeable in the hours that matter here.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/station-blackout-decay-heat-operating-time.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fraction` (the Way-Wigner decay fraction), `energy` (its exact integral over an interval), `timeBelow` (when the heat falls under a level), `duration` and the latent heat `H_FG` from `src/lib/sci/eng-decayheat.ts`, the maths behind the [[decay-heat|decay heat estimator]].

The thermal power, the four operating histories, the 100 s blackout time and the event times are from the paper's text and tables. The 869 GJ energy budget is **our** average of the four cases, not a value from the paper, and the step-3 prediction is ours, not the paper's method.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/decay-heat.mjs`, which pins this tool against SciPy:

- the decay fraction at 24 combinations of time and operating history, to 1 part in 10¹³ (for example 1.0125 % one hour after a year of operation);
- the energy integral over 16 intervals against adaptive quadrature, to 1 part in 10⁹, and separately against a 200,000-step midpoint sum;
- the time to fall below 5 %, 1 % and 0.1 % against Brent root-finding, for four operating histories;
- New with this article: a 3,250 MW core must give 34.4 MW an hour after 1,350 days of operation and 25.2 MW after one week, and the energy released from 100 s to 9.74 h and to 15.37 h must be 910 GJ and 792 GJ.

## Check it yourself

On the [[decay-heat|decay heat estimator]] page:

1. In the *Reactor* card, type *Thermal power before shutdown P₀ (MW)* **3250**, *Time at that power* **1350** and set *Unit* to **days**.
2. The *Decay heat* table reads about **2.59 %** (84 MW) one minute after shutdown and **1.06 %** (34.4 MW) after one hour, with the water it would boil each hour beside it.
3. Set *Find when heat falls below (% of P₀)* to **1**: it answers about **1.28 hours**. Change *Time at that power* to **7 days** and the same question answers about **26 minutes**.
4. In *Energy over your own interval*, set *From* **0.0278**, *To* **9.74** with *Unit* on **hours** (that is 100 s to the paper's base-case core-damage time). The energy released is about **910 GJ**, or 2.8 × 10⁵ MW·s per MW of rating.
5. Change *Time at that power* to **7** days and *To* to **15.37** hours: the energy is about **792 GJ**, within 10 % of the first answer although the time is 58 % longer.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a severe-accident timeline** before running a system code: a fixed energy budget and the Way-Wigner integral get the ordering and the spacing of events right.
- **Size an emergency water inventory**: the tool gives the tonnes of water boiled per hour directly, so a tank volume turns straight into hours of grace.
- **Show how much the operating history is worth**, which matters for shutdown-state and refuelling risk assessments as much as for full-power ones.
- **Plan a sensitivity study**: pick the operating times that actually separate the curves, instead of running them all.
- **Teach the difference between power and energy** in accident analysis, which is where intuition usually fails.

## The fine print

- Way-Wigner is a rough fit to the fission-product mixture, generally quoted as fair from about 10 s to a few months after shutdown. It carries no actinide or activation heat, and serious work uses ANS-5.1 or a full inventory calculation.
- Our 869 GJ budget is an average over the paper's four cases, so step 3 is a consistency check, not an independent prediction. The plant model includes stored heat, metal structures, pressuriser discharge and cladding oxidation, none of which the tool knows about.
- The boil-off figures assume every joule goes into boiling water already at 100 °C at atmospheric pressure. A pressurised circuit is hotter, and the latent heat is smaller there.
- The paper's own event list has the base case's core damage (35,064 s) slightly before full core uncovery (35,564 s); we used its stated core-damage times as given.
