When two monomers go into the same pot, the polymer that comes out is almost never a copy of the feed. Two numbers decide how far it differs: the **reactivity ratios** r₁ and r₂, each one the preference of a growing chain end for its own monomer over the other. A 2026 paper in *Polymers* makes three styrene copolymers with carboxyphenylmaleimides and does the generous thing of printing both its composition measurements and the ratios it derived from them — which means anyone can check the derivation. We did, with the [[copolymer-composition]] tool, and the ratios move a long way: r₁ goes from the reported 0.10 to 0.20 up to about **0.57**, and r₂ down to about **0.005**. For the steps after composition, the [[polymer-molar-mass]] tool handles chain length and the [[glass-transition-calculator]] the softening point.

## The paper in brief

The authors synthesised the ortho, meta and para isomers of carboxyphenylmaleimide and copolymerised each with styrene by free-radical initiation at five feed ratios: 90/10, 75/25, 50/50, 25/75 and 10/90 mol% styrene to maleimide. The copolymer compositions were found by spectroscopy. The pattern is striking and the paper's main finding: **every copolymer is styrene-rich, and once the feed drops below half styrene the copolymer stops changing**, settling at about 50 to 54 mol% styrene even when the feed is only 10 % styrene. That is the signature of a strongly **alternating** pair — maleimide units almost never follow one another.

The authors also report reactivity ratios by the **Fineman-Ross** method: r₁ = 0.20 and r₂ = 0.10 for the ortho isomer, 0.15 and 0.08 for the meta, 0.10 and 0.05 for the para, and read an ortho > meta > para order out of them. They then give Alfrey-Price Q and e values, melting points of 275, 256 and 265 °C for the three isomers, and antifungal activity strongest for the ortho copolymer. Two small arithmetic slips are visible in the text: the stated r₁r₂ product for the para pair is 0.05, where 0.10 × 0.05 is 0.005, and the methods and the table disagree on the polymerisation solvent.

## Checked with the tools

This is a straight **refit**: the five feed fractions and the measured copolymer compositions from the paper go into the same Mayo-Lewis maths the [[copolymer-composition]] tool runs in the browser, and the three estimators it offers — Fineman-Ross, Kelen-Tüdős and non-linear least squares on the composition curve itself — come out with their own r₁ and r₂. Nothing is simulated.

**Step 1: the paper's own ratios do not reproduce the paper's own data.** Put r₁ = 0.20 and r₂ = 0.10 into the Mayo-Lewis equation and the predicted copolymer at the 90/10 feed is 0.735 styrene, where 0.856 was measured; at the 10/90 feed it is 0.350, where 0.508 was measured. The root-mean-square gap over the five feeds is **0.117 in mole fraction** for the ortho isomer, 0.126 for the meta and 0.138 for the para — ten to twenty times the scatter of the measurements themselves. Figure 1 shows the dashed curve passing well below the points at both ends.

**Step 2: refitting the same five points.** Non-linear least squares on the composition equation gives r₁ = **0.561**, r₂ = **0.0063** (ortho), 0.569 and 0.0052 (meta), 0.567 and 0.0028 (para), with root-mean-square residuals of 0.011, 0.005 and 0.003 — down by a factor of 10 to 40. The refitted curve runs through all five points (Figure 1). Averaging the three isomers' compositions first and fitting once gives r₁ = 0.565, r₂ = 0.0048, residual 0.0055.

**Step 3: what the change means chemically.** With r₂ ≈ 0.005, a maleimide chain end adds another maleimide about once in two hundred chances: the pair is **strongly alternating**, and r₁r₂ ≈ 0.003 rather than the 0.02 the paper quotes. That is what makes the copolymer stick near 50 mol% styrene at maleimide-rich feeds, and it is a stronger version of the paper's own conclusion, not a contradiction of it. The refit also puts the three isomers within 1.5 % of one another in r₁, so the composition data alone do **not** support an ortho > meta > para ordering of reactivity; the ordering the paper sees in viscosity and biological activity has to come from somewhere other than these five points.

**Step 4: a free extra from the same two numbers.** The tool reports an **azeotropic feed** at f₁ = 0.696: a batch started with 69.6 mol% styrene makes polymer of the same composition from the first chain to the last, so it never drifts. Away from that point the drift is large. Starting at f₁ = 0.5 (Figure 2), the feed falls from 0.5 to 0.40 by half conversion and the chains being made go from 0.609 styrene down to 0.579, then collapse towards pure maleimide as the styrene runs out, while the whole batch averages 0.596. A paper that stops at low conversion never sees this; a scale-up that runs to 80 % does.

The third row is the likely cause of the whole discrepancy. Fineman-Ross puts the data into a linear form that weights the extreme feeds heavily and can return a **negative** reactivity ratio, which is chemically impossible; on the ortho data it does exactly that (r₂ = −0.025). Kelen-Tüdős, which rescales the same points, stays positive and lands near the least-squares answer. When a published r₂ is very small, it is worth asking which estimator produced it.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/styrene-carboxyphenylmaleimide-reactivity-ratios-refit-alternating.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `F1` (the Mayo-Lewis composition equation), `finemanRoss`, `kelenTudos`, `fitNLLS` (Nelder-Mead on the composition curve), `azeotrope`, `behaviour` and `drift` (Skeist's equation, integrated with Runge-Kutta) from `src/lib/sci/copolymer.ts`, the maths behind the [[copolymer-composition]] tool.

Inputs from the paper: the five feed fractions, the fifteen measured copolymer compositions (three isomers × five feeds) and the reported reactivity ratios. The points drawn in Figure 1 are the **average of the three isomers at each feed**, worked out by our script, not a table from the paper. Figure 2 is **our calculation** of composition drift from the refitted ratios; the paper reports no conversion-dependent compositions. One judgement is **our assumption**: that the paper's compositions were measured at low enough conversion for the instantaneous Mayo-Lewis equation to apply, as the method requires.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` and `tests/sci-tools/copolymer-composition.mjs` pin this maths against textbook results:

- Ideal copolymerisation (r₁ = r₂ = 1) must give F₁ = f₁ exactly, and perfect alternation (r₁ = r₂ = 0) must give F₁ = 0.5 at any feed.
- For the standard styrene/methyl methacrylate pair (r₁ = 0.52, r₂ = 0.46) the composition at f₁ = 0.3 must be 0.37099 and the azeotrope must be f₁ = 0.52941, where the drift calculation must stay flat to within 10⁻⁶ over the whole run.
- Data generated from known ratios must come back: Fineman-Ross, Kelen-Tüdős and the non-linear fit all have to recover 0.52 and 0.46 from exact points.
- New with this article: from the paper's five ortho-isomer compositions, the non-linear fit must give r₁ = 0.561 and r₂ = 0.0063 with a residual under 0.011, Fineman-Ross must return a negative r₂, and the paper's own 0.20/0.10 pair must miss the same points by more than 0.11 in mole fraction.

## Check it yourself

On the [[copolymer-composition]] tool:

1. In the box *Feed f₁ and copolymer F₁ (mole fractions of monomer 1), one pair per line*, clear the demo data and paste the paper's ortho series: **0.90, 0.856**; **0.75, 0.740**; **0.50, 0.600**; **0.25, 0.520**; **0.10, 0.508**.
2. The fit panel shows **Fineman-Ross r₁ 0.550, r₂ −0.025** (flagged, because a negative ratio has no meaning), **Kelen-Tüdős r₁ 0.569, r₂ 0.017** and **least squares r₁ 0.561, r₂ 0.006**.
3. Press *Use the fitted values above*. The *r₁* and *r₂* boxes fill with the least-squares pair and the curve redraws through your points.
4. Set *Monomer 1 in the feed (mole fraction f₁)* to **0.5**: the copolymer reads about **F₁ = 0.608**, and the page names the behaviour *tends to alternate* with an azeotropic feed of **0.693**.
5. Now type **0.2** in *r₁* and **0.1** in *r₂*, the paper's values, and watch the curve drop away from the points: at f₁ = 0.9 it predicts 0.735 instead of 0.856.
6. Put the fitted pair back, set *Conversion to follow up to (%)* to **90** and read the drift chart: by 75 % conversion the feed is down to f₁ = 0.249 and the chains coming off are only 0.538 styrene.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published pair of reactivity ratios** in two minutes: paste the paper's composition data and see whether its own ratios reproduce it ([[copolymer-composition]]).
- **Choose the estimator before the experiment.** Fineman-Ross is still common and still returns negative ratios on alternating systems; run all three on your data and report the spread.
- **Find the feed that will not drift** (the azeotrope) when you need one composition from start to finish, or pick a feed deliberately away from it when you want a gradient.
- **Plan the stopping point** for a batch: the drift chart says how much the composition has moved at the conversion you can actually reach.
- **Join the numbers up**: feed the composition into the [[glass-transition-calculator]] for a Fox-equation estimate of the softening point, and use the [[polymer-molar-mass]] tool for the chain-length side.

## The fine print

- We refitted the paper's published compositions; we did not remeasure anything. If a composition is misprinted, our ratios inherit the error.
- The Mayo-Lewis equation holds at **low conversion**. The paper does not state the conversions its compositions were measured at, so this is **our assumption**.
- The terminal (two-parameter) model assumes only the last unit on a chain matters. Maleimides with charge-transfer complexes can need a penultimate-unit or complex-participation model, which the tool does not fit.
- Reactivity ratios depend on temperature and solvent. The paper's methods and table disagree on the solvent, so the numbers should be treated as that system's, not as general constants.
- Our Figure 1 points are the mean of the three isomers, drawn to keep the chart readable; the fits in the table are per isomer.
