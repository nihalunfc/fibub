Most stars come in pairs, and most of what we know about star masses comes from watching the two partners swing towards and away from us. Their light shifts blue, then red, then blue again: the **Doppler effect**. A paper in the *Revista Brasileira de Ensino de Física* turns that into a bench-top experiment: two smartphones, each playing a steady tone, ride on the ends of a spinning bar while a fixed microphone records how their pitch rises and falls. This article works out what that microphone should hear with the [[doppler-beats]] calculator's maths, then runs the problem backwards the way an astronomer would, from frequency shifts to speeds, radii and a mass ratio. To record the tones yourself, use the [[audio-spectrum-analyser]]; for real stars, the [[radial-velocity-fitter]] does the orbit fit.

## The paper in brief

The authors fixed two phones on opposite ends of a 1.20 m bar: phone 1 plays **4.0 kHz** at **0.60 m** from the axis, phone 2 plays **4.4 kHz** at **0.36 m**. The bar spins at ω = **15.3 s⁻¹** (about 2.4 turns a second), and a microphone in the plane of rotation, **0.70 m** from the axis, feeds a computer. A time-frequency plot (a spectrogram, which shows how the pitch changes from moment to moment) shows two wavy lines moving in opposite directions, just like the spectral lines of a double-lined binary star.

They write the heard frequency in terms of α = R/d₀, the radius over the microphone's distance, fit the curves for ω and a starting angle, and report that the measured curves match the model very well, within an experimental error of about 5 %. The point for teaching: the phone on the shorter arm moves more slowly, just as the heavier star in a binary moves on the smaller orbit, so the ratio of the two speeds gives the ratio of the "masses".

## Checked with the tools

The paper shows its measurements only as spectrograms, so we did not refit its data. Instead we **recomputed** what the microphone should hear from the set-up values in the text, with the calculator's own `soundDoppler`, and then recovered the speeds from those frequencies with its `sourceSpeed`. The air temperature is not given; we used **20 °C (our assumption)**, which makes the speed of sound 343.21 m/s.

**Step 1: how fast the phones move.** Each phone's speed is its radius times ω: 0.60 × 15.3 = **9.18 m/s** for phone 1 and **5.51 m/s** for phone 2. One turn takes 2π/15.3 = **0.411 s**.

**Step 2: the pitch at the microphone.** A phone moving straight at the microphone at 9.18 m/s is heard at 4000 × 343.21 / (343.21 − 9.18) = **4109.9 Hz**, and moving straight away at **3895.8 Hz**. Note the shift is not symmetric: +109.9 Hz up but only −104.2 Hz down, because the speed sits in the denominator. Phone 2 swings between **4330.5 and 4471.8 Hz**. Figure 1 shows both over two turns, always moving in opposite directions, as two stars around their common centre do.

**Step 3: does the microphone's closeness matter?** With the microphone only 0.70 m from the axis, phone 1 passes within 10 cm of it, and the line-of-sight speed is not a simple sine. Working it out with geometry, the speed towards the microphone is −V d₀ sin θ / r, where θ is the phone's angle on the circle and r its distance to the microphone. Its peak is still exactly V, reached where the line to the microphone just grazes the circle (31° either side of the closest point), but the swing from +V to −V happens in just 62° of the turn, about **71 ms** of the 411 ms period. From 6 m away (α = 0.10) the same phone gives a plain sine (Figure 2). So the **size** of the shift tells you the speed whatever the distance, while the **shape** tells you how close you are, much as an eccentric orbit bends a star's velocity curve away from a sine.

**Step 4: backwards, like an astronomer.** Feed the top frequency back in: 4109.9 Hz from a 4000 Hz phone gives a speed of **9.18 m/s**, and the lowest frequency gives the same speed going away. Divide by ω and you get the radius back, **0.60 m**; phone 2 gives **0.36 m**. The speed ratio is 5.51 / 9.18 = **0.60**, so in the binary-star picture the "star" on the short arm is 1/0.6 ≈ 1.67 times heavier.

|---|---|---|

| Phone 2 speed | 0.36 m × 15.3 s⁻¹ | 5.51 m/s, heard 4330.5 to 4471.8 Hz |

The last row matters: if you get the air temperature wrong by 10 °C, every speed you recover is off by about 1.7 %, a third of the paper's 5 % error budget. The two tones, 4.0 and 4.4 kHz, are also 400 Hz apart, far too fast to hear as beats: the calculator's *Beats between two tones* mode says such a pair sounds rough, or as two notes.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/smartphone-binary-star-doppler-simulation-rotating-bar-radial-velocity.mjs`. It calls `airSpeed`, `soundDoppler`, `sourceSpeed` and `beats` from `src/lib/sci/phdoppler.ts`, the maths behind [[doppler-beats]]. The script adds only the circle geometry: the phone's position, its speed along the line to the microphone, and the sound's travel time to the microphone.

The tones, radii, ω and microphone distance come from the paper's text. The 20 °C air and the 6 m comparison distance are **our assumptions**. The curves are **calculated**, not the paper's recordings.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/doppler-beats.mjs`, which pins the calculator against NumPy, SymPy and SciPy:

- A 700 Hz siren approaching at 25 m/s in 20 °C air must be heard at 754.99 Hz, and receding at 652.47 Hz, and `sourceSpeed` must turn both back into 25 m/s.
- The pass-by curve for that siren 20 m away must match SciPy's root-finding solution, for example 728.56 Hz at 0.5 s before the closest-approach sound arrives.
- New with this article: a 4000 Hz phone at 9.18 m/s in 20 °C air must be heard at 4109.93 Hz approaching and 3895.80 Hz receding, and a measured 4110 Hz must give back 9.186 m/s.

## Check it yourself

On [[doppler-beats|the Doppler and beats calculator]]:

1. Set *Situation* to **Sound: moving source or listener**, *Speed units* to **m/s** and *Waves travel through* to **Air**, with *Air temperature (°C)* **20**.
2. Type **4000** in *Frequency sent out (Hz)*, **9.18** in *Source speed* with *Source is moving* **towards the listener**, and **0** for the listener and wind.
3. It reads **Heard: 4109.93 Hz**, and *Driving past a listener standing still* shows **4109.93 Hz → 3895.80 Hz**.
4. Type **4110** in *Measured frequency, to find the source speed (Hz, optional)*: *Source speed from the measured frequency* shows about **9.186 m/s towards**. Divide by 15.3 for the radius: 0.60 m.
5. Change *Air temperature (°C)* to **30** and watch the recovered speed rise to about 9.34 m/s: that is why the temperature belongs in your report.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan the experiment**: pick tones, radii and spin rate in [[doppler-beats]] so the shift (here about 110 Hz) is many times the frequency resolution of your recording.
- **Record and read the shifts** with the [[audio-spectrum-analyser]], which finds peaks to a fraction of a hertz in its spectrogram.
- **Teach the astronomy link**: turn shifts into speeds, then use the [[radial-velocity-fitter]] on real star velocities to see the same physics give a planet's or companion's mass.
- **Budget the errors**: change the air temperature or the tone and see which input dominates the uncertainty in speed and radius.
- **Check sonar and radar numbers** with the echo mode, where the shift is doubled because the wave goes out and back.

## The fine print

- Our frequencies are **calculated** from the set-up in the paper's text, not read from its spectrograms.
- We assumed the microphone lies in the plane of the bar, at rest, in still air at 20 °C; the paper does not state the temperature.
- We take ω = 15.3 s⁻¹ as an angular speed in radians per second. A different reading would scale every speed and shift.
- In the "binary star" picture the mass ratio comes from the speed ratio because both phones share the same ω around the same axis. Real binaries add an unknown tilt of the orbit, which is why astronomers often quote minimum masses.
