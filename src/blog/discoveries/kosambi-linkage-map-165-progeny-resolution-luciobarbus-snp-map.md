A genetic linkage map puts markers in order along each chromosome and measures the gaps in **centimorgans (cM)**, where 1 cM means about one recombinant offspring in a hundred. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Genetics* built such a map for the Aral barbel, a fish that is farmed for food, from just 165 offspring of one cross, and it holds more than 164,000 SNP markers (single-letter DNA differences) with gaps as small as 0.01 cM. This article asks a simple question that anyone can check: how small a gap can 165 offspring ever show? We answer it with the [[linkage-map-calculator]]. For the next step, test whether markers segregate as expected with the [[mendelian-chi-square]] test and plan how many offspring you need with the [[sample-size]] tool.

## The paper in brief

The authors crossed two parent fish and raised 200 offspring, of which 165 were measured for six growth traits and sequenced along with the parents. Reading the whole genome gave over 14 million high-quality SNPs; 164,435 of them were placed on **50 linkage groups**, one per chromosome (the fish has 50). Distances use the **Kosambi mapping function**, which turns the share of recombinant offspring into a map distance while allowing for the fact that one crossover makes a second one nearby less likely. The map is 6,429.95 cM long, the average gap between neighbouring markers is about 0.10 cM, 99.62 % of gaps are under 5 cM, and the largest gap is 28.37 cM. The map was then used to look for regions (QTLs) linked to body size.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from numbers stated in the paper plus the standard mapping functions. We did not have the offspring's genotypes.

**Step 1: the smallest step the data can show.** Two markers on the same chromosome can only be told apart if at least one of the 165 offspring has a crossover between them. One recombinant out of 165 is a recombination fraction of 1/165 = 0.0061, and the tool's Kosambi function turns that into **0.606 cM**. Two recombinants give 1.212 cM, three give 1.819 cM, five give 3.034 cM and ten give 6.091 cM (Figure 1). So between one marker pair and the next, the map can only move in jumps of about 0.6 cM. A spacing of 0.10 cM, or 0.01 cM for the densest group, is far smaller than one recombinant. If both parents carry informative markers, the data holds up to 2 × 165 = 330 meioses (the cell divisions that make eggs and sperm) and the first step falls to **0.303 cM**: still three times the stated average spacing. This uses **our assumption** that both parents are informative; the paper does not say how many markers were informative in each.

**Step 2: why the numbers still make sense.** Most neighbouring SNPs sit on the same stretch of chromosome with no crossover between them in any of the offspring, so they have identical patterns and the true distance between them is 0 cM to within what the data can resolve. Software that orders thousands of SNPs spreads them out with tiny, model-based gaps. The long-run picture is easy to count. A map of 6,429.95 cM holds one crossover per 100 cM per meiosis, so about **64.3 crossovers per meiosis across the genome**, or 21,219 in 330 meioses (our assumption). That is the most separate positions the data can distinguish, against 164,435 markers: **at least 7.7 markers per distinguishable position on average**. The paper's headline of 164,435 markers is genuine, but they carry far fewer independent mapping signals than the count suggests.

**Step 3: the paper's two spacings are not the same thing.** The total of 6,429.95 cM divided by 164,435 SNPs gives **0.039 cM** per interval. The paper's stated average of 0.10 cM matches something else: the mean of the 50 per-group averages in its Table 4, which weights a sparse group (about 220 SNPs, 0.5 cM spacing) the same as a dense one (about 12,000 SNPs, 0.01 cM). Both numbers are correct; they answer different questions.

**Step 4: the big gap is well supported.** A 28.37 cM Kosambi gap is a recombination fraction of **0.2567**, about **42 recombinant offspring out of 165** (35.7 with the Haldane function, which ignores interference), with a standard error of 0.034 on the fraction. A 5 cM gap, the paper's cut-off for a "large" interval, is 0.0498, or **8.2 recombinants** (Figure 2). So the intervals the authors call gaps are well measured, while the tiny ones are not.

**Step 5: a small slip in the paper.** The text gives the map length as 6,425.95 cM but Table 4 as 6,429.95. We added the 50 group lengths from Table 4 as printed and got 6,429.96, so the table is right and the text has a typing slip. Their stated average of 128.59 cM per group fits the table (6,429.95 ÷ 50 = 128.60), not the text (128.52).

| Mapping function | Kosambi | 165 offspring: first step 0.606 cM |
| Average group length | 128.59 cM | 128.60 cM from the table total |
| Largest gap, 28.37 cM | a real gap | 0.2567 recombination fraction, 42 recombinants |

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/kosambi-linkage-map-165-progeny-resolution-luciobarbus-snp-map.mjs` calls `rfToCm` and `cmToRf` (the Haldane and Kosambi mapping functions and their inverses) from `src/lib/sci/linkmap.ts`, the maths behind the [[linkage-map-calculator]]. The offspring count (165), group count (50), SNP count, total length and the 28.37 cM gap come from the paper's text and Table 4. Both-parents-informative (330 meioses) and one crossover per 100 cM per meiosis are **our assumptions**. Nothing is simulated: every point is the mapping function evaluated at a whole number of recombinants.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/linkage-map-calculator.mjs`. It pins the mapping functions against an independent Python implementation: RF 0.2 must give 25.54 cM (Haldane) and 21.18 cM (Kosambi), 25 cM must give an RF of 0.1967 and 0.2311, and the round trip from RF to cM and back must return the start. New with this article: one recombinant in 165 must give 0.606 cM, 0.303 cM in 330, and a 28.37 cM Kosambi gap must give an RF of 0.2567 (Haldane 0.2165).

## Check it yourself

On the [[linkage-map-calculator]], in the panel *1. Recombination frequency and map distance*:

1. Leave *Convert* on **Recombination frequency → cM**, pick the **Kosambi** mapping function, and type **0.00606** (1 in 165) as the recombination frequency. It should show about **0.606 cM**.
2. Change the frequency to **0.003** (1 in 330): about **0.30 cM**.
3. Change *Convert* to **cM → recombination frequency**, type **28.37**: the Kosambi row shows **0.2567** and the Haldane row **0.2165**.
4. Type **5** cM: Kosambi gives **0.0498**. Multiply by 165 to get 8.2 expected recombinants.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Checking a map's resolution** before trusting small gaps: divide 1 by the number of meioses and convert with the tool.
- **Planning a mapping cross**: choose how many offspring give the smallest distance you care about, together with the [[sample-size]] tool.
- **Converting between mapping functions** when comparing your map with one made by another group.
- **Teaching** why a map with more markers is not always a map with more information.
- **Screening markers**: markers that depart from the expected ratio, checked with the [[mendelian-chi-square]] test, should be removed before mapping.

## The fine print

- We had only the paper's summary numbers, not its genotypes, so we cannot say how many markers were informative in each parent. Our 330 meioses is an upper bound.
- The mapping function is only a model. Real crossover patterns, and the paper's software, handle genotyping errors in ways a single formula does not.
- The paper's own check of its map against other evidence is not repeated here. This article makes no claim about its QTL results.
- Our point is about resolution, not quality: a dense map of 165 offspring can still be very useful for finding regions, just not for placing neighbouring markers.
