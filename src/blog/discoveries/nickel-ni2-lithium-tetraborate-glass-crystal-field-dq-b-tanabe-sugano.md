Nickel turns glass green or brown, and the exact colour tells a chemist how the nickel ion sits among its oxygen neighbours. A 2026 paper in *Materials* measured the absorption, glow and excitation spectra of Ni²⁺ in lithium tetraborate glass (Li₂B₄O₇, a borate glass studied for radiation detectors and optics) and fitted them with a **Tanabe–Sugano analysis**, the standard way to turn band positions into the ligand-field splitting and the electron repulsion. This article rebuilds that fit with the [[tanabe-sugano]] and checks every band the paper assigns. For a first look at the d-orbital splitting use the [[crystal-field-calculator]], and to turn band heights into concentrations, the [[beer-lambert-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

Ni²⁺ has eight d electrons (d⁸). Surrounded by six oxygens in an octahedron, its ground state is ³A₂g, and it has three **spin-allowed** absorption bands, to ³T₂g, ³T₁g(F) and ³T₁g(P), plus weaker **spin-forbidden** ones to singlet states. Two numbers set where they fall: **Dq**, the ligand-field strength (the octahedral splitting Δ is 10Dq), and the **Racah parameter B**, how strongly the d electrons repel each other. A third, **C**, matters only for the singlets.

The authors melted glass with 1.0 mol % NiO and found the three spin-allowed bands at 1417, 815 and 428 nm (7057, 12,270 and 23,364 cm⁻¹; wavenumber in cm⁻¹ is 10,000,000 divided by the wavelength in nm). A least-squares fit of all three gave **Dq = 720 ± 15 cm⁻¹ and B = 929 ± 10 cm⁻¹**, so Dq/B = 0.776, a weak field typical of six-coordinated nickel in glass. The singlet ¹T₁g(G), seen as an excitation band at 381 nm, then fixes **C = 3950 cm⁻¹** (C/B = 4.25). B is about 90 % of the free-ion value (the **nephelauxetic ratio** β = 0.90), meaning the Ni–O bonds are mostly ionic. The glass also glows: green at 525 nm, red at 684 nm from the ¹Eg singlet, and two near-infrared bands.

Four of six bands agree within 60 cm⁻¹ (1 to 3 nm). The two lowest triplets miss by 146 and 102 cm⁻¹ in opposite directions: one octahedral Dq cannot place both exactly, a sign that the site is somewhat distorted, as the authors say.

**Step 2: the fit itself.** Searching Dq and B on a 0.1 cm⁻¹ grid for the smallest squared misfit to the three triplet bands, the tool's levels give **Dq = 720.3 and B = 928.7 cm⁻¹**, the paper's numbers to the last digit, with an rms misfit of 103 cm⁻¹. The paper's two earlier two-band estimates come back too: the lowest band with the highest (ν1 and ν3) gives Dq 705.7 and B 943.2, and the middle with the highest (ν2 and ν3) gives Dq 727.5 and B 920.6 cm⁻¹ (paper: 706 and 943; 727 and 921).

**Step 3: why the third band matters.** The tool also has a quick "Δ and B from a spectrum" box that uses only the two lowest bands. Given 7057 and 12,270 cm⁻¹ it returns **B = 1481 cm⁻¹ and Δ/B = 4.76**, and predicts ν3 at 31,122 cm⁻¹ (321 nm), far from the measured 428 nm. That B is larger than the free ion's, which is impossible for an ion in a solid. Figure 2 shows why: for d⁸ in a weak field the ratio ν2/ν1 hardly changes with Δ/B (1.76 at 3, 1.69 at 7.76), so a 3 % shift in ν2 (a distorted site, or a broad band read a little off its centre) moves Δ/B all the way from 7.76 to 4.76. The ratio ν3/ν1 is steep, and pins Δ/B close to the paper's value. The paper was right to fit all three.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/nickel-ni2-lithium-tetraborate-glass-crystal-field-dq-b-tanabe-sugano.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `levels`, `readAt` and `diagram` (the full d⁸ energy matrix with electron repulsion and the octahedral field) and `fitTwoBands` from `src/lib/sci/tanabe.ts`, the maths behind the [[tanabe-sugano]]. The least-squares grid search over Dq and B is a few lines in the script, built on `levels`.

All band positions, Dq, B, C/B and free-ion B values come from the paper's text. Nothing is simulated: this is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated values. The singlet bands come from emission and excitation spectra, not absorption; emission sits a little below absorption, so the 684 nm line is a slightly loose check.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/tanabe-sugano.mjs`, which pins the tool against independent results:

- Every level of d² to d⁸ at Δ/B = 7.3 and 25 matches an independent NumPy calculation (built from complex orbitals and Wigner 3j symbols) to 10⁻⁸ B.
- For d³ and d⁸, the three spin-allowed bands match the textbook closed form, 7.5B + 15Dq ∓ ½√(225B² + 100Dq² − 180B·Dq), at four field strengths.
- The two-band fit matches the closed form B = (2ν1² + ν2² − 3ν1ν2)/(15ν2 − 27ν1) for four test spectra.
- New with this article: at Dq 720.3, B 928.7, C/B 4.25 the tool must give ³T₂g 7203, ³T₁g(F) 12,168, ³T₁g(P) 23,372, ¹T₂g(D) 21,408 and ¹T₁g(G) 26,241 cm⁻¹ (to 1 cm⁻¹), and ν1 with ν3 must give B = 943 cm⁻¹.

## Check it yourself

On the [[tanabe-sugano]]:

1. Under *Ion and parameters*, set *d electrons* to **d⁸ (Ni²⁺)** and *C/B* to **4.25**.
2. Under the diagram, type **7.76** in *Read at Δ/B* and **928.7** in *B (cm⁻¹, to convert)*.
3. The table lists ³T₂g(F) at about **7207 cm⁻¹ (1388 nm)**, ³T₁g(F) **12173 (821 nm)**, ¹Eg(D) **14676 (681 nm)**, ¹T₂g(D) **21412 (467 nm)**, ³T₁g(P) **23377 (428 nm)** and ¹T₁g(G) **26245 (381 nm)**. (Δ/B rounded to 7.76 moves each by a few cm⁻¹ from our table above.)
4. In *Δ and B from a spectrum*, type **7057** in *ν1, lowest band* and **12270** in *ν2, second band*. It shows *Racah B* **1481 cm⁻¹**, *Δ/B* **4.76** and *Predicted ν3* **31122 cm⁻¹ (321 nm)**. Type **1030** in *Free-ion B*: β comes out **1.438**, above 1, the warning sign from Step 3.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Assign a d–d spectrum**: read the diagram at your fitted Δ/B and B to see which spin-forbidden singlet sits under a shoulder (here ¹A₁g(G) falls within 70 cm⁻¹ of ³T₁g(P)), with the [[tanabe-sugano]].
- **Check a published Dq and B** in a minute: type them in, read the levels, compare with the stated bands.
- **Spot an ill-posed two-band fit**: if β comes out above 1 or ν3 is predicted far from where it is seen, add the third band.
- **Teach ligand-field theory** with a real glass spectrum instead of a textbook complex, and compare the splitting and spin state with the [[crystal-field-calculator]].
- **Plan a doped glass or crystal**: predict where Ni²⁺ or Cr³⁺ bands will fall in a new host from an expected Dq and B, then size the doping from band heights with the [[beer-lambert-calculator]].

## The fine print

- This is a recomputation from values in the paper's text; we did not have the spectra.
- The tool treats a perfect octahedron. A glass gives a spread of distorted sites, so bands are broad and no single Dq fits every band exactly.
- Our grid search fits the three triplet bands equally weighted, as we read the paper's method; its Monte Carlo error bars are not redone here.
- C/B = 4.25 comes from the paper; the triplet bands do not depend on C at all, only the singlets do.
