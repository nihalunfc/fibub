The large-eye dentex, *Dentex macrophthalmus*, is a small red sea bream of the Mediterranean and the east Atlantic, caught mostly as by-catch by trawlers. Almost nothing was known about how fast it grows in the eastern Mediterranean. A 2025 paper in *Fishes* fills that gap for the Pagasitikos Gulf in Greece, and also tests whether machine learning can guess a fish's age from simple measurements. This article shows how the [[von-bertalanffy-fitter]] and the [[fish-length-weight]] tool let anyone rebuild the growth and weight rules, and how the [[yield-per-recruit]] model takes them further.

## The paper in brief

The team measured and weighed 305 dentex and read their **otoliths**, the small ear stones of bony fish that lay down one light and one dark band a year, like tree rings. Three readers aged every fish, and the fish fell into eight age groups, from 1 to 8 years. The authors fitted the **von Bertalanffy growth curve**, L(t) = L∞ (1 − e^(−K (t − t0))), where L∞ is the length an average fish would reach if it lived for ever, K is how fast it gets there (per year) and t0 is the age at which the curve would give zero length. For both sexes together they report **L∞ = 25.97 cm, K = 0.16 per year and t0 = −2.69 years**, with no clear difference between the sexes. Both sexes gain weight more slowly than the cube of their length (**negative allometry**), and the mortality figures (natural mortality M = 0.37 per year, fishing mortality F = 0.26) suggest the stock is not over-fished. The machine-learning half of the paper predicts age from length, weight and otolith size; we do not check that part here.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated parameters** or a **fit to simulated fish**; we do not have the paper's otolith readings.

**Step 1: length at each age.** Putting the paper's three numbers into the von Bertalanffy equation gives mean lengths of **11.6 cm at age 1** and **21.3 cm at age 8**. The paper's text says "about 12 cm" and "about 22 cm", so the curve and the reported lengths agree. Females and males give almost the same lengths at every age from 2 to 8 (within 0.3 cm), which fits the paper's finding of no difference between the sexes, even though their L∞ values (26.4 against 30.9 cm) look far apart.

**Step 2: can the fitter get the curve back?** We made up 305 fish with ages spread from 1 to 8 years, most of them 3 to 5 years old, lengths on the paper's curve plus a random spread of 1.5 cm (our assumption), and fitted them with the growth fitter's maths. It finds L∞ = 27.6 cm and K = 0.135 per year, with 95 % intervals of 21.8 to 33.4 cm and 0.057 to 0.213. Both contain the paper's values (Figure 1). The wide intervals are the lesson: with no fish older than 8 years, the top of the curve is not pinned down, and a larger L∞ with a smaller K fits almost as well. The paper's own male curve (L∞ 30.9 cm, K 0.097) shows the same trade-off.

**Step 3: growth performance φ'.** The index φ' = log10 K + 2 log10 L∞ (L∞ in cm) lets you compare growth across species and seas. With the rounded values in the paper, it comes out at **2.033**, a little below the paper's **2.076**. The paper's φ' would need K = 0.177, so it was probably worked out from unrounded estimates or a different fit. The difference is small, but it is the kind of thing worth checking before you put a φ' into a comparison table.

**Step 4: natural mortality.** The formula of Then and colleagues (2015), M = 4.118 K^0.73 L∞^−0.33, gives **M = 0.369 per year** from the paper's L∞ and K, matching its 0.37.

**Step 5: weight at length.** The paper's female and male equations give 75 g and 72 g for a 17 cm fish (about the average length in the sample) and 137 g and 139 g at 22 cm (Figure 2). We drew weights for our 305 simulated fish from the female equation with an 8 % scatter and fitted them with the length-weight maths: b = 2.30 (95 % interval 2.24 to 2.36), flagged as **negative allometric**, as in the paper.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/large-eye-dentex-growth-otoliths-pagasitikos.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls:

- `vb` and `fitVB` from `src/lib/sci/vbgf.ts`, the maths behind the [[von-bertalanffy-fitter]] (a Levenberg-Marquardt least-squares fit, with φ' and the Ford-Walford check);
- `fitLW` from `src/lib/sci/fishlw.ts`, the maths behind the [[fish-length-weight]] tool.

The growth and length-weight parameters, M and the "about 12 cm" and "about 22 cm" come from the paper. The 305 simulated fish, their spread of ages, the 1.5 cm length scatter and the 8 % weight scatter are **our assumptions**, chosen to look like a typical otolith sample. The natural mortality formula is worked out in the script itself; no fibub tool has it yet.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs the checks in `tests/sci-tools/von-bertalanffy-fitter.mjs` and `tests/sci-tools/fish-length-weight.mjs`:

- On 50 made-up fish, the growth fit must match SciPy's `curve_fit`: L∞ 60.878, K 0.21225 and t0 −0.6973, with matching standard errors, and φ' 2.8958.
- New with this article: the mean dentex lengths at ages 1 to 8 (11.58 to 21.27 cm) must give back L∞ 25.95 cm, K 0.1604 and t0 −2.687, and φ' 2.033.
- The length-weight fit must match SciPy's `linregress` on 20 made-up fish (b 3.0892, its 95 % interval 3.0249 to 3.1535) and the t test of b against 3 must give t = 2.913, p = 0.0093.

## Check it yourself

**The growth curve, on the [[von-bertalanffy-fitter]]:**

1. Clear the box *One row per fish: age, length* and type these eight rows (age, mean length in cm): **1,11.58**, **2,13.71**, **3,15.52**, **4,17.07**, **5,18.38**, **6,19.5**, **7,20.46**, **8,21.27**.
2. Leave *Length unit* on **cm**.
3. The result reads about **L∞ 25.9 cm, K 0.16 per year, t₀ −2.7 years**, and *Growth performance φ'* shows **2.03**.
4. The *Ford-Walford estimate* line gives nearly the same L∞ and K from the mean lengths alone.

**The weight rule, on the [[fish-length-weight]] tool:**

1. Type five rows of length and weight from the paper's female equation: **12, 33.1**, **15, 55.8**, **18, 85.4**, **21, 122.4**, **24, 167.3**.
2. Leave *Length in* on **cm** and *Weight in* on **g**.
3. It reads **W = 0.0995 × L^2.337 (negative allometric)**, and *b against 3* says **negative allometric**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Fit growth from your own otolith or scale readings** with the [[von-bertalanffy-fitter]], and see from the intervals whether old fish are missing from the sample.
- **Sanity-check a growth table**: put published L∞ and K into φ' and length-at-age, and compare them with the text, as we did here.
- **Compare sexes or regions** by fitting each group and looking at lengths at the ages you actually sampled, not only L∞.
- **Turn growth into management advice**: carry K, t0, M and the weight rule into the [[yield-per-recruit]] model to see how catch per fish changes with fishing pressure and the age at first capture.
- **Teach** why L∞ and K trade off against each other when the oldest fish are rare.

## The fine print

- Our fish are **simulated**; the paper's otolith readings and raw lengths are not used here.
- The paper assumes, but has not yet proved, that the ear stones lay down one band pair a year. If they do not, every age and growth figure shifts.
- The φ' difference comes from rounding in the published values; it does not mean the paper's fit is wrong.
- Weight-at-length rules apply only within the measured lengths (roughly 12 to 22 cm here).
