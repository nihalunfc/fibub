When a chemist reports a new compound, three simple numbers have to agree: the formula, the molar mass and the elemental analysis. They are also the numbers that readers can check without a laboratory. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Chemistry* describes an iron porphyrin complex with a bicarbonate ligand, grown as crystals with a potassium cryptand counter-ion, and prints all three, together with the reagent amounts. The [[molar-mass-calculator]] recomputes each, and two of the printed values turn out to be telling. For the stock and dilution arithmetic around such a synthesis use the [[solution-calculator]], and for the mass-spectrum pattern of a formula the [[isotope-pattern-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors reduce an iron(III) porphyrin (the "picket-fence" porphyrin TpivPP, with a triflate and a water on the metal) to iron(II) with zinc amalgam, then add cryptand-2,2,2 and potassium hydrogen carbonate (KHCO₃) in chlorobenzene. The product is crystallised by slow diffusion of hexane. The paper then characterises it by X-ray diffraction, UV-visible and infrared spectroscopy and computations. In the synthesis section it gives the formula with one chlorobenzene per complex, the molecular weight, the calculated and found percentages of carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen, and the amount of each starting material in milligrams and millimoles.

## Checked with the tools

**Molar mass and calculated percentages.** The formula C₈₉H₁₀₆ClFeN₁₀KO₁₃ gives **1654.277 g/mol** in the molar mass calculator (the paper prints 1654.25), with carbon 64.62 %, hydrogen 6.46 % and nitrogen 8.47 % (paper: 64.61, 6.47, 8.46). The crystallographic formula without the solvent, C₈₃H₁₀₁FeKN₁₀O₁₃, gives 1541.721 g/mol (paper 1541.68), and the difference, 112.556 g/mol, is exactly one chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl. The printed numbers are right to within the last digit.

**The "found" values fit the solvent-free formula.** Carbon 64.65, hydrogen 6.61 and nitrogen 9.08 % are the measured values. Against the solvate they are off by +0.03, +0.15 and **+0.61** percentage points, so nitrogen looks too high by a wide margin (many journals accept up to 0.4 points). Against the solvent-free formula the tool gives C 64.66, H 6.60 and N 9.09 %, and the differences are **−0.01, +0.01 and −0.01** points. The best reading, which is **our inference, not a statement in the paper**, is that the bulk powder analysed had lost its chlorobenzene, as crystals with lattice solvent often do when dried, so the analysis matches the desolvated compound while the "calculated" column was worked out for the solvate.

**The mmol of one reagent do not match its mass.** From the printed masses the tool gives 0.0812 mmol for the starting complex (paper 0.081) and 0.3187 mmol for the cryptand (0.319), both in agreement. For KHCO₃, 190 mg is **1.898 mmol** (M = 100.114 g/mol), but the paper prints 2.262 mmol, which is 19 % higher. The printed figure matches the molar mass of **sodium** hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO₃ (84.006 g/mol): 190 mg is 2.262 mmol. Either the mmol was calculated with the wrong salt or the reagent was written as KHCO₃ in error; the product contains potassium, which points to a slip in the mmol. The excess of bicarbonate over iron is then 23.4-fold, not the 27.9-fold the printed numbers imply.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/fe-porphyrin-bicarbonate-complex-molar-mass-elemental-analysis-percent-recomputed.mjs` imports `parseFormula`, `molarMass` and `composition` from `src/lib/sci/chem.ts`, the maths of the Molar mass calculator. The formulas, masses, printed mmol and found percentages come from the paper's synthesis section. The starting complex's formula is built from its name (our assumption) and checked against the printed 0.081 mmol. Figure 1 plots found minus calculated for the two formulas; Figure 2 plots printed mmol divided by mmol recomputed from the mass and a molar mass.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/molar-mass-calculator.mjs` is new with this article. It pins the tool to hand calculations: C₈₉H₁₀₆ClFeN₁₀KO₁₃ gives 1654.277 g/mol and its solvent-free partner 1541.721 g/mol; their difference equals the molar mass of chlorobenzene (112.556 g/mol); the percentages of C, H and N are 64.62, 6.46 and 8.47 for the solvate and 64.66, 6.60 and 9.09 for the dry formula; KHCO₃ is 100.114 and NaHCO₃ 84.006 g/mol; and the percentages of a compound add up to 100. It runs on every change with `node tests/sci.mjs`.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[molar-mass-calculator]] and type `C89H106ClFeN10KO13` in **Formula**.
2. The molar mass reads 1654.28 g/mol, and "What it is made of" gives carbon 64.62 %, hydrogen 6.46 % and nitrogen 8.47 %.
3. Replace the formula by `C83H101FeKN10O13`: the molar mass is 1541.72 g/mol and nitrogen is 9.09 %, the paper's found 9.08 %.
4. Type `KHCO3` and, under "Grams, moles and particles", set **Mass (g)** to 0.19. The amount is about 0.0019 mol (1.898 mmol). Do the same with `NaHCO3` and you get 2.262 mmol.

## What researchers can use it for

- Checking a new compound's characterisation: formula, molar mass and calculated percentages should take a minute, and any mismatch is worth asking about before review.
- Finding a hidden solvate: if the found percentages fit the formula without solvent, say so in the paper.
- Cross-checking a synthesis table: mass divided by molar mass should give the printed mmol; a mismatch such as KHCO₃ against NaHCO₃ is caught at once.
- Preparing the dilutions that follow a synthesis with the [[solution-calculator]].
- Teaching how formula weight and elemental analysis connect, with a real compound.

## The fine print

This recomputes arithmetic from numbers printed in the paper. It does not test the structure, the spectra or the computed results, and it cannot tell whether the discrepancies are typing slips or real differences. The "lost chlorobenzene" explanation and the identity of the starting complex's formula are our reading. Standard atomic weights differ slightly between tables, which explains the last digit of the molar masses. Education, not a reason to doubt the authors' crystal structure.
