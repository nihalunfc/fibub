Some pairs of stars orbit so close that their surfaces touch. These **contact binaries** whirl round each other in less than a day and share one outer layer, like a peanut. TV UMi, in the constellation of the Little Bear, is one of them, but it only just touches, and we see its orbit at a shallow tilt, so its eclipses are faint. A new study combines twenty sectors of TESS light curves with old, unused spectra to weigh and measure both stars to a few percent. This article shows how the [[radial-velocity-fitter]], the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]] and the [[blackbody-calculator]] let you check the orbit, the speeds and the light.

## The paper in brief

The light from TV UMi rises and falls almost like a smooth wave every 0.4155 days, about 10 hours. A smooth wave can come from many things, so the team needed a second kind of data. They found radial velocities, the speeds of the stars towards and away from us, measured years ago at the David Dunlap Observatory in Canada and never analysed. Fitting the light and the speeds together gave an orbit tilted **48.3 degrees** from face-on, masses of **1.254 and 0.929 times the Sun's**, and radii of **1.240 and 1.080 times the Sun's**. Both stars are a little hotter than the Sun, near 6,200 to 6,300 K.

The stars overfill their shared boundary by only about **2%**: a "marginal" contact. A dark spot on one star drifts over time, and its movement matches small shifts in the eclipse times. The team classes TV UMi as a young W-subtype contact binary, about 2 billion years old. In a W-subtype system the smaller star is the hotter one, as here.

## Checked with the tools

We used the paper's period, tilt, masses, radii and temperatures, and fibub's maths. We did not have the spectra, so the velocity fit in Step 2 uses **simulated** speeds and says so.

**Step 1: how close are they?** Kepler's third law, with the two masses together (2.18 Suns) and the 0.4155-day period, puts the centres of the stars **3.04 solar radii** apart. The two radii add up to 2.32 solar radii, **76%** of that gap. Each star's radius is 0.408 and 0.355 of the separation. The Roche lobe, the region each star's gravity controls, is 0.405 and 0.353 of the separation for this mass ratio (Eggleton's formula). So each star just overfills its lobe, by under 1% in radius, which fits the paper's "marginal contact".

**Step 2: weighing the stars from their speeds.** At the paper's tilt, the masses predict speed swings (semi-amplitudes, K) of **117.6 km/s** for the heavier star and **158.7 km/s** for the lighter one. We made 24 pretend spectra over 40 nights with 8 km/s of scatter, and gave them to the Radial velocity fitter's maths with only a search range for the period. It found a period of 0.41546 to 0.41555 days and swings of **118.5 ± 1.8** and **157.3 ± 2.4 km/s** (Figure 1). From those two numbers alone, the masses come out as 0.515 and 0.388 Suns times sin³ *i*, a factor that hides how tilted the orbit is. Dividing by sin³ 48.28° = 0.416 gives **1.24 and 0.93 Suns**, back to the paper's masses. The speed ratio gives the mass ratio, 0.753, against the paper's 0.741.

**Step 3: why the tilt matters so much.** Spectra only see motion along our line of sight. Figure 2 shows how the combined swing K₁ + K₂ would change with the tilt for these same stars: **370 km/s** edge-on, but only **276 km/s** at 48 degrees. Masses go as the cube of the speeds, so near 48 degrees a 1 degree error in the tilt moves the masses by about 4.7%. That is why the shallow, hard-to-model eclipses limit the precision, and why the paper's few-percent masses are a careful result.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/tv-umi-marginal-contact-binary-tess-masses.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls the tools' own functions:

- `fitOrbit`, `rv` and `binary` from `src/lib/sci/rvfit.ts`, the engine of the [[radial-velocity-fitter]].
- `semiMajor` and `semiAmplitude` from `src/lib/sci/rvmass.ts`, the maths behind the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]]. The formulas are the same for a planet and a star.
- `star` from `src/lib/sci/blackbody.ts`, the maths behind the [[blackbody-calculator]].

Where the inputs come from: the period, tilt, masses, radii, temperatures and luminosities are from the paper. The Roche lobe check uses Eggleton's 1983 formula, worked in the script, not a fibub tool. The number of pretend spectra (24), their spread over 40 nights, the 8 km/s scatter and a systemic speed of zero are our own choices, labelled in the script.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks these functions against known answers:

- The orbit maths must give Jupiter's pull on the Sun as 12.47 m/s, and 51 Pegasi b's orbit as 0.053 au.
- The Radial velocity fitter must recover a simulated eccentric binary (period 12.345 days, swing 23.4 km/s, eccentricity 0.42) within three times its quoted errors, and its mass function must match the textbook form 1.0361 × 10⁻⁷ (1 − e²)^1.5 K³ P.
- A circular fit to 30 noisy velocities must recover a 4.2308-day period and a swing of 55.9 within three times its errors.
- The blackbody maths must give the Sun exactly one solar luminosity and an absolute magnitude of 4.82.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a binary's masses** against its speeds, period and tilt before using them in a model.
- **Fit velocities from spectra** without installing anything; the data stay on your computer.
- **See how the tilt limits the masses:** try a few inclinations in the mass calculator and watch the speeds change.
- **Compare a star's size and temperature with its brightness**, as a quick check on a table of stellar parameters.
- **Teach** how double stars are weighed, and why eclipses are so valuable for fixing the tilt.

## The fine print

- The velocities in Figure 1 are **simulated** from the paper's numbers. They show the fitter can recover orbits like these, not what the observatory's spectra contain.
- In a contact binary the stars are not spheres. The radii here are "equal-volume" radii, and the Roche lobe check is only a rough guide.
- The luminosities treat each star as a uniform black body; spots and the shared envelope make the real light more complex.
- We assumed a circular orbit, as is normal for stars this close.
