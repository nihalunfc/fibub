New minerals are still approved every year, and each one starts the same way: someone holds a dark, shiny grain and asks what it is. A 2026 paper in the *European Journal of Mineralogy* names **xianhuaite-(Ce)**, a niobium mineral from the huge Bayan Obo deposit in Inner Mongolia, China. It makes a fine test for a beginner's tool: a rock hound with a streak plate, a scratch kit and a scale would see hardness, lustre, streak and cleavage, but would the simple method get anywhere near the answer? Try the [[mineral-id-key]], then turn a formula into a molar mass with the [[mineral-formula]] tool and a weight in air and water into a density with the [[specific-gravity]] tool.

## The paper in brief

Xianhuaite-(Ce) has the ideal formula K₂CeNb₅O₁₅. It crystallises in the tetragonal system with a framework of corner-sharing NbO₆ octahedra, in a structure type known as a **tetragonal tungsten bronze**; the authors say it is the first natural mineral with that structure. Potassium, barium and cerium and other rare-earth elements sit in the channels. It comes from skarn-type rock in two places in the Bayan Obo deposit and occurs with other niobium minerals such as fersmite and columbite-(Fe).

The hand-specimen properties the authors report: dark brown to black-brown in hand specimen, pale yellow-brown to brownish-red in thin fragments, **white streak**, **adamantine lustre** (the sparkle of a diamond), **Mohs hardness 5 to 6**, a Vickers microhardness averaging 469.5 kg/mm², brittle with conchoidal fracture, **perfect cleavage** on one plane {001}, translucent, no fluorescence under long-wave ultraviolet light, not magnetic and a **calculated density of 5.23 g/cm³** (no measured density is reported). The cell dimensions are a = 12.5355 Å, c = 3.9213 Å for one crystal, and a = 12.5458 Å, c = 3.9279 Å from powder, with two formula units per cell.

## Checked with the tools

We gave the key the paper's hand-specimen properties: hardness 5.5 (the middle of 5 to 6, since the page takes one number), specific gravity 5.23 (the calculated value, as the paper has no measured one), adamantine lustre, white streak, one cleavage direction, brown colour, not strongly magnetic and no fizz in cold acid. The last two are partly **our assumptions**: the paper reports no magnetism and does not test acid, and a niobium oxide is not a carbonate. We also took "dark brown to black-brown" as brown.

**Step 1: the key finds no match.** With the page's default strictness (hardness within ±0.5, density within ±0.2, one mismatch allowed) the key's list is **empty**. Allowing two mismatches gives 11 minerals, led by augite, biotite, cassiterite, gypsum, hornblende and kyanite, none better than 5.5 points out of 8: each one is wrong on density and on something else. The best "answer" is simply the least bad.

**Step 2: why.** Hardness 5.5 and density 5.23 alone fit chromite, hematite and magnetite exactly (and ilmenite and pyrite nearly); five minerals in all have no mismatch. They are metallic or dark with dark or red-brown streaks, which this mineral is not. The only common minerals with a white streak and a density above 4.5 are barite (4.3 to 4.6) and cassiterite (6.8 to 7.1), and neither fits both density and hardness. In plain terms, **a dense, hard, white-streaked, adamantine mineral with one cleavage is outside the key's table**. Figure 1 shows how the count of fitting minerals grows as the density tolerance is opened: with hardness and density alone it climbs to 17 within ±1.5 g/cm³, but with all eight observations it stays at zero. Only when we open the hardness tolerance to ±1 and the density tolerance to ±2 g/cm³ does one mineral survive, sillimanite, an unconvincing match, since its density is about 2 g/cm³ lower.

**Step 3: recomputing the density.** Density from a unit cell is Z × M ÷ (N_A × V), with M the formula mass in daltons and V the volume in Å³. The cell volume is 12.5355² × 3.9213 = 616.19 Å³. For the ideal formula K₂CeNb₅O₁₅ (M = 922.83) the density is **4.97 g/cm³**. For the authors' empirical formula, which has barium, other rare earths and some iron (M = 955.54), we get **5.15 g/cm³** (5.13 with the powder cell). The paper's 5.23 g/cm³ is 1.5 % higher than our 5.15; we do not know which atomic masses, cell or formula it used, so we simply report that our own sum lands close but not on top. Either way, the density is far above any common silicate or carbonate: that single number rules out most of the key's list.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/xianhuaite-ce-new-mineral-simple-key-identification.mjs` calls `rank` and the table `MINERALS` from `src/lib/sci/mineralkey.ts`, the maths of the [[mineral-id-key]], and counts, for each tolerance, how many minerals have no mismatch. The density sum is plain arithmetic with the standard atomic weights listed in the sources.

The hand-specimen properties, the cell and the empirical formula come from the paper's text. The brown colour, the "not strongly magnetic" and "no fizz" entries, and using the calculated density as the specific gravity are **our assumptions**.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/mineral-id-key.mjs`. It checks that the table is sane (at least 40 minerals, unique ids, hardness 1 to 10, known categories), that 40 random observation sets rank exactly as an independent Python version does, and that textbook cases come out right: hard, glassy, white streak and no cleavage gives quartz; metallic with grey streak and cubic cleavage and density 7.5 gives galena; strongly magnetic and black gives magnetite. For this article we added three checks: the dense white-streaked case above has no match within one mismatch and 11 within two; hardness 5.5 with density 5.23 gives chromite, hematite and magnetite first, with five minerals having no mismatch; and only sillimanite survives with hardness ±1 and density ±2 g/cm³.

## Check it yourself

On the [[mineral-id-key]] page, under *What you see*:

1. Set *Hardness from* to **a number I type (Mohs)** and type **5.5** in *Mohs hardness*. Type **5.23** in *Specific gravity*.
2. Set *Lustre* to **Adamantine**, *Streak* to **White**, *Cleavage* to **1 direction (sheets or flat flakes)** and *Colour* to **Brown**.
3. Under *Simple tests*, set *Strongly magnetic* to **no** and *Fizzes in cold dilute acid* to **no**.
4. Under *How strict*, leave the tolerances at **± 0.5** and **± 0.2** with *Allow mismatches* **1**. The page says that no mineral fits. Change *Allow mismatches* to **2**: **11** of 51 minerals appear, none scoring more than **5.5** out of 8.
5. Remove the specific gravity (clear the box) and the key suggests augite, biotite, cassiterite and gypsum at **5.5 of 7**, each with one mismatch: without the density the key is lured towards common silicates.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Teaching the limits of a key:** show students what "no match" looks like, and why a rare mineral needs X-ray diffraction, not a streak plate. A peak list from the [[xrd-peak-calculator]] is the next step.
- **Sanity-checking a description:** type a new mineral's published properties and see which common minerals it could be mistaken for in the field.
- **Checking a calculated density:** redo the Z × M ÷ (N_A × V) sum from the cell and formula, as above.
- **Choosing tolerances:** widen or narrow them and watch the count of matches, as in Figure 1, to learn how much each observation helps.
- **Field kits:** list which tests (streak, hardness, density by weighing) would separate the look-alikes.

## The fine print

- The key covers about 50 common minerals with rounded values, not the thousands of known species. A "no match" result means "not in this table", not "unknown to science".
- Our specific gravity is the paper's calculated value; a measured one could differ.
- The colour, magnetism and acid entries are partly our assumptions, as noted above.
- Our 5.15 g/cm³ comes from standard atomic weights, the single-crystal cell and the empirical formula in the text; the paper's 5.23 may rest on other choices we cannot see.
