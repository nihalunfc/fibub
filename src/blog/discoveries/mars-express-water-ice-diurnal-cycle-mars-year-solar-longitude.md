Clouds on Mars keep Martian time, not ours. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Astronomy and Space Sciences* works through fourteen Earth years of Mars Express spectra to measure how the planet's **water-ice clouds** change through the Martian day and through the Martian year, and every date in it is given as a Mars Year and a **solar longitude** (Ls), the angle Mars has travelled round the Sun since its northern spring equinox. Those are the two numbers fibub's [[mars-time]] converts, so we rebuilt the paper's whole time frame from its stated Mars Years and solar longitudes. For the planet's own numbers behind the geometry, the [[planet-data-explorer]] has them, and if the Martian equation of time surprises you, compare it with Earth's in the [[sun-position-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The Planetary Fourier Spectrometer on Mars Express measures infrared spectra; its short-wavelength channel can be inverted for the amount of water ice in the column of air below the spacecraft. The authors process retrievals from **orbit 652 (July 2004, Mars Year 27, Ls ≈ 64°) to orbit 18,970 (December 2018, Mars Year 34, Ls ≈ 315°)** — about eight Mars years — and bin them in 2.5° of latitude by 5° of Ls.

Their subject is the **aphelion cloud belt**: a band of water-ice cloud over the tropics, roughly 10° S to 30° N, that forms between Ls 30° and 180° and is sharpest around Ls 75° to 135°. Because Mars Express is in an elliptical, drifting orbit, its observations cover roughly 06:00 to 18:00 local time, which lets the team build a daily cycle. They find cloud opacity highest in the early morning near 06:00, lowest between 10:00 and 14:00, and rising again after 14:00 — a swing of about **25 % of the daily maximum**. The Mars Climate Database, by contrast, predicts a swing of a **factor of 8 to 10**. Peak opacities come out near 0.22 to 0.25.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation** of Mars's own calendar and geometry from the paper's stated Mars Years and solar longitudes, with the Mars24 algorithm. No data are fitted and nothing is simulated.

**Step 1: the two ends of the dataset land exactly where the paper says.** Ask the tool when Mars reached Ls 64° in Mars Year 27 and it answers **22 July 2004**; Ls 315° in Mars Year 34 is **29 December 2018**. Both match the paper's "July 2004" and "December 2018" without adjustment, which means the paper and the tool are counting Mars Years the same way (from Ls 0 on 11 April 1955).

| Check | Paper says | Mars time gives |
|---|---|---|
| Orbit 652: MY27, Ls ≈ 64° | July 2004 | **22 July 2004** |
| Orbit 18,970: MY34, Ls ≈ 315° | December 2018 | **29 December 2018** |
| Cloud-belt season Ls 30° to 180° | "aphelion" season | aphelion falls at **Ls 71°**, inside it |
| Length of the Ls 45° to 150° window | not stated | **230.3 Earth days, 224.1 sols**, every Mars year |
| Subsolar latitude over Ls 45° to 150° | belt at 10° S to 30° N | **+17.7° to +25.4° to +12.4°**, inside the belt |

**Step 2: the belt really is an aphelion phenomenon.** The tool's own orbit model puts Mars farthest from the Sun, **1.666 AU, at Ls 71°**, and closest, **1.381 AU, at Ls 251°**. Sunlight at perihelion is therefore **1.46 times** stronger than at aphelion. The paper's Ls 30°–180° cloud season brackets Ls 71° almost symmetrically: the belt forms because the planet is coldest then and water ice can condense over the tropics.

**Step 3: every Mars year's window, in Earth dates.** A diurnal study needs the same season in every year, and the paper uses Ls 45° to 150°. That window lasts **230.3 Earth days (224.1 sols)** every time, but it slides through the Earth calendar by about 1.9 Earth years per Mars year, which is exactly why mission planning needs a converter:

| Mars Year | Ls 45° starts | Ls 150° ends | Aphelion (Ls 71°) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27 | 9 June 2004 | 25 January 2005 | 7 August 2004 |
| 28 | 27 April 2006 | 13 December 2006 | 25 June 2006 |
| 29 | 14 March 2008 | 30 October 2008 | 12 May 2008 |
| 30 | 30 January 2010 | 17 September 2010 | 30 March 2010 |
| 31 | 18 December 2011 | 4 August 2012 | 15 February 2012 |
| 32 | 4 November 2013 | 22 June 2014 | 2 January 2014 |
| 33 | 22 September 2015 | 9 May 2016 | 20 November 2015 |
| 34 | 9 August 2017 | 27 March 2018 | 7 October 2017 |

Through the whole of that window it is **northern spring turning into northern summer** (southern autumn into winter), and the Sun stands overhead between about 12° N and 25° N — inside the cloud belt's own 10° S to 30° N band. The belt is lit from almost straight above all season, which is why it has a daily cycle worth measuring at all.

**Step 4: the Martian equation of time is big, and it lands on the paper's bins.** The paper bins by **local true solar time** — sundial time, where noon is when the Sun crosses the meridian. Mean solar time, the clock a lander keeps, differs from it by the **equation of time**, and on Mars that term is far larger than on Earth: the tool gives a range from **−51.0 minutes at Ls 330° to +40.0 minutes at Ls 180°**, a swing of **91 minutes**, against about 31 minutes on Earth. Inside the paper's own Ls 45°–150° window it moves from **−8.7 minutes at Ls 45° to +14.8 at Ls 90° and +32.9 at Ls 150°**. A 4-hour morning bin (06:00 to 10:00 true solar time) therefore shifts by more than half an hour against the mean-time clock across one season, and more than a quarter of the bin width if the two time scales are ever mixed. Any diurnal result quoted to the hour has to say which of the two it means; this paper does, and that is worth copying.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/mars-express-water-ice-diurnal-cycle-mars-year-solar-longitude.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `lsTime` (when a Mars Year reaches a solar longitude), `marsTime` (Mars Sol Date, Coordinated Mars Time, Ls, equation of time, heliocentric distance and subsolar latitude) and `season` from `src/lib/sci/marstime.ts`, the maths behind [[mars-time]].

The inputs are the paper's own stated values: Mars Years 27 to 34, Ls 64° and 315° for the two ends of the dataset, Ls 30°–180° for the cloud season, Ls 45°–150° for the diurnal window and 10° S to 30° N for the belt's latitudes. The dates, distances, subsolar latitudes and equation-of-time values are computed, not taken from the paper. We sampled one Mars year (Mars Year 34) for the curves; the orbit changes so little between Mars years that the curves are the same to the width of the line.

## How we test the maths

`npm test` runs `tests/sci.mjs` on every change, and its Mars-time section pins the algorithm against NASA's own published Mars24 output:

- For 6 January 2000, 00:00 UTC, the worked example in Allison and McEwen (2000), every intermediate quantity must match: mean anomaly **21.74558°**, fictitious mean Sun **272.74566°**, Ls **277.18758°**, equation of time **−5.18774°**, Mars Sol Date **44795.99976**, Coordinated Mars Time **23.9942 h** and heliocentric distance **1.39358 AU**.
- Curiosity's landing must come out as **sol 0** at Gale crater at 15.05 h local mean solar time, and its sol 1000 must begin on 30 May 2015.
- Mars Years 36, 37 and 38 must begin (Ls 0°) on **7 February 2021**, **26 December 2022** and **12 November 2024**, and 1 January 2025 must fall in Mars Year 38.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/mars-time.mjs`: Mars Year 27 must reach Ls 64° on 22 July 2004 and Mars Year 34 must reach Ls 315° on 29 December 2018, the two ends of this paper's dataset; the Ls 45° to 150° window must last **230.3 Earth days** in each of Mars Years 27 to 34; and the equation of time must run between **−51.0 and +40.0 minutes**.

## Check it yourself

On the [[mars-time]] page:

1. Press **Earth date** and type **2004-07-22 12:00:00** into *Earth date and time (your clock)*. Leave *Where on Mars* on its first entry.
2. The *Season* fact reads **Ls 63.83°, Mars Year 27**, with "northern spring, southern autumn" under it. That is the paper's first orbit.
3. *Distance from the Sun* reads **1.6646 AU** (249 million km) — within a thousandth of an AU of aphelion. *Sun overhead at* reads **22.68° latitude**, inside the cloud belt.
4. Scroll to the *Mars Year 27* table. *Aphelion (farthest from the Sun)* is listed at **Ls 71°** on **7 August 2004**, and *Next Mars Year starts* on **21 January 2006**.
5. Now type **2018-12-29 12:00:00**: *Season* becomes **Ls 314.94°, Mars Year 34**, and the note adds "dust storm season". That is the paper's last orbit, fourteen Earth years and seven Mars years later.
6. Watch the *Local true solar time* fact on each of those two dates. Its small print gives the equation of time: **+3.6 min** in July 2004 and **−48.6 min** in December 2018. That single number is the gap between sundial time and clock time, and it is why a diurnal study has to name its time scale.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a paper's Ls into a date** (or a date into an Ls and a Mars Year) before you go looking for overlapping observations from another spacecraft.
- **Plan a seasonal campaign**: the same Ls window recurs every 687 days, so the table in Step 3 is the shape of any multi-year Mars observing plan.
- **Check a diurnal binning**: read the equation of time for your season and decide whether true or mean solar time changes your answer.
- **Convert rover sols to Earth dates** for Curiosity, Perseverance or InSight, which the tool does directly from a sol number.
- **Sanity-check a season label**: the tool names the northern and southern season and flags the Ls 180°–330° dust-storm season, which is where cloud retrievals get hard.
- **Teach orbital eccentricity**: Figure 1 and the 1.46 insolation ratio explain Mars's lopsided seasons in one picture, next to the planet's bulk numbers in the [[planet-data-explorer]].

## The fine print

- We checked the paper's **time frame and geometry**, not its retrievals. The opacity values, the shape of the daily cycle and the comparison with the Mars Climate Database are the paper's own work and need its spectra to test.
- The tool's Mars Year numbering follows Clancy et al. (2000) and NASA's Mars24; a paper using a different epoch would be offset by a whole Mars year, and the agreement in Step 1 is itself the check that this paper does not.
- The paper gives no separate numeric morning and afternoon means in its text, so there is no "paper says / tool gives" row for the daily cycle itself.
- Local true solar time on Mars Express depends on where the spacecraft is, not just on the date; our equation-of-time figures are the seasonal term, which applies everywhere on the planet at once.
- Mars Express's orbit drifts in local time slowly, so "06:00 to 18:00 coverage" is built up over years, not available in any one season. Mixing years is unavoidable in this kind of study and is a real limitation of the result, not of the clock.
