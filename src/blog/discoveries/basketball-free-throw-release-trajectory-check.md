A free throw is one of the few shots in sport whose physics is completely fixed: the same distance, the same ring, no defender. So when a biomechanics paper reports the release height, release angle and release speed of made and missed shots, those three numbers can be flown as a projectile and checked against the ring. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* did the measuring, on 25 trained players and 25 novices; here we put its four group means through the [[projectile-drag]] tool and see where the ball actually arrives. If you want to measure your own release numbers from a phone video, start with the [[video-motion-tracker]], and carry the camera's pixel error through to the speed with the [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

Fifty right-handed men aged 18 to 24 each took 20 free throws, filmed from the side at 240 frames per second and digitised in two dimensions against a one-metre calibration grid (about 0.0025 m per pixel). The trained group made 67.8 % of their shots, the novices 38.9 %.

The paper then splits every shot into made and missed and reports group means for joint angles and for the three release variables. Made shots came off the hand **higher** (2.28 m for players, 2.05 m for novice misses), at a **steeper angle** (52.2° down to 47.9°) and **faster** (7.82 down to 7.21 m/s), and release height and elbow extension correlated most strongly with success (r = 0.71 and r = 0.75 in the player group). The authors are careful to add that after a Holm-Bonferroni correction for twelve tests, none of the single variables stayed significant, and that a 2D side view cannot see sideways error.

## Checked with the tools

We flew each group mean as a point-mass ball with quadratic air drag, using the maths behind the Projectile drag tool: mass 0.6235 kg, diameter 24.1 cm, drag coefficient 0.5 (**our assumption**, the usual range for a basketball is about 0.45 to 0.55), still air, standard gravity. The ball must arrive at the ring 3.048 m above the floor and 4.191 m from the free-throw line. The paper gives the shooting distance as 4.57 m, which is the line-to-backboard distance; the centre of the ring is 4.191 m from the line. We assume the ball leaves the hand directly above the line (**our assumption**; a release 0.4 m in front of the line shortens the flight, and we show below what that does).

For the ball's **centre** to pass inside the ring, it has to cross ring height within 0.108 m of the ring's centre (half the ring's inner diameter minus the ball's radius).

**The speed the physics wants runs the other way.** Shooting from higher and steeper needs *less* speed, not more. Figure 2 shows the release speed that drops the ball on the ring centre for every release angle, at the highest and lowest mean release height in the paper. The four requirements are 7.33, 7.39, 7.43 and 7.55 m/s, in that order — the exact reverse of the measured order (7.82, 7.46, 7.52, 7.21).

So the measured speeds are 0.09 to 0.49 m/s too high for players and 0.34 m/s too low for the novices' misses. Two things are mixed up in that gap, and the tool can separate them:

- **Averaging each variable on its own.** A mean height, a mean angle and a mean speed do not have to be a shot that goes in, because the three trade off against each other within every shooter. This is the honest reading of the players' rows, and it is worth remembering before any group mean of release variables is treated as a template.
- **A possible scale error in the 2D speeds.** The players' made mean needs 7.33 m/s and 6.7 % less speed than reported. A release 0.4 m in front of the line only lowers the requirement further, to 7.01 m/s, so geometry does not explain the gap; nor does drag, which only adds 0.30 m/s to the 7.03 m/s a vacuum would need. At 0.0025 m per pixel and 240 frames per second, one pixel of digitising error over a two-frame interval is already about 0.3 m/s, so a few per cent on speed is within what a side-view digitisation can do.

| Check | What the tool gives |
|---|---|
| Speed needed, player made mean (Cd 0.5) | 7.33 m/s |
| Same with no drag at all | 7.03 m/s |
| Same with Cd 0.45 / 0.54 / 0.6 | 7.30 / 7.36 / 7.40 m/s |
| Same with the release 0.4 m in front of the line | 7.01 m/s |
| Speed window for the ball's centre to stay inside the ring | 7.25 to 7.42 m/s |

The last line is the useful one for coaching: at a fixed height and angle, the whole margin for error in release speed is about **0.17 m/s**, a little over 2 %. The paper's own standard deviations for release speed are 0.30 to 0.41 m/s, two to three times that window, which is a neat quantitative reason why nobody makes every free throw.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/basketball-free-throw-release-trajectory-check.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `simulate` from `src/lib/sci/phprojectile.ts` — the fourth-order Runge-Kutta integration of Newton's second law with quadratic drag that powers the Projectile drag tool — and reads the point where the falling ball's path crosses 3.048 m straight out of the tool's own path arrays. The required release speed is found by bisection on that same simulation.

Inputs from the paper: the four group mean release heights, angles and speeds, the filming rate and the pixel scale. From general reference data: ring height 3.048 m, ring inner diameter 0.4572 m, line-to-ring-centre 4.191 m, ball mass 0.6235 kg, ball diameter 24.1 cm. **Our assumptions**: drag coefficient 0.5, still air, air density 1.225 kg/m³, release directly above the free-throw line, and the ball treated as a point mass with no spin (backspin adds a small lift the tool does not model).

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/projectile-drag.mjs`, which pins the solver against an independent SciPy `solve_ivp` integration with event detection:

- A 145 g ball at 40 m/s and 35° from 1 m must give a range of 94.8501 m, a flight of 4.10186 s and an apex of 21.2472 m at x = 51.748 m.

## Check it yourself

On the [[projectile-drag]] tool page:

1. Set *Object* to **Custom (type your own)**.
2. Type *Launch speed (m/s)* **7.82**, *Launch angle above level (°)* **52.2**, *Launch height above the landing ground (m)* **2.28**, *Mass (kg)* **0.6235**, *Diameter (cm)* **24.1**, *Drag coefficient Cd* **0.5**. Leave the wind at 0, the air density at 1.225 and gravity at 9.80665.
3. *Range* reads **6.70 m**, *Highest point* **4.11 m** (reached 2.77 m along), *Time of flight* **1.54 s**, *Landing speed* **9.35 m/s** and *Terminal speed* **20.9 m/s**. The ball flies 6.7 m before hitting the floor: far past a ring 4.19 m away.
4. Press *Download path (CSV)* and look for the row where the height passes **3.048 m on the way down**. It happens at about **4.81 m** — the overshoot in Figure 1.
5. Change *Launch speed* to **7.33** and repeat: *Range* falls to **6.08 m** and the same CSV crossing lands on **4.19 m**, the ring centre.
6. Set *Drag coefficient Cd* to **0** to see the air's share: the needed speed drops to 7.03 m/s, so the air costs a free throw only about 0.3 m/s.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a release table** before building a model on it: fly the group means and see whether the ball reaches the target at all (Projectile drag).
- **Report a tolerance, not just a mean**: the speed window that still scores (here 0.17 m/s) says more about skill than a group difference does (Projectile drag).
- **Separate measurement error from technique**: carry your pixel scale and frame rate through to a speed uncertainty with the [[uncertainty-calculator]], then compare it with the window above.
- **Digitise your own throws** from a phone video with the [[video-motion-tracker]], and use the jump and reach measures in the [[jump-height]] tool to see how much release height a player can really gain.
- **Teach the trade-off**: Figure 2 is a one-slide answer to "why do tall players shoot flatter?" — more height means less speed needed at the same angle.

## The fine print

- The paths are **our simulations** from the paper's stated group means, not the paper's own trajectories; the paper did not report where its shots crossed ring height.
- A point mass with a fixed drag coefficient ignores backspin lift and the ball's small Magnus force, both of which slightly lengthen a real shot and so would widen the gap we found, not close it.
- Court and ball figures are standard reference values, not measurements from this paper; the paper's 4.57 m is the distance to the backboard.
- Group means of separately averaged variables need not describe any single shot. Our reading of the gap as partly a scale effect is an inference, not the paper's claim.
- A side-view (2D) digitisation cannot see left-right error, which is a large part of real misses.
