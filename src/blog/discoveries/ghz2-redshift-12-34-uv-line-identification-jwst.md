At redshift 12.34 the universe was about **353 million years old**, and a galaxy's whole spectrum is squeezed into the infrared. A 2026 paper in *The Astrophysical Journal* argues that GHZ2, one of the brightest galaxies known at that distance, hosts an **active galactic nucleus** — a feeding black hole — and the argument rests on two emission lines: the **C IV λλ1548,1551** doublet with a rest-frame equivalent width of 45 Å, and **C III] λ1908**. Two lines is not many, so this article uses fibub's [[spectral-line-identifier]] to ask the questions a careful reader asks: are those identifications unique, how tightly do they pin the redshift, and what else should have been visible? For the distance and age behind it, the [[cosmology-calculator]] and the [[redshift-calculator]] do the rest.

## The paper in brief

GHZ2 (also called GLASS-z12) sits behind the Abell 2744 lensing cluster and has a spectroscopic redshift of 12.34. The team analyses its JWST spectrum and finds the **C IV** doublet strong — a rest-frame equivalent width of 45 Å — which is hard to produce with hot stars alone. Fitting models in which an active nucleus and a young stellar population both contribute, they attribute **97 %** of the C IV emission and **64 %** of C III] to the nucleus, and derive a black hole of **log(M/M☉) = 7.19**, about 6 % of the galaxy's stellar mass. That ratio is far above the 0.1 % typical of nearby galaxies, and is part of why these early black holes are a puzzle.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated redshift and line names, using the tool's own NIST rest wavelengths. We do not have the spectrum, so we place the lines where that redshift puts them and then ask the tool to work backwards, as a reader with a line list would.

**Step 1: where the lines land.** At z = 12.34 every wavelength is multiplied by 13.34. The tool's table, in vacuum wavelengths:

This is the structural fact behind the whole paper. The standard way to tell a feeding black hole from hot stars in a nearby galaxy uses the ratios of **Hα, Hβ, [O III] and [N II]** — and at z = 12.34 every one of them has moved past 6 µm, outside NIRSpec's range. The ultraviolet lines are all anyone has, so an ultraviolet argument is not a shortcut here; it is the only road.

**Step 2: the identifications are consistent to 0 km s⁻¹.** Give the tool the two observed wavelengths (20,664.5 Å and 25,462.5 Å) with the redshift set to 12.34, a 20 Å tolerance and the quasar, nebula and star line lists switched on, and each wavelength has exactly **one** match: C IV and C III], both with a velocity offset of **0 km s⁻¹**. No other line in the list comes within 20 Å of either. At this redshift, 1 Å of measurement error is only **14.5 km s⁻¹**, so the observed positions are a sharp lever on the redshift: 1 Å of rest-frame error is a redshift shift of **0.00065**.

**Step 3: with only two lines, there is one rival redshift — and it does not survive.** Asked to find the redshift from the two wavelengths alone with a loose 20 Å tolerance, the tool returns **two** candidates: z = 12.34 (C IV + C III]) and z = **4.244**, where the same two wavelengths would be Ca II K and Hβ. The alias is an artefact of a loose tolerance: the two implied redshifts are 4.2532 and 4.2363, which differ by 1,200 km s⁻¹, and when the tool is then asked to identify the lines **at** z = 4.244 it finds nothing within 20 Å of either. Tighten the tolerance to 10 Å and the alias disappears entirely, leaving only z = 12.34.

| Lines given | Tolerance | Candidate redshifts the tool returns |
|---|---|---|
| C IV, C III] | 20 Å | 4.244 (Hβ + Ca II K), **12.34** (C IV + C III]) |
| C IV, C III] | 10 Å or 5 Å | **12.34** only |
| Lyα, C IV, C III] | 20 Å | **12.34 with all three lines**; four rival redshifts with two lines each |
| Lyα, C IV, C III] | 3 Å | **12.34** only |

Add Lyman α at 1.622 µm — which GHZ2 shows — and z = 12.34 is the only redshift where **three** lines agree, while the rivals never manage more than two. That is the right way to read the tool's output: not the top candidate, but how many lines it ties together. Figure 2 makes the same point by hand: of the 24 strong lines in the tool's list, the three measured wavelengths propose 69 candidate redshifts between them, and only at 12.34 do all three agree to better than 0.05 in z. The nearest-looking rival, near z = 3.35, is off by thousands of km s⁻¹ once you measure it.

**Step 4: the C IV doublet needs resolving power to be a doublet.** The two components, 1548.19 and 1550.77 Å, arrive at **20,652.9 and 20,687.3 Å**, separated by **34.4 Å**. A spectrograph at resolving power R = 1,000 smears each line over about **20.7 Å**, so the doublet is a single blended feature — which is exactly how fibub's line list carries it, as one entry at 1549.06 Å. At R = 2,700 the resolution element is **7.6 Å** and the two components separate. If you are reading the paper's equivalent width of 45 Å, that is a width measured on the blend, and comparing it to model predictions means comparing blends with blends.

**Step 5: the context numbers.** The cosmology calculator puts z = 12.34 at a universe **352.5 million years** old, a lookback time of **13.433 billion years**, and a scale of **3.661 kpc per arcsecond** — so the galaxy's whole measured extent is a small fraction of one NIRSpec shutter, and the lensing magnification is doing real work.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ghz2-redshift-12-34-uv-line-identification-jwst.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `SPEC_LINES`, `restIn`, `identify` (match measured wavelengths at a given redshift, with velocity offsets) and `findRedshift` (the redshifts at which several measured lines agree at once) from `src/lib/sci/speclines.ts`, the maths behind the [[spectral-line-identifier]], and `cosmology` from `src/lib/sci/cosmo.ts` for Step 5.

Inputs: the redshift 12.34 and the line names C IV and C III] come from the paper. The rest wavelengths are the tool's own NIST values. The **observed wavelengths are ours**, worked out from that redshift, because we do not have the paper's spectrum; Step 2 therefore tests internal consistency and uniqueness, not the paper's measurement. The 0.6–5.3 µm NIRSpec range, the tolerances and the resolving powers of 1,000 and 2,700 are **our choices**, typical of JWST prism and medium-resolution gratings.

## How we test the maths

`npm test` runs `tests/sci.mjs` on every change, and it pins this line maths against published values:

- The air-to-vacuum conversion must follow Morton (2000) as the IAU and SDSS use it: Hα must convert from **6564.61 Å** in vacuum to **6562.80 Å** in air and back, to 0.01 Å.
- Three lines (Hβ, [O III] λ5007 and Hα) shifted by a factor 1.158 must give back **z = 0.158** with all three agreeing, to 10⁻⁶.
- On a simulated emission-line galaxy spectrum at z = 0.0832, the peak finder must find all **10** emission lines and `findRedshift` must return 0.0832 to 2 × 10⁻⁴; the telluric O₂ A band dip must be found at 7,600 Å and matched as a band, at z = 0.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/spectral-line-identifier.mjs`: C IV and C III] placed at z = 12.34 must be identified with a zero velocity offset and no competing match within 20 Å; `findRedshift` on those two wavelengths must return **12.34 and the 4.244 alias** at a 20 Å tolerance and **only 12.34** at 10 Å; and adding Lyman α must make z = 12.34 the only candidate with three lines.

## Check it yourself

On the [[spectral-line-identifier]] page:

1. In *Paste wavelengths, one per line*, type the three observed wavelengths in ångströms, one per line: **16217.0**, **20664.5**, **25462.5**.
2. Leave *Wavelength unit* on **ångström (Å)**. Set *Measured in* to **vacuum (SDSS, space telescopes)**, because JWST wavelengths are vacuum wavelengths. Put **10** in *Match within (same unit)*.
3. Under *Look among*, tick **Quasars** and leave **Nebulae and galaxies** and **Stars** ticked; untick **Earth's atmosphere**, which does not apply to a space telescope.
4. Set *Redshift* to **I know it** and type **12.34** in *Redshift z*.
5. The headline reads *Redshift z = 12.3400; 3 of 3 lines identified*, and the table names **Lyman α**, **C IV** and **C III]**, each with an offset under **1 km/s** (the rounding in our typed wavelengths).
6. Now change *Redshift* back to **find it from the lines**. The tool's automatic search covers z = −0.01 to 7, so it will not reach 12.34; with a loose tolerance it offers the spurious **z = 4.244** instead. This is the limit to know about: above redshift 7, type the redshift in rather than hunting for it.
7. Put **3** in *Match within* and set the redshift to **12.34** again: the offsets stay at 0 km/s, so a 3 Å window is enough. Then try **4.244** instead: *0 of 3 lines identified*.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published line identification** in a minute, with the velocity offset printed next to each match, before you build a result on it.
- **Count how many lines support a redshift**, not just whether a redshift fits one line: the tool's candidate list reports the number of lines in each group.
- **Plan which grating to use**: Step 1's table says where each line will fall, and Step 4 says what resolving power splits a doublet at your redshift.
- **Decide whether a diagnostic is reachable at all** before writing a proposal: at high redshift the optical diagnostics leave the band, and the tool shows exactly where.
- **Sort out air and vacuum wavelengths** when combining ground-based and space spectra, which is a silent source of tens of km s⁻¹ of error.
- **Teach redshift** with Figure 1: one chart shows why JWST sees what Hubble could not.

## The fine print

- We did **not** test the paper's measurement. Its article page refuses automated reading, so we worked from its abstract and the publisher's Crossref record, which states CC BY 4.0. Our observed wavelengths are computed from the paper's redshift, not read off its spectrum.
- Nothing here bears on the AGN interpretation itself. The 97 % and 64 % contributions come from population-synthesis and nucleus models that no fibub tool reproduces; we checked the line identifications those models are fed.
- The tool's automatic redshift search stops at z = 7, so the headline redshifts of the JWST era have to be typed in. Setting the redshift yourself gives the full identification.
- Our line list carries the C IV doublet as one blended entry, which suits prism and R ≈ 1,000 spectra and not higher-resolution data.
- The NIRSpec range of 0.6 to 5.3 µm is nominal; the exact short-wavelength cut depends on the disperser and filter chosen.
