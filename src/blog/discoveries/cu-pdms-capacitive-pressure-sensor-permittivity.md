A soft capacitive pressure sensor is a sandwich: two flexible electrodes with a rubbery layer between them. Press it and the layer thins, so the capacitance rises. The trouble is that the whole signal is a couple of picofarads, which makes stray capacitance and the sensor's own packaging matter as much as the pressure. A 2025 paper in *Sensors* attacks the problem from the materials side, loading PDMS silicone with copper powder and carbon black to raise its **relative permittivity** from 3.14 to 5.56, and reports a finished sensor of **4.57 pF** with a sensitivity of 0.0083 per kPa. All of that is one formula away from the sensor's drawing, which is what the [[capacitor-inductor-calculator]] does — including the layers the packaging adds. For the fields inside such a stack, see the [[electrostatics-solver]] and the [[field-line-plotter]].

## The paper in brief

The sensor is 11 × 11 mm. Its dielectric is a 1 mm layer of PDMS filled with about 3 µm copper particles (40 wt% chosen after a sweep from 0 to 50 wt%) and a little carbon black; above and below it sit two 0.1 mm layers of pure PDMS as encapsulation, and the electrodes are conductive fabric 0.1 mm thick: **four 5 × 5 mm pads on top** over one 11 × 11 mm plate underneath.

The measured permittivity rises from **3.14** (pure PDMS) to **4.46** with copper alone and **5.56** with copper plus carbon black at 25 °C, falling to 5.38 at 55 °C. The finished sensor measures **4.57 pF** at 1 V and 100 kHz, keeps 94.3 % of that after 5000 press cycles, and over 0 to 10 kPa its capacitance rises by **8.29 %**, a sensitivity of 0.0083 kPa⁻¹ that flattens to about a quarter of that between 40 and 50 kPa. The paper's model is the textbook pair: C = εrε0S/d, and ΔC/C₀ = Δd/(d₀ − Δd).

## Checked with the tools

The sensor is exactly the shape the [[capacitor-inductor-calculator]] calls *parallel plates with dielectric layers*: a plate area, one or two layers in series. Four 5 × 5 mm pads in parallel make 100 mm², which is **1 cm²** — a convenient number for the tool.

**The pure-PDMS measurement is reproduced exactly.** With A = 1 cm², d = 1 mm and εr = 3.14 the series-stack formula gives

**C = 2.7802 pF**, against the paper's measured **2.78 pF** (and its simulated 2.9 pF).

Each 5 × 5 mm pad on its own is 0.695 pF. Turning the measurement round, 2.78 pF over that geometry implies εr = **3.1398**, which is the paper's 3.14 to three decimal places. Nothing is missing from the simple model at this stage: the electrodes, the fabric and the encapsulation make no measurable difference when the dielectric is the same material as the skin.

**The finished sensor is a different story.** Taking the paper's composite permittivity of 5.56 for the 1 mm layer:

| Model | Capacitance |
|---|---|
| 1 mm composite alone | **4.9229 pF** |
| 1 mm composite + 0.2 mm pure-PDMS skin in series | **3.6355 pF** |
| Measured by the paper | 4.57 pF |

The measurement sits **between** the two, which is the useful finding: the encapsulation layers cannot be fully inside the capacitor (or the sensor would read 3.64 pF), and the composite layer cannot be the full 5.56 either (or it would read 4.92 pF). Reading 4.57 pF backwards gives an implied permittivity of **5.16** if no skin lies between the electrodes, rising to **7.69** if all 0.2 mm does. Figure 2 shows that whole family of answers. The paper's own 5.56, measured separately on a slab, is consistent with a thin effective skin of about 0.03 mm — about what you would expect if the fabric electrodes press into the surface.

**How far does it actually squash?** The paper's measured 8.29 % rise over the first 10 kPa, put through the series-stack maths, needs **76.6 µm** of compression of the 1 mm layer (a strain of 7.66 %) if the dielectric alone is between the plates, and **103.7 µm** if the 0.2 mm skin is in series as well, because the stiff, unsquashed skin dilutes every change. The same reading therefore implies a tangent stiffness of about **131 kPa** (10 kPa over 7.66 % strain), which is soft-silicone territory and consistent with the paper's choice of a hyperelastic model rather than a single Young's modulus. Over 40 to 50 kPa the sensor adds only 2.46 %, which, treated as a further step from the unpressed state, the same maths turns into another 20 µm — the material is stiffening, exactly the saturation the paper reports.

**Where the voltage sits.** With the full stack at the paper's 1 V test voltage, the tool's layer fields are **0.000738 kV/mm in the composite and 0.001308 kV/mm in the skin**: the thin, low-permittivity encapsulation carries **1.77 times** the field of the composite, the ratio of the two permittivities. That is harmless at 1 V, but it is the reason a thin skin costs so much sensitivity — the field, and so the stored energy, concentrates where nothing moves.

**The 5000-cycle drift.** The fall from 4.57 to 4.31 pF is 5.69 %. Read as a permittivity change at constant geometry, it is a drop from 5.16 to **4.87** — a plausible amount of filler rearrangement, and a reminder that a capacitance drift can be geometric or dielectric, and the two look identical from outside.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/cu-pdms-capacitive-pressure-sensor-permittivity.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `plates` (C = ε0A / Σ(dᵢ/εrᵢ), the series stack) and `plateFields` (the field in each layer, from the displacement being the same in all of them) from `src/lib/sci/ph-capind.ts`, the maths behind the [[capacitor-inductor-calculator]]. The permittivity and compression inversions are the same formula solved the other way (by algebra and by bisection).

From the paper: the electrode sizes, the 1 mm dielectric, the two 0.1 mm encapsulation layers, the permittivities, the measured capacitances and the two relative capacitance changes. **Our assumptions**: that the four top pads act as four capacitors in parallel over the same bottom plate with no fringing, that the dielectric compresses uniformly and the encapsulation does not, and that the permittivity does not change as the filler is squeezed closer together (it does a little, which is part of the paper's own point).

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/capacitor-inductor-calculator.mjs`, which pins this module against SciPy and against closed forms:

- 100 cm² plates 1 mm apart in vacuum must give 88.542 pF, that is ε0 exactly, with ε0 = 8.8541878188 × 10⁻¹² F/m (CODATA 2022);
- two 0.5 mm layers of εr 4.4 and 1 in series must give 144.29046816 pF, as SciPy's integration of the same stack does, and the layer fields must add up to the applied voltage with their ratio exactly 4.4;
- a coaxial line of 0.9 and 2.94 mm diameters filled with εr 2.25 must give 105.74 pF/m, 236.75 nH/m and a characteristic impedance of 47.318 Ω.
- **New with this article**: the sensor's own geometry (1 cm², 1 mm, εr 3.14) must give 2.78021 pF; with the 0.2 mm skin added it must give 2.31685 pF; the composite stack must give 4.92288 pF bare and 3.63548 pF encapsulated; and the field in the skin must be exactly 5.56/3.14 times the field in the composite.

## Check it yourself

On the [[capacitor-inductor-calculator]] page:

1. Leave *What to work out* on **Parallel plates (with dielectric layers)**.
2. Type **1** into *Plate area (cm²)* — four 5 × 5 mm pads in parallel — **1** into *Layer 1 thickness (mm)*, **3.14** into *Layer 1 εr*, **0** into *Layer 2 thickness (mm, 0 = none)* and **1** into *Voltage (V)*.
3. *Capacitance* reads **2.78 pF**, the paper's measured value for the pure-PDMS sensor, with the charge at 1 V and the stored energy beneath it.
4. Change *Layer 1 εr* to **5.56**: the capacitance becomes **4.92 pF**, above the 4.57 pF the finished sensor measures.
5. Now add the packaging: *Layer 2 thickness* **0.2** with *Layer 2 εr* **3.14**. The capacitance drops to **3.64 pF**, and the two *Field in layer* lines show 0.000738 and 0.00131 kV/mm — the skin takes 1.77 times the field.
6. Finally make the pressed sensor: set *Layer 1 thickness* to **0.9234** (76.6 µm of squash) with *Layer 2 thickness* back to **0** and εr 5.56. The capacitance is **5.33 pF**, 8.3 % above the 4.92 pF of step 4, which is the paper's measured change over the first 10 kPa.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published capacitance against its own drawing** before trusting a sensitivity: two picofarads of disagreement here is a whole packaging layer.
- **Read a permittivity out of a device measurement**, and see how much the answer depends on what you assume about the packaging — the 5.16-to-7.69 range above is a real uncertainty, not a rounding.
- **Turn a capacitance change into a displacement**, which is how capacitive gauges, touch pads and thickness meters are calibrated.
- **Decide how thin the encapsulation must be**: the field ratio tells you how much of the signal a stiff skin eats, and the tool recomputes the whole stack in one line.
- **Design the read-out** around the result: feed the few picofarads into an RC time constant in the same tool, or a bridge in the [[circuit-simulator]].

## The fine print

- The parallel-plate formula ignores **fringing fields** at the edges of the pads. For 5 × 5 mm pads 1 mm apart the fringe adds a few per cent, in the direction that would reduce the implied permittivity; it is not enough to explain the gap between 4.57 and 4.92 pF, but it is part of it.
- A copper-filled composite near its percolation threshold is **not a simple dielectric**: it has loss, and its permittivity depends on frequency, so an LCR reading at 100 kHz and our single εr are a simplification the paper itself discusses.
- We assume the encapsulation does not compress. In reality both layers squash, which puts the true compression between our two numbers.
- Our implied compressions and stiffness come from the paper's *reported* relative capacitance changes, not from its raw data.
- Nothing here is a reproduction of the paper's figures or tables; it is a recomputation from the values printed in its text.
