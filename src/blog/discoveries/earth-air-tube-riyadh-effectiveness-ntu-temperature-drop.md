Bury a pipe deep enough and the ground around it stays near the yearly average temperature all summer. Draw outdoor air through it and the air comes out cooler, for the price of a fan. A 2026 paper in *Energies* does exactly that under a Solar Decathlon house in Riyadh — one vitrified clay pipe, 19.8 m long, 0.30 m across, buried 6 m down — and reports an honest, modest result: a mean 4.8 K of cooling, 534 kWh of sensible cooling a year, and 2.4 % off the house's air-conditioning bill. A buried pipe is a heat exchanger with soil on one side, so almost all of that can be rebuilt with the [[heat-exchanger-sizing]] tool. The air-side flow regime comes from [[dimensionless-numbers]], and the house load from [[hvac-room-load]].

## The paper in brief

The house is small: **81 m² of conditioned floor, about 243 m³**. Its ventilation requirement under ASHRAE 62.2 is **20 L/s** of outdoor air, which the authors route through the buried pipe before it reaches the air handler. The soil at 6 m sits between **23.3 and 27.5 °C** through the year, averaging **25.4 °C**, against a Riyadh cooling design dry bulb of **42.3 °C**.

Their reported performance:

- Pipe **effectiveness 0.54 at NTU 0.78** at their tool's design point of 43.5 L/s per pipe, and **0.65 at the actual 20 L/s**, with a simulated median of 0.59.
- Mean air temperature drop **4.8 K** over 4623 cooling hours, maximum **12.6 K**; mean rise **7.8 K** over 360 heating hours.
- **534 kWh/yr** of sensible cooling delivered, **247 kWh/yr** of air-conditioning electricity saved, **68 kWh/yr** of electric heating displaced, **45 kWh/yr** of fan energy, leaving **270 kWh/yr** net — **2.4 %** of the house's HVAC electricity.
- Push the ventilation up to 6.4 air changes per hour and the saving reaches **32 %**, but that is no longer a ventilation system.

The paper's own conclusion is that ground-coupled ventilation does not close the gap to zero carbon on its own. The numbers below say why, and they are all checkable.

## Checked with the tools

A buried pipe is the easiest kind of heat exchanger to analyse: the soil is so much larger than the airstream that its capacity rate is effectively infinite, so the capacity ratio Cr is zero and the effectiveness is simply ε = 1 − e^(−NTU), whatever the arrangement. That is the branch [[heat-exchanger-sizing]] takes when one stream dwarfs the other.

**Step 1: the paper's own pair is consistent.** Feeding NTU = 0.78 with Cr = 0 into the tool's `effectiveness` gives **ε = 0.5416**, against the paper's 0.54. Going the other way, ε = 0.54 needs **NTU = 0.7765**. The paper's 0.65 at 20 L/s needs **NTU = 1.0498**. So the two published effectiveness values are not independent claims: they say the pipe's NTU rises by 35 % when the flow is cut by more than half.

**Step 2: back out the overall heat transfer coefficient, and see where it comes from.** The pipe's surface area is π × 0.30 × 19.8 = **18.66 m²**. At 43.5 L/s the air's capacity rate is 52.6 W/K, so NTU = 0.78 means UA = 41.1 W/K and **U = 2.200 W/m²K**. At 20 L/s the capacity rate is 24.2 W/K and NTU = 1.0498 means **U = 1.361 W/m²K**.

Is that physical? [[dimensionless-numbers]]'s maths puts the air at **0.615 m/s and Re = 12,300** at the design point, and **0.283 m/s and Re = 5650** at 20 L/s — turbulent both times, but only just at the lower flow. Applying the Dittus-Boelter correlation (a textbook formula, not a fibub tool) gives an air-side film of **3.318 W/m²K** at the design point. Subtracting it from the overall 2.200 leaves **0.153 m²K/W** for the clay wall and the soil around it, which does not change with the airflow.

Now predict the low-flow case from that split. The film falls to **1.782 W/m²K**, the fixed 0.153 stays, and the overall U becomes **1.400 W/m²K** — against the **1.361** the paper's own 0.65 implies. Turned back into effectiveness, our model gives **0.660** where the paper gives 0.65. The two chains agree to 1.5 %.

That also explains the shape of the thing. If the air film were the only resistance, U would scale as the flow to the power 0.8. The paper's two points imply a power of **0.618**, because the soil is in series and does not care how fast the air goes.

**Step 3: the energy adds up exactly.** At 20 L/s the air carries **24.20 W/K**. Multiply by the mean 4.8 K and you get **116 W** of cooling; over 4623 hours that is **537 kWh**, against the paper's **534 kWh/yr** — 0.6 % apart, which is our rounded air density. The heating side: 24.20 W/K × 7.8 K × 360 h = **68.0 kWh**, exactly the paper's 68. So the paper's annual energies are internally consistent with a single capacity rate, and anyone can re-derive them in one line.

Running it the other way is more interesting. If the effectiveness really is 0.65, a mean drop of 4.8 K means the **mean outdoor air during cooling hours was 32.8 °C**, and the maximum drop of 12.6 K means a peak of **44.8 °C** — both believable for Riyadh, and a check that the reported drops belong to the reported effectiveness.

**Step 4: why 19.8 m is not enough, and why 40 m would not fix it.** Figure 2 stretches the buried run at 20 L/s. Effectiveness climbs steeply to about 20 m and then flattens, because each extra metre works on a smaller remaining temperature difference. Reaching **ε = 0.80 needs 29.5 m** and **ε = 0.90 needs 42.2 m** — more than twice the pipe for a quarter more of the available drop, and every one of those metres has to be dug 6 m deep.

Rated as the tool rates an exchanger — U·A = 26.1 W/K against 42.3 °C inlet air and 25.4 °C soil — the pipe delivers **270 W** and drops the air by **11.2 K**, to 31.1 °C. On the worst afternoon of the year, a buried pipe hands the air handler air that is still 6 K above the room setpoint. It is a pre-cooler, not a cooling system, which is the paper's point made with its own numbers.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/earth-air-tube-riyadh-effectiveness-ntu-temperature-drop.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `effectiveness`, `ntuFor` and `rate` from `src/lib/sci/eng-hx.ts`, the maths behind [[heat-exchanger-sizing]], and `dimensionless` from `src/lib/sci/phdimless.ts`, the maths behind [[dimensionless-numbers]], for the velocity, Reynolds and Prandtl numbers.

From the paper: the pipe's length, bore and depth, the two airflows, the published NTU and both effectiveness values, the soil temperature, the cooling and heating drops and hours, and the annual energies. **Our assumptions:** air taken at the tool's rounded 20 °C properties (1.204 kg/m³, 1005 J/kgK, 0.0257 W/mK) over the whole flow range rather than at each local temperature; the Dittus-Boelter exponent 0.3 for a cooled gas; only the horizontal 19.8 m counted as heat transfer surface, not the two risers; and the series-resistance split in Step 2, which is a model fitted to the paper's design point, not a value the paper publishes.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/heat-exchanger-sizing.mjs` runs inside `tests/sci.mjs` on every change to fibub:

- The effectiveness-NTU relations for counterflow, parallel flow, shell-and-tube and all three crossflow cases are pinned against an independent Python implementation, including the exact unmixed-crossflow series.
- `ntuFor` must invert `effectiveness` to within 1 part in 10⁹ for every arrangement, and the log-mean temperature difference and the 1-2 shell correction factor F are checked against published worked examples.
- New with this article: `effectiveness('counter', 0.78, 0)` must give 0.5415939886947765 and `ntuFor('counter', 0.65, 0)` must give 1.0498221244986778, the Cr = 0 limit this article rests on; and the rating of a 26.12 W/K earth tube against 42.3 °C air and 25.4 °C soil must give an outlet of 31.14211923595775 °C, an effectiveness of 0.6602296310084169 and a duty of 270.02517764212797 W.

## Check it yourself

On the [[heat-exchanger-sizing]] tool page, to reproduce Step 4's rating:

1. In *Streams*, set *Hot inlet (°C)* to **42.3**, *Hot flow (kg/s)* to **0.02408** (20 L/s of air at 1.204 kg/m³) and *Hot cp (kJ/kg·K)* to **1.005**.
2. Set *Cold inlet (°C)* to **25.4**, *Cold flow (kg/s)* to **10000** and *Cold cp (kJ/kg·K)* to **1**. That huge flow is how you tell the tool "the other side is the ground": it makes the capacity ratio essentially zero.
3. In *Exchanger*, choose *Counterflow (double pipe)* — with one side infinite, every arrangement gives the same answer.
4. Set *Overall U (W/m²·K)* to **1.4** and *Find* to **Outlets for an area (rating)**, then *Area (m²)* to **18.66**.
5. The *Results* card reads **NTU about 1.08**, **effectiveness 0.66**, a duty near **270 W** and a hot outlet of about **31.1 °C** — an 11.2 K drop, Step 4's number.
6. Now change *Area (m²)* to **39.8** (a 42.2 m run) and watch the effectiveness climb only to about 0.90 while the duty rises from 270 to 368 W. Changing *Hot flow (kg/s)* to **0.05236** (43.5 L/s) and *Overall U* to **2.2** returns the paper's design point: NTU 0.78, effectiveness **0.54**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published effectiveness against its NTU** in one step, before building anything on it: with one stream much larger than the other, ε = 1 − e^(−NTU) and nothing else.
- **Back out an overall U** from a reported effectiveness and known geometry, then ask whether a film correlation can supply it, as in Step 2.
- **Separate a flow-dependent film from a fixed resistance**, which tells you whether a system will respond to a bigger fan or only to more surface.
- **Size a buried pipe, a coil or a recovery wheel** for a target effectiveness and see the diminishing returns before quoting a length.
- **Carry the pre-cooled air into the room** with [[hvac-room-load]], where its sensible and latent effect on the coil can be read off.

## The fine print

- The series-resistance split in Step 2 is **our model**, pinned to the paper's own design point. It reproduces the paper's second effectiveness to 1.5 %, which is support, not proof.
- We counted only the 19.8 m horizontal run as heat transfer surface. The two 6.3 m risers pass through shallower, warmer soil and would add some area and some loss, so our U is an upper figure.
- Air properties were taken as constant at the tool's rounded 20 °C values. At 42 °C the density is about 11 % lower, which moves the capacity rate and the annual energy by about the same amount.
- Soil temperature at depth is itself a yearly wave, not a constant. The paper's 23.3 to 27.5 °C range matters: run the Step 4 rating at 27.5 °C and the peak drop falls from 11.2 to about 9.8 K.
- A buried pipe does nothing about moisture, and in humid climates it can condense inside the tube. Riyadh is dry; do not copy the design to a coastal site without checking the dew point.
