Accelerated shelf-life testing rests on one idea: store the food warm, watch it spoil quickly, and scale the answer back to the fridge. The scaling factor is the **Q10**, how many times shorter the shelf life gets for every 10 °C warmer. A 2025 paper in *Foods* worked out processing conditions and shelf-life models for vacuum-packed ready-to-eat crayfish, storing it at 4, 25 and 37 °C. Its numbers make a good lesson in how much a Q10 depends on how finely you sampled: with the [[shelf-life-estimator]] the paper's two warm end points give a Q10 of only 1.27, which predicts a cold shelf life its own 4 °C samples contradict. For heat steps in the same process use the [[thermal-process-calculator]], and for the nutrition panel on the pack use the [[recipe-nutrition-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors cooked crayfish in a spiced brine (their optimum was 0.12 % spice, 0.80 % salt and 70 minutes of stewing, found with a response-surface design), vacuum-packed it, and stored it at three temperatures. They followed five quality measures: **total viable count** (TVC, the living bacteria per gram), **volatile basic nitrogen** (TVB-N, the ammonia-like compounds that build up as protein breaks down), **acid value** (AV, fat going rancid), and the texture measures **hardness** and **springiness**, together with a sensory panel.

Acceptability limits were 6 log CFU/g for TVC, 35 mg/100 g for TVB-N and 5.0 mg KOH/g for AV. The results in the paper's text:

- **4 °C**: nothing reached its limit inside the 10-day study. TVC rose only from 0.57 to 1.7 log CFU/g, and the sensory score stayed at 7.08.
- **25 °C**: shelf life **2 days** by TVC and by the panel, 1.5 days by TVB-N and AV. TVC ended at 6.83 log CFU/g.
- **37 °C**: shelf life **1.5 days** by TVC and the panel, 1 day by TVB-N. TVC ended at 7.8 log CFU/g.

At 25 and 37 °C the samples were taken every **12 hours**, so a shelf life can only come out as a multiple of half a day. The authors then fitted zero- and first-order kinetics to each measure and tied the rate constants together with the **Arrhenius equation**, k = A·exp(−Ea/RT), where Ea is the activation energy: the bigger it is, the more temperature matters.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from values printed in the paper**. Nothing was simulated and nothing was read off a figure.

**Step 1: the Q10 the two warm tests give.** Pasting the two TVC shelf lives, 2 days at 25 °C and 1.5 days at 37 °C, into the tool's *Fit Q10 and activation energy from test points* card gives a **fitted Q10 of 1.27** and an activation energy of **18.4 kJ/mol**. With two points the fit is exact, so there is no R² to warn you. Asked to predict at 4 °C, it answers **3.31 days** by the Q10 model and **3.51 days** by the Arrhenius form (the two differ because one is a straight line in temperature and the other in 1/T).

That is the problem. The paper's own 4 °C samples were still fine at **10 days**, nearly three times longer (Figure 1).

**Step 2: what Q10 would be needed.** Working backwards from 2 days at 25 °C, a shelf life of at least 10 days at 4 °C needs a **Q10 of at least 2.15**, which is an activation energy of about **50.7 kJ/mol** near fridge temperature (Figure 2). A Q10 between 2 and 3 is the usual range for chilled, spoilage-limited foods, so 2.15 is the believable number and 1.27 is not.

**Step 3: the paper's own kinetics agree with the larger value.** The paper's Arrhenius fits to the full curves, not to the end points, give an activation energy of **45.96 kJ/mol** for TVC and **70.74 kJ/mol** for TVB-N. Fed into the tool's *Q10 from Ea* conversion, those become Q10 values of **2.00** and **2.91** at 4 °C. Starting again from 2 days at 25 °C, and using the Q10 each activation energy gives at 15 °C (the middle of the range), they predict about **7.7 days** and **16 days** at 4 °C: the first just short of the observation, the second comfortably past it. So the paper's kinetic models are consistent with its cold-storage result; only the coarse pair of end points is not.

**Step 4: why the end points mislead.** Take the TVC counts in the text at face value and assume a straight log-linear rise. From 0.57 log CFU/g, reaching 6.83 in 2.5 days at 25 °C is **2.50 log per day**; reaching 7.8 in 2.5 days at 37 °C is **2.89 log per day**. The ratio over 12 °C is a Q10 of **1.13**. Spoilage at 37 °C was barely faster than at 25 °C, which is what you expect once a mixed spoilage flora is already near its fastest growth. Those same rates put the 6 log CFU/g limit at **2.17 days** at 25 °C and **1.88 days** at 37 °C, which the 12-hour sampling grid rounds to 2 and 1.5 or 2 days. Half a day of rounding on a two-day shelf life is a 25 % error in the ratio that sets the Q10.

If the 37 °C shelf life had been recorded as 1 day rather than 1.5, the same pair would have given a Q10 of 1.78 and 6.7 days at 4 °C; at 1.25 days, a Q10 of 1.48 and 4.6 days. The answer is dominated by one half-day reading.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ready-to-eat-crayfish-shelf-life-arrhenius-q10-cold-storage.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitShelf` (both models fitted to accelerated test points), `q10From`, `q10Shift` and `q10FromEa` from `src/lib/sci/shelflife.ts`, the maths behind the [[shelf-life-estimator]].

Every input is from the paper's text: the three storage temperatures, the sampling grid, the shelf lives at 25 and 37 °C, the 10-day cold observation, the TVC counts and limit, and two of its fitted activation energies. **Our assumptions** are only two: that TVC rises in a straight line on a log scale between the first and last count (used in step 4), and that a shelf life "of at least 10 days" at 4 °C can be treated as exactly 10 days when we solve for the Q10, which makes 2.15 a lower bound.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/shelf-life-estimator.mjs`, which pins the tool against SciPy:

- Made-up test points of 180, 75 and 32 days at 25, 35 and 45 °C must give a fitted Q10 of 2.3717082 with R² 0.99993716, and predictions of 1008.5057 days at 5 °C and 276.11276 days at 20 °C, as `scipy.stats.linregress` gives.
- The same points must give an activation energy of 68097.458 J/mol with R² 0.99988323, and Arrhenius predictions of 1304.2419 and 289.08022 days.
- Two points must fit exactly: 180 days at 25 °C and 75 at 35 °C give Q10 2.4, R² 1 and 75 days back at 35 °C.
- New with this article: the paper's pair (2 days at 25 °C, 1.5 days at 37 °C) must give Q10 1.27091239, Ea 18431.9947 J/mol, 3.30881728 days at 4 °C by the Q10 model and 3.51322839 days by the Arrhenius model; and the Q10 needed for 10 days at 4 °C must be 2.15200293.

## Check it yourself

On the [[shelf-life-estimator|Shelf life tool]]:

1. In *Fit Q10 and activation energy from test points*, clear the box and type the paper's two warm results, one per line: **25, 2** and **37, 1.5**.
2. Leave *Time unit* on **days** and set *Predict at (°C)* to **4**.
3. It reads **At 4 °C: 3.31 days (Q10 model), 3.51 days (Arrhenius)**, with *Fitted Q10* **1.27**, *Activation energy Ea* **18.4 kJ/mol**, *Q10 from Ea at 4 °C* **1.32** and *Test points* **2**. Both R² values are **1.0000**, because two points always lie on a line.
4. Now use the first card, *Shelf life at another temperature*. Put **2** in *Known shelf life*, **25** in *At temperature (°C)*, **4** in *New temperature (°C)* and **2** in *Q10*: it reads about **8.57 days**.
5. Raise *Q10* to **2.15** and it reads about **9.98 days**, just short of the paper's 10-day observation. That is the lower bound on the real Q10 for this product.
6. Finally set *Q10* to **2.91**, the value the paper's own TVB-N activation energy gives at 4 °C: the shelf life reads about **18.8 days**. The arithmetic never changes; the Q10 you feed it decides everything.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check an accelerated test** before trusting it: fit the Q10, then ask whether it predicts the chilled shelf life you already know.
- **Design the sampling grid**: work out how much the fitted Q10 moves if a warm end point shifts by one sampling interval, and sample more often if that changes the answer.
- **Convert between Q10 and Ea** at the temperature you care about, so a paper's activation energy and your own Q10 can be compared.
- **Set a storage-temperature specification**: with a Q10 you trust, see what two degrees of cold-chain abuse cost in days.
- **Teach the difference between the two models**: the Q10 line and the Arrhenius line agree where you measured and drift apart outside that range, as step 1 shows.

## The fine print

- A shelf life "not reached in 10 days" is a lower bound. The true 4 °C shelf life may be far longer, which would push the required Q10 above 2.15.
- The paper's activation energies come from its Arrhenius table, and we quote two of them as facts. We did not refit its kinetic curves; the counts behind them are not in its text.
- Step 4 assumes TVC rises in a straight line on a log scale. Real growth has a lag and a plateau, so those daily rates are averages, not maximum growth rates.
- Different indicators give different shelf lives, and in this study TVB-N and acid value always ran ahead of the microbial count. Which one you call "the" shelf life changes the Q10.
- Shelf life belongs to one product in one pack. These numbers are for checking the paper's arithmetic, not for setting a date on anything.
