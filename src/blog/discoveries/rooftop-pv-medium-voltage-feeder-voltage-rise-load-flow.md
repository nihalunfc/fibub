When a solar plant is added at the far end of a distribution feeder, two things happen at once: the voltage there goes up, and the losses in the line go down. A 2025 case study in *Solar* measured both for a **230 kWp** plant at Palestine Polytechnic University in Hebron, connected 2.9 km from the substation at the end of an **11 kV** feeder. The voltage at the point of common coupling rose by **0.51 %**, the substation busbar barely moved (0.03 %), and real losses at solar noon fell by about **half**. This article rebuilds that feeder in the [[load-flow|load flow tool]] and asks what those measurements imply about the cable and about how much load the feeder was actually carrying at noon. To size the plant that goes on the end of such a feeder, start with the [[solar-pv-yield|solar PV yield estimator]] and the [[home-battery-sizing|home battery calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The campus takes power through an 11/0.4 kV, 1000 kVA transformer at the end of an 11 kV feeder, which is fed from a 33/11 kV substation with two 10 MVA transformers. The PV plant is 230 kWp through eleven 21 kW inverters and makes about **200 kW at solar noon**; the authors put its penetration at **14.7 %** of the feeder. They modelled the network in ETAP and compared two cases, with and without the plant, using measured daily load profiles.

Their results: the worst-case voltage drop along the feeder is about 2.2 %, and every load bus stays above 0.95 per unit either way. Adding the PV lifts the main 11 kV busbar by 0.03 % and the point of common coupling by **0.51 %**, well inside the IEEE 1547 limits. Real power losses at peak generation fall by about **50 %** and reactive losses by **23 %**. The one unwelcome result is the **power factor**: because the inverters run at unity power factor, they cut the real power the feeder carries but not the reactive power, so the measured power factor on the campus low-voltage side falls to **0.53** at peak generation, against 0.87 at the main bus. The authors recommend reactive support from the inverters.

## Checked with the tools

The paper does not publish cable impedances, so we **cannot** reproduce its model line by line. Instead we built the smallest feeder that carries its stated facts and let the [[load-flow|load flow tool]] do the rest: a three-bus radial feeder of two 1.45 km sections, with the substation busbar as the slack bus, the rest of the feeder's load halfway out, and the campus (plus its PV) at the end, 2.9 km away. **Our assumptions:** an 11 kV system on a 1 MVA base (so 121 Ω per unit), a feeder peak load of 1.565 MW (the 230 kWp plant being 14.7 % of it), 0.40 MW of that on the campus, every load at power factor 0.87, no line charging, and a cable reactance 0.4 times its resistance, as for a medium-voltage aluminium cable.

**Step 1: calibrate the line, then stop fitting.** We tuned one number only, the cable resistance, until the no-PV voltage at the end of the feeder was 0.978 per unit, which is the paper's 2.2 % worst-case drop. That gives **0.747 Ω/km** of resistance and 0.299 Ω/km of reactance, so each 1.45 km section is R = 0.008951 and X = 0.003580 per unit. Everything after this is the tool's answer, not a fit.

**Step 2: the voltage rise.** With the feeder at its peak load, Newton-Raphson converges in 3 iterations and gives 1.00000, 0.98249 and 0.97800 per unit along the feeder. Switch on 200 kW at the end and the three become 1.00000, 0.98435 and 0.98170: a rise of **0.37 % at the point of common coupling** and 0.19 % at the middle of the feeder. The paper measured 0.51 % at the coupling point and 0.03 % at the substation busbar. Our substation bus is the slack bus, so it cannot move at all, which is the right idealisation of a 20 MVA substation: the paper's 0.03 % is the real thing almost not moving.

**Step 3: a voltage rise barely cares about the load.** Run the same injection at every load level and the rise at the coupling point stays between 0.359 % and 0.370 %. That is worth remembering when planning a connection: the rise is set by the injected power and the impedance back to the source, not by what the feeder is carrying. It also means the paper's 0.51 % implies a slightly weaker connection than its 2.2 % drop does. Asking the tool to produce 0.51 % needs **1.016 Ω/km**, 36 % more resistance, which would make the no-PV drop at the end **3.0 %** instead of 2.2 %. Those two statements in the paper are about 0.8 percentage points of voltage apart; the likeliest cause is the campus transformer and its low-voltage cabling, which sit between the feeder and the measuring point and which our model leaves out.

**Step 4: the halved losses say how busy the feeder was.** Losses go as the square of the current, so the percentage a fixed 200 kW saves depends strongly on the load. At the feeder's peak load the tool makes the saving only **20.9 %** (32.04 kW down to 25.34 kW). To reach the paper's 50 %, the feeder has to be carrying about **0.48 MW**, less than a third of its peak, where the 200 kW of PV is covering 42 % of the load (2.91 kW of losses down to 1.45 kW). Figure 2 shows the whole curve.

That inference is checkable against a completely different number in the paper. If the campus draws P kW with reactive demand P × tan(φ) at power factor 0.87, and 200 kW of unity-power-factor PV is subtracted from P only, the measured power factor becomes 0.53 when **P is about 310 kW**. So the power factor and the loss reduction tell the same story: at solar noon the feeder was lightly loaded, and the campus was taking roughly 0.3 MW of the 0.48 MW on it. The 14.7 % "penetration" is a ratio of nameplate to peak load; at the moment of measurement the plant was supplying something closer to 40 % of the power on the feeder.

One number we could not reproduce: the paper's **23 % reactive-loss** reduction beside its 49 to 50 % real-loss reduction. In a passive radial feeder both losses are I²R and I²X, so they must fall by the same percentage; our model gives 50 % and 50 %. A split like 50 % and 23 % needs something our two cables do not have, such as transformer magnetising reactance or cable charging, which stay put while the through-current falls.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/rooftop-pv-medium-voltage-feeder-voltage-rise-load-flow.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `loadFlow` from `src/lib/sci/eng-loadflow.ts`, the maths behind the [[load-flow|load flow tool]]: the bus admittance matrix, the polar Newton-Raphson solution with the full Jacobian, and the line flows and losses it reports. The cable resistance, the load level that halves the losses and the resistance that gives a 0.51 % rise are each found by bisection on repeated solutions of the same power flow.

From the paper: 230 kWp, 200 kW at solar noon, 14.7 % penetration, the 2.9 km distance, the 2.2 % drop, the 0.51 % and 0.03 % rises, the 50 % and 23 % loss reductions and the 0.87 and 0.53 power factors. **Our assumptions:** the three-bus layout, the 1.565 MW feeder peak and the 0.40 MW campus share, power factor 0.87 at every load, no line charging, a reactance-to-resistance ratio of 0.4, and 11 kV on a 1 MVA base. This is **not** the paper's network model; it is the simplest network consistent with the facts it publishes.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/load-flow.mjs` on every change to fibub, and it pins the solver against an independent NumPy Newton-Raphson written for the purpose (complex admittance matrix, MATPOWER-style Jacobian, solved to 1e-12):

- On the tool's own 5-bus example, both Newton-Raphson and Gauss-Seidel must give the same bus voltages to 1e-7 per unit and the same angles to 1e-5 degrees, with the slack bus supplying 114.5617 MW and 35.7199 Mvar and total losses of 4.56169 MW.
- Every line flow must match at both ends to 1e-4 MW, and the losses must equal generation minus load to 1e-6.
- Two random networks with parallel lines must match the same reference, and a two-bus lossless line carrying 50 MW on X = 0.1 pu must give exactly δ = −arcsin(0.05) and zero losses.
- A network with no slack bus, two slack buses or an island is refused with a plain message rather than a wrong answer.

## Check it yourself

On the [[load-flow|load flow tool]]:

1. Paste this into *Buses*: **bus, type, Pd_MW, Qd_Mvar, Pg_MW, V_pu** · **SUB, slack, 0, 0, 0, 1** · **MID, PQ, 1.165, 0.66, 0, 1** · **PCC, PQ, 0.4, 0.2267, 0, 1**
2. Paste this into *Lines*: **from, to, R_pu, X_pu, B_pu** · **SUB, MID, 0.008951, 0.00358, 0** · **MID, PCC, 0.008951, 0.00358, 0**
3. Set *Base power (MVA)* to **1** and leave *Method* on **Newton-Raphson**.
4. The headline reads **Converged in 3 iterations (Newton-Raphson); losses 0.032 MW**, the slack bus supplies **1.60 MW**, and the bus table shows **0.9825 pu** at MID and **0.9780 pu** at PCC: the paper's 2.2 % drop.
5. Now give the PV its 200 kW: change the PCC row's generation to **PCC, PQ, 0.4, 0.2267, 0.2, 1**. PCC rises to **0.9817 pu** (+0.37 %), MID to **0.9843 pu**, and the losses fall to **0.025 MW**, a 21 % cut at peak load.
6. Scale both loads to about a third (MID **0.355, 0.201**; PCC **0.122, 0.069**) and solve again with and without the 0.2 MW. The headline losses now fall from **0.003 MW to 0.001 MW** (2.91 kW to 1.45 kW): the 50 % the paper measured at noon.
7. Put the generation up to **0.23** (the nameplate) and watch PCC reach **0.9975 pu** on the light feeder: still far inside the 1.05 pu limit, which is why this plant caused no trouble.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published voltage rise** before trusting it: a rise and a feeder drop together fix the impedance, and the two should agree, as the 0.37 % against 0.51 % here shows.
- **Separate penetration from coincidence.** Nameplate over peak load (14.7 % here) is not the share of power the plant supplies at the moment of measurement (about 40 %). The tool turns one into the other.
- **Plan a connection study** with nothing installed: calibrate a two- or three-bus model to one measured voltage drop, then test injections, as in step 5 above.
- **Check loss claims for consistency**: real and reactive losses in a passive feeder must fall by the same percentage, so a quoted pair like 50 % and 23 % points to equipment outside the cable model.
- **Size what sits behind the inverter** with the [[solar-pv-yield|solar PV yield estimator]], and look at storing midday surplus with the [[home-battery-sizing|home battery calculator]].

## The fine print

- Our three-bus feeder is **not** the paper's network. It is calibrated to one published number (the 2.2 % drop) and tested against the others.
- The 1.565 MW feeder peak comes from reading "14.7 % penetration" as nameplate power over feeder peak load. If the authors meant energy, the peak load would be different and the loss percentages would move.
- The campus transformer, its low-voltage cabling and the substation transformers are all left out. They are the most likely reason our voltage rise is smaller than the measured one.
- The paper's conclusion gives 0.45 % for the rise where its results section gives 0.51 %, and 49 % for the loss cut where the results say about 50 %. We used the results-section values.
- Power factor 0.87 at every load is our assumption, taken from the paper's main-bus figure. A different value changes the reactive flows and so the loss numbers a little.
- Nothing here is a connection study. A real one needs the utility's own impedances, fault levels and protection settings.
