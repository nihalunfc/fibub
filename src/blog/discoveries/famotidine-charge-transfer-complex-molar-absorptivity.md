Famotidine is a common stomach-acid tablet, and it has no strong colour of its own. A 2024 paper in *Scientific Reports* gets around that in the classic way: mix the drug with an electron-poor reagent, *ortho*-chloranil, let the two form a **charge-transfer complex** (a loosely bound pair where electron density shifts from one molecule to the other, which makes it absorb visible light), and read the colour at 546 nm. The paper reports a molar absorptivity, a Sandell sensitivity, a linear range and detection limits. Every one of those can be checked in a browser with the [[beer-lambert-calculator]], and that is what this article does. For the steps on either side, the [[calibration-curve]] fits the standards and gives the detection limit from them, and [[method-validation]] turns the recovery and repeatability numbers into the usual validation statistics.

## The paper in brief

The authors add *ortho*-chloranil to famotidine in an alkaline aqueous medium, wait for the colour to form and measure it against a water blank at 546 nm. They report that Beer's law holds from **2 to 28 µg/mL** with R² = 0.9977, a **molar absorptivity (ε) of 2,159.648 L/(mol·cm)**, a **Sandell sensitivity of 0.15625 µg/cm²**, a limit of detection of **0.3024 µg/mL** and a limit of quantification of **1.471 µg/mL**. Job's method and the molar-ratio method both point to a 2:1 reagent-to-drug complex, and recovery from tablets is about 99.9 %. The method needs no organic solvent in the measurement step, which is its selling point: it is a cheap, green assay for a cheap drug.

Two of those numbers are independent of the instrument, so anyone can test them: ε and the Sandell sensitivity. The rest say where the method is comfortable, and that is where a Beer-Lambert check earns its keep.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: do ε and the Sandell sensitivity agree?** The Sandell sensitivity is the mass of analyte per square centimetre of light path that gives an absorbance of 0.001, and for a Beer-Lambert absorber it is simply the molar mass divided by ε. Famotidine is C₈H₁₅N₇O₂S₃, which the [[molar-mass-calculator]] puts at **337.435 g/mol**. The paper's two numbers multiply to 0.15625 × 2,159.648 = **337.445 g/mol**. The pair is self-consistent to 0.003 %, so the two sensitivity figures were worked out from the same calibration and the same molar mass. That is a check worth running on any spectrophotometric paper: when ε and the Sandell value do not multiply to the analyte's molar mass, one of them is wrong.

**Step 2: where does the published range sit on the absorbance scale?** With ε = 2,159.648 L/(mol·cm) and the usual 1 cm cuvette, the calibration line has a slope of **0.0064 absorbance units per µg/mL**. So the paper's range of 2 to 28 µg/mL is a range of absorbance from **0.0128 to 0.1792**. The whole calibration lives in the bottom tenth of the scale. The tool's own reading-quality note calls everything under A = 0.05 "very low: noise and the blank matter a lot", which covers every standard below **7.8 µg/mL**, more than a fifth of the stated range.

Figure 1 shows the line, the strip the paper calibrated over, and the 0.1 to 1 absorbance window that an ordinary spectrophotometer measures most accurately. A 5 cm cell, the cheapest change possible, lifts the same concentrations to A = 0.064 to 0.896, almost exactly into that window.

**Step 3: what the low absorbance costs.** Suppose the instrument reads absorbance to within ±0.002 A, which is ordinary for a routine single-beam spectrophotometer (**our assumption**; the paper does not state its photometric noise). That error carries straight into the result as 0.002 ÷ A. At the bottom of the published range it is **15.6 %**; at the top, **1.1 %**. In a 5 cm cell the worst case drops to **3.1 %** (Figure 2). The paper's own repeatability is far better than 15 %, which tells you its instrument is better than our assumed 0.002 A, or that the low standards were not the ones repeated.

**Step 4: the detection limit, read as an absorbance.** A limit of detection of 0.3024 µg/mL means a signal of **0.0019 A** in a 1 cm cell, about two thousandths of full scale. Working backwards through the usual 3.3σ/slope rule, that limit needs the blank's standard deviation to be **0.00059 A**. If the blank instead wobbles by 0.002 A, the same rule gives a detection limit of **1.03 µg/mL**, three times higher. Note too that the paper's quantification limit is 4.86 times its detection limit, while the conventional 10σ and 3.3σ definitions give a ratio of 3.03 — so the two limits come from different rules, and the paper says the detection limit came from the absorbance of its lowest standard rather than from blank statistics.

| Blank noise implied by the stated LOD | LOD 0.3024 µg/mL | σ = 0.00059 A |
| LOQ ÷ LOD | 4.86 | 3.03 for the 10σ/3.3σ convention |

None of this says the assay is wrong. It says the assay is running on a tenth of the dynamic range it could use, and that a longer cell (or a more concentrated sample) would buy accuracy for nothing.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in them come from `scripts/blog-figures/famotidine-charge-transfer-complex-molar-absorptivity.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `solveBL` and `absQuality` from `src/lib/sci/beer.ts`, the maths behind the [[beer-lambert-calculator]], and `parseFormula` with `molarMass` from `src/lib/sci/chem.ts`, which powers the [[molar-mass-calculator]].

`solveBL` fills in whichever one of A, ε, path length and concentration is left empty, so the script uses it in both directions: forwards to turn each concentration into an absorbance, and backwards to find the concentration that gives A = 0.1 or A = 1. The inputs taken from the paper are ε, the Sandell sensitivity, the 2 to 28 µg/mL range, the detection and quantification limits and the 546 nm wavelength. The 1 cm and 5 cm path lengths, and the ±0.002 A reading error, are **our assumptions**. Nothing here is a fit to the paper's measurements; the paper's calibration points are only in its figures.

## How we test the maths

Beer's law is one multiplication, so the test is that the tool never slips a unit:

- `tests/sci-tools/beer-lambert-calculator.mjs` pins the textbook NADH reading: ε = 6,220 L/(mol·cm) at 340 nm, 0.1 mmol/L in a 1 cm cell must give A = 0.622, and 10 % transmittance must come back as A = 1 exactly.
- It checks the four ways round: given any three of A, ε, l and c, the fourth must come back, and asking for two at once must raise an error rather than guess.
- New with this article: famotidine's molar mass from C₈H₁₅N₇O₂S₃ must be 337.435 g/mol, the Sandell sensitivity 0.15625 µg/cm² times ε 2,159.648 must come back to that molar mass within 0.05 %, 2 µg/mL must give A = 0.01280 (flagged `warn`) and 28 µg/mL A = 0.17921 (flagged `good`), and the concentration that gives A = 1 in a 1 cm cell must be 156.2 µg/mL.

Those checks run with `node tests/sci.mjs` on every change to the site, together with every other science tool's checks.

## Check it yourself

On the [[beer-lambert-calculator]], in the *A = ε l c* card:

1. Leave the first box on **Absorbance** and clear it, so the tool solves for absorbance.
2. Put **2159.648** in *ε, molar absorptivity (L/(mol·cm))* and **1** in *Path length (cm)*.
3. In *Concentration* type **5.9271** and set the unit to **µmol/L** (that is 2 µg/mL of famotidine). Type **337.435** in *Molar mass (g/mol, optional)*.
4. The big line reads **Absorbance: 0.013**, and under it *Absorbance* **0.0128** with the note "very low: noise and the blank matter a lot", *Transmittance* **97.1 %**, and *Mass concentration* **2.0 mg/L**.
5. Change *Path length (cm)* to **5**: the absorbance becomes **0.0640**, still low. Now set *Concentration* to **82.979 µmol/L** (28 µg/mL) and the path back to **1**: you get **0.1792**, "in the most accurate range".
6. To find the concentration that fills the scale, clear *Concentration*, put **1** in the absorbance box and leave ε and the path length: the tool answers **463.0 µmol/L**, and the mass concentration line shows **156.2 mg/L**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published spectrophotometric method in a minute**: multiply the Sandell sensitivity by ε and see whether you get the analyte's molar mass.
- **Choose the cell before buying the instrument**: try 1, 5 and 10 cm path lengths against your expected concentrations and pick the one that lands in A = 0.1 to 1.
- **Set a sensible calibration range**: read off the concentrations for A = 0.1 and A = 1 at your ε, then dilute your standards to sit inside them.
- **Judge a detection limit**: turn it into an absorbance and ask whether your instrument can really see that signal above its blank; fit the standards properly with the [[calibration-curve]], which gives the limit from the residual scatter.
- **Convert units safely**: enter the molar mass once and the tool reports mol/L, mmol/L, µmol/L and mg/L together, which is where most assay arithmetic goes wrong.

## The fine print

- We used only the values printed in the paper's text. Its calibration points, slope and intercept are in a figure and a table we did not reuse.
- The 1 cm path length is the standard cuvette, but the paper does not state its cell, so every absorbance here is "per the path length shown".
- The ±0.002 A reading error is our assumption for an ordinary instrument, not a measurement of theirs.
- Charge-transfer complexes drift as they age and with temperature. A Beer-Lambert check tells you nothing about that; it only tests the arithmetic of the numbers reported.
- The paper's own text gives recovery as 99.93 % in one place and 99.56 % in another. We report both rather than pick one.
