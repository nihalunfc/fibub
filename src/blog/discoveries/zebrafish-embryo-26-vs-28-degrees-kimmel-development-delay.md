Almost every zebrafish timetable is written for one temperature, 28.5 °C, and converted to others with a single line of arithmetic from the 1995 Kimmel staging paper: development runs at a rate of 0.055 T − 0.57 relative to 28.5 °C. Rooms, incubators and ethics rules all depend on that line. A 2025 PLOS One paper timed early development at 26 °C and 28 °C closely enough to test it, and found the formula optimistic for late embryos. This article puts the same formula, as the [[embryo-development-timer]] uses it, against each delay the paper measured. For the same idea in other species see [[insect-degree-days]], and for the error bars on a converted time, [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors raised zebrafish embryos at 26 °C and at 28 °C, two temperatures that are both legal and common, and watched them. One series of 47 to 48 embryos per group was imaged hourly to about 40 hours post fertilisation (hpf) and every two hours to 119 hpf, scoring Kimmel's stages, heartbeat onset and hatching. A second series was fixed at 72, 96 and 119 hpf and measured for body length, eye area and yolk sac area.

Warmer embryos were ahead at every landmark. The heartbeat started at a median 25 hpf at 28 °C, 4 hours earlier than at 26 °C. Hatching came at a median 60 hpf against 68 hpf. By the end of incubation at 119 hpf the gap had grown: 18.6 hours by body length, 19.8 hours by yolk sac consumption, 13 hours by eye size.

The point is not academic. German animal-welfare law treats a zebrafish embryo as an independent, protected animal from 120 hpf, and an earlier extrapolation with the Kimmel formula put the equivalent moment at 26 °C at 139.5 hpf. The authors argue that a straight-line rate law should not be stretched that far, show that Kimmel's own digitised data bend away from it at the extremes (predicted rate 0.80 against an observed slope of 0.77 ± 0.02 at 25 °C; 1.24 against 1.11 ± 0.02 at 33 °C), and recommend extending the limit to at least 125 hpf for embryos kept at 26 °C.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the formula** with the paper's own stated times. We did not refit the paper's data; its measurements live in its figures.

**Step 1: the rate factor itself.** The tool's zebrafish model is Kimmel's factor, f(T) = 0.055 T − 0.57, the hours of 28.5 °C development that pass per hour at T. It gives 0.860 at 26 °C and 0.970 at 28 °C, so 26 °C embryos should run at 88.7 % of the 28 °C pace. The same line hits 0.805 at 25 °C and 1.245 at 33 °C, against the paper's re-analysed slopes of 0.77 and 1.11: the formula is 5 % fast at the cool end and 12 % fast at the warm end (Figure 1). Extended down, it reaches zero at 10.36 °C, which the tool reports as the base temperature of the equivalent degree-hours model.

**Step 2: where the formula works.** Feeding the paper's 28 °C medians into the tool's *Convert a time* card gives the 26 °C times, and the delay is the difference:

| Landmark | Time at 28 °C | Tool's 26 °C time | Tool's delay | Paper's measured delay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heartbeat onset | 25 hpf | 28.2 h | 3.2 h | 4 h (CI 3.5 to 5) |
| About 1.5 dpf | 36 hpf | 40.6 h | 4.6 h | at least 3.25 h (CI 3.25 to 5) |
| Hatching | 60 hpf | 67.7 h | 7.7 h | 8 h (CI 2.25 to 9) |
| End of incubation | 119 hpf | 134.2 h | 15.2 h | 18.6 h, 19.8 h, 13 h by three measures |

To 3 days the formula is excellent: hatching is predicted 7.7 hours late against a measured 8, and the 1.5 dpf delay sits inside the paper's confidence interval. After hatching it drifts. At 119 hpf it predicts 15.2 hours where body length gives 18.6 (CI 15.9 to 21.3) and yolk sac 19.8 (CI 13.4 to 26.3): the prediction is just below the body-length interval and inside the much wider yolk interval. Figure 2 shows the predicted delay growing in proportion to age, with the paper's four points on top.

**Step 3: the 139.5 hpf figure comes straight out of the formula.** Type 120 hours at 28.5 °C into the tool and ask for 26 °C: it answers **139.53 h**. That is the published extrapolation, reproduced to the hour, so the disagreement between the two papers is not arithmetic but the reach of a straight line. Turn the question round and the stakes are clearer: an embryo that has spent 120 hours at 26 °C has had only 120 × 0.860 = **103.2 hours** of 28.5 °C development, and at the authors' proposed 125 hours it has had **107.5**. Either limit releases a 26 °C embryo earlier in its development than a 28.5 °C embryo at 120 hpf; 125 hpf is a cautious step, not a fix.

**Step 4: a mixed-temperature history.** Labs rarely hold one temperature. The tool's *Temperature history* card adds up equivalent 28.5 °C hours step by step, so 48 hours at 26 °C followed by 48 at 28 °C comes to 48 × 0.860 + 48 × 0.970 = **87.8 equivalent hours** after 96 real ones: a 8.2-hour lag, and the same answer whichever order the steps come in. That additivity is what makes the law's fixed hour count awkward, and it is worth logging the temperature as carefully as the clock.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number here come from `scripts/blog-figures/zebrafish-embryo-26-vs-28-degrees-kimmel-development-delay.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `kimmelFactor`, `convert`, `rate` and `accumulate` from `src/lib/sci/embryo.ts`, the maths behind the [[embryo-development-timer]], and `KIMMEL_BASE` for the base temperature.

The inputs are the paper's stated 28 °C medians (heartbeat 25 hpf, hatching 60 hpf, the 1.5 dpf landmark at about 36 hpf, the end of incubation at 119 hpf), its measured delays and confidence intervals, and its re-analysed rate slopes at 25 and 33 °C. The 120 hpf and 125 hpf limits come from the paper's discussion of German law. Nothing is simulated and no data table or figure is copied; reading the 1.5 dpf landmark as 36 hpf is **our assumption**, since the paper gives the stage, not the hour.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/embryo-development-timer.mjs`, which pins the formula by hand arithmetic:

- The factor at 28.5 °C must be 0.9975, exactly as the published formula gives (it is not 1, and the tool handles 28.5 °C as the reference anyway).
- 24 hours at 28.5 °C must take 24 ÷ 0.805 = 29.8137 hours at 25 °C, and 22.2222 hours at 30 °C.
- Converting 10 hours at 25 °C to 31 °C must give 10 × 0.805 ÷ 1.135 = 7.0925 hours, and every conversion must round-trip.
- Kimmel's line must equal a degree-hours model with a base of 10.3636 °C, and a stage must carry the same degree-hours at every temperature.
- New with this article: 120 hours at 28.5 °C must come out as 139.53 hours at 26 °C, the published extrapolation; 60 hours at 28 °C must give 67.67 at 26 °C; and 48 hours at 26 °C plus 48 at 28 °C must accumulate 87.84 equivalent hours.

## Check it yourself

On the [[embryo-development-timer]]:

1. Leave *Species and model* on **Zebrafish: Kimmel et al. (1995)**.
2. In *Convert a time*, type **120** in *Time (hours)*, **28.5** in *At (°C)* and **26** in *Same stage at (°C)*. It reads **139.5 h** (139.53), the figure the welfare debate turns on.
3. Change *Time (hours)* to **60** and *At (°C)* to **28**: now **67.7 h**, a 7.7-hour delay against the paper's measured 8.
4. Try *Time (hours)* **119**, *At (°C)* **28**: **134.2 h**, so 15.2 hours late where the paper measures 18.6 to 19.8.
5. In *Plan the stages*, set *Incubation temperature (°C)* to **26** and replace the example list with your own staging times, one `name, hours` per line, to get the clock times for a 26 °C run.
6. In *Temperature history*, type `48, 26` on one line and `48, 28` on the next: it totals **87.8** equivalent hours at 28.5 °C for 96 hours of real time.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a sampling schedule at your real room temperature**, so a "24 hpf" sample is taken at the stage you mean rather than the hour someone else meant.
- **Compare experiments run at different temperatures** by converting both to equivalent 28.5 °C hours before you compare stages, sizes or toxicity endpoints.
- **Check a welfare or regulatory cut-off** for the temperature actually used, and say which model and base temperature you used when you report it.
- **Audit a temperature excursion**: put the real history into the tool's history card and see how many equivalent hours were lost or gained.
- **Use the same arithmetic for other species** with [[insect-degree-days]] or the tool's degree-hours model and your own base temperature, then put an uncertainty on the answer with [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The fine print

- Kimmel's formula is stated for roughly 25 to 33 °C; the tool shows that range and will refuse temperatures at or below 10.36 °C, where the line reaches zero.
- The paper's own staging ran about 15 % slower than Kimmel's, so absolute hours from any staging table are a guide, not a clock.
- A single rate factor assumes every process in the embryo slows by the same amount. The paper's divergence after hatching, and the different delays for body length, eye and yolk, say plainly that it does not.
- The delays in our table are differences of medians, computed from the paper's stated medians; the paper's own delays come from its statistical comparison of the whole distributions, which is why they do not match to the hour.
- Nothing here is advice on what any law requires. Check the rules that apply to your facility.
