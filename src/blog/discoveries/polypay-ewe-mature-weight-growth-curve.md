"Mature weight" decides how much a ewe eats, how big a lamb she can raise and when it is worth keeping her. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Animal Science* followed 2,941 Polypay ewes at the U.S. Sheep Experiment Station over twenty-one birth years and reported their weights at four points in the production cycle for every age from one to eight, but it fitted no growth curve: the finding is stated as "body weight generally increased until 5 years of age". That is a sentence a fibub tool can turn into numbers. Using the [[growth-curve-tracker]]'s own maths we fit the three usual growth curves to the paper's eight mid-lactation means and get a mature weight of **76.4 ± 1.2 kg**, with 95 % of it reached before age four. For the jobs on either side, plan matings and lambing dates with the [[breeding-calendar]] and test a flock-level time series with the [[trend-analyser]].

## The paper in brief

The flock is a Polypay nucleus near Dubois, Idaho, with records from ewes born between 1995 and 2016. The paper compares ewes that first lambed as yearlings (2,399 of them) with those that waited until two (542), and follows lifetime output: yearling-lambing ewes produced more lambs over a lifetime (7.31 against 5.17 born), were more likely to still be in the flock at seven (0.14 against 0.09) and contributed about 4.2 times more genetic influence to the current generation. The flock's mean ewe age is 3.02 years with a 30.9 % annual replacement rate.

Body weight is a secondary result. The authors give least-squares means, adjusted for year and litter size, at mid-lactation, weaning, post-breeding and mid-gestation for each ewe age, and note that weight rose to about five years and then became variable. The mid-lactation series runs 57.1, 64.9, 70.9, 73.2, 74.3, 74.2, 74.1 and 78.6 kg for ages one to eight, with standard errors of 0.3 kg up to age six, 0.4 at seven and 1.0 at eight, where few ewes remain.

## Checked with the tools

We gave the tool the eight mid-lactation means as one "animal" weighed once a year (ages in days, as the tool expects), and asked for all three curves. This is a **fit to the paper's published means**, not to individual ewes, and not a simulation.

**1. All three curves agree on the mature weight and on nothing else.** The Gompertz curve gives A = **76.43 ± 1.16 kg**, the logistic 76.26 ± 1.09 and the von Bertalanffy 76.50 ± 1.19, with R² = 0.964 and AICs of 10.88, 11.02 and 10.84. Those AICs are within 0.2 of each other, which means the data cannot tell the three models apart: anyone who reports "the Gompertz fitted best" on a record like this is reading noise. The agreement on A is the useful part, because A is set by the plateau the data do show.

**2. 95 % of mature weight comes before age four.** The fitted curves cross 95 % of A at **3.85 years** (Gompertz), 3.74 (logistic) and 3.89 (von Bertalanffy), and 99 % at 6.2 to 6.6 years. The paper's "increased until 5 years" is consistent with this, and the tool's own gain table says why the eye stops there: the year-on-year gains are **7.8 kg** from age one to two, 6.0 kg to three, 2.3 kg to four, **1.1 kg** to five and then −0.1 kg in each of the next two years. Growth does not stop at a birthday; it fades, and by the fourth year it is already under 2 kg a year, or about 6 g a day.

**3. The curves are honest about what the record cannot show.** Every fitted inflection point lands **before** age one (−1.0 years for the Gompertz, −0.6 for the logistic), because the record starts after the fast phase of growth. Run the same curves back to age zero and they predict a newborn of **44 to 46 kg**. No lamb weighs that. This is the standard trap in fitting sigmoid growth models to adult data: A and the late shape are well determined, b and k are not, and the early part of the curve is pure extrapolation. The tool reports a standard error on A so you can see which is which.

**4. How much of a lifetime do you need to measure?** Refitting with the record cut short gives A = 77.5 ± 2.6 kg from ages one to four, 76.2 ± 1.0 to five, 75.3 ± 0.7 to six, 74.9 ± 0.5 to seven and 76.4 ± 1.2 with all eight. Two readings matter here: by age five the mature weight is already settled within one standard error of the final answer, so a five-year record is enough; and the age-eight mean, the one the paper itself flags with a standard error three times the others, **raises** the fitted mature weight by 1.5 kg and drops R² from 0.993 to 0.965 on its own. With survivor groups this small, the oldest point should be shown but treated gently.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/polypay-ewe-mature-weight-growth-curve.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitGrowth`, `MODELS` and `gains` from `src/lib/sci/growth.ts`, the maths behind the [[growth-curve-tracker]]: Levenberg-Marquardt least squares with several starting points, the same code the tool page runs.

From the paper: the eight mid-lactation least-squares means, their standard errors, the ewe ages and the flock description. **Our choices**: using ages in whole years converted to days (365 per year), fitting the mid-lactation series rather than one of the other three, and reading 95 % and 99 % of A by root-finding on the fitted curve. The error bars in Figure 1 are the paper's standard errors, not a fit uncertainty.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/growth-curve-tracker.mjs` on every change to fibub:

- On the tool's example calf records, every fit must match `scipy.optimize.curve_fit` to within 2 parts per million on A and 1 part in 10,000 on k: Gompertz A 514.8776 with a standard error of 12.679 and sum of squares 279.606, logistic A 463.1053, von Bertalanffy A 554.6843, and the same for the pooled records.
- Data generated from a known curve must come back exactly: all three models recover their own A, b and k to 1 part in a million with R² above 0.999999.
- The inflection formulas are checked in closed form, a fit with fewer than four weighings must be refused, the per-interval gains must be exact (41 kg in 45 days) and the body-condition slope must match `scipy.stats.linregress`.
- New with this article: the eight Polypay means must give Gompertz A 76.43 ± 1.16 kg, k 1.672 × 10⁻³ per day and R² 0.96457; the same series without the age-eight point must give A 74.88 ± 0.51 and R² 0.99314; and the fitted weight at age zero must stay above 40 kg, which pins the extrapolation warning in this article.

## Check it yourself

On the [[growth-curve-tracker]] page:

1. In the box *One weighing per line: animal, date or age in days, weight, and optionally a body condition score*, paste the paper's mid-lactation means as ages in days: **Polypay ewe, 365, 57.1** / **Polypay ewe, 730, 64.9** / **Polypay ewe, 1095, 70.9** / **Polypay ewe, 1460, 73.2** / **Polypay ewe, 1825, 74.3** / **Polypay ewe, 2190, 74.2** / **Polypay ewe, 2555, 74.1** / **Polypay ewe, 2920, 78.6** (one per line).
2. Leave *Weight unit* as **kg** and *Curve for the figure* on **Gompertz**.
3. The headline reads *average daily gain 0.00841 kg/day over 2555 days*.
4. The *Growth curves* table gives **Gompertz A 76.43 ± 1.2, k 0.00167, b 0.5364, inflection day −373, R² 0.9646, AIC 10.9**, with the logistic and von Bertalanffy beside it within 0.2 of AIC. A negative inflection day is the tool telling you the fast phase happened before the first weighing.
5. The *Average daily gain between weighings* table shows the fade, rounded to three decimals: 0.021, 0.016, 0.006, 0.003, −0.000, −0.000 and 0.012 kg/day (7.8, 6.0, 2.3, 1.1, −0.1, −0.1 and 4.5 kg per year).
6. Delete the last line (age 2920) and look again: A drops to **74.88 ± 0.51** and R² rises to **0.9931**. One survivor group, 1.5 kg of mature weight.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a published weight-by-age table into a mature weight** with a standard error, so flocks, breeds or treatments can be compared on one number instead of a sentence.
- **Set a target weight for replacement ewes.** If mature weight is 76 kg and 95 % comes at 3.8 years, a yearling at 57 kg is at 75 % of mature weight, which is the number feeding standards actually ask for.
- **Decide how long a growth trial must run**: our refits say the plateau, not the early points, fixes A, and five years of this record was enough.
- **Check a model-selection claim.** If the AICs of Gompertz, logistic and von Bertalanffy sit within two units, no model won; the tool prints all three and marks the lowest AIC.
- **Spot an extrapolation.** Read the fitted weight at age zero and at twice the oldest age; if either is impossible, quote A only, as we have.

## The fine print

- We fitted the paper's **least-squares means**, not individual ewes, so our standard error on A describes the fit to those eight points, not the spread between animals.
- The means are adjusted for year and litter size at first lambing, and mid-lactation weight depends on how many lambs a ewe is feeding, so this curve is a management-weighted growth curve, not pure skeletal growth.
- Ages are whole years in the paper; we treated them as exactly 365 days apart.
- The age-eight mean rests on the fewest ewes (its standard error is 1.0 kg against 0.3 kg) and is heavier than ages six and seven, which is most likely survivor bias rather than renewed growth.
- The other three series in the paper (weaning, post-breeding, mid-gestation) would each give a slightly different A; mid-gestation, which includes a pregnancy, plateaus near 80.6 kg.
