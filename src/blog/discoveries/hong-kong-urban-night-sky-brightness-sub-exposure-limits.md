Light pollution is usually reported as a map colour or a single number, and neither tells you what it does to a camera. A 2026 paper in *Remote Sensing* reports about a decade of night sky brightness readings from a network of sky quality meters across Hong Kong, and states the result as how many times brighter than a natural sky each site is: about 119 times for the urban stations, and about 250 times at the brightest one. This article turns those ratios back into mag/arcsec², the unit instruments report, and uses the [[sub-exposure-calculator]] to work out what each sky level does to a deep-sky exposure. If you want to compare your own site first, start with the [[light-pollution-calculator]], check your sampling with the [[telescope-calculator]], and stack what you shoot with the [[image-stacker]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ran SQM-LE photometers (sky quality meters with a lens, about 20° wide, reading every 30 s) at urban, suburban and rural stations around Hong Kong. The main run covers 1 July 2019 to 30 June 2023, with earlier 2010-2013 and 2014-2019 data brought in for the long view. After throwing away everything touched by sunlight, about 11.1 million readings remain, and about 3.25 million of those are also free of moonlight.

Three findings matter here:

- **How bright the city is.** Against the IAU reference for a naturally dark zenith sky, 21.6 mag/arcsec², the urban stations average about 119 times brighter, the suburban ones about 9 times, and the whole network about 43 times. King's Park, in dense Kowloon, is about 250 times brighter; Astropark, in a country park, about 6 times.
- **Early evening is brighter than late night.** Every station gets darker as the night goes on. The step from early to late night is largest in the city: 1.71 mag at Tsim Sha Tsui in 2010-2013, 1.38 mag in 2019-2023, and 1.00 then 0.75 mag at King's Park.
- **Clouds brighten a city sky.** Infrared sky temperature, used as a cloud gauge, correlates strongly with brightness, most strongly in urban areas, because clouds send city light back down.

## Checked with the tools

The paper's ratios and magnitudes are two ways of saying the same thing: a sky *n* times brighter than the reference sits at 21.6 − 2.5 log₁₀ *n* mag/arcsec². That gives the numbers an imager can use.

| Station group (paper) | Times brighter than 21.6 | Sky brightness we get |
|---|---|---|
| King's Park, dense urban | 250 | 15.61 mag/arcsec² |
| Urban stations, average | 119 | 16.41 mag/arcsec² |
| Whole network, average | 43 | 17.52 mag/arcsec² |
| Suburban stations, average | 9 | 19.21 mag/arcsec² |
| Astropark, darkest station | 6 | 19.65 mag/arcsec² |

Those magnitudes are the input the Sub-exposure calculator wants. For a rig we chose ourselves (**our assumption**: an 80 mm f/6 refractor, 3.76 µm pixels, 80 % quantum efficiency, 1.5 e⁻ read noise, no dark current, and the tool's own 80 % optical throughput), the tool's sky model gives the signal each sky produces, and then the shortest sub-exposure in which read noise costs no more than 5 % extra noise.

**Step 1: the city sky swamps read noise almost at once.** At King's Park's 15.61 mag/arcsec², a red, green or blue channel collects about **47.9 e⁻ per pixel per second**. The shortest useful sub is **0.46 s**. At the suburban average of 19.21 it is 12.6 s, and at the IAU reference sky 114 s. Read noise, the thing a cooled camera is sold on, stops mattering in a city: the sky does the job in half a second. Figure 1 shows the whole range.

**Step 2: narrowband is what the ratio really costs.** A 7 nm narrowband filter cuts the sky by about a factor of 14 on this model, so the same skies need **7 s** (King's Park), **14 s** (urban average), **3.01 min** (suburban average) and **27.2 min** (IAU reference) per sub. Read the table the other way and the paper's headline becomes a practical statement: the 119-fold urban sky lets a narrowband sub be about 120 times shorter than a natural sky would allow. Short subs are not a bonus. They mean more frames, more files, more stacking, and every frame carrying the read noise again. Figure 2 shows what each length is worth when the total time is fixed.

**Step 3: the early-to-late-night step is worth a factor of 2 to 5.** A step of *Δm* magnitudes changes the sky signal by 10^(0.4 Δm), and the shortest sub moves by the same factor. Tsim Sha Tsui's 1.71 mag step in 2010-2013 is a factor of **4.83**; its 1.38 mag step in 2019-2023 is **3.56**; King's Park's two steps are **2.51** and **2.00**. Anyone imaging through a city evening is working with a sky that changes by a factor of a few before midnight, which also means a stack of subs taken across the evening does not share one background level.

## How we made the graphs

Every number and both charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/hong-kong-urban-night-sky-brightness-sub-exposure-limits.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls three functions from `src/lib/sci/subexp.ts`, the maths behind the [[sub-exposure-calculator]]:

- `skyFromSqm` turns a sky brightness in mag/arcsec² into electrons per pixel per second, for a given aperture, obstruction, focal length, pixel size, filter bandwidth, quantum efficiency and throughput. It assumes 1000 photons per second per cm² per ångström at magnitude 0 in V.
- `subLength` returns R² / ((S + D)((1 + p)² − 1)), the shortest sub in which read noise R raises the noise by no more than the share p over sky and dark shot noise.
- `efficiency` returns √((S + D)t / ((S + D)t + R²)), the share of a single perfect exposure's signal-to-noise that subs of length t reach in a fixed total time.

The brightness ratios (250, 119, 43, 9, 6), the magnitude steps (1.71, 1.38, 1.00, 0.75) and the 21.6 mag/arcsec² reference come from the paper's text. The conversion 21.6 − 2.5 log₁₀ *n* is standard. The telescope, camera, filter bandwidths and the 5 % read-noise rule are **our assumptions**, not the paper's: it measures the sky, not an imaging rig. The sky model is an estimate good to roughly a factor of 2, because the colour of city light, the real filter curve and the airmass all change the answer.

## How we test the maths

`npm test` runs `tests/sci.mjs` on every change, and it pins this tool's maths:

- The worked example on the tool's own page: a sky of 3.125 e⁻ per pixel per second with 1.5 e⁻ read noise must give a 5 % sub length of 1.5² / (3.125 × 0.1025) = 7.0 s, to within 1 part in 10⁹.
- At that length, `efficiency` must return exactly 1 / 1.05, which is what a 5 % noise penalty means.
- **New with this article**, in `tests/sci-tools/sub-exposure-calculator.mjs`: the chain used here is pinned end to end. A sky 250 times the 21.6 reference must be 15.61 mag/arcsec²; that sky must give 47.88 e⁻ per pixel per second in a 100 nm channel and 3.352 in 7 nm; the shortest subs must be 0.458 s and 6.549 s; the suburban and reference skies must need 180.4 s and 1,630 s in narrowband; and a 1.38 mag darkening must multiply the sub length by exactly 10^(0.4 × 1.38).
- `skyFromSqm` is checked against its own definition in a deliberately bare case: 20 mag/arcsec², a 112.8379 mm aperture (100 cm² of area), a focal length of 206,265 mm and 1000 µm pixels (so one pixel is one arcsecond square), 0.1 nm of bandwidth, and perfect optics, must give 1 × 10⁻³ e⁻ per pixel per second, the photon rate of a magnitude-20 source at 1000 photons s⁻¹ cm⁻² Å⁻¹.

## Check it yourself

On the [[sub-exposure-calculator]]:

1. In the *Camera* card leave *Read noise (e⁻)* at **1.5** and *Dark current (e⁻/pixel/s)* at **0**.
2. In the *Sky* card press **From sky brightness**. Type **15.61** in *Sky brightness (mag/arcsec²)*: King's Park, the paper's brightest station. Leave *Filter* on **Red, green or blue (≈ 100 nm)**, *Aperture (mm)* **80**, *Central obstruction (%)* **0**, *Focal length (mm)* **480**, *Pixel size (µm)* **3.76**, *Quantum efficiency (%)* **80**.
3. The *Sub length* card reads **At least 0.46 s per sub**, with *Sky signal* **47.9 e⁻ per pixel per second**. The other two tolerances show **2 s** for 2 % and **0.22 s** for 10 %.
4. Change *Filter* to **Narrowband 7 nm**: the sky signal falls to **3.35** and the shortest sub becomes **7 s**.
5. Now type **19.21** (the paper's suburban average) with the same narrowband filter: **3.01 min**. Type **21.6**, the IAU dark-sky reference, and it becomes **27.2 min**. That ratio, about 270 to 1 in time, is the paper's 9-fold brightness ratio seen from the camera's side.
6. Set *Extra noise from read noise* to **2 % (strict)** and watch every length roughly double. The chart under the numbers shows how little is lost by going longer.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Translate a light-pollution figure into an observing cost.** A sky quality meter reading, a map value or a published ratio all go into the same field, and the answer is in seconds, which is what a telescope schedule is made of.
- **Plan a survey from a bright site.** With the sky signal in electrons per pixel per second you can see at once whether read noise or the sky limits you, and whether a narrower filter buys back more than it costs.
- **Check a camera choice.** Change *Read noise* between two candidate cameras at your own sky brightness: in a city the difference in shortest sub is often under a second, so it may not be the thing to spend money on.
- **Teach the shot-noise argument.** Figure 2's flat top is the whole reason for stacking short exposures, and it moves visibly when you change the sky.
- **Sanity-check a published sky brightness.** If a paper gives a ratio, convert it and compare with the [[light-pollution-calculator]]'s Bortle classes before using it.

## The fine print

- The paper measures the sky. The telescope, camera and filters here are **ours**, picked as a common small imaging rig; your own numbers will differ.
- `skyFromSqm` is an estimate, roughly within a factor of 2. City light is not the colour of the V band, filter curves are not rectangles, and the SQM's 20°-wide view is not the patch your camera sees.
- The paper's own caveats apply to the ratios: sensor ageing could attenuate SQM readings by up to 2.5 mag/arcsec², and one station's data carries a 0.76 mag/arcsec² offset for its shield.
- Sky brightness is not the only thing a bright site does to an image. Gradients, flaring from nearby lights and reduced contrast on faint extended objects are not in this calculation.
- The late-minus-early steps are differences between parts of the night at one station, not a trend over years.
