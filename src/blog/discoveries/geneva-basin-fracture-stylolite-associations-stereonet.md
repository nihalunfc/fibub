Geneva wants heat from the ground. Under the city, limestones from the Early Cretaceous carry hot water mainly through cracks, so geologists need to know which way those cracks run. Two test wells have been drilled and imaged, but a borehole sees only a narrow strip of rock. A team of geologists has gone to the limestone outcrops around the basin (the Parmelan plateau, the Jura, the Vuache and the Salève) to see how fractures and **stylolites**, jagged seams where rock dissolved under pressure, form together. This article shows how the [[stereonet]] checks the geometry behind their method, and how a researcher can test whether a fit could happen by chance.

## The paper in brief

Rocks squeezed in one direction crack in a predictable pattern. The squeeze direction is called **σ1**, the largest principal stress. Stylolites grow at right angles to it, veins open parallel to it, and pairs of small faults (a **conjugate pair**) form on either side of it. The team calls such a family of features formed together an **association**.

At 28 stations, they mapped these associations and found that they all fit two old stress fields. In **event E1**, σ1 was horizontal and trended **135°** (northwest to southeast). In **event E2**, it trended about **097°** (roughly west to east), about 45° anticlockwise from E1. Each event made low-angle reverse faults (the rock above the fault pushed up and over) and strike-slip vein arrays.

Then they turned to the borehole image logs from two geothermal wells. In well GEo-01, **350 of 820** natural fractures matched the orientations predicted by the two events. In GEo-02, 104 of 211 did. Overall, **about 40 to 50%** of the fractures in the wells belong to this "background" network. The rest may come from local faults or folds. A fracture was counted as a match if its dip direction plus dip differed from a predicted one by less than 30°.

## Checked with the tools

We used the paper's approximate strikes, σ1 directions, fault dip, vein angle and tolerance, and the Stereonet's maths. To keep things simple, we treated bedding as horizontal. The fracture data in Steps 3 and 4 are **simulated**, not the paper's borehole data.

**Step 1: σ1 from conjugate vein arrays.** For a pair of near-vertical vein arrays, σ1 should bisect the acute angle between them. On the Stereonet, the mean pole of two vertical planes gives exactly that bisecting plane. For the Parmelan association PA2 (sinistral 150°, dextral 120°), the mean plane strikes **135°**, matching E1's σ1 exactly, with each array 15° from σ1, just as the paper assumes. PA3 (080° and 120°) gives **100°**, against E2's 097°. The Jura associations give 140° for JA2 and 107.5° for JA4, within 5 and 10° of the event's σ1. The arrays sit 15 to 33° from σ1, so the strike values are approximate.

| Association | Strikes (paper, approx.) | Bisector (Stereonet) | σ1 of its event (paper) |
|---|---|---|---|
| PA2, Parmelan | 150° and 120° | 135.0° | 135° (E1) |
| PA3, Parmelan | 080° and 120° | 100.0° | 097° (E2) |
| JA2, Jura | 170° and 110° | 140.0° | 135° (E1) |
| JA4, Jura | 075° and 140° | 107.5° | 097° (E2) |

**Step 2: the reverse faults.** E1's small faults strike about 045° and dip about 30°, in both directions: one set to the southeast and one to the northwest. The two planes meet along a **horizontal line trending 045°**: that is σ2, the middle stress. The mean of their poles is **vertical**: that is σ3, the smallest stress, pointing straight up, as expected for reverse faulting. σ1 is then the horizontal direction at right angles to both, **135°**. The faults meet at 60° on the side facing σ1, the classic angle for faults that break fresh rock.

**Step 3: a simulated fracture log.** We built 400 simulated fractures (Figure 1). 45% came from E1's five predicted planes, with 8° of random scatter. The other 55% had completely random orientations. Recovering one set, the faults dipping northwest, with Fisher statistics (a standard way to average directions), gave a mean plane of about **225 / 30** (true value 225 / 30) and a 95% cone of confidence of **3.3°** (k = 53, 36 fractures). Clustered sets give large k and small cones.

**Step 4: how often does a random fracture fit?** A match within 30° sounds strict, but fractures can match *any* of the predicted planes. With our simplified list of ten planes (five for each event), **52%** of completely random orientations pass a "dip direction plus dip within 30°" test. By the looser "angle between the poles under 30°" test, **84%** pass (Figure 2). In our simulated log, 71% of fractures "fit", although only 45% were drawn from the sets.

This does not show the paper's 40 to 50% is chance. The paper predicts specific sets for each well and works relative to the tilt of bedding. Vertical wells also rarely cut steep fractures, so real borehole fractures are not random. But it shows why a **random baseline** is worth computing: the share expected by chance depends strongly on how many planes are predicted and on the tolerance, and a fit only means something when it beats that baseline.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/geneva-basin-fracture-stylolite-associations-stereonet.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls the [[stereonet]]'s own functions from `src/lib/sci/stereonet.ts`:

- `pole`, `planeFromPole`, `vec` and `line` to turn planes into poles and back, and to find where two planes meet.
- `fisher` for mean directions, k and the 95% cone of confidence.
- `project` and `greatCircle` for the lower-hemisphere equal-area net in Figure 1.

From the paper: the vein and fault strikes, σ1 directions, the 30° fault dip, the 15° vein angle, the 30° tolerance and the well counts. Our assumptions, labelled in the script: horizontal bedding, our list of ten predicted planes, and the simulated fractures (45% from the sets, 8° scatter, the rest uniformly random). Run the script and it prints every number and redraws both charts.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks the stereonet maths against known answers:

- The pole of a plane striking 030° and dipping 40° must plunge 50° towards 300°, and turning that pole back into a plane must give 030 / 40 again.
- On the equal-angle net, a plane dipping 30° must plot at a distance of tan 30° from the centre, and on the equal-area net any horizontal line must lie on the outer circle.
- The Fisher mean of four poles (planes 030/40, 025/45, 035/38 and 028/50) must trend 299.27° with a 95% cone of 6.90°, and two opposite ends of one horizontal line must count as the same direction.

## Check it yourself

On the [[stereonet]]:

1. Set *Planes are typed as* to **Strike, dip (right-hand rule)**. Clear the *Planes* box and the *Lines* box.
2. Type two planes, one per line: **150 90** and **120 90**. Under the net, *Mean plane* reads **135 / 90** and *Mean pole* reads **0° → 045**: the bisector, E1's σ1 direction of 135°.
3. Replace them with **080 90** and **120 90**: *Mean plane* reads **100 / 90**, close to E2's 097°.
4. Replace them with **045 30** and **225 30**, the conjugate reverse faults. *Mean pole* reads **90° → 225**: a vertical line (the trend of a vertical line is arbitrary), σ3 for reverse faulting. Draw both great circles and you will see them cross on the outer circle at 045° and 225°: σ2.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Find palaeostress directions** from conjugate fractures and veins on the [[stereonet]], without installing software.
- **Average fracture sets** with Fisher statistics, and report the mean plane, k and the 95% cone.
- **Compare outcrop and borehole data** by plotting both on one equal-area net.
- **Build a random baseline:** before claiming that a set of fractures "fits" a model, check how many random orientations would fit too.
- **Teach** how stress directions control fracture patterns.

## The fine print

- The strikes are the paper's approximate values; many are given as rounded azimuths, so our bisectors are good to a few degrees at best.
- We treated bedding as horizontal. The paper works relative to bedding, which tilts by 10 to 20° (and up to about 60° in part of GEo-01).
- Our ten predicted planes and our two matching tests simplify the paper's method. The fracture log is simulated. The random baseline is ours, not a result of the paper.
- 350 of 820 is 42.7%; we quote the counts rather than a rounded percentage.
