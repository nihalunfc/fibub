An abandoned oil or gas well can keep leaking methane for decades, and nobody knows how much unless someone measures it. A 2025 paper in *Atmospheric Measurement Techniques* did so for a venting well in New Mexico and found that a plume model with a **fixed stability class**, the usual shortcut, gave only a fraction of the true rate. This article reruns the Gaussian plume equation with the [[gaussian-plume-calculator]] to see which part of the shortcut fails. To turn ppm into mass concentrations, use the [[air-quality-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

On 19 April 2023 the team put four methane sensors at 7.5, 15, 22.5 and 47 metres downwind of a vent, in a steady wind of about 6.6 m/s. They also had a direct measurement: a flow meter on the vent gave **9.0 kg of methane per hour**. The question was how well a sensor reading alone can recover that number.

The standard route is to pick a **stability class** (a letter from A, very unstable air, to F, stable air) and read the plume's spread from a table. The authors found this badly wrong. Classes B to F gave rates of 3.72, 1.86, 0.71, 0.35 and 0.14 kg/h, all below the truth. Their fix was to measure the sideways spread of the plume from the wind-direction scatter during the test, and to set the vertical spread to 58 % of it. With that, a four-sensor inversion gave **10.53 ± 1.16 kg/h**, about 15 % above the direct value.

## Checked with the tools

We did not have the raw sensor traces, only the peak readings in the paper's text. So this is a **recomputation**: we take the four peak enhancements and ask what emission rate each would imply under each class, using the tool's maths. The wind (6.6 m/s at plume height), the release height 1.2 m and the sensor height 1.6 m are from the paper. Converting ppm to grams per cubic metre needs the air's density, and the paper's text did not give it, so we assumed air at 88 kPa and 20 °C (**our assumption**, for a site about 1,100 m above sea level).

**Step 1: invert each sensor.** The tool's open-country Briggs spread for class B gives 8.0, 8.1 and 11.7 kg/h at 15, 22.5 and 47 m, an average of 9.2 for those three. Class C gives 4.6, 4.6 and 5.7; neutral class D gives 2.8, 2.9 and 3.4. So moving from B to D cuts the answer by a factor of about three, the same direction and size of error the paper reports for its stability-class models (which all read low). Class D was what the field conditions suggested (the paper says C to D); the paper also notes that its measured spread looked like class A near the vent and B to C further out.

**Step 2: the paper's own spread.** Using the same plume equation with the paper's printed sideways spreads (2.55 to 6.69 m, which we read as σy, **our assumption** because the column is labelled with a "2σ width") and σz = 0.58 σy, the four peaks give 15.4, 10.5, 7.7 and 7.6 kg/h. Their mean is 10.3 kg/h, close to the paper's 10.53. The scatter between sensors is large (15.4 at 7.5 m against 7.6 at 47 m), which fits the paper's single-sensor spread of 6.4 to 11.7 kg/h and why it combines four sensors.

**Step 3: run it forward.** If the true rate is 9.0 kg/h, what should the sensors read? The class B curve predicts 88, 30.7, 16.9 and 4.6 ppm; the observed peaks were 53.4, 27.2, 15.1 and 6.0 ppm. Beyond 15 m the match is good. Class D predicts 260, 89, 46 and 16 ppm, about three times too high at 15 to 47 m (2.7 to 3.3 times), which is the same error as above seen from the other side. The 7.5 m sensor is the odd one out in every class, because it sits so close to the vent that the point-source equation stops being a good picture (the Briggs curves are fitted for distances of about 100 m and more, so everything here at 7.5 to 47 m is outside their range).

The paper's class numbers are lower than ours because it used its own sensor-specific inputs (distance, averaging and Pasquill-Gifford spread), not the peak readings and Briggs curves we used. What carries over is the pattern: **the answer moves several-fold with the class**, and the neutral classes read low.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/orphaned-well-methane-gaussian-plume-stability-class-underestimate.mjs` calls `conc` (the Gaussian plume with ground reflection), `sigmasFor` (the Briggs open-country spread by class) and `vertical` (the reflected vertical term) from `src/lib/sci/plume.ts`, the maths of the [[gaussian-plume-calculator]]. The distances, peaks, wind, heights, the 58 % ratio and the printed spreads are from the paper's text. The air density, the choice of open-country curves and the reading of the spread column as σy are **our assumptions**. The sensors' data themselves are not used, only their peak values.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/gaussian-plume-calculator.mjs`:

- **New with this article:** for the ground-level centreline of a 2.5 g/s source at 1.2 m in a 6.6 m/s wind, the class B concentration at 22.5 m must equal the hand formula Q ÷ (π u σy σz) × exp(−H² ÷ 2σz²) with the open-country σy = 0.16 x ÷ √(1 + 0.0001 x) and σz = 0.12 x, which is 16.9 ppm of methane in our air (9,768 µg/m³ at a 1.6 m receptor).
- The class D value at 22.5 m must be 26,865 µg/m³ (2.75 times class B), and the same point at ground level must be higher than at 1.6 m, because the source is at 1.2 m.
- The older checks in `tests/sci.mjs` pin a 50 m stack, 100 g/s case against hand sums (906.5 µg/m³ at 1 km).

## Check it yourself

On the [[gaussian-plume-calculator]]:

1. Set the **Emission rate** to **2.5** with **Unit** g/s (that is 9 kg/h) and the **Stack height (m)** to **1.2**.
2. Enter **Wind at 10 m (m/s)** of **6.6**, set **Stability** to **B: moderately unstable**, **Ground** to **Open country**, and untick **Add plume rise**.
3. Set **Receptor: distance downwind (m)** to **22.5**, **off the centreline** to **0** and **height above ground** to **1.6**.
4. The receptor should read about **9,768 µg/m³**. Divide by 579.4 µg/m³ per ppm (our air density) to get 16.9 ppm of methane.
5. Change **Stability** to **D: neutral**. It should read about **26,865 µg/m³**, 2.75 times higher (46.4 ppm). Set the distance to **47** and it reads 9,312 µg/m³ (16.1 ppm).

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-checking a leak estimate** from one sensor: how far does the rate swing if the class is a letter off?
- **Planning sensor spacing** around a small source: where is the highest ground-level reading for a given wind?
- **Teaching** why a plume model is only as good as its spread numbers.
- **Comparing sources** by running the same peak reading through two classes.
- **Choosing a gas unit** (ppm against mass per cubic metre) with the [[air-quality-calculator]].

## The fine print

- Our wind, air density and spread curves are assumptions; the paper's own inversion is more careful and uses all the sensor data.
- The Briggs curves are meant for about 100 m to 10 km, not for a 7.5 to 47 m ground release.
- Reading the paper's "2σ width" column as σy is our interpretation; a different reading would change the second set of rates.
- The paper's own stability-class rates are quoted from its text; we could not reproduce them exactly.
