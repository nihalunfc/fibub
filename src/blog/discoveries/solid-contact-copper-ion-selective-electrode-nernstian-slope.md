An ion-selective electrode is judged almost entirely by one number: how many millivolts its potential moves for every tenfold change in the ion it is built for. The Nernst equation fixes that number exactly, and for a doubly charged ion such as Cu²⁺ at 25 °C it is 29.58 mV. A 2025 paper in *BMC Chemistry* builds a solid-contact copper electrode (a membrane electrode with no inner filling solution, using polyaniline nanoparticles to carry the signal to the wire) and measures **28.05 mV per decade** — which the authors fairly call near-Nernstian. This article uses the [[nernst-calculator]] to ask a sharper question: how much of that 1.5 mV shortfall is the electrode, and how much is simply what happens when you calibrate against concentration instead of activity? Along the way the [[calibration-curve]] handles the straight-line fit and its detection limit, and [[cyclic-voltammetry]] is the tool for the other half of an electrode-characterisation paper.

## The paper in brief

The electrode is a PVC membrane holding β-cyclodextrin and Reinecke's salt, coated onto a solid contact of polyaniline nanoparticles. The authors report a response over **six decades, from 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻² mol/L Cu²⁺**, a slope of **28.05 mV per decade**, a detection limit of **7.5 × 10⁻⁹ mol/L**, a response time of **10 s or less** and a stable reading between **pH 6 and 9**. They then use the sensor for something unusual: watching copper nanoparticles form and dissolve in real time, which needs a fast electrode rather than a sensitive one, and measuring copper in spiked water with 95.8 to 98.9 % recovery.

The detection limit is worth converting before anything else. At copper's molar mass of 63.546 g/mol, 7.5 × 10⁻⁹ mol/L is **0.48 µg/L**: the electrode sees copper roughly a thousand times below the milligram-per-litre levels where drinking-water rules sit (check your own country's official limit, which changes).

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: what the ideal slope is, to four figures.** The Nernst slope is RT ln10 / zF. The tool's constants give **29.5797 mV per decade** for z = 2 at 25.00 °C (and 59.159 mV for a singly charged ion, the familiar number). The paper's 28.05 is **94.8 %** of it.

**Step 2: can temperature explain the gap?** No. The slope rises by only **0.099 mV per decade per °C**: 29.084 mV at 20 °C, 30.076 mV at 30 °C. To reach 28.05 mV per decade by temperature alone, the solution would have to sit at about **9.6 °C** (Figure 2). So a lab at 20 to 30 °C can move the slope by half a millivolt, not one and a half.

**Step 3: activity, the usual culprit.** The Nernst equation is written in **activity**, the effective concentration once ions get in each other's way, and activity falls below concentration as a solution gets saltier. For Cu²⁺ in a CuCl₂ solution the ionic strength is three times the concentration, and the extended Debye-Hückel expression gives an activity coefficient of **0.52 at 10⁻² mol/L**, 0.92 at 10⁻⁴ and 0.99 at 10⁻⁶. So the top decade of the calibration is pulled down while the bottom decades are untouched, and a straight line fitted to potential against **concentration** across those six decades has a slope of only **28.62 mV per decade** even for a perfect electrode.

That accounts for about **two-thirds** of the gap between 29.58 and the measured 28.05. Figure 1 draws all three lines together, anchored to agree in the middle of the range: they differ by less than 5 mV anywhere in six decades, which is the real message. The electrode is behaving close to thermodynamically; the slope label alone cannot tell you otherwise unless the calibration states whether it is in activity or in concentration, and at what ionic strength.

**Step 4: what a 1.5 mV slope error costs in a result.** If you calibrate at one concentration and then read a sample some decades away, using 29.58 mV where the electrode really gives 28.05 mV multiplies your answer by 10^(1.53/29.58) per decade, that is **1.13× per decade** and **2.04× over six decades**. Over the whole published range the electrode's own span is 168.3 mV, against 177.5 mV for the ideal line. So: calibrate with standards that bracket your samples, and the slope question almost disappears; extrapolate six decades and it doubles your answer.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/solid-contact-copper-ion-selective-electrode-nernstian-slope.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `thermalV`, `halfE`, `half`, `convertRef` and `qFromE` from `src/lib/sci/nernst.ts`, the maths behind the [[nernst-calculator]], plus `logGamma` from `src/lib/sci/buffer.ts` (the activity coefficient used by the [[buffer-recipe-calculator]]) and `molarMass` from `src/lib/sci/chem.ts`.

The slope, the range, the detection limit and the pH window come from the paper's abstract. The standard potential of Cu²⁺/Cu (+0.3419 V vs SHE) comes from the tool's own table. **Our assumptions** are the chemistry behind the activity calculation: that the calibration standards were CuCl₂ in water with no added background salt, so the ionic strength is three times the copper concentration, and that the three lines should be anchored to agree at 10⁻⁵ mol/L. The paper's own calibration points are not reproduced here; nothing in these figures is its data.

## How we test the maths

The Nernst maths is pinned in `tests/sci.mjs`, which runs on every change to the site:

- The Daniell cell: E°cell must be +1.1037 V, and with 0.01 mol/L Cu²⁺ the cell must read **+1.0445 V**.
- The slope itself: RT ln10 / F at 25 °C must be **59.159 mV**, and every one of the 44 standard half-reactions must balance for charge and atoms.
- Reference conversions: 0.500 V against SCE must become **0.544 V** against Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl), and a potential against Ag/AgCl converted to RHE at pH 13 must shift by 0.197 + 0.769 V.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/nernst-calculator.mjs`: the divalent slope at 25 °C must be **29.5797 mV/decade** and change by 0.0992 mV per °C; a Cu²⁺/Cu electrode must read **+0.2827 V** at 10⁻² mol/L and **+0.1053 V** at 10⁻⁸ mol/L vs SHE; and the straight line through potential against log concentration with Debye-Hückel activities over those six decades must come out at **28.62 mV/decade**.

## Check it yourself

On the [[nernst-calculator]]:

1. In the *Electrode or cell* card, press **One electrode**.
2. Choose **+0.342 V  Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)** as the electrode and leave *Temperature (°C)* at **25**.
3. Under *Concentrations*, set the Cu²⁺ box to **0.01** (the tool treats the number you type as an activity). The result line reads **E = +0.2827 V vs SHE**, and the facts underneath give the same potential against Ag/AgCl and SCE, with the Nernst slope for this half-reaction.
4. Change the activity to **1e-8**: the potential falls to **+0.1053 V**. The two readings differ by 177.5 mV, which is six decades at 29.58 mV each.
5. Set *Temperature (°C)* to **9.6** and look at the slope the tool reports: 28.05 mV per decade, the paper's number, which is how you can see that temperature is not where it came from.
6. In the *Reference electrode converter* card, type **0.2827** in *Potential (V)*, set *Measured against* to **SHE / NHE** and read the table: **+0.086 V** against Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) and +0.042 V against SCE.
7. In *Find Q or a concentration from a measured potential*, put **0.194** in *Measured E (V)*, **0.3419** in *E° (V, same reference)* and **2** in *Electrons n*: Q comes back as about **1.0 × 10⁵**, which is 1/activity for 10⁻⁵ mol/L Cu²⁺.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Grade a new sensor honestly**: work out the ideal slope at your temperature and your ion's charge before calling a slope sub-Nernstian.
- **Decide whether to fix the ionic strength**: compute the activity coefficient at the top of your range; if it costs you more than a millivolt per decade, add a constant background salt and say so in the paper.
- **Set the calibration range**: pick standards that bracket the samples, and use the slope-error numbers above to show what extrapolation would cost.
- **Convert potentials between references** when comparing papers, including to RHE at a given pH, so Ag/AgCl and SCE numbers can be read side by side.
- **Turn a measured potential back into a concentration** (the Q box) when a calibration has drifted, and fit the calibration itself, with its detection limit, in the [[calibration-curve]].

## The fine print

- We worked from the paper's abstract: its figures, calibration table and selectivity coefficients were not available to us, so nothing here is a fit to its measurements.
- The activity calculation assumes CuCl₂ in water with no background electrolyte. If the authors fixed the ionic strength, activity explains less of the slope and the electrode's own response explains more.
- The extended Debye-Hückel expression is reliable to roughly 0.1 mol/L ionic strength; 10⁻² mol/L CuCl₂ is inside that, but only just.
- A real membrane electrode's slope also drifts with ageing, with the film thickness and with interfering ions. A thermodynamic check cannot separate those; it only sets the ceiling.
- The Cu²⁺/Cu potentials here are for a copper metal electrode, shown as the thermodynamic yardstick. The paper's device is a membrane electrode, whose standard potential depends on its own chemistry.
