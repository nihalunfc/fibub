A car part fails a customer check, and the supplier has to prove the problem is gone. The usual proof is a **capability index**: a single number comparing how wide the process spreads with how wide the drawing allows. A 2025 case study in *Processes* follows one such fix through the eight-discipline (8D) problem-solving method, on a riveted joint whose shear force must land between 20 and 25 kN, and reports the capability rising from Cpk 0.67 to Cpk 1.67. This article recomputes both numbers with the [[control-charts]] tool, turns them into the scrap rate they actually promise, and adds the piece of the story the paper leaves out: how sure you can be of a capability index from a handful of parts. Before trusting any such index, check the measuring instrument with the [[gauge-rr|gauge R&R study]] and check that the tolerance itself is achievable with the [[tolerance-stackup]].

## The paper in brief

The authors work through a real automotive complaint with the 8D method: contain the problem, find the root cause, correct it, then prove the correction holds. The part is a riveted assembly. Two things were wrong: a rivet of the wrong diameter was reaching the line (8.6 mm instead of the 12.3 mm that passes), and the joint's **shear force**, the force the joint carries before it slips or breaks, was too variable.

The drawing allows 20 to 25 kN. After the corrective action the paper reports a mean of **22.5 kN** with a standard deviation of **0.5 kN**, and from those two numbers **Cp = 1.67 and Cpk = 1.67**. Before the action it reports **Cp = Cpk = 0.67**. It sets 1.33 as the acceptance target (with 1.67 as the short-term PPAP reference), asks that a process use no more than about 75 % of the tolerance band, and asks for Cpk at or above 1.33 for three months running before the problem is closed.

Two definitions, because everything below rests on them. **Cp** is the tolerance width divided by six standard deviations: how well the process *could* fit if it were centred. **Cpk** also counts where the mean actually sits: it is the distance from the mean to the nearer limit, divided by three standard deviations. Cp and Cpk are equal only when the mean sits exactly in the middle.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from the two numbers the paper states** (mean 22.5 kN, standard deviation 0.5 kN) against its own limits. Nothing is taken from the paper's figures, and the paper does not publish the individual measurements.

**The indices themselves come out exactly.** With the limits 5 kN apart, the tool's capability maths gives Cp = (25 − 20) / (6 × 0.5) = **1.6667** and, because 22.5 kN is dead centre, Cpk = **1.6667** too. The paper's 1.67 is simply this rounded. For the "before" state the paper gives only Cp = Cpk = 0.67, which is the same arithmetic run backwards: a centred process with that capability has a standard deviation of **1.2438 kN**, two and a half times the one it ended with.

**What those indices promise in parts per million.** This is where an index earns its keep. For a centred normal process the tool reads out the expected fraction outside the limits:

| Cpk (centred) | Standard deviation it implies | Expected out of specification |
|---|---|---|
| 0.67 (the paper's "before") | 1.2438 kN | 44,430 ppm, about 1 part in 23 |
| 1.00 | 0.8333 kN | 2,700 ppm |
| 1.33 (the paper's target) | 0.6266 kN | 66 ppm |
| 1.67 (the paper's "after") | 0.4990 kN | 0.54 ppm |
| 2.00 | 0.4167 kN | 0.002 ppm |

So the fix is worth roughly a factor of 80,000 in expected rejects, which is the real headline. It also settles the paper's **97.7 %**, a figure its text attaches to the "before" state as a probability of failure. Ask the tool for a *one-sided* specification with Cpk exactly 2/3 and it returns 22,750 ppm outside, that is **97.725 % inside** the limit. The 97.7 % is the share of parts that pass against one limit, not the share that fail: a Cpk of 0.67 means about 2.3 % of parts fall outside a single limit, or 4.4 % outside two. Read the other way round, it would make the before state sound forty times worse than it was.

**The paper's 75 % tolerance rule is the same thing as Cp 1.33.** Six standard deviations at 0.5 kN is 3.0 kN, which is **60 %** of the 5 kN band. A process that uses exactly 75 % of the band has Cp = 1.333. The two acceptance rules in the paper are one rule written twice.

**How much the mean may drift.** The 0.5 kN spread leaves room to spare, but not much room to wander. Figure 1 sweeps the mean across the whole tolerance band at three spreads. At 0.5 kN, Cpk stays at or above 1.33 only while the mean is between **21.995 and 23.005 kN**, a window of ±0.505 kN; at the target of 1.67 there is no window at all, because 1.67 is only reachable dead centre. At the "before" spread of 1.2438 kN the curve never reaches 1.33 anywhere. That is the practical reading of the paper's three-month monitoring requirement: the X̄ chart is not decoration, it is what protects the 1.33.

**The number the paper does not give: how sure is 1.67?** A capability index computed from a sample is an estimate, and the tool reports a confidence interval for it (chi-square for Cp, Bissell's 1990 approximation for Cpk). The paper never says how many parts it measured, so the counts in Figure 2 are **our assumption**. Measure 20 parts and an estimate of 1.67 carries a 95 % interval of **1.12 to 2.22**: the data cannot even rule out a Cpk below the 1.33 target. You need about **52 parts** before the lower bound clears 1.33, and 100 parts to reach 1.43 to 1.91.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/shear-force-process-capability-cpk-ppm-confidence-intervals.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `capability` (and, for the worked example below, `xbarChart` and `runRules`) from `src/lib/sci/eng-cc.ts`, the maths behind the [[control-charts]] tool. `capability` wants a list of values as well as a within-subgroup spread, so the script builds a list whose mean and standard deviation are exactly the paper's two numbers; the indices then depend only on those.

From the paper: the 20 and 25 kN limits, the mean of 22.5 kN, the standard deviation of 0.5 kN, the before-and-after indices and the 1.33 target. **Our assumptions:** that the before state was centred (the paper reports Cp = Cpk, which implies it), that the force is normally distributed, that the within-subgroup and overall spreads are the same, and every sample size in Figure 2.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/control-charts.mjs` on every change to fibub. It pins the chart and capability maths against an independent NumPy and SciPy script:

- On a made-up set of 25 subgroups of 5, the X̄-R chart must give a centre line of 500.23648, limits 499.64120 and 500.83176, R̄ = 1.032 and a within-subgroup σ of 0.4436937, with d2 and d3 computed from the normal distribution rather than read off a table.
- The same data with limits 498.5 and 501.5 must give Cp 1.1062334, Cpk 0.9318320, Pp 0.9201113 and Ppk 0.7750527, a 95 % interval for Cp of 0.953 to 1.259, and 2,652 ppm within against 10,730 ppm overall.
- The Western Electric and Nelson rule flags must match a separately written Python implementation, point for point.
- New with this article: the paper's own case. Mean 22.5 kN, σ 0.5 kN and limits 20 to 25 kN must give Cp and Cpk of 1.666667 and 0.5443 ppm; σ 1.2438 kN must give 0.67 and 44,431 ppm; a one-sided limit at Cpk exactly 2/3 must give 22,750.1 ppm; and the 95 % Cpk interval from 20 values must be 1.117 to 2.216.

## Check it yourself

The paper publishes no individual readings, so here is a **simulated** run of 18 parts drawn from its mean and spread (6 subgroups of 3, our own numbers, not the paper's data). On the [[control-charts]] tool:

1. Leave *Chart* on **X̄ and R (subgroups)** and *Run rules* on **Western Electric (4 rules)**.
2. In *Values*, paste one subgroup per line: **22.2 22.07 22.43**; **22.85 22.15 23.11**; **22.61 22.53 22.04**; **23.18 22.45 22.67**; **22.39 21.41 22.73**; **22.6 21.98 23.28**.
3. The X̄ chart shows a centre line of **22.4822** with limits **21.589** and **23.376** (A2 = 1.0233 for n = 3), and the R chart R̄ = **0.8733** with an upper limit of **2.248** (D4 = 2.5746). No point breaks a rule.
4. Type **20** in *Lower spec limit (LSL)* and **25** in *Upper spec limit (USL)*. Capability appears: **Cp 1.615**, **Cpk 1.604**, Pp 1.774, Ppk 1.761, a within-subgroup σ of **0.516** and 0 ppm expected out of specification (1 ppm from the within-subgroup spread).
5. Look at the intervals printed under each index: Cp is 0.978 to 2.252 and Cpk 0.944 to 2.263. Eighteen parts simply cannot tell a brilliant process from a mediocre one.
6. Change *Confidence for intervals* to **0.99** and watch both intervals widen further.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn someone else's capability index into a reject rate** before you accept it, as in the table above: Cpk, the spread it implies and the expected ppm are one calculation.
- **Report an interval, not just an index.** Give the number of parts behind a Cpk and read its interval off the tool; a lone "Cpk = 1.67" from a dozen parts says very little.
- **Size a capability study in advance**: pick the Cpk you must demonstrate, then find the sample size whose lower bound clears it (52 parts here, for 1.67 against a target of 1.33).
- **Set a drift budget for the operator**: work out the window the mean may wander in before Cpk drops below target, and mark it on the X̄ chart.
- **Separate measurement noise from process noise** first with the [[gauge-rr|gauge R&R study]]; a study whose instrument eats a third of the tolerance will flatter or ruin the capability number.
- **Check that the drawing is achievable** with the [[tolerance-stackup]] before blaming the process for a tolerance nobody could hold.

## The fine print

- The paper publishes no individual shear-force readings, so every number here is recomputed from its stated mean, standard deviation and limits. The 18 values in "Check it yourself" are simulated by us.
- Capability indices assume a stable, roughly normal process. The paper's own three-month monitoring requirement is what establishes stability; an index from an unstable process describes its past only.
- We read the paper's "probability of failure 97.7 %" as the conforming share at a Cpk of 2/3, because that is exactly what the number is. If the authors meant something else, the arithmetic above still stands.
- The confidence intervals use Bissell's normal approximation for Cpk, which is the common choice but not exact; for very small samples treat it as indicative.
- The paper's wider subject is the 8D method and its quality planning, which no tool can check. Only the capability arithmetic is checked here.
