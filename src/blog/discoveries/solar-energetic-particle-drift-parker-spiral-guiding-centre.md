A solar flare throws protons into a magnetic field that is not straight. The Sun's rotation winds the field into the **Parker spiral**, and a curved, weakening field makes a charged particle slide sideways, out of the plane it started in. That sideways slide, the **drift**, is why energetic particles from one active region can reach spacecraft far from the flare. A 2025 paper in *The Astrophysical Journal* asks how much of that drift survives when the field is also turbulent, and finds the answer is often a small fraction of the textbook value. This article rebuilds the textbook value itself with the [[particle-drifts|Particle drift simulator]], from the field strength and curvature the paper quotes, and shows where a non-relativistic tool can and cannot be trusted. For the plasma around the particle, try the [[plasma-parameters]]; for the energy-to-speed step, the [[relativistic-kinematics|relativistic kinematics calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors trace protons of 10, 100 and 1000 MeV from 2 solar radii outwards for 48 hours, in a monopolar Parker field with a 400 km s⁻¹ solar wind, and measure how far each particle wanders in colatitude (north-south) by the time it reaches 1 au. They run three cases: no scattering, scattering off a mean free path, and full turbulence with a relative variance δB²/B² of 0.2, 0.6 or 2.0 at 1 au.

Guiding-centre theory predicts drifts of about **17, 160 and 1300 km s⁻¹** at the three energies. The scattering runs stay close to that. The turbulent runs do not: the measured drift falls to **3.5 km s⁻¹ at 10 MeV** and **82 km s⁻¹ at 100 MeV**, while 1000 MeV protons keep **780 km s⁻¹**. The authors explain it with a **drift reduction factor** f_s, the fraction of the theoretical drift that survives when the field direction decorrelates in a time comparable with the gyration: 0.21 at 10 MeV, 0.66 at 100 MeV and 0.92 at 1000 MeV. Low-energy particles, whose orbits are small enough that the local field direction changes before they finish a turn, barely drift at all.

The quantity f_s multiplies is the theoretical drift. So the theoretical drift has to be right, and that is a number anyone can check.

## Checked with the tools

The Particle drift simulator has a **curved field** case: the field runs around a circle of radius R and falls as 1/R, like the field in a torus. Near 1 au the Parker spiral is close to that shape, because the wound-up azimuthal part dominates and falls as 1/r. For a particle with energy W⊥ across the field and W∥ along it, guiding-centre theory gives the combined curvature and gradient drift

**v_d = (m / q B R) × (v∥² + v⊥²/2)**

where m and q are the particle's mass and charge, B the field strength and R the radius of curvature. The tool works this out and, separately, pushes the real orbit with the **Boris method** so you can watch the drift appear and check the two against each other.

**Step 1: the field.** The paper does not print the field strength at 1 au, but it does print the non-relativistic proton gyrofrequency there, 0.49 s⁻¹. Turn that around (B = ωm/q) and the field is **5.115 nT**, a normal value for the solar wind at the Earth's orbit. With R = 1 au and an isotropic pitch-angle split (W⊥ = 2W/3, W∥ = W/3; **our assumption**, as a drift is normally quoted for an isotropic distribution), everything else follows.

**Step 2: the drift at 10 MeV.** The tool's theory gives **17.4 km s⁻¹** and its Boris orbit measures **17.4 km s⁻¹**, against the paper's **17**. The orbit is enormous (gyroradius 0.00049 au, about 73,000 km) and takes 12.8 s to go round, yet the drift is only tens of km s⁻¹ because the field changes over an astronomical unit.

**Step 3: where a non-relativistic tool stops working.** The tool is non-relativistic: it turns the energy you type into a speed with v = √(2W/m). At 10 MeV a proton moves at 0.145 c and the error is under 1 %. At 100 MeV (0.428 c) the tool reads 174 km s⁻¹ against the paper's 160, and at 1000 MeV (0.875 c) it reads 1742 against 1300, a third too fast. There is a clean way round it. The relativistic drift is the same formula with γm in place of m, so if you type the **effective energy γmv²/2** instead of the kinetic energy, the tool gives the relativistic answer: type 95.18 MeV for a 100 MeV proton and it reads **165.8 km s⁻¹**; type 742.04 MeV for a 1000 MeV proton and it reads **1292.9**. Figure 1 shows both lines against the paper's three values.

**Step 4: the pitch angle matters less than you would think.** A particle's drift depends on how its energy splits between motion along the field and motion around it, through v∥² + v⊥²/2. Field-aligned gives v², purely gyrating gives v²/2, so the whole range is a factor of two (Figure 2). The isotropic average, 2v²/3, falls at a pitch angle of 54.7°, and that is where the paper's 160 km s⁻¹ sits.

**Step 5: putting the paper's reduction factors back.** The paper's own relation is v_turb = f_s × v_scat. With our tool's relativistic theory as the anchor, 0.21 × 17.3 = 3.6 km s⁻¹ against the measured 3.5; 0.66 × 165.8 = 109 against 82 (the paper multiplies f_s by its scattering-run drift of 110-130, not by the theory, and 0.66 × 124 = 82); 0.92 × 1292.9 = 1190 against 780, where the paper again starts from its scattering value of 840 and gets 773. The lesson for anyone using these numbers: f_s is a factor on the **scattering** drift, not on the pure theory, and the two differ most at low energy.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/solar-energetic-particle-drift-parker-spiral-guiding-centre.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `theoryDrift` and `simulate` from `src/lib/sci/phdrift.ts`, the guiding-centre formulas and the Boris pusher behind the [[particle-drifts|Particle drift simulator]].

From the paper: the gyrofrequency 0.49 s⁻¹ at 1 au, the three proton energies, the three theoretical drifts, the scattering and turbulence drifts and the reduction factors. **Our assumptions:** that the Parker field at 1 au can be treated as a 1/R azimuthal field (the paper's own expression carries an extra geometric factor of 1.072 at 1 au with its spiral scale of 0.93 au, so the simple picture is about 7 % low), that the pitch-angle distribution is isotropic, and that the radius of curvature is 1 au exactly. The proton mass and elementary charge are CODATA values from the tool's own code. Nothing here is a fit to the paper's simulated particles; it is a recomputation of the theory the paper's reduction factors multiply.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/particle-drifts.mjs`, which pins the simulator against hand formulas and against an independent SciPy DOP853 integration of the Lorentz force:

- A 100 eV proton in 1 mT must have a gyroradius of 1.44497 m and a gyro-period of 65.6 µs, and must trace a circle twice that wide.
- In crossed fields the drift must be exactly E/B = 10⁵ m s⁻¹, and the same for an electron.
- In a field with a 20 m gradient length the drift must be 5000 m s⁻¹ by theory and 5003.26 m s⁻¹ in SciPy, with the sign following the charge.
- In a curved (toroidal) field with R = 20 m the theory must give 15,000 m s⁻¹ and the Boris orbit must match SciPy's 14,919 m s⁻¹ to better than 0.5 %, with the kinetic energy conserved to 10⁻¹².
- New with this article: the Parker-spiral case, B = 5.115 nT and R = 1 au, must give 17.42 km s⁻¹ for an isotropic 10 MeV proton and 1292.9 km s⁻¹ for a 1000 MeV proton entered with its relativistic effective energy, with the Boris orbit within 0.1 % of theory.

## Check it yourself

On the [[particle-drifts|Particle drift simulator]] page:

1. Set *Drift* to **Curvature and grad-B drift (curved field, as in a torus)** and leave *Particle* on **Proton**.
2. Type **5.115e-6** in *Magnetic field B (mT)*. That is 5.115 nT, the solar wind field at 1 au.
3. Type **1.496e11** in *Radius of curvature R (m)*: one astronomical unit.
4. For an isotropic 10 MeV proton, type **6.6667e6** in *Energy across B, W⊥ (eV)* and **3.3333e6** in *Energy along B, W∥ (eV)*. Leave *Gyrations to follow* on 10 or raise it to 20.
5. The headline reads **Drift 1.743e4 m/s up (+z); theory 1.742e4 m/s (+0.01 %)**: 17.4 km s⁻¹, the paper's 17. *Gyroradius* shows **7.294e7 m** and *Gyro-frequency* **0.07798 Hz** (0.49 rad s⁻¹).
6. Now the relativistic fix for 1000 MeV: type **4.947e8** and **2.473e8** in the two energy boxes (that is γmv²/2 = 742.04 MeV, split 2:1). The drift reads **1.293e6 m/s**, the paper's 1300 km s⁻¹. Typing the plain 1000 MeV instead (**6.6667e8** and **3.3333e8**) gives 1.743e6 m/s, a third too high.
7. Press **Play again** to watch the orbit crawl sideways, and **Download the path (CSV)** if you want the orbit itself.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published drift speed** before building on it: field strength, radius of curvature, energy and pitch angle are all you need, and the tool gives both the formula and an independent orbit.
- **See whether guiding-centre theory applies at all**: the tool refuses a radius of curvature smaller than four gyroradii, which is exactly the regime where published drift formulas start to fail.
- **Teach the reduction-factor idea**: set the drift from theory, then ask students what happens when the field direction changes in less than one gyration (the paper's f_s), using the gyro-period the tool prints.
- **Convert energies properly** with the [[relativistic-kinematics|relativistic kinematics calculator]] before typing them into any non-relativistic code, and carry the uncertainty with the [[uncertainty-calculator]].
- **Scale to other heliospheric distances or to Mercury's orbit** by changing B and R together, and compare with spacecraft field measurements.

## The fine print

- The tool is **non-relativistic**. Above about 20 MeV for protons you must feed it the effective energy γmv²/2, as we did; the article's table shows the size of the error if you do not.
- We treat the Parker spiral at 1 au as a 1/R azimuthal field. The paper's full expression adds a geometric factor of 1.072 there, and the agreement with its rounded "17 km s⁻¹" is at that level of care, not better.
- The isotropic pitch-angle split is our choice. Figure 2 shows the full range it could have been.
- Nothing here tests the paper's turbulence result. The reduction factors come from its own simulations; we only check the theoretical drift they are applied to, and point out which number they multiply.
