"The combination index was below 1, so the two drugs act synergistically" is one of the most common sentences in cancer pharmacology, and one of the easiest to accept without checking. A 2025 paper in *PLOS ONE* packed **ABT-199** (venetoclax, which blocks the survival protein BCL-2) and **vorinostat** (which blocks histone deacetylases) into PLGA nanoparticles and reported a combination index below 1 against two colorectal cancer cell lines. This article uses the [[drug-synergy]] tool's own maths to turn the paper's two single-drug IC50 values into the exact dose that additivity predicts, which is the number any synergy claim has to beat. Fit each drug's own curve first with the [[dose-response|dose-response fitter]], and keep the dosing side of a nanoparticle study honest with the [[nca-pk|NCA PK]] calculator.

## The paper in brief

The authors made PLGA nanoparticles of about 200 to 210 nm loaded with ABT-199, with vorinostat, and with both, encapsulating 73 % and 86 % of each drug, and tested them on the colorectal lines HT-29 and HCT116. Alone, vorinostat halved growth at **1.32 µM** in HT-29 and **2.04 µM** in HCT116; ABT-199 needed **4.04 µM** and **5.49 µM**. Combined, the abstract reports a combination index below 1 at every molar ratio tested, with the strongest effect at a **1:1 molar ratio**, and the dual-loaded nanoparticles were built at 1:2.

A **combination index** (CI) compares the doses you actually used with the doses that would be needed if the drugs simply added up: CI = *d*₁/*D*₁ + *d*₂/*D*₂, where *D* is the dose of each drug alone that gives the same effect. CI below 1 means you needed less than additivity predicts. The whole claim therefore rests on what additivity predicts, and that is a number anyone can work out from the two IC50 values.

The journal's page would not load from our machine, so we have only the abstract. Everything below is arithmetic on the four IC50 values it states.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the additive dose for an equal-molar mixture.** At the 50 % effect level, the dose of each drug alone that halves growth is its IC50, so additivity means vorinostat/1.32 + ABT-199/4.04 = 1 in HT-29. On an equal-molar ray, where both drugs are at half the total dose *D*, that gives *D* = 2 / (1/1.32 + 1/4.04) = **1.99 µM total** (0.995 µM of each). For HCT116 the additive total is **2.97 µM**. Those two numbers are the benchmark: a 1:1 mixture that halves growth at a total dose *below* them is synergistic, above them antagonistic.

**Step 2: what a given CI demands.** The 50 % isobole, the line of all mixtures that halve growth under additivity, runs straight from one IC50 to the other (Figure 1). A combination index of 0.6 would put the measured half-effect mixture on a parallel line 40 % closer to the origin: a total of **1.19 µM** in HT-29 or **1.78 µM** in HCT116 for an equal-molar mix. Figure 2 reads the other way round: given whatever total dose actually halved growth, it shows the CI that follows. A mixture that halves HT-29 growth at 1.0 µM total has CI 0.50; at 1.5 µM, CI 0.75; at 2.5 µM, CI 1.26, which would be mild antagonism even though both drugs are below their own IC50.

**Step 3: what the tool reads off a whole checkerboard.** The paper reports no dose matrix, so we **simulated** two, both using the paper's HT-29 IC50 values, a Hill slope of 1 and a maximum effect of 100 % (**our assumptions**), on a grid of six vorinostat doses (0 to 5.28 µM) by six ABT-199 doses (0 to 16.16 µM):

- A **sham** matrix, in which every well is exactly Loewe-additive. The tool recovers EC50s of 1.320 and 4.040 µM with slopes of 1.000, a CI geometric mean of **1.000** across all 25 combination wells, and a mean Loewe excess of **−0.001** percentage points. That is the sanity check every synergy pipeline should pass.
- A **two-fold synergy** matrix, in which each drug behaves as if twice as potent while the other is present (single-drug rows unchanged). The tool gives a CI geometric mean of **0.500**, all 25 wells synergistic, and a mean excess over Loewe additivity of **11.6** percentage points of inhibition, peaking at **17.1** points at 0.33 µM vorinostat with 2.02 µM ABT-199.

**Step 4: the reference model changes the verdict.** On the exactly additive sham matrix, Loewe is flat, as it must be, but the same wells come out **5.8 percentage points below** Bliss independence and **7.0 points above** the highest single agent. In other words, a perfectly additive combination looks faintly antagonistic under Bliss and clearly synergistic under HSA. Any paper's single number labelled "synergy" is a statement about a model, and a CI value cannot be compared with a Bliss score from another paper.

Nothing here contradicts the paper: without its dose matrix we cannot recompute its CI. What the arithmetic does give is a yardstick. If you want to repeat this experiment, you now know that an equal-molar mixture must halve HT-29 growth below a total of 1.99 µM to be synergistic at all, and that a reported CI near 0.6 implies a half-effect around 1.2 µM total.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/abt199-vorinostat-additivity-benchmark.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `hillInv` (the dose of one drug alone that gives a chosen effect), `loeweExpected` (the Loewe-additive effect of a pair of doses, found by bisection on the isobole equation) and `analyse` (the Hill fits, the Bliss, HSA and Loewe surfaces and the combination index of every well) from `src/lib/sci/synergy.ts`, the maths behind the [[drug-synergy]] tool.

From the paper: the four IC50 values and the 1:1 ratio. **Our assumptions**: a Hill slope of 1 and a maximum effect of 100 % for both drugs, the six-by-six dose grid, and the two-fold potency shift used to build the simulated synergistic matrix. The matrices are **simulated** and are not the paper's measurements, which it does not publish.

## How we test the maths

`npm test` runs `tests/sci.mjs`, which pins the synergy maths against an independent Python implementation (SciPy `curve_fit` Hill fits with several starts, `brentq` on the Loewe surface):

- On the tool's example matrix and 20 random matrices, the Hill parameters must agree with Python to 3 × 10⁻⁵ and every well's excess over each reference model to 3 × 10⁻⁴ percentage points.
- A **sham combination**, a drug paired with itself, must give a combination index of 1 in every well: the standard test that catches a wrongly built Loewe surface.
- The single-drug curves must come from the matrix's zero row and column, as SynergyFinder does, so Bliss and HSA use observed single-drug responses rather than fitted ones.
- New with this article: `tests/sci-tools/drug-synergy.mjs` pins the two matrices above, built from the paper's HT-29 IC50 values: the additive one must give a CI geometric mean of 1.0000822 with all 25 wells additive, and the two-fold one 0.5000565 with all 25 synergistic and a mean Loewe excess of 11.5578 points.

## Check it yourself

On the [[drug-synergy]] page:

1. In *Dose matrix (% inhibition)*, replace the example with our simulated synergistic matrix. The first row is the ABT-199 doses after an empty corner cell, and each later row starts with a vorinostat dose:

   ```
   , 0, 1.01, 2.02, 4.04, 8.08, 16.16
   0, 0, 20, 33.33, 50, 66.67, 80
   0.33, 20, 50, 60, 71.43, 81.82, 89.47
   0.66, 33.33, 60, 66.67, 75, 83.33, 90
   1.32, 50, 71.43, 75, 80, 85.71, 90.91
   2.64, 66.67, 81.82, 83.33, 85.71, 88.89, 92.31
   5.28, 80, 89.47, 90, 90.91, 92.31, 94.12
   ```

2. Put **Vorinostat** in *Drug A name* and **µM** in *Drug A unit*, **ABT-199** in *Drug B name* and **µM** in *Drug B unit*.
3. Leave *Top of single-drug curves* on **Fixed at 100 %**.
4. Under *Single-drug curves* it reports EC50 **1.32 µM** for vorinostat and **4.04 µM** for ABT-199, both with a slope of **1.00**, which are the paper's IC50 values put back out.
5. Under *Synergy scores* the combination index geometric mean is **0.50**, with **25** of 25 wells synergistic, and the mean excess over Loewe additivity is **11.6** percentage points, its largest value **17.1** at 0.33 and 2.02 µM.
6. Switch the *Heatmap* between **Bliss**, **Loewe** and **Observed** and watch the picture change: the mean Bliss excess is **5.8** points, the mean highest-single-agent excess **18.5**.
7. Now make the sham matrix: change every combination well to the additive value (the article's script prints it) and the combination index returns to **1.00** with no synergistic wells.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Work out the additivity benchmark before the experiment**, so the doses you plan can actually show synergy if it is there.
- **Audit a published CI**: put the paper's single-drug IC50 values and the combination dose it used into the tool and see whether its CI follows.
- **Run a sham check** on your own pipeline, a drug against itself, and confirm it returns 1.
- **Report the model you used**, not just the word synergy: the same wells can be antagonistic under Bliss and synergistic under HSA, as the table above shows.
- **Design a checkerboard** around each drug's IC50 with the [[dose-response|dose-response fitter]], then test the matrix here before committing to a large plate run.

## The fine print

- We used only the paper's **abstract**; its full text would not load from our machine on 24 November 2026, so we do not know its dose matrix, its software or its reported CI values beyond "below 1".
- The Hill slope of 1 is **our assumption**. Real slopes above 1 steepen the dose-response curves but do not move the 50 % isobole, which depends only on the IC50 values; they do change the CI at other effect levels.
- The two simulated matrices are ours, not the paper's data.
- A combination index is a statement about doses and effects in cells, not about patients, and nanoparticle formulation changes exposure in ways no cell experiment can show.
- This is general science education, not medical advice.
