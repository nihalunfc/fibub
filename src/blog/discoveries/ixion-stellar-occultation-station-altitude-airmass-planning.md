A **stellar occultation** is the sharpest ruler in the outer Solar System. When a distant body passes in front of a star, the star blinks out, and the length of that blink multiplied by the shadow's speed gives a chord across the body, often to a kilometre or two. The catch is geography: the shadow is only as wide as the body, a few hundred kilometres, and it sweeps across the Earth in minutes. A 2026 paper in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* put eight such events together to measure the trans-Neptunian object **(28978) Ixion**, and its station list is spread from Illinois to Chile to New South Wales. This article uses the [[object-altitude|Target altitude planner]] to recompute, for every one of those eight nights, how high the star stood, how much air each station looked through, how dark the sky was and where the Moon sat. To turn published sexagesimal coordinates into the numbers the planner wants, use the [[sky-coordinates|sky coordinate converter]]; for a whole list of candidate sites at once, the [[batch-airmass|batch airmass planner]].

## The paper in brief

The authors collected eight occultations by Ixion between August 2020 and July 2023, from 37.3 to 39.3 au away, with shadow speeds of 11.6 to 24.7 km s⁻¹. Four of them gave several chords at once. Fitting an ellipse to the chords gives projected semi-axes of **363.4 and 334.0 km**, an equivalent diameter of **696.8 km** and an apparent oblateness of 0.081. Combined with a phase-curve fit (H_V = 3.845), that makes Ixion's geometric albedo **0.106**: a dark surface, and a body comfortably in the size range where an icy object is expected to be round under its own gravity. No atmosphere was detected.

The paper is also a plain record of how hard this work is. The best-covered event, on 28 April 2021, had **16 stations and only 6 positive chords**: 5 saw nothing because the shadow missed them, and 5 produced nothing at all through weather or technical trouble. The 13 October 2020 event, on a star bright at G = 10.3, gave the finest sampling of all, about half a kilometre per data point.

None of the chord fitting is something a browser tool should pretend to redo. What a browser tool can do is reconstruct the observing conditions of all eight nights, which is exactly what you need when you plan the next campaign.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below comes from the eight star positions in the paper's Table 2 and the eight mid-times in its Table 3, with nothing else: no fitting, no data from the paper's light curves.

**Step 1: where on Earth the star was overhead.** Each event has a point on the Earth where the star sat exactly at the zenith; its latitude is the star's declination, about −30°, and its longitude follows from sidereal time. Those longitudes alone sort the campaign: −84° and −144° for the two 2020 events (the Americas and the Pacific), −59° and −68° in 2021 (South America), +129° and +109° for the two single-chord events (Australia), −12° on 30 June 2022 (the Atlantic, so South America at a good angle) and −126° in July 2023.

**Step 2: the altitude at the mid-time, site by site.** Figure 2 shows the star's altitude at the moment of each event from one site per continent, with points left out where it was below the horizon. The pattern is the paper's station list. Australia is the only continent in the list for 2 June 2022 (56.6° up at a site in New South Wales) and 17 August 2021 (72.9°, almost overhead), and those two events gave exactly one chord each, both Australian. South America is high for 28 April 2021 and 30 June 2022, and those campaigns were Brazilian, Argentinian, Uruguayan and Chilean.

**Step 3: one night in detail.** For the 16-station event of 28 April 2021 the planner's night track (Figure 1) puts the star almost straight overhead from Chile: it crossed the meridian at **08:24 UT at 89.98°**, 49 minutes after the occultation, and the lowest airmass of the night was **1.00**. From Pico dos Dias in Brazil the meridian came at 06:43 UT at 82.4°. From Illinois the same star never climbed past **18.1°**, so it never reached airmass 2 at all: at the mid-time it was 13.2° up, airmass 4.3.

**Step 4: twilight and Moon, which the altitude alone hides.** Two of the American campaigns ran in twilight rather than night. On 17 August 2020 the Sun was only **9.2° below the horizon** at the Illinois station when the star blinked, and 16.5° below in New York; on 13 October 2020 it was 13.3° below at the Arizona site, with the star at 18.8° and airmass 3.1. Both events still produced clean chords, which says a lot about modern cameras. The 28 April 2021 event had a different problem: the Moon was **98 % lit and 62° above the horizon** from Chile. Sixteen stations, a near-full Moon overhead and a star at G = 14.8 is a fair explanation for five stations reporting nothing usable.

**Step 5: a number from the paper we can check with plain arithmetic.** For the 13 October 2020 star the paper adopts an angular diameter of 0.670 mas and quotes a stellar radius of 128 ± 10 solar radii. Its Gaia parallax is 0.565 mas, so the distance is 1/0.565 ≈ 1770 pc and the radius is 0.335 mas × 1770 pc = 0.593 au = **127 solar radii**. That is their number, and it matters because the star's size smooths the occultation profile: 0.67 mas at 39 au is about 19 km projected at Ixion, which is why that event's half-kilometre sampling still cannot resolve a sharp limb.

The two rows in brackets are the honest limit of this exercise. On 17 August 2020 and 20 May 2021 the star was far higher from Chile than from the United States, and the campaign was American anyway, because **the shadow has to pass over the station as well**. Altitude tells you whether a site *can* observe; the predicted shadow track tells you whether it is *worth* observing. A planner answers the first question only, and that is still the question that rules out most of the Earth before anyone looks at a track.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/ixion-stellar-occultation-station-altitude-airmass-planning.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `parseTargets`, `altAz`, `airmass` and `plan` from `src/lib/sci/airmass.ts`, fibub's shared observing maths: the same astronomy-engine positions (precession, nutation, annual aberration and standard refraction) and the same Kasten-Young airmass that the [[object-altitude|Target altitude planner]] draws its night charts from, and that power the [[batch-airmass|batch airmass planner]]. The Sun's and Moon's altitudes and the Moon's illuminated fraction come from astronomy-engine directly, as they do in the tool.

**From the paper:** the eight star positions, the eight mid-times, the station regions, the chord counts, the shadow speeds and the stellar angular diameter and parallax. **Our inputs:** the site coordinates. The paper's own station coordinates are in its appendix table; we used standard published positions for Cerro Tololo, Cerro Burek, Pico dos Dias, Happy Jack in Arizona, Naperville, Lake Placid and Yass, rounded to 0.001°, as stand-ins for whole regions. An error of a few kilometres in a site position changes an altitude by far less than the 0.1° we quote.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs the per-tool checks in `tests/sci-tools/`, which pin this observing maths against **astropy 8.0**:

- `batch-airmass.mjs`: altitudes and azimuths for three targets at three times must match astropy's `AltAz` within 2 arcseconds, and refracted altitudes within 15 arcseconds above 9°.
- The same file pins all four airmass formulas against NumPy at 10°, 30° and 60° altitude, and the inverse (the altitude where the airmass is 2) to 10⁻⁹.
- A real campaign check: NGC 1566 from Paranal on 23 August 2025 must transit at 10:50:19 UT, 59.7676° up, airmass 1.1567.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/object-altitude.mjs`: the 28 April 2021 Ixion star must stand 79.51° up from Cerro Tololo and 13.17° up from Naperville at 07:35:37 UT, with Kasten-Young airmasses of 1.0166 and 4.314, and must transit at 08:24:02 UT at 89.98°; the 2 June 2022 star must be 25° or more below the horizon from Chile, Brazil and the USA and 56.58° up from New South Wales.

## Check it yourself

On the [[object-altitude|Target altitude planner]] page, for the 16-station night:

1. In the place row, open the coordinate boxes and type *Latitude* **-30.169**, *Longitude* **-70.806**, *Height (m)* **2200** (Cerro Tololo). Set *Times in* to **UTC** so the numbers match the paper's.
2. Set *Night of* to **2021-04-27**. The planner's night runs from local noon to local noon, so that is the night the 28 April 07:35 UT event falls in.
3. In *Targets*, type `Ixion star, 18 05 34.6814, -30 09 09.703` and press **Add**. That is the paper's occulted star.
4. Leave *Lowest useful height* on **30° (airmass 2)**. The chart shows the star rising out of twilight and passing through the top of the frame.
5. The *Summary* row gives **Meridian (pier flip) 08:24** and a **Lowest airmass of 1.00**. Open **Hour by hour: height, airmass and parallactic angle**: 07:00 reads about 71.8° and airmass 1.05, 08:00 about 84.8° and 1.00.
6. Now change *Latitude* to **41.785**, *Longitude* to **-88.147** and *Height* to **215** (Naperville, Illinois). The same star never crosses the dashed 30° line; the meridian row reads **18°** and the hour-by-hour table gives about 13° and airmass 4.3 around 07:35.
7. For the Australian event, set the place to *Latitude* **-34.84**, *Longitude* **148.91**, the night to **2022-06-02**, and add `Ixion star 2, 18 09 36.9353, -30 43 37.051`: it reaches 56.6° at 18:10 UT (airmass 1.20).

## What researchers can use it for

- **Decide which sites to invite to a campaign.** Put the predicted shadow track beside the altitude chart: a station is only useful when the star is both on the track and high enough, and the planner settles the second half in seconds.
- **Record the real conditions of an observation** (altitude, airmass, Sun depth, Moon distance and illumination) in an observing log, so that a marginal light curve can be judged later. The [[sky-tonight|sky tonight]] page gives the twilight and Moon side of it for a whole night.
- **Check a published observing log** for consistency, the way we did here, before building on its photometry: an airmass of 3 in nautical twilight is a different measurement from airmass 1 in full darkness.
- **Plan the slit or the dispersion direction** with the hour-by-hour parallactic angle, which the planner lists beside the airmass.
- **Teach why occultation campaigns look the way they do**: one declination, eight dates, and the Earth's rotation decides which continent gets the event.

## The fine print

- The site coordinates are ours, not the paper's; they stand in for regions. The altitudes would shift by a fraction of a degree for a neighbouring site.
- Altitudes include standard refraction at 1010 hPa and 10 °C, which lifts a 15° star by about 3 arcminutes and a 3° star by nearly 15. Airmass at 10° and below is sensitive to the formula; we use Kasten-Young throughout.
- We check visibility, not the shadow track. Two of the eight events were observed from stations where the star was low while it was high somewhere else, because the shadow decides.
- The stellar radius check is plain arithmetic from the paper's own angular diameter and parallax, not an independent measurement.
- Nothing here tests the paper's size, shape or albedo. Those come from fitting its chords, which needs the light curves themselves.
