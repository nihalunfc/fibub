Every fixed-time traffic signal is timed on one number per approach: the **saturation flow**, the rate at which a queue discharges once it is moving. Get that number wrong and every other number — the cycle length, the green split, the capacity, the queue — is wrong with it. A 2024 paper in *PLOS ONE* measured saturation flow on eight approaches where most of the traffic is motorcycles and lanes are not used as lanes, and found the standard width-based formula out by as much as 21 %. This article takes the paper's own fitted equations and pushes them through the [[signal-timing]] tool to see what that error actually costs. For the traffic theory behind the discharge rate, and for the geometry of the approaches themselves, see [[traffic-flow]] and [[road-curve-design]].

## The paper in brief

The Indonesian Highway Capacity Manual gives a **base saturation flow** of 600 passenger car units per hour for every metre of effective approach width (S₀ = 600·We). It was calibrated in the 1990s on traffic that behaved like lanes of cars. The authors recorded 480 signal cycles across eight approaches, 40 cycles each, in traffic that was about **74 % motorcycles**, and kept 360 cycles to calibrate against. Motorcycles fill gaps rather than queue in lanes, so the discharge rate does not scale with width the way the manual assumes.

They fitted two shapes against the measured flows — a one-term line through the origin and a two-term line with an intercept — twice over, once with the manual's own passenger-car equivalents and once with equivalents they re-estimated themselves (their rickshaw value differs from the manual's by up to 56 %). The results:

| Model | With the manual's equivalents | Error | With the paper's own equivalents | Error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Manual, S₀ = 600·We | — | 257 PCU/h (21 %) | — | 127 PCU/h (12 %) |
| One term | S₀ = 493·We | 77 PCU/h (8 %) | S₀ = 622·We | 117 PCU/h (10 %) |
| Two terms | S₀ = 783 + 368·We | **42 PCU/h (4 %)** | S₀ = 1020 + 459·We | 64 PCU/h (4 %) |

The two-term shape wins both times. Its intercept is the point: a narrow approach still discharges a useful flow, and a wide one does not gain as much as a straight line through the origin predicts. The paper also reports a **start-lag of 4 to 5 s** and an **end-lag of about 3 s**, which is where our lost time below comes from.

## Checked with the tools

Two things can be checked here. The first is pure recomputation: drawing the published equations out across the widths the paper actually observed. The second is the consequence, which is what the paper's title promises and what [[signal-timing]] is for.

**Step 1: the models disagree most where streets really are.** Figure 1 recomputes all four published equations from 2.8 to 8 m, the observed range. The manual's line crosses the best-fitting two-term line at **3.38 m**: below that it under-predicts, above it over-predicts, and at 8 m it is **28.8 % too high** (4800 against 3727 PCU/h). With the paper's own equivalents the crossing moves out to **7.23 m**, so the manual's formula is too low over almost the whole range instead.

**Step 2: time a real signal on each number.** We built a two-phase, four-approach fixed-time signal: north and south together, east and west together. The effective widths (7.0, 6.0, 4.0 and 3.5 m) sit inside the paper's range and the flows (1500, 1100, 700 and 550 PCU/h) are **our assumption** — the paper publishes models, not per-approach counts. Lost time is **5.5 s per phase**, worked out from the paper's own lags: a 4.5 s start-lag plus the 1 s of a 4 s intergreen that the end-lag of about 3 s leaves unused. Only the saturation flows change between the three cases.

| What the tool gives | Manual, 600·We | Paper, 493·We | Paper, 783 + 368·We |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saturation flow, north (PCU/h) | 4200 | 3451 | 3359 |
| Critical flow ratio sum Y | 0.649 | 0.790 | 0.757 |
| Webster optimum cycle C₀ (s) | 61.2 | 102.2 | 88.5 |
| Cycle used, rounded up to 5 s | 65 | 105 | 90 |
| Effective green, phase 1 / phase 2 (s) | 29.7 / 24.3 | 51.7 / 42.3 | 46.6 / 32.4 |
| Capacity, north (PCU/h) | 1921 | 1701 | 1739 |
| Degree of saturation X | 0.781 | 0.882 | 0.862 |
| Average delay (s per vehicle) | 17.7 | 30.0 | 24.6 |

A 21 % error in one input moves the optimum cycle from **61 s to 89 s** and the average delay from **17.7 to 24.6 s per vehicle**. Figure 2 shows the whole delay-against-cycle curve for each case, with the three optimum cycles marked.

**Step 3: the dangerous part is not the delay.** Suppose an engineer trusts the manual, sets the 65 s cycle and the 29.7 / 24.3 s greens it gives, and the street then discharges at the paper's measured rate. Re-running that fixed timing with the two-term saturation flows, the tool reports an average delay of **24.1 s per vehicle** — almost exactly the 24.6 s of the properly timed 90 s cycle, and slightly better. Webster's delay curve is famously flat near its minimum, so a cycle that is 25 s short costs almost nothing in average delay.

What it does cost is headroom. The same run puts the **degree of saturation at 0.911** on the critical approaches, not the 0.781 the manual's numbers promised, and the north approach's capacity at **1646 PCU/h instead of 1921** — 275 PCU/h of capacity that was never there. The longest queue on that approach grows from 23 to 25 vehicles. A signal believed to be at 78 % of capacity is really at 91 %, which is the difference between absorbing a bad ten minutes and spilling back into the junction upstream. That, not the average delay, is what a mis-calibrated saturation flow buys you.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/saturation-flow-effective-width-webster-cycle-delay.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `webster` and `delayCurve` from `src/lib/sci/eng-webster.ts`, the maths behind the [[signal-timing]] tool: flow ratios y = q/s, the critical ratio in each phase, total lost time L, Webster's optimum cycle C₀ = (1.5L + 5)/(1 − Y), greens in proportion to the critical ratios, capacity s·g/C, degree of saturation and the three-term delay formula.

From the paper: the six saturation flow equations, the error statistics, the 2.8 to 8 m width range and the start and end lags. **Our assumptions:** the four approach widths, the four flows, the two-phase staging, the 4 s intergreen and the 4.5 s start-lag taken as the midpoint of the paper's 4 to 5 s. Nothing here is the paper's measured data, which is published as models and figures.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/signal-timing.mjs` runs inside `tests/sci.mjs` on every change to fibub, and pins Webster's method to values worked out independently:

- A hand-worked made-up example with Y = 1/3 + 9/32 and L = 8 s must give C₀ = 17/(1 − Y) = **44.1081081081081 s**, and the minimum workable cycle L/(1 − Y).
- The four approaches' delays must match an independent NumPy evaluation of Webster's formula to 1 part in 10⁸: **14.74857114, 12.02033521, 18.01894144 and 15.44979723 s**, averaging 14.947565552932014 s.
- Scanning delay over 200 cycle lengths, the delay at C₀ must be within **3 %** of the true minimum — the flatness this article leans on in Step 3.
- New with this article: the two-term case above must give Y = 0.7569827…, C₀ = 88.4710905… s, a 90 s cycle, X = 0.8623854… and an average delay of 24.6071108… s per vehicle, and the mis-timed 65 s run must give X = 0.9111829… with 24.1374015… s of delay.

## Check it yourself

On the [[signal-timing]] tool page:

1. In *Approach, phase, flow, saturation flow*, replace the example with four tab- or comma-separated rows: **North, 1, 1500, 3359**; **South, 1, 1100, 2991**; **East, 2, 700, 2255**; **West, 2, 550, 2071**. (Those saturation flows are 783 + 368·We at We = 7.0, 6.0, 4.0 and 3.5 m.)
2. Set *Lost time per phase (s)* to **5.5** and leave *Intergreen per phase, yellow + all-red (s)* at **4**.
3. Leave *Round the cycle up to* on **5 s** and the two cycle limits empty.
4. The *Timing* card reads a cycle of **90 s**, Y of **0.757**, total lost time **11 s** and an average delay near **24.6 s per vehicle**; the *Phases* table gives effective greens of **46.6 s** and **32.4 s** (displayed 48.1 s and 33.9 s), and the *Approaches* table gives capacity **1739 PCU/h** and degree of saturation **0.862** for North.
5. Now change the four saturation flows to the manual's 600·We values — **4200, 3600, 2400, 2100** — and watch the cycle fall to **65 s** and the delay to **17.7 s per vehicle**.
6. Put the measured saturation flows back, then type **65** into *Use this cycle instead (s)*. The delay barely moves (**24.1 s**) but the degree of saturation jumps to **0.911** and North's capacity drops to **1646 PCU/h**. That is Step 3 on the real tool.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Test a published saturation flow model before adopting it**: put your own approach widths and counts in, swap the saturation flows and read the cycle, capacity and delay side by side, as in Step 2.
- **Report the degree of saturation, not only the delay.** The tool shows both, and this paper's case is a clean example of delay hiding a capacity problem.
- **Turn measured start and end lags into lost time** instead of defaulting to 4 s, then see how much the optimum cycle moves.
- **Size a queue bay** from the longest-queue column, which uses flow × effective red ÷ (1 − y), and sanity-check it against the upstream link with [[traffic-flow]].
- **Teach the flatness of Webster's optimum**: the delay-against-cycle chart makes it obvious why a signal can be badly mis-timed and still look acceptable on average delay.

## The fine print

- The intersection in Step 2 is **made up**. The paper gives saturation flow models, not the flows, widths or staging of a particular junction, so only the saturation flows in our runs come from it.
- Webster's method suits isolated fixed-time signals below saturation. It knows nothing about motorcycle filtering, platoon arrivals from a nearby signal, pedestrian stages or right-turn conflicts.
- Passenger car units are not interchangeable between studies. The paper's point is partly that its own equivalents differ from the manual's, so a saturation flow in PCU/h is only meaningful with the equivalent set that produced it.
- The two-term model's intercept of 783 PCU/h has no physical meaning below the observed widths; do not extrapolate it to a 1 m approach.
- For study and planning. A real signal needs the local design manual and a qualified engineer.
