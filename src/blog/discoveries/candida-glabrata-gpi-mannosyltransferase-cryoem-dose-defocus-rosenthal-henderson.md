*Candida glabrata* is one of the yeasts that cause serious bloodstream infections, and it anchors many of its surface proteins to the cell with a sugar-and-lipid tether called a GPI anchor. The enzyme that adds the first mannose to that tether, GPI mannosyltransferase I, is a membrane complex with no counterpart in humans, which makes it an antifungal target worth knowing the shape of. A 2025 paper in the *Journal of Fungi* solved it by cryo-electron microscopy at 3.48 Å. Like most cryo-EM papers it prints every setting of the microscope, and every one of those settings is a sum the [[cryo-em-calculator]] does: pixel size, dose, contrast transfer function, box size and particles against resolution. Once you have the model, the [[pdb-viewer]] opens it, the [[ramachandran-plot]] checks its backbone and the [[protein-calculator]] gives the mass and extinction coefficient of each chain.

## The paper in brief

The authors purified the complex, froze it on grids and collected 5,008 movies on a Titan Krios at **300 kV** with a Gatan K3 camera in super-resolution mode, at a physical pixel size of **1.088 Å**. Each movie took **3.2 s** and **32 frames** for a total dose of **56 electrons per Å²**, at defocus values from **−1.7 to −2.1 µm**. Particle picking with Topaz (trained on 43,298 hand-picked particles) extracted **1,141,564** particles; after two-dimensional and three-dimensional classification, **80,856** survived into a map with a global resolution of **3.48 Å** at the gold-standard FSC = 0.143 threshold.

What they see is a complex of the catalytic subunit with a partner subunit that stabilises it, with the active site facing the inside of the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, which is where the mannose donor sits. The point of this article is not the biology but the settings, because they are exactly the numbers anyone planning their own collection has to choose.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the settings stated in the paper**, with two exceptions we flag: the spherical aberration Cs (2.7 mm) and the amplitude contrast (0.1) are **our assumptions**, because the paper does not state them. Both are the usual values for a Titan Krios and for CTF fitting.

**Step 1: sampling.** A camera in super-resolution mode records each physical pixel as 2 × 2, so the movie is written at **0.544 Å per pixel**; processing at the stated 1.088 Å means binning by two again. The Nyquist limit, the finest detail two pixels can carry, is then **2.176 Å**, and the finished map at 3.48 Å sits at **1.60 × Nyquist**. Pixel size was never the limit here, which is the right place to be: a map that creeps up on its own Nyquist limit is a map that needed a finer pixel.

**Step 2: dose.** The tool works from a dose rate per camera pixel per second, so we convert: 56 e⁻/Å² in 3.2 s at 1.088 Å per pixel is **20.72 e⁻ per camera pixel per second**, or **17.5 e⁻/Å²/s** at the specimen. Out of that:

| Quantity | Tool result |
|---|---|
| Total dose | **56 e⁻/Å²** (the paper's figure, recovered) |
| Dose per frame | **1.75 e⁻/Å²** |
| Frame time | **100 ms** |
| Electrons per camera pixel per frame | **2.07** |

1.75 e⁻/Å² per frame is a little above the roughly 1 e⁻/Å² per frame often used for motion correction, and about 2 electrons per camera pixel per frame is comfortably inside a counting detector's linear range. If they had wanted 1 e⁻/Å² per frame instead, the tool's *Target dose per frame* box says it would have taken 56 frames.

**Step 3: the contrast transfer function.** At 300 kV the electron wavelength is **0.019687 Å** (0.022391 Å if you forget the relativistic correction, a 12 % error). With Cs 2.7 mm and 0.1 amplitude contrast:

| Defocus | First CTF zero | Zeros before 3.48 Å | Zeros up to Nyquist |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.7 µm | **18.58 Å** | 26 | 66 |
| 2.1 µm | **20.66 Å** | 33 | 82 |

That range is a deliberate compromise, and Figure 2 shows the trade. More defocus gives more contrast at low resolution, which makes particles easier to see and to align, but it packs more and more CTF oscillations in before the target resolution: 26 of them at 1.7 µm, 33 at 2.1 µm, and 48 if they had gone to 3 µm. Every one of those has to be fitted correctly or the high-resolution signal is added with the wrong sign.

**Step 4: how big the box has to be.** Defocus also smears each detail sideways in the image, by up to λΔf/d + Csλ³/d³. At 3.48 Å that is **101 Å** at 1.7 µm defocus and **124 Å** at 2.1 µm, on each side of the particle. For a particle 130 Å across (**our assumption**, as the paper does not state a diameter) the box has to hold 130 + 2 × 124 = **377 Å**, which rounds up to a **360-pixel** box at 1.088 Å per pixel. The familiar rule of thumb, 1.5 to 2 times the diameter, would have given 180 to 240 pixels and quietly thrown away part of the high-resolution signal. This is the single most common way to leave resolution on the table, and it is one division away from being obvious.

**Step 5: particles against resolution.** Rosenthal and Henderson's relation says 1/d² rises with ln N, with the slope set by a B-factor: the better the data, the lower the B-factor and the fewer particles each extra fraction of an Å costs. The paper gives one point on that line, 80,856 particles at 3.48 Å. Drawing the line through it for three plausible B-factors:

Read that against the paper's own numbers: 1,141,564 particles were extracted and only 80,856, about **7 %**, reached the final map. If even a third of the discarded particles had been usable, the same line puts 3.0 Å within reach at any of these B-factors. That is not a criticism of the paper, which reports a solid structure; it is the arithmetic that tells a group where the next map's resolution will come from, and here it says classification, not more microscope time.

## How we made the graphs

Every number and all three charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/candida-glabrata-gpi-mannosyltransferase-cryoem-dose-defocus-rosenthal-henderson.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `sampling`, `dose`, `wavelength`, `wavelengthClassical`, `ctfZeros`, `ctfCurve`, `delocalisation`, `boxAdvice`, `rhResolution` and `rhParticles` from `src/lib/sci/cryoem.ts`, the maths behind the [[cryo-em-calculator]].

From the paper: 300 kV, the 1.088 Å physical pixel, super-resolution mode, 56 e⁻/Å², 32 frames, 3.2 s, the 1.7 to 2.1 µm defocus range, 80,856 particles, 1,141,564 particles and 3.48 Å. **Our assumptions**: Cs 2.7 mm, amplitude contrast 0.1, a particle diameter of 130 Å for Step 4, and the three B-factors in Step 5. The Rosenthal-Henderson lines are drawn through the paper's single point; the paper does not report a B-factor, so none of the three lines is its own.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/cryo-em-calculator.mjs` on every change to fibub, against an independent Python and NumPy script:

- The relativistic wavelength must be 0.019687489006848795 Å at 300 kV and 0.025079340450548007 Å at 200 kV, from the electron's total energy and momentum.
- At 300 kV, Cs 2.7 mm, 0.1 amplitude contrast and 1.5 µm defocus there must be exactly **92** CTF zeros up to 0.6 per Å, the first at 0.05728904672287838 per Å (17.455 Å), found in NumPy by scanning four million points and bisecting every sign change. With Cs set to zero, the first zero must match the closed-form √((π − asin A)/(πλΔf)).
- A B-factor of 100 Å² with 20,000 particles at 4 Å must need 227,303.886 particles for 3 Å, and feeding two points of that line back through `rhB` must return 100 Å².
- New with this article: the paper's own settings, pinned to a processing pixel of 1.088 Å with Nyquist 2.176 Å, a total dose of 56 e⁻/Å² at 1.75 per frame, first CTF zeros of 18.5850 and 20.6592 Å at 1.7 and 2.1 µm, and 336,818 particles for 3.0 Å from 80,856 at 3.48 Å with B = 100 Å².

## Check it yourself

On the [[cryo-em-calculator]] page, from the top:

1. In *Camera and pixel size*, set *Pixel size from* to **A calibrated pixel size**, type **1.088** in *Calibrated pixel (Å, one camera pixel)*, tick **Super-resolution** and set *Binning* to **2**. The headline reads **1.088 Å per pixel, Nyquist 2.176 Å**, with *Super-resolution Nyquist* **1.088 Å**.
2. In *Dose*, type **20.72** in *Dose rate (e⁻ per camera pixel per second)*, **3.2** in *Exposure time (s)* and **32** in *Number of frames*. You get *Dose rate at the sample* **17.5 e⁻/Å²/s**, *Total dose* **56 e⁻/Å²** and *Dose per frame* **1.75 e⁻/Å²** (100 ms per frame, 2.07 e⁻ per camera pixel).
3. Put **1** in *Target dose per frame (e⁻/Å², optional)*: it answers **56** frames.
4. In *Microscope and CTF*, leave *Voltage* on **300 kV**, type **2.7** in *Spherical aberration Cs (mm)*, **0.1** in *Amplitude contrast* and **2.1** in *Defocus (µm, underfocus +)*. You get *Electron wavelength* **0.01969 Å**, *First CTF zero* **20.66 Å** and **82** *CTF zeros up to Nyquist*. Change the defocus to **1.7** and the first zero moves to **18.58 Å** with **66** zeros.
5. In *Box size*, type **130** in *Particle diameter (Å)* and **3.48** in *Target resolution (Å)*. At 2.1 µm defocus it asks for a **360 px** box (391.7 Å), notes the signal spread of up to **123.7 Å** on each side, and shows that the 1.5 to 2 × rule would have given only **180 to 240 px**.
6. In *Particles and resolution*, type **100** in *B-factor (Å²)*, **80856** in *Known point: particles* and **3.48** in *Known point: resolution (Å)*. It reads *With twice the particles* **3.22 Å**, *With ten times the particles* **2.788 Å** and, because 3.48 is still in the target box, *Particles for 3.48 Å* — change *Target resolution* above to **3** and it says **337,000** particles.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a collection**: pick the pixel size from the resolution you want (aim for 1.5 to 2 × Nyquist), then the dose and frame count, then the defocus range, in that order.
- **Choose a box size properly**, from the delocalisation at your target resolution rather than from the 1.5 × rule, which is the cheapest resolution anyone ever found.
- **Decide whether to collect more data or re-classify**: the Rosenthal-Henderson line turns "we need 0.5 Å more" into a particle count, which you can compare with the particles you already threw away.
- **Sanity-check a methods section** you are reviewing: dose per frame, Nyquist against the claimed resolution and the number of CTF rings before it are three quick tests of internal consistency.
- **Teach the CTF** with the real settings of a real paper, including how much the first zero moves across a defocus range.

## The fine print

- Cs and amplitude contrast are **our assumptions**; the CTF zeros and delocalisation shift a little with other values.
- The particle diameter in Step 4 is ours. The paper reports no diameter, so treat the 360-pixel box as an illustration of the method, not as advice about this complex.
- The Rosenthal-Henderson relation is a rough guide. Real datasets flatten out when something other than particle number limits them: sample flexibility, ice thickness, or the microscope.
- A B-factor cannot be read from one point. Ours are three plausible values, not measurements, and the paper reports none.
- Resolution at FSC 0.143 is one convention among several. Compare 3.48 Å only with other numbers quoted at the same threshold.
