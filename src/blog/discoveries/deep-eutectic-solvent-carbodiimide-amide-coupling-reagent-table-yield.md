The amide bond holds proteins together and sits in a large share of medicines, so chemists make it all the time, usually in solvents that are toxic or hard to dispose of. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Chemistry* shows it can be made in a **deep eutectic solvent**, a low-cost liquid made by mixing two solids (here choline chloride and urea, known as "reline"), using a newer carbodiimide as the coupling agent. Every synthesis starts with a reagent table that says what to weigh and what to expect, which is what the [[reaction-equivalents]] tool builds. Molar masses come from the [[molar-mass-calculator]], and the overall equation can be balanced with the [[equation-balancer]].

## The paper in brief

A **carbodiimide** is a reagent that activates a carboxylic acid so that an amine can attach to it, forming the amide. It is used with an additive, here **OxymaPure**, that keeps the activated acid from decomposing. The authors tested three carbodiimides (QCD, TBDC·TsOH and TBDC·HCl) in three deep eutectic solvents (reline, ethaline and glyceline) on a model reaction of phenylacetic acid with 2-phenylethylamine. All runs used 1 mmol of each reagent in 2.5 g of solvent. Reline gave the best isolated yields: **90 %** with TBDC·TsOH, 78 % with QCD and 68 % with TBDC·HCl, within 30 minutes at 60 °C. Dropping the base DIPEA from the 90 % run still gave **88 %**, which makes the process simpler. They then widened the scope to dipeptides and long-chain fatty acid amides, with yields of 79 to 97 % (the scope yields are reported in a figure we could not read).

## Checked with the tools

The tool takes each reagent's formula, equivalents, and the scale, and returns the mass to weigh. For the model reaction at 1 mmol (the paper's scale), with the molar masses worked out from the formulas:

| Reagent | Formula | Molar mass (g/mol) | Equivalents | To weigh at 1 mmol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phenylacetic acid | C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>2</sub> | 136.15 | 1 | 136.15 mg |
| OxymaPure | C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>6</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> | 142.11 | 1 | 142.11 mg |
| 2-Phenylethylamine | C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>11</sub>N | 121.18 | 1 | 121.18 mg |
| DIPEA (base screens only) | C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>19</sub>N | 129.25 | 2.5 | 323.12 mg (about 435 µL, at 0.742 g/mL) |
| Reline | choline chloride + 2 urea | 259.74 per unit | solvent | 2.5 g (about 9.6 mmol of choline chloride) |

The amide's formula is C<sub>16</sub>H<sub>17</sub>NO, 239.32 g/mol, so 1 mmol of the limiting reagent has a **theoretical yield of 239.3 mg**. The paper states yields as percentages only, so the tool turns each into a mass:

| Reported isolated yield | Mass of amide at 1 mmol |
|---|---|
| 90 % (TBDC·TsOH, with base) | 215.4 mg |
| 88 % (TBDC·TsOH, base-free, the optimum) | 210.6 mg |
| 78 % (QCD) | 186.7 mg |
| 68 % (TBDC·HCl) | 162.7 mg |

Going the other way, an isolated 210 mg of amide at 1 mmol is **87.8 %**, so the tool's percentage and the paper's 88 % agree. Scaling is linear: to isolate 1 g of the amide at 88 % you need about **4.75 mmol** of acid, which is 647 mg of phenylacetic acid, 675 mg of OxymaPure and 575 mg of amine (the tool's "Set by" menu scales the whole table from a mass of any one reagent: 500 mg of the acid is 3.67 mmol). Another number that falls out is the amount of solvent: 2.5 g of reline for 210.6 mg of amide is **11.9 g of solvent per gram of product**. That is a lot, which is the usual trade-off of a green solvent that must be recovered or reused.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/deep-eutectic-solvent-carbodiimide-amide-coupling-reagent-table-yield.mjs` imports `table`, `yieldOf` and `scaleFrom` from `src/lib/sci/equivalents.ts` (the Reaction equivalents tool) and `parseFormula` and `molarMass` from `src/lib/sci/chem.ts`. The quantities (1 mmol of each reagent, 2.5 mmol of DIPEA, 2.5 g of solvent) and the four yields are from the paper's text. The formulas and the product's formula are standard chemistry and our own working-out; the carbodiimide is omitted because its formula was not in the text we could read. Figure 1 multiplies the theoretical yield by each percentage over a scale from 0 to 10 mmol; Figure 2 is the mass column of the tool's table at each scale.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/reaction-equivalents.mjs` runs with `node tests/sci.mjs` on every change. It checks the table's masses and the volume from the density, the purity correction, scaling by mass or volume, and the percent yield. New with this article it pins the amide case: phenylacetic acid, OxymaPure and 2-phenylethylamine at 1 mmol weigh 136.15, 142.11 and 121.18 mg, the product has a molar mass of 239.32 g/mol, and 88 % of the theoretical yield is 210.6 mg, which the tool turns back into 87.8 % when given 210 mg.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[reaction-equivalents]] tool and press **Start empty**, then **Add a reagent** twice more.
2. Fill the three rows: **Formula** C8H8O2 with **Equivalents** 1; C5H6N2O3 with 1; and C8H11N with 1. The molar masses fill in as 136.15, 142.11 and 121.18.
3. Under **Scale**, leave **Set by** as the first reagent and type 1 in **Amount** with the unit mmol: the table shows 136.15, 142.11 and 121.18 mg.
4. Under **Yield**, type C16H17NO as the **Product formula or molar mass**, 1 in **Product per limiting reagent**. The theoretical yield reads about 239 mg.
5. Type 210 in **You isolated (mg)**: the tool reports 87.8 %.

## What researchers can use it for

- Writing a reagent table for a new coupling in seconds, including the base and the additive, and rescaling it when you change the batch size.
- Converting a paper's percentage yields into the grams you should actually see on the balance, to catch an over-optimistic or impossible figure.
- Comparing the mass of reagents used per gram of product between a solvent-based and a solvent-free method, as a first step towards the [[green-chemistry-metrics]].
- Teaching limiting reagents and equivalents with a real reaction that students could run.
- Checking a published experimental section: if the stated masses and mmol do not agree with the formula, the table will show it.

## The fine print

This recomputes reagent masses and yield conversions from numbers stated in the paper; it does not repeat the chemistry or test the paper's claims about the carbodiimides, the effect of the solvent, or the scope. We could not read the product masses or the per-compound yields from the paper's figure, the carbodiimide's formula was not in the text, and the product's formula is our own working-out of the model reaction. Real reactions need the paper's procedure, a safety review and proper laboratory training: do not use these numbers as a recipe.
