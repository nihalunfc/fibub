A thin-layer plate is the cheapest separation in chemistry, and the number it gives, the **Rf**, is just one distance divided by another. That makes it easy to compute and easy to mis-record. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Nutrition* optimised an extraction from cacao beans and then standardised the extract by **high-performance thin-layer chromatography** (HPTLC), quantifying two marker flavanols, (+)-catechin and (−)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), from their bands on a silica plate. This article takes the paper's own numbers and reruns them through the [[tlc-rf-calculator]]: the migration distances behind each Rf, the separation between the two bands, and what those Rf values would cost on a flash column. For the steps either side, the [[chromatography-calculator]] handles the HPLC comparison and the [[calibration-curve]] turns peak areas into concentrations.

## The paper in brief

The authors used a quality-by-design approach, a planned set of experiments rather than one-factor-at-a-time, to pick the extraction solvent and conditions for *Theobroma cacao* beans, and then built an HPTLC method to measure the two markers in the chosen extract. Plates were silica gel 60 F254, 20 × 10 cm. Samples went on as 8 mm bands with 4 mm gaps, 15 tracks to a plate, and the plate was developed upward over **70 mm** in a twin-trough chamber saturated for 15 minutes with ethyl acetate : toluene : formic acid, 9:9:2 by volume. Bands were measured by densitometry at 254 nm.

The method's validation is the kind of thing a reviewer checks line by line: linear from 5 to 25 ng mL⁻¹ with correlation coefficients of 0.999 (catechin) and 0.998 (EGCG), detection limits of 1.98 and 3.02 ng per spot, quantification limits of 6 and 8.94 ng per spot, recoveries of 102 % and 98.42 %, and relative standard deviations under 1 % within and between days. The content found in the extract was 22.43 µg of catechin and 18.20 µg of EGCG.

And then the Rf values. The abstract reports **catechin 0.49 and EGCG 0.23**. The results, discussion and conclusion all report **0.45 and 0.29** instead, and one sentence in the discussion lists them in the opposite order. Nothing else in the paper depends on which pair is right, but anyone trying to reproduce the separation has to choose.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the numbers printed in the paper**, not a simulation and not our own measurement. Rf is defined as the distance the band moved from the baseline divided by the distance the solvent moved, so with the paper's 70 mm development distance each Rf fixes a migration distance in millimetres.

| Reading | Catechin | EGCG | ΔRf | Gap on the plate | ΔCV |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Abstract (0.49, 0.23) | 34.3 mm, 2.04 CV | 16.1 mm, 4.35 CV | 0.26 | 18.2 mm | 2.31 |
| Body text (0.45, 0.29) | 31.5 mm, 2.22 CV | 20.3 mm, 3.45 CV | 0.16 | 11.2 mm | 1.23 |

**Step 1: the bands are well separated either way.** The tool's verdict on a pair of spots is the difference in column volumes, ΔCV, because that is what decides whether a flash column can keep them apart: a ΔCV of 1 or more usually separates. The abstract's pair gives ΔCV 2.31 and the body text's gives 1.23, so both readings clear the bar, and both put the two bands more than a centimetre apart on a plate developed over 70 mm. The paper's own description, "good band separation", survives the recheck. On the tool's own advice scale, EGCG sits in the sweet spot (Rf 0.15 to 0.40, "good for a flash column") under both readings, while catechin is merely "readable": fine on a plate, a little fast for a column.

**Step 2: what each Rf would cost on a column.** A compound needs roughly 1/Rf column volumes of the same eluent to come off a flash column packed with the same silica. Figure 1 shows that curve with the paper's four values on it. The practical message for anyone scaling this separation up: EGCG needs 3.5 to 4.4 column volumes and catechin 2.0 to 2.2, so a 10 g column run with this eluent would elute catechin almost immediately. Shifting to a slightly less polar eluent, aiming for catechin near Rf 0.3, would make the preparative version far easier without hurting the plate separation.

**Step 3: the two readings cannot both come from one misread front.** Rf is a ratio, so a wrong solvent-front reading moves every Rf on the plate in the same direction. Figure 2 holds the body text's migration distances (31.5 mm and 20.3 mm) and varies the assumed front. To turn catechin's 0.45 into the abstract's 0.49, the front would have to be 64.3 mm, shorter than stated; to turn EGCG's 0.29 into 0.23, it would have to be 88.3 mm, longer. The disagreements pull opposite ways, so this is a transcription slip between abstract and text, not a measurement artefact. Note how gentle the slopes are, though: a ±2 mm uncertainty in judging where the solvent stopped moves an Rf of 0.45 by only about ±0.013, which is why Rf is a robust number when it is recorded once and carefully.

**Step 4: a sanity check on the band layout.** Fifteen bands of 8 mm with fourteen 4 mm gaps between them need 15 × 8 + 14 × 4 = 176 mm of the 200 mm plate width, leaving 12 mm at each edge. That is tight but standard, and it is worth checking before a run, because overlapping tracks are the most common way an HPTLC quantification goes wrong.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/cacao-hptlc-catechin-egcg-rf-separation.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `rf`, `columnVolumes`, `separation` and `rfAdvice` from `src/lib/sci/tlc.ts`, the maths behind the [[tlc-rf-calculator]], and nothing else.

Inputs taken from the paper: the 70 mm development distance, the two pairs of Rf values, the plate size and the band and gap widths. Nothing is assumed, simulated or fitted; the migration distances are the paper's Rf values multiplied by its own development distance.

## How we test the maths

The TLC maths is pinned in `tests/sci.mjs`, which runs on every change to fibub:

- `rf(18, 0, 52)` must give 0.3462: a spot 18 mm above the baseline on a plate run 52 mm.
- Rf read from a photo must come out the same in pixel rows as in millimetres, with the y axis running downward: `rf(387.2, 560, 80)` is exactly 0.36.
- `columnVolumes(0.25)` must be 4, and `separation(0.36, 0.58).dCV` must be 1.0536.
- The lane grouping must put six spot positions on a 480-pixel-wide plate into lanes 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2.

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/tlc-rf-calculator.mjs` pins this paper's own numbers: both readings must give the migration distances and column volumes in the table above, the body-text pair must give ΔRf 0.16 and ΔCV 1.23, and the front distances needed to reconcile the two readings must come out at 64.3 mm and 88.3 mm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[tlc-rf-calculator]] page, scroll to the *Measured distances* card (you do not need a photo):

1. Set *Baseline to solvent front (mm)* to **70**.
2. In *Baseline to each spot centre (mm), one per line, with an optional name*, clear the example and type two lines: **20.3 EGCG** and **31.5 catechin**.
3. The table shows Rf **0.29** for EGCG with **3.4** column volumes and the note *good for a flash column*, and Rf **0.45** for catechin with **2.2** column volumes and the note *readable*.
4. Under the table you should read *Closest pair: ΔRf 0.16, ΔCV 1.2. A flash column with this eluent should separate them.*
5. Now change the two distances to **16.1** and **34.3** (the abstract's reading). ΔRf becomes **0.26** and ΔCV **2.3**, and EGCG's column volumes rise to **4.3**.
6. Put the distances back to **20.3** and **31.5** and change the front to **64** to see how little the Rf values move: catechin reads 0.49 and EGCG 0.32.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recheck a published separation before you try it.** Two Rf values and the development distance are enough to know whether the bands will be far enough apart on your plate, with the [[tlc-rf-calculator]].
- **Decide whether a TLC separation will survive scale-up** to a flash column, from ΔCV rather than from how the plate looks.
- **Record Rf properly.** Measure to the centre of the band, from the baseline, with the front marked immediately after development; one number per band, once.
- **Turn the densitometry into concentrations** with the [[calibration-curve]], which also gives the detection and quantification limits a method-validation table needs, and check precision and recovery with [[method-validation]].
- **Compare a plate method with the HPLC version** using the [[chromatography-calculator]] for retention factors, plate numbers and resolution, so that the two methods in a validation report can be judged on the same footing.

## The fine print

- The paper quotes two different Rf pairs for the same bands. We work both through rather than choosing one; neither changes the conclusion that the bands are well separated.
- ΔCV is a rule of thumb for flash chromatography on silica with the same eluent, not a theoretical resolution. Plate-based resolution needs band widths, which the paper does not give.
- The 22.43 µg and 18.20 µg marker contents in the paper are not tied in its text to a stated mass of extract, so we did not recompute a percentage from them.
- Rf depends on the plate, the chamber saturation, the temperature and the humidity, all of which the paper states (17–18 °C, 40–45 % RH). Reproducing an Rf to ±0.02 on a different day is normal; to ±0.005 is luck.
