Most papers about home batteries simulate them. This one measured one: a single-family house near Kraków with 7.28 kWp of panels, a heat pump and a 10 kWh battery, logged for a year at the inverter. That makes it a rare chance to ask a plain question about a browser tool: given only the three numbers a homeowner knows (yearly use, array size, yearly yield), how close does a simple hour-by-hour simulation get to a real year? This article runs that test with the [[home-battery-sizing|Home battery tool]]. Work out the yearly yield first with the [[solar-pv-yield]] estimator, size an off-grid system with [[battery-solar-sizing]], and compare lifetime costs with the [[lcoe-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The house sits in southern Poland. Ten panels face south and six face west on a 25° roof, 7.28 kWp in all, with a 10 kWh battery that the inverter charged to 100 % and discharged only to 20 %, at up to 5 kW. Over the year from 1 September 2023 the panels made **6474.59 kWh**, a modest **909.35 kWh per kWp**, and the house used **6885.44 kWh**. Of the solar, **2201.48 kWh** went straight to the house, **1432.44 kWh** went through the battery and **2840.67 kWh** was exported. So the battery lifted the share of solar used on site from 34 % to **56 %**.

Two things make the year unusual. First, the heat pump: January use was 990.61 kWh against 475.05 kWh in May, while December solar was only 83.19 kWh against 936.99 kWh in May. Second, the tariff: on a variable Polish tariff the owner also charged the battery **from the grid** in cheap hours, 1326.32 kWh over the year, which is why the paper's yearly bill saving (over EUR 1400) is much larger than anything solar alone could give.

## Checked with the tools

The Home battery tool builds a made-up template year from four numbers (daily use, array size, yield per kWp and a summer–winter swing), then walks through it hour by hour: solar serves the house first, the surplus charges the battery up to its power and space, the rest is exported; a shortfall comes from the battery, then the grid. We gave it the paper's yearly figures: **18.86 kWh a day** of use, **7.28 kWp**, **2.44 kWh per kWp per day** (the paper's 909.35 kWh per kWp spread over 365 days) and a **usable** battery of **8 kWh** (10 kWh worked between 20 % and 100 %) at 5 kW. The round-trip efficiency of 90 % and the 80 % summer–winter swing are **our assumptions**; the paper gives neither.

**Step 1: does the shape look like Poland?** With an 80 % swing the tool's template year gives 85.9 kWh of solar in December and 1102 kWh in July, against the paper's measured 83.19 kWh in December and 936.99 kWh in May. The template's house use, though, is nearly flat through the year (it rises only about 12 % in winter), while this house swings from 475 to 991 kWh a month. So we tilted the load towards winter with a cosine, peaking on 10 January, strong enough to give a January-to-May ratio of 2.2 against the measured 2.1, and rescaled it to the same yearly total. That tilt is **our own** addition, not the tool's; on the tool page you would paste a real profile instead. Figure 1 shows the result.

**Step 2: the self-consumption test.** On that year the tool's battery balance gives:

| Check | Paper (measured) | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Solar used on site, no battery | 34.0 % | 34.9 % |
| Solar used on site, with the battery | 56.1 % | 56.5 % |
| Solar exported | 2840.67 kWh | 2819 kWh |
| Solar into the battery | 1432.44 kWh | 1395 kWh |
| Share of house use met by solar | 52.8 % | 51.1 % |
| Full battery cycles a year | not stated | 157 |

Four independent quantities land within about 3 % of the measured ones, which is better than a made-up profile has any right to do and comes from the fact that self-consumption is mostly set by the ratio of yearly solar to yearly use (0.94 here) and by the size of the battery against the daily surplus.

The load tilt matters more than the battery model. Typed into the tool exactly as it stands, with the flat template load, the same house comes out at **67.5 %** self-consumption instead of 56.5 %: an 11-point error, all of it from putting too much of the year's use in the sunny months. That is the practical lesson for anyone sizing a battery from yearly totals, and it is why the tool offers a paste-your-own-profile option.

**Step 3: how big a battery is worth having?** Figure 2 is the tool's sweep from 0 to 20 kWh of usable capacity. The curve bends hard: the first 4 kWh add 15 points of self-consumption, the next 4 add 6.5, and everything past 10 kWh adds less than one. The tool's own recommendation (the smallest size reaching 90 % of the best import saving) is **8 kWh** — exactly the usable capacity the house has.

At the paper's average price of 0.33785 EUR per kWh, the imports the battery avoids are worth about **EUR 424** a year in the tool's accounting. The paper's "over EUR 1400" is a different sum: it includes the export payments, the avoided peak-hour prices and the cheap grid energy stored overnight, none of which a self-consumption model sees. Both numbers are right about different things, and mixing them is the commonest mistake in home-battery payback claims.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/residential-pv-battery-self-consumption-poland.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `template` (the made-up hourly year), `simulate` (the step-by-step energy balance), `sweep`, `sizeSteps` and `recommend` from `src/lib/sci/eng-homebattery.ts`, the maths behind the [[home-battery-sizing|Home battery tool]].

From the paper: the yearly solar and house use, the array size, the battery's 10 kWh and 5 kW, its 20 % to 100 % working window, the four monthly figures in Figure 1 and the 0.33785 EUR per kWh price. **Our assumptions:** the 90 % round-trip efficiency, the 80 % summer–winter solar swing, the winter tilt of the load (amplitude 0.4, peak 10 January) and no reserve kept for backup. The hourly profiles are **made up by the tool**, not the paper's measurements — the paper publishes monthly and yearly sums, not hourly data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/home-battery-sizing.mjs`, which pins this model:

- A made-up 14-day profile is simulated for five battery sizes at hourly, half-hourly and quarter-hourly steps and must match a separate Python re-implementation.
- The energy-balance identities must hold exactly: load equals solar minus export plus import plus the battery's losses and any change in charge, and the self-consumption and self-sufficiency shares must follow from those sums.
- New with this article: the paper's year, rebuilt from its yearly totals with the assumptions above, must give 34.9 % self-consumption with no battery, 56.5 % with 8 kWh, 2819 kWh exported, 1395 kWh of solar into the battery and a recommended size of 8 kWh.

## Check it yourself

On the [[home-battery-sizing|Home battery tool]] page:

1. Leave *Profile* on **Made-up year from a template**. Set *Home use per day (kWh)* to **18.86**, *Solar array size (kWp)* to **7.28**, *Solar yield (kWh per kWp per day, yearly average)* to **2.44**, *Summer–winter swing (%)* to **80** and *Hemisphere* to **Northern**.
2. Set *Usable capacity (kWh)* to **8**, *Most charge or discharge power (kW)* to **5**, *Round-trip efficiency (%)* to **90** and *Kept for backup (% of capacity)* to **0**.
3. The year comes out as about 6877 kWh of use and 6484 kWh of solar. With the battery you should see self-consumption **67.5 %** and self-sufficiency **61.1 %**, grid import about **2675 kWh**, export **2105 kWh** and **198** cycles a year. Set *Usable capacity* to 0 and self-consumption falls to **40.4 %**.
4. Set *Largest size to try (kWh)* to **20** and read the *Battery size sweep*: 40.4 % at 0 kWh, 56.2 % at 4, 67.5 % at 8, 70.9 % at 20. With *Recommend the smallest size giving* on **90 %**, the recommendation is **10 kWh (6.3 kW)**.
5. Those 67.5 % are 11 points above the measured 56.1 %, because the template year spreads the house's use evenly through the seasons. To see the fix, switch *Profile* to **My own data** and paste a year of your own hourly load and solar: a winter-heavy load brings the share back down.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published self-consumption figure** before citing it: yearly solar, yearly use and the usable capacity are nearly enough to predict it, and a figure far off the curve usually means grid charging, a different battery window or a tariff effect.
- **Separate the battery from the tariff.** Run the sweep with no prices to get the physical saving, then add prices; the difference is what the tariff, not the battery, is worth.
- **Size a battery for a monitoring campaign**: the sweep shows where extra capacity stops paying, so a trial can be built around the knee rather than the catalogue.
- **Teach seasonality**: the 11-point gap between a flat and a winter-heavy load year, on exactly the same solar, makes the point faster than any lecture.
- **Plan the next step**: feed the yearly yield from [[solar-pv-yield]] into the tool, then take the battery cost per kWh to the [[lcoe-calculator]].

## The fine print

- The hourly load and solar are **made up by the tool** from yearly totals, not the paper's measurements. Only monthly and yearly sums in the paper could be compared.
- The round-trip efficiency, the solar swing and the winter tilt of the load are our assumptions. Changing the efficiency to 95 % or the swing to 70 % moves the self-consumption figures by a point or two.
- The tool has no time-of-use tariff and cannot charge from the grid, so it cannot reproduce the paper's economics, only its energy flows.
- The paper's import figure (3251.52 kWh) includes the 1326.32 kWh charged into the battery from the grid, so it is not comparable with the tool's import; we compare the export and the solar-into-battery figures instead.
- Battery ageing, inverter clipping and the 253 V grid voltage limits the paper discusses are outside the model.
