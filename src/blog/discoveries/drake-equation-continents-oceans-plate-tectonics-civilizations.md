The **Drake equation** multiplies seven factors to estimate N, the number of civilisations in our galaxy that are sending signals we could detect right now. It runs from the rate stars are born (R*), through the share with planets (fp), habitable planets per system (ne) and the chances of life (fl), intelligence (fi) and signals (fc), to how long a civilisation keeps signalling (L). In 2024 the geologists Robert Stern and Taras Gerya argued in *Scientific Reports* that one factor hides two hard requirements. Complex, technological life seems to need a world with both **continents and oceans**, and **plate tectonics** that keeps running for at least half a billion years. Both are rare, they argue, and together they shrink N by a factor of 500 to about 30,000. This article reruns their numbers in the [[drake-equation|Drake equation calculator]], which treats every factor as a range rather than a single guess. For the planets themselves, see how far a star's habitable zone reaches with the [[habitable-zone-calculator]], and compare Earth with its neighbours in the [[planet-data-explorer]].

## The paper in brief

The authors start from Drake's own 1961 values: one new star a year, 20 to 50 % of stars with planets, 1 to 5 habitable planets per system, and life and intelligence certain wherever conditions allow (fl = fi = 1). Then 10 to 20 % of civilisations send signals, which last 1,000 to 100 million years. That gave Drake somewhere between hundreds and tens of millions of civilisations.

Stern and Gerya replace fi with the product of two new factors. **foc** is the share of habitable planets with substantial continents and oceans. They estimate it from how finely a planet's water must be balanced, neither a water world nor a desert, at about **0.0002 to 0.01**. **fpt** is the share of those where plate tectonics has run for at least 500 million years. They argue Earth's modern plate tectonics began late, in the Neoproterozoic (less than a billion years ago), and sped up the rise of complex life; they set an upper limit of 0.33 and then cut it to **below 0.17**. Together, fi = foc · fpt is below 0.00003 to 0.002. Their headline is that N falls to **below 0.006 to below 100,000** civilisations. That could help explain the **Fermi paradox**, the puzzle of why we see no sign of anyone else.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the arithmetic of the ends.** The new fi range is 0.0002 × 0.17 = **0.000034** to 0.01 × 0.17 = **0.0017**, which the paper rounds to 0.00003 and 0.002. Multiplying every factor at its high end, the calculator's ranges give a highest N of **85,000** (the paper's 100,000 uses the rounded 0.002). The low end needs care. Every factor at its low end gives 20 civilisations with Drake's values, not the 200 the paper quotes as his stated range; times 0.000034 that is **0.00068**. The paper's 0.006 is 200 × 0.00003. Both say the same thing: the lowest case is far below one civilisation.

**Step 2: what is likely, not just possible.** The two ends are extreme cases where every factor is at its edge at once. The calculator instead draws each factor at random across its range, with every power of ten equally likely (a **log-uniform** draw), and works out the chances exactly. To use it we fixed fpt at its upper limit of 0.17 (**our assumption**), so every result here is an upper limit, as in the paper.

| With 200,000 draws (seed 1) | Drake's 1961 ranges | With foc and fpt |
|---|---|---|
| Lowest and highest possible N | 20 to 50,000,000 | 0.00068 to 85,000 |
| Middle estimate (every factor mid-range) | 31,600 | 7.6 |
| Median N | 31,200 | 7.5 |
| 90 % of draws | 167 to 5,900,000 | 0.026 to 2,220 |
| Chance N is below 1 (exact) | 0 % | 32.4 % |
| Chance N is 1,000 or more (exact) | 80 % | 9.3 % |

The new factors move the whole picture down by a factor of about 4,000, from tens of thousands of civilisations to a handful (Figure 1). With Drake's ranges it is impossible to be alone; with the new factors, there is a one-in-three chance that no other signalling civilisation exists in the galaxy right now.

**Step 3: which factor still dominates.** The calculator splits the spread of log N between the factors. With Drake's ranges, L carries **97 %** of it; with the new factors L still carries **87 %**, and fi only **10 %**. Even after the geology, the biggest unknown is how long civilisations last. With every other factor at its middle value, N reaches one only if civilisations keep signalling for about **42,000 years** (Figure 2). At 1,000 years N is 0.024, and at 100 million years it is 2,400.

## How we made the graphs

The table and charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/drake-equation-continents-oceans-plate-tectonics-civilizations.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `pointN`, `logMoments`, `exactCdf`, `sampleLogN`, `quantile` and `shareBelow` from `src/lib/sci/drake.ts`, the maths behind the [[drake-equation|Drake equation calculator]].

All seven ranges and the foc and fpt values come from the paper's text. Fixing fpt at 0.17 and drawing every factor log-uniformly are **our assumptions**; the paper gives ranges but does not say how likely each value inside them is. Figure 1 uses the exact formula for a sum of uniform variables, so it has no random scatter.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/drake-equation.mjs`, which pins the calculator against NumPy:

- For the calculator's "very uncertain" example, the mean of log₁₀ N must be −2.3495 and the exact chance of N below 1 must be 0.8003, matching a numerical convolution and a 2-million-draw Monte Carlo.
- The Monte Carlo must match the exact answers: with 200,000 draws, the chance of N below 1 must be within four standard errors of 0.8003, and the median within 0.03 of −2.358.
- New with this article: Drake's 1961 ranges must give a middle estimate of 10^4.5 = 31,623 and a 20 % chance of N below 1,000; with foc and fpt the middle estimate must be 7.603 and the exact chance of N below 1 must be 0.3238.

## Check it yourself

On the [[drake-equation|Drake equation calculator]], in the *Factors* card, set *Start from* to **My own ranges**, then:

1. Type the *Low* and *High* values for Drake's 1961 ranges: R* **1** and **1**, fp **0.2** and **0.5**, ne **1** and **5**, fl **1** and **1**, fi **1** and **1**, fc **0.1** and **0.2**, L **1000** and **100000000**.
2. Leave *Random draws* at **100000** and *Seed* at **1**. The result reads a median of about **32,000**, and the *Middle estimate* is **32,000**. *Chance N ≥ 1,000* is about **79.9 %**, with an exact **80 %**.
3. Change fi to Low **0.000034** and High **0.0017**. The median falls to about **7.6**, with a **32.5 %** chance of fewer than 1. The *Which factor matters most* table gives L about **87 %** of the spread and fi **10 %**.
4. Narrow L to **10000** and **1000000** and see how much of the spread moves to the geology factor.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a qualitative argument into odds:** put any proposed new factor into one of the seven ranges and read the change in the median and in the chance of N below 1, with the [[drake-equation|Drake equation calculator]].
- **Find the factor worth measuring:** the share-of-spread table shows which unknown dominates; here, still L.
- **Compare ends with likely values:** papers often quote only the product of the extremes. The 90 % range and the median are fairer summaries.
- **Teach uncertainty:** the same ranges give "20 to 50 million" as ends and "about 30,000" as a median; students see why multiplying ranges needs care.

## The fine print

- We used the paper's ranges as stated. The paper's own lowest N uses Drake's stated 200; a plain product of his low values gives 20, ten times lower.
- fpt is an upper limit (below 0.17), so every N with the new factors is an upper limit too.
- The paper reduces N further, to below 0.0004 to below 20,000, for reasons we could not turn into a range for the calculator, so we did not rework that step.
- How likely each value inside a range is (log-uniform here) is a choice. A different choice changes the median and the chances, but not the ends.
- The Drake equation is a way to organise ignorance, not a measurement. The paper's case rests on Earth's geological history, which these tools do not test.
