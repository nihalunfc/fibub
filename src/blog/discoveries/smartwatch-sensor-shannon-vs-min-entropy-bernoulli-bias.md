If you want to make a cryptographic key out of sensor noise, the number that decides how much noise you need is **not** Shannon entropy. It is **min-entropy**, which counts only the most likely outcome, because an attacker guesses that one first. A 2025 paper in *Sensors* asked whether an ordinary smartwatch's accelerometer and gyroscope hold enough randomness to make keys on the watch itself, and reports both numbers: 0.997 and 0.918 when the watch is shaken, 0.991 and 0.851 when it lies still. With the [[entropy-calculator]] we show that each pair is exactly what a slightly biased coin would give, pin down the bias the paper does not state, and turn the result into the only figure a developer actually needs: how many raw bits to collect per key. For the neighbouring ideas, see [[huffman-coding]] for what entropy means for compression, [[error-correcting-codes]] for noisy channels and [[cipher-teacher]] for the classical ciphers that keys protect.

## The paper in brief

The authors collected accelerometer and gyroscope frames from commercial smartwatches in two conditions: **shake mode**, where the wearer moves the watch, and **still mode**, where it rests. Each frame was vectorised with a timestamp and sensor identifier, normalised, discretised to 8 bits and then hashed with SHA-256 to form key material. They took 4800 usable samples per mode after discarding a preliminary batch and a displaced tail.

The headline measurements are two entropies per mode. Shake mode gives **Shannon entropy 0.997 and min-entropy 0.918**; still mode **0.991 and 0.851**. The authors conclude that a smartwatch can serve as a practical entropy source as long as the output is post-processed by hashing, and are careful to say that only a subset of the NIST SP 800-90B test suite was applied, with full validation left for later work.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: what source gives those two numbers?** The paper does not say whether its entropies are per bit, per byte or normalised. The pair of values settles it. For a source of independent bits whose commoner value has probability *p*:

> Shannon entropy = −*p* log₂ *p* − (1 − *p*) log₂(1 − *p*)  and  min-entropy = −log₂ *p*

Take the paper's min-entropy for shake mode, 0.918 bits. That means *p* = 2^(−0.918) = **0.529**. Feed that back through the Shannon formula with the entropy calculator's own code and you get **0.9975**, which rounds to the paper's 0.997. Do the same for still mode: min-entropy 0.851 implies *p* = **0.554**, and the Shannon entropy of that coin is **0.9914**, the paper's 0.991.

| Mode | Paper's min-entropy | Implied bias *p* | Shannon entropy the tool gets | Paper's Shannon entropy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shake | 0.918 | 0.52924 | **0.99753** | 0.997 |
| Still | 0.851 | 0.55440 | **0.99144** | 0.991 |

Both agree to the three decimals the paper prints. So the paper's numbers are **entropy per bit of an essentially memoryless binary source**, and its sensors are biased by about 3 percentage points when shaken and 5.5 when still. That reading is worth having, because the paper itself never states the bias.

**Step 2: Shannon entropy is a terrible way to report a near-uniform source.** Run the inference the other way and the rounding bites. The paper's Shannon entropy of 0.997 implies *p* = 0.53223 and a min-entropy of **0.9099**, not 0.918. The difference comes from the shape of the curve: near *p* = 0.5 Shannon entropy is flat (its slope is zero at the top), while min-entropy falls steadily. Going from *p* = 0.5292 to 0.5322 changes Shannon entropy by **0.00053** and min-entropy by **0.0081**, fifteen times more. Figure 1 shows both curves over the paper's range.

The practical lesson: a Shannon entropy quoted to three decimals pins the bias of a near-uniform source only loosely, so entropy sources should be reported by their min-entropy, exactly as NIST SP 800-90B requires.

**Step 3: how many raw bits per key?** This is where the gap between the two measures stops being academic. To get a 128-bit key you must gather at least 128 bits of min-entropy, so

- **shake mode**: 128 ÷ 0.918 = **140 raw bits** (279 for a 256-bit key);
- **still mode**: 128 ÷ 0.851 = **151 raw bits** (301 for a 256-bit key).

Had you counted Shannon entropy instead, you would have asked for **129** bits in shake mode and **130** in still mode: short by 8 % and 14 %, and a key seeded that way has less strength than its length advertises. Figure 2 draws both answers against the bias. It also shows why the paper's recommendation to hash the output is the right one: hashing concentrates the min-entropy of many weak bits into fewer strong ones, and the figure says how many weak bits to feed it.

**Step 4: how different are the two modes, really?** The tool's divergence card answers that. Between the shake and still bit distributions the Kullback-Leibler divergence is **0.00184 bits** each way and the Jensen-Shannon divergence **0.00046 bits**. As distributions they are almost the same coin. The whole practical difference between the modes, 11 extra raw bits per key, comes from the tail behaviour that min-entropy measures and divergence does not.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/smartwatch-sensor-shannon-vs-min-entropy-bernoulli-bias.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `h2` (the binary entropy function), `summarise` (Shannon, maximum, min-entropy, collision entropy, redundancy and perplexity of a distribution) and `divergence` (Kullback-Leibler and Jensen-Shannon) from `src/lib/sci/entropy.ts`, the maths behind the [[entropy-calculator]]. The bias implied by a Shannon entropy is found by bisection on `h2`.

The only inputs from the paper are its four measured values, 0.997 and 0.918 for shake mode and 0.991 and 0.851 for still mode, and the key lengths 128 and 256 bits are the standard ones. The model of the source, **independent bits with a fixed bias**, is **our assumption**; it is the simplest source consistent with the paper's two numbers, and the fact that both pairs fit it to three decimals is the article's main result, not an input. Nothing is simulated and no table from the paper was copied.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/entropy-calculator.mjs`, which pins this maths against `scipy.stats.entropy`, `scipy.spatial.distance.jensenshannon`, scikit-learn's `mutual_info_score` and a separate `scipy.optimize` maximisation for channel capacity:

- A distribution of 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8 must give exactly 1.75 bits, 1.2130076 nats and 0.5268025 hartleys.
- The counts 3, 1, 1, 7, 0, 2 must give Shannon entropy 1.9211855, min-entropy exactly 1, collision entropy 1.6147098 and a maximum of log₂ 6, with the support reported as 5 of 6.
- Kullback-Leibler between 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1 and the uniform distribution must be 0.15356066 bits, and the Jensen-Shannon divergence 0.04020158.
- New with this article: the binary entropy of 0.52924 must be 0.9975316 with min-entropy 0.9180060, and of 0.5544 must be 0.9914442 with min-entropy 0.8510008, so that the paper's two pairs are reproduced; and the Jensen-Shannon divergence between those two distributions must be 0.00045990 bits.

## Check it yourself

On the [[entropy-calculator]]:

1. In *What to measure* choose **Distribution**, and set *Units* to **bits**.
2. Clear the box and type the shake-mode coin: **0.52924, 0.47076**.
3. The *Result* card should read entropy **0.997532**, *Maximum, log 2* **1**, *Efficiency H / max* **99.75 %**, *Redundancy* **0.2468 %**, *Perplexity* **1.99658**, *Min-entropy H∞* **0.918006** and *Collision entropy H₂* **0.995075**. The min-entropy is the paper's 0.918.
4. Now type the still-mode coin: **0.5544, 0.4456**. Entropy becomes **0.991444** and *Min-entropy H∞* **0.851001**, the paper's second pair.
5. Switch to the **Divergence** card. Put **0.52924, 0.47076** in *P (the true or observed distribution)* and **0.5544, 0.4456** in *Q (the model or approximation)*. You should see *Kullback-Leibler divergence* **0.00184240**, *D(Q‖P)* **0.00183719** and *Jensen-Shannon divergence* **0.000459899**.
6. For the key-length answer, divide 128 by the min-entropy you read in step 3: 128 ÷ 0.918006 = 139.4, so **140 raw bits**. In still mode, 128 ÷ 0.851001 = 150.4, so **151**.

Either box works: type both probabilities into *Distribution* as above, or type *p* into the *Two outcomes: chance of the first, p* shortcut and let the tool fill in the complement. (Writing this article turned up a rounding slip in that shortcut, which cut the second probability to two decimals and shifted the min-entropy in the fourth decimal; it is fixed.)

## What researchers can use it for

- **Rate an entropy source properly.** Report min-entropy, not Shannon entropy, and state the alphabet. The tool gives Shannon, min-entropy, collision entropy, efficiency and redundancy from one paste.
- **Size the collection stage of a key generator.** Divide the target key length by the measured min-entropy per bit, as in step 6, and round up. That single division is what separates a 128-bit key from a 118-bit one.
- **Reverse-engineer a bias from published entropies**, as we did here, when a paper reports entropy but not the underlying distribution.
- **Compare two conditions or two devices** with Kullback-Leibler and Jensen-Shannon divergence instead of eyeballing two entropies that both round to 0.99.
- **Teach the difference** between average-case and worst-case uncertainty: Figure 1 makes it obvious in one picture, and the same contrast drives Huffman coding in [[huffman-coding]].

## The fine print

- The paper does not state whether its entropies are per bit or over a larger alphabet. Our reading, per bit, is an inference from the two values fitting a biased-coin source; if the source is not binary the bias figures do not apply, although the min-entropy argument still does.
- Real sensor bits are **not** independent: successive accelerometer readings are correlated, so the true min-entropy per bit of a long stream is lower than a per-bit figure suggests, and more raw bits are needed than our 140 and 151.
- The paper applied only part of the NIST SP 800-90B suite and says so. Our numbers are consistency checks on its published values, not a validation of the source.
- Min-entropy is also what a hash function's output length should be judged against; hashing never creates entropy, it only concentrates it.
- General information about information theory and key generation, not a security review of any device.
