Stomata are the pores a leaf opens to take in carbon dioxide and loses water through, so how many of them a leaf carries per square millimetre sits right at the centre of how a crop trades water for growth. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Plant Science* trained two neural networks to find and outline maize stomata, then counted them through the reproductive season on a hybrid and both of its parent inbred lines. Its headline is that the hybrid has **fewer, larger** stomata than the average of its parents. This article recomputes that heterosis from the published means with the [[stomatal-density-counter]], and asks the question the paper's own method raises: how many microscope fields does a density like this need? Then scale a leaf with the [[leaf-area-analyser]] and plan the counts with the [[sample-size]].

## The paper in brief

The authors grew the hybrid **728** and its parents, the inbred lines **2416** and **MC01**, at six plants per square metre, and sampled leaves every seven days from the three-leaf stage, nine times in all, from the lower, middle and upper layers of the canopy. Leaf impressions were photographed at two magnifications: **350×**, covering a square 767 µm on a side, and **900×**, covering 298 µm.

A YOLOv5 detector found each stoma and a U-Net outlined the guard cells and pore. Against hand measurements the pipeline reached R² 0.97 for stomatal width and **0.92 for density**, with a root-mean-square error of 1.63 stomata per mm².

The results: mean density was **87.21 ± 10.97** stomata per mm² in inbred 2416, **78.64 ± 9.41** in MC01 and **74.95 ± 11.82** in the hybrid, while the hybrid's stomata were the longest and largest. Density rose up the canopy, **74.35** in the lower layer, 80.01 in the middle and **87.19** in the upper, and size fell. The paper reports a stomatal density heterosis of **−0.09**, with positive heterosis for the size traits.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is **recomputed from the published means**, plus one simulation that uses no data from the paper at all.

**Step 1: the heterosis.** The mid-parent value is (87.21 + 78.64) ÷ 2 = **82.925** stomata per mm². The hybrid's 74.95 is therefore **−9.62 %** of the mid-parent, which is the paper's **−0.09** to the precision it is quoted at. Against the denser parent the shortfall is −14.06 %, and against the thinner parent −4.69 %, so the hybrid is below both: real negative heterosis, not just a mid-parent artefact.

**Step 2: what one field of view holds.** The tool needs the area you counted, and that area decides everything about precision. A 767 µm square is **0.5883 mm²**, so at 87.21 stomata per mm² one 350× field holds about **51 stomata**. A 298 µm square is **0.0888 mm²**: about **7.7 stomata**, and for the hybrid 6.7. Figure 1 puts that on the familiar microscope scale, a round field from a 22 mm eyepiece field number, using the tool's own field-of-view functions.

**Step 3: how much spread counting alone produces.** Stomata are not laid out on a grid; to a first approximation they fall at random over the epidermis. We scattered 8,721 points at random over a 10 × 10 mm patch with the tool's own point-placing function (so, exactly 87.21 per mm², **our simulation**, no leaf involved), then counted them field by field. The result: **51.2 ± 7.4 stomata** in a 350× field, which is **87.0 ± 12.5 per mm²**, a coefficient of variation of **14.4 %**. In a 900× field it is 7.7 ± 2.7, a CV of **35.0 %**.

Now compare that with the paper. Its three lines have CVs of **12.6 %, 12.0 % and 15.8 %**. Random placement in a single 350× field gives 14.4 %. In other words, essentially **all** of the reported spread between samples can be explained by where the stomata happened to fall in the field: date, plant and layer differences are riding on top of a noise floor of about the same size.

**Step 4: so how many fields?** Figure 2 is the practical answer. To bring the relative standard error of a density below 5 % you need **9 fields at 350×**, or **50 at 900×**. The paper used four to five fields per leaf, which gives about 6 to 7 % on one leaf: fine for the seasonal and canopy patterns it draws from nine dates and three layers, but not enough on its own for a single-leaf comparison. The hybrid's gap below the mid-parent is **7.97 stomata per mm²**; the standard error of that difference from five fields per line is **7.93**, so one leaf each cannot see it, while 40 fields per line brings it to 2.80, under three standard errors. The paper's design reaches that by pooling dates and layers, and its figure of −0.09 is believable for that reason, not from any one photo.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/maize-hybrid-stomatal-density-leaf-layers.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fovDiameter`, `circleArea`, `placePoints` and `summary` from `src/lib/sci/plantimg.ts`, the maths behind the [[stomatal-density-counter]] (`summary` is what the tool uses for the mean and standard deviation across the fields you add to its list).

From the paper: the two field sizes, the three line means with their standard deviations, the layer means and the number of fields per leaf. **Our own**: the 10 × 10 mm patch, the random seed, and the assumption that stomata fall independently of one another. Real epidermis spaces stomata slightly apart, the "one-cell spacing rule", which makes the counts a little **less** variable than our simulation, so our noise floor is a fair upper bound, and the paper's CVs sitting just below it is exactly what that predicts.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/stomatal-density-counter.mjs`, which pins the tool:

- The example epidermis is drawn with 23 stomata at known centres, and the automatic suggestion must find all 23, each within a few pixels of where it was drawn.
- The field of view from a 22 mm field number at 40× must be 0.55 mm across, an area of 0.237583 mm², and the stomatal index of 23 stomata with 37 other cells must be 38.33 %.
- New with this article: a 767 µm square field must be 0.588289 mm² and a 298 µm square 0.088804 mm²; and the 169 simulated 350× fields of Figure 2, from the same seed, must summarise to 86.99 ± 12.54 stomata per mm².

## Check it yourself

On the [[stomatal-density-counter]]:

1. Press **Use the example** to load the made-up epidermis, then, under *Automatic suggestion*, press **Suggest stomata**. The legend reads **Stomata: 23**.
2. In *Field of view*, set *Area counted* to **I type the area** and put **0.588289** in *Area (mm²)*, the paper's 350× field. The *Stomatal density* card reads about **39 stomata per mm²**.
3. Change *Area (mm²)* to **0.088804**, the 900× field. The same 23 marks now read about **259 per mm²**. The marks did not change; only the area did. This is the single most common way a published density goes wrong.
4. Switch *Area counted* to **Round field (field number and objective)** and leave *Field number (mm)* **22** with *Objective (×)* **40**: the help line gives a field 0.55 mm across and an area of **0.2376 mm²**, and the density falls to about **97 per mm²**.
5. Press **Add this field to the list**, change the area, and add again: the *Fields counted* table gives the mean and standard deviation across fields, the two numbers Figure 2 is about.
6. For the stomatal index, switch *A tap adds* to **Epidermal cell** and tap the 37 plain cells of the example too: the index is stomata ÷ (stomata + cells) × 100, so 23 ÷ 60 = **38.3 %**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan the magnification before you photograph.** Figure 1 and Figure 2 together say how many fields your chosen objective will cost you; use the [[sample-size]] for the leaves and plants on top of that.
- **Check a published density** by turning it back into stomata per field: if that number is under about 10, the standard deviation quoted is mostly counting noise.
- **Compare lines fairly.** Count the same number of fields of the same size on each line, and report the field area, not just the magnification.
- **Recompute heterosis** from any two parents and a hybrid in one line of arithmetic, as in step 1, and state whether it is mid-parent or better-parent.
- **Tie density to gas exchange and leaf size** with the [[leaf-area-analyser]] and the photosynthesis tools, where density becomes conductance per unit leaf area.

## The fine print

- We did not use the paper's per-date or per-layer tables, only the means quoted above; the heterosis we recompute is therefore for the season as a whole.
- Our noise floor assumes stomata fall independently. The real spacing rule makes counts slightly more even, so treat 14.4 % as an upper bound for a 350× field.
- The paper's −0.09 is reported for the hybrid against its parents; whether that is mid-parent or better-parent heterosis changes the figure (−9.6 % against −14.1 %).
- Densities measured on impressions depend on where on the leaf the impression was taken: base, middle and tip differ, as do the two surfaces.
- A neural network counting stomata inherits the bias of whoever labelled its training images. The paper's R² 0.92 against hand counts is the figure to compare with your own repeatability.
