Most stable metal carbonyls have **18 valence electrons** around the metal, the same as the next noble gas, and chemists use that count to guess which complexes will exist. A 2024 study in *Molecules* made gas-phase carbonyl cations containing two metals from the vanadium group (vanadium, niobium, tantalum), recorded their infrared spectra, and explained the structures with calculations and the 18-electron rule. The rule is just arithmetic, so we can run it ourselves with the [[electron-count-calculator]]. What the structures look like is a question for the [[point-group-finder]], the d-orbital picture belongs to the [[crystal-field-calculator]], and masses of the ions come from the [[molar-mass-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors used a laser to knock carbon monoxide off ions and recorded the infrared spectrum of what was left (infrared photodissociation spectroscopy), then matched the bands to computed structures. For the cations with nine CO ligands, VM(CO)<sub>9</sub><sup>+</sup> with M = V, Nb or Ta, the best match is a **quartet** (three unpaired electrons) with a V–M bond. The picture they draw is a niobium or tantalum atom carrying six CO ligands, joined to a vanadium atom that carries three CO ligands and the positive charge. In the all-vanadium cation the V–V distance is 2.868 Å and the calculated bond index is only 0.40, which the authors read as a half bond; side-on bridging CO groups, with bands near 1646 and 1700 cm⁻¹, hold the two metals together. They also say that the heavier metal reaches an **18-electron** configuration through the bond to vanadium, and that V(CO)<sub>6</sub><sup>+</sup> is a saturated 16-electron cation that does not take a seventh CO strongly under their conditions.

## Checked with the tools

The tool's rule is: valence electrons = group number + 2 × (number of L ligands) + X ligands + metal–metal bonds − charge. Each CO is an L ligand, so each one adds 2.

| Species | Count | Tool gives | Paper says |
|---|---|---|---|
| V(CO)<sub>6</sub><sup>+</sup> | 5 + 12 − 1 | 16 electrons | 16-electron, saturated |
| V(CO)<sub>7</sub><sup>+</sup> | 5 + 14 − 1 | 18 electrons | strongly bonded 18-electron form prohibited |
| Nb(CO)<sub>6</sub> fragment, no bond | 5 + 12 | 17 electrons | (a fragment) |
| Nb(CO)<sub>6</sub> with one Nb–V bond | 5 + 12 + 1 | **18 electrons** | heavier metal reaches 18 electrons |
| Ta(CO)<sub>6</sub> with one Ta–V bond | 5 + 12 + 1 | 18 electrons | same |
| V(CO)<sub>3</sub><sup>+</sup> fragment, no bond | 5 + 6 − 1 | 10 electrons | (a fragment) |
| V(CO)<sub>3</sub><sup>+</sup> with one V–Nb bond | 5 + 6 + 1 − 1 | **11 electrons** | not stated |

So the paper's electron-count statements all reproduce. The niobium or tantalum end closes its shell with a single metal–metal bond, because group 5 gives 5 electrons, six CO give 12 and the bond gives 1. The vanadium end is a different story: it holds only 11 electrons and would need **eight** metal–metal bonds from its bare count of 10 to reach 18, which is impossible. **Our reading** is that this electron-poor vanadium is why the cations are high-spin quartets with a vanadium atom carrying most of the unpaired electrons, and why the V–V bond index of 0.40 is small: the 18-electron rule says something sensible about the heavier metal but cannot be applied to both metals at once. As a bookkeeping check, the two counts add to 29, which is the 27 valence electrons of the whole cation (5 + 5 + 18 − 1) plus 2, because the two electrons of the shared bond are counted once at each metal.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/vanadium-niobium-tantalum-dinuclear-carbonyl-cations-electron-count-18-electron.mjs` imports `count` and `METALS` from `src/lib/sci/ecount.ts`, the maths of the 18-electron rule tool. The species, charges and CO numbers come from the paper's text; the fragment split into (OC)<sub>6</sub>M and V(CO)<sub>3</sub><sup>+</sup> is the paper's own description, and counting one metal–metal bond at each end is our assumption. We did not count the bridging CO groups separately, since the tool treats every CO as a two-electron L ligand. Figure 1 is the count for V(CO)<sub>n</sub><sup>+</sup> with n from 3 to 8; Figure 2 raises the number of metal–metal bonds credited to each metal from 0 to 8.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/electron-count-calculator.mjs` runs with `node tests/sci.mjs` on every change. It pins the counts of the textbook cases (ferrocene 18, Fe(CO)<sub>5</sub> 18, Ni(CO)<sub>4</sub> 18, Mn<sub>2</sub>(CO)<sub>10</sub> 18 per Mn with one Mn–Mn bond). New with this article it checks V(CO)<sub>6</sub><sup>+</sup> = 16, V(CO)<sub>7</sub><sup>+</sup> = 18, Nb(CO)<sub>6</sub> with one Nb–V bond = 18, V(CO)<sub>3</sub><sup>+</sup> with one bond = 11, and that the two ends of the cation add to the cation's total plus two.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[electron-count-calculator]]. Set **Metal** to V (group 5) and **Charge of the complex** to 1; set **Metal–metal bonds** to 0.
2. Choose **Ligand 1** "CO (carbonyl)" and type 6 in **How many**. The tool reads 16 electrons, V(I), d⁴.
3. Change **How many** to 7: 18 electrons.
4. Now set **Metal** to Nb, **Charge** to 0, **How many** CO to 6 and **Metal–metal bonds** to 1: 18 electrons, Nb(0), d⁵.
5. Set **Metal** to V, **Charge** to 1, CO to 3 and **Metal–metal bonds** to 1: 11 electrons.

## What researchers can use it for

- Sanity-checking an electron-count claim in a paper in seconds, before reading the details of the calculation.
- Spotting the electron-poor end of a heterodinuclear complex, which is where spin density and extra ligand binding are likely to be found.
- Teaching how a metal–metal bond is counted at both metals, and why the two counts add to the total plus twice the bond order.
- Finding how many CO ligands a metal can take before the count passes 18: for the V⁺ ion it is seven, so the six-CO cation sits two electrons short of a full shell.
- Pairing with the [[crystal-field-calculator]] to see how the d-electron count of each oxidation state splits.

## The fine print

This checks only the paper's electron-counting statements with the standard covalent rule. The paper's spectra, computed energies, spin states and bond indices are not recomputed, and the rule is a heuristic that needs adjusting for bridging groups, weak bonds and charges placed on a particular metal. We counted each CO as a plain two-electron donor and credited one metal–metal bond to each end, so our counts for vanadium are a bound on the answer rather than the paper's calculation. The gas-phase cations are not stable compounds you can buy; they exist in an instrument for a fraction of a second.
