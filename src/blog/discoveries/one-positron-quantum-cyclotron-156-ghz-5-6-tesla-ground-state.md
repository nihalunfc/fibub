Antimatter made the science news this week with a single particle: a team at Northwestern University has held **one positron**, the antimatter twin of the electron, in a trap for a month and put its circular motion into its **quantum ground state**. It is the first "quantum cyclotron" built from an antiparticle, and it is the tool needed to compare the positron's magnetism with the electron's to extraordinary precision, one of the cleanest tests of whether matter and antimatter obey the same laws. The [[quantum-statistics]] plotter shows why the trap has to be so cold, and the [[plasma-parameters]] calculator gives the frequency the positron circles at. To watch a charged particle gyrate in a field, open [[particle-drifts]].

## The paper in brief

A charged particle in a magnetic field moves in circles at its **cyclotron frequency**. For something as light as an electron or positron in a strong field, the circular motion is quantised: it can only have energies in steps of ħωc, like the rungs of a ladder. In a **Penning trap** a strong magnetic field holds the particle radially and electric fields hold it along the axis. If the trap is cold enough, the particle sits on the bottom rung and stays there.

The team loaded a single positron from a weak sodium-22 source into a trap inside a **5.6 tesla** magnet, where the cyclotron and spin frequencies are both about **156 GHz**. A dilution refrigerator keeps the trap electrodes at **300 millikelvin**. A small "magnetic bottle" makes the positron's slow axial bounce, at about 206 MHz, shift by a few hertz with each cyclotron quantum, so the team can read the quantum number without disturbing it. They saw the positron climb two rungs when driven and fall back by spontaneous emission one quantum at a time.

The trap also works as a microwave cavity. In free space the first excited level would decay in **83 ms**; inside the trap it lasts about **4 seconds**, long enough to watch. The spin, which the paper says is not yet measured quantitatively, is the next step: the difference between the spin and cyclotron frequencies gives the positron's magnetic moment.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated field and temperature**. A positron has exactly the electron's mass, so every electron formula applies with the charge's sign flipped, which changes the direction of the circling but not its frequency.

**Step 1: the frequency.** The [[plasma-parameters]] calculator's *Electron cyclotron frequency* at **5.6 T** is **156.76 GHz**. The paper says "approximately 156 GHz"; exactly 156.0 GHz would need **5.573 T**, so the field is 5.6 T to the precision the paper prints. The trap's own fields shift the measured cyclotron frequency only by the magnetron frequency, about 135 kHz, less than one part in a million.

**Step 2: one quantum as a temperature.** One rung of the ladder is hf = **0.6483 meV**, or **7.52 K** when divided by Boltzmann's constant. The paper gives **7.5 K**. This is the number that decides everything: heat with an energy far below 7.5 K cannot lift the positron one rung.

**Step 3: how often heat lifts it.** A cyclotron oscillator in thermal contact with its surroundings has a Bose-Einstein mean quantum number with zero chemical potential, the same formula as photons in a cavity. At **300 mK**, (E − μ)/kT is **25.08**, and the [[quantum-statistics]] plotter gives a mean occupation of **1.285 × 10⁻¹¹**. The chance of being in any excited level, the Maxwell-Boltzmann factor e^(−25.08), is the same **1.285 × 10⁻¹¹**: "slightly more than a part in 10¹¹", as the paper says. With the paper's rounder 156 GHz it is 1.45 × 10⁻¹¹, still the same statement.

**Step 4: why a plain helium-cooled trap is not enough.** At **4.2 K**, the temperature of liquid helium and of the magnet itself, the mean quantum number would be **0.200** and the positron would be out of its ground state **16.7 %** of the time, about one moment in six. The mean quantum number only drops to 0.1 at **3.14 K** and to 0.01 at **1.63 K**. The steep fall in Figure 1 is why the dilution refrigerator, and not just a colder magnet, makes the quantum cyclotron possible.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/one-positron-quantum-cyclotron-156-ghz-5-6-tesla-ground-state.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `plasma()` from `src/lib/sci/phplasma.ts`, the maths behind the [[plasma-parameters]] calculator, for the electron (and so positron) cyclotron frequency at each field, and `occupancy()` with the constants from `src/lib/sci/phqstat.ts`, the maths behind the [[quantum-statistics]] plotter, for the Bose-Einstein mean quantum number and the Maxwell-Boltzmann excitation chance at each temperature. The temperatures for a mean quantum number of 0.1 and 0.01 come from bisection on that same `occupancy()` function.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/quantum-statistics.mjs`, which checks the occupancies at simple points (1/2 for Fermi-Dirac at E = μ, 1/(e − 1) for Bose-Einstein at x = 1), and the Fermi-Dirac and Bose-Einstein integrals against mpmath at 30 digits. `tests/sci-tools/plasma-parameters.mjs` pins the electron cyclotron frequency to the NRL Plasma Formulary's 140 GHz at 5 T. New with this article: the positron at 5.6 T must give a 7.523 K quantum, a thermal chance of 1.285 × 10⁻¹¹ at 300 mK and a mean quantum number of 0.200 at 4.2 K.

## Check it yourself

On the [[plasma-parameters]] page, with *Start from* on **My own values**:

1. Type **5.6** in *Magnetic field B* with *Field unit* **tesla**. The density and temperatures do not matter for this step.
2. Under the frequencies, *Electron cyclotron frequency fce* reads **157 GHz** (156.76 GHz to more figures). It is the positron's frequency too.

Then on the [[quantum-statistics]] page, in the distributions part:

3. Set *Energy unit* to **meV** and *Temperature (K)* to **0.3**.
4. Set *Chemical potential μ* to **0**, and *Read off at energy E* to **0.6483**, one cyclotron quantum. Give *Energy from* **0.01** and *Energy to* **1** for the plot.
5. Keep **Bose-Einstein** and **Maxwell-Boltzmann** ticked. Both read **1.285 × 10⁻¹¹**: the mean quantum number and the chance of an excited positron.
6. Change *Temperature (K)* to **4.2**. Bose-Einstein now reads **0.2001** and Maxwell-Boltzmann **0.1668**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a trap or cavity experiment**: find how cold a mode at any frequency must be to sit in its ground state, by turning the frequency into an energy and reading the Bose-Einstein occupation.
- **Check thermal photon numbers** in superconducting qubits, microwave cavities and millimetre-wave receivers, which follow the same formula with μ = 0.
- **Get cyclotron frequencies for electrons and positrons** at any field with the [[plasma-parameters]] calculator, and for ions by changing the ion mass.
- **Teach quantised motion** with a real experiment: one field, one frequency, one temperature, and the reason for a dilution refrigerator drops out.
- **Show the classical picture next to the quantum one**: [[particle-drifts]] animates the same circular motion with a Boris pusher, before quantum mechanics turns it into rungs.

## The fine print

- We read the authors' preprint, not the published version, for the numbers; the title, authors, licence and absence of corrections come from the Crossref record of the published paper.
- The Bose-Einstein formula assumes the cyclotron motion is in thermal equilibrium with the 300 mK electrodes, which is what the paper says it is. Any stray heating or noise in a real trap would raise the occupation above our numbers.
- The plasma calculator is written for plasmas, but its cyclotron frequency is the single-particle eB/m, so it applies to one positron without change. Relativistic corrections at the ground state are parts per billion and are left out.
- The 83 ms and 4 s lifetimes and the cavity's suppression of decay are the paper's; fibub has no tool that computes cavity-modified spontaneous emission, so we have not checked them.
