In summer a deep lake splits into a warm, light upper layer and a cold, dense lower one. Wind tilts the boundary between them, and when the wind drops the boundary rocks back and forth for days in slow, hidden **internal waves**, many metres high, that stir nutrients and oxygen. A 2025 paper in *Water* modelled Lake Biwa, Japan's largest lake, under warmer, cooler, windier and calmer years and found that warming strengthens the internal waves near the **inertial period**, the time the Earth's spin takes to swing moving water round in a circle. This article recomputes the paper's seasonal wave speeds and inertial period with the maths of the [[ocean-currents]] calculator and adds the **Rossby radius**, the distance over which rotation starts to steer the waves. To get the layer numbers from a real temperature profile, use the [[ctd-cast-viewer]]; for surface waves, the [[wave-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The author ran a three-dimensional model of the lake (a 36 km by 65.5 km grid at 500 m spacing, 86 layers, down to 107.5 m) and changed one thing at a time: air temperature, wind speed and rain. The internal waves were then split into three bands by period: **super-inertial** (6 to 16 h), **near-inertial** (about 16 to 30 h, around the inertial period of about 21 h at 35.2° N) and **sub-inertial** (longer than 30 h, the slow basin-wide waves).

The headline findings: **warmer air** strengthens the stratification and boosts the near-inertial waves, mostly in the thermocline (about 10 to 20 m down); cooler years favour the slow sub-inertial waves. **Stronger wind** adds wave energy at all depths, most in the faster band. Rain changes mainly the surface layer. To explain the seasons, the paper treats the lake as **two layers** in an effective depth of 44 m and gives, for spring, summer and autumn, the upper-layer thickness, the density step, the **reduced gravity** g′ (gravity scaled down by the small density difference, which is why internal waves are so slow), the wave speed and the period of the first basin-scale wave.

## Checked with the tools

The [[ocean-currents]] calculator's Rossby radius card takes the latitude, the water depth, the upper-layer depth and the density step and gives g′ = gΔρ/ρ₀, the two-layer wave speed c = √(g′ h₁h₂/H) and the Rossby radius c/|f|, where f = 2Ω sin(latitude) is the Coriolis parameter. This is a **recomputation** from the numbers in the paper's text, with ρ₀ = 1000 kg/m³ (**our reading**: it is the value that turns the paper's density steps into its g′).

**Step 1: the inertial period.** At 35.2° N the tool's f is 8.41 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹, so the inertial period 2π/f is **20.76 h**, the paper's "about 21 h", in the middle of its 16 to 30 h near-inertial band.

**Step 2: the seasonal wave speeds.** From the layer numbers alone the tool gives g′ = **7.46 × 10⁻³**, **2.65 × 10⁻²** and **6.87 × 10⁻³ m/s²**, and wave speeds of **0.240**, **0.512** and **0.274 m/s** for spring, summer and autumn: the paper's 0.240, 0.511 and 0.273 to the last digit or one unit in it (Figure 1). Summer's internal waves travel about twice as fast as spring's because the density step is three and a half times larger. The barotropic (surface) wave in the same 44 m of water runs at 20.8 m/s, 40 to 90 times faster.

**Step 3: the basin-scale period.** The first basin wave takes the time to cross the lake and back, T = 2L/c. Dividing the paper's three periods (146, 68.5 and 128 h) by our wave speeds gives the same length every time, **63.1 km**, close to the 65.5 km length of the model grid. The paper's periods and speeds are therefore consistent with one basin length.

**Step 4: what the paper does not give, the Rossby radius.** Wave speed over f gives the internal Rossby radius: **2.9 km** in spring, **6.1 km** in summer and **3.3 km** in autumn (Figure 2). All are many times smaller than the 36 km width of the model grid (6 to 13 times), so the Earth's rotation steers the internal waves: they hug the shore as Kelvin waves and swing near the inertial period offshore. In summer the radius doubles: stronger stratification gives the waves more room before rotation takes over, a simple scale to keep beside the paper's finding that warm years shift energy into the near-inertial band (the link is our reading, not tested here).

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/lake-biwa-internal-waves-two-layer-wave-speed-rossby-radius-inertial-period.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `coriolis` and `rossby` from `src/lib/sci/oceancurrents.ts`, the maths behind the [[ocean-currents]] calculator.

The latitude, effective depth, layer thicknesses, density steps, wave speeds and periods come from the paper's text. ρ₀ = 1000 kg/m³ is **our reading** of the paper's g′; the tool uses g = 9.81 m/s². The 63.1 km basin length and the Rossby radii are **our recomputation**, not values in the paper.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/ocean-currents.mjs`, which checks the calculator against hand-worked values:

- The Coriolis parameter at 45° N must be 1.031 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹, negative in the south and zero at the equator.
- A northerly wind at 40° N must move the Ekman transport due west, 90° to the right of the wind, with M = τ/(ρf).
- The two-layer Rossby radius must be undefined when the upper layer fills the whole depth.
- New with this article: the paper's three seasons at 35.2° N must give g′ of 7.456 × 10⁻³, 2.649 × 10⁻² and 6.867 × 10⁻³ m/s², wave speeds of 0.240, 0.512 and 0.274 m/s, Rossby radii of 2.86, 6.09 and 3.26 km, and an inertial period of 20.76 h.

On the [[ocean-currents]] calculator:

1. In *Place and sea water*, type **35.2** for *Latitude* and **1000** for *Sea water density ρ₀*. The line below reads **f = 8.41 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹** (2π ÷ f ÷ 3600 = 20.76 h).
2. In the *Rossby radius* card, type **44** for *Water depth H*, **15** for *Upper layer h₁* and **2.7** for *Density step Δρ*, and clear *Buoyancy frequency N*.
3. *Two-layer baroclinic* reads **6.09 km (g′ = 0.0265 m/s², wave speed 0.512 m/s)**: the paper's summer.
4. Change h₁ to **10** and Δρ to **0.76**: **2.86 km**, g′ **0.00746**, wave speed **0.24 m/s** (spring).
5. *Barotropic* reads **247 km (wave speed 20.8 m/s)**: far bigger than the lake, so the surface seiche feels the shores before it feels the Earth's spin.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a lake or fjord paper** in a minute: type its layer depths and density step into the [[ocean-currents]] calculator and compare wave speeds and periods.
- **Decide whether rotation matters**: compare the internal Rossby radius with the basin width before choosing a model or a mooring layout.
- **Get the layer numbers from data**: read the thermocline depth, density step and N² from a temperature profile with the [[ctd-cast-viewer]].
- **Set analysis bands**: work out the inertial period at your latitude to split a current or temperature record into the paper's super-, near- and sub-inertial bands.
- **Teach stratified flow**: show how a density step of under 1 kg/m³ slows waves from 20 m/s to 0.2 m/s.

## The fine print

- We recomputed the paper's simple two-layer numbers, not its three-dimensional model or the energy changes between climate runs, which come from the model alone.
- The two-layer picture is a simplification; real stratification is smooth, and the first-mode Rossby radius from N (which the tool also gives) can differ.
- ρ₀ = 1000 kg/m³ and the 63.1 km basin length are our reading and inference.
- The wave speed rounds to 0.512 and 0.274 m/s where the paper gives 0.511 and 0.273, a difference in the last digit.
