The Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index System asks for one weather reading a day: temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and the last 24 hours of rain, taken at **local noon**. That is easy at a weather station and awkward on a global grid, because a reanalysis like ERA5 stores whole fields by the hour in UTC, and a single UTC hour is local noon in only one strip of a country 95 degrees of longitude wide. A 2026 dataset paper in *Earth System Science Data* rebuilt the whole Canadian FWI record from ERA5 weather taken at **each grid cell's own solar noon**, and found the index rises by up to 3.4 in the west. The [[fire-weather-index]] tool runs the same equations on weather you supply, so the size of that effect can be rebuilt from first principles. To bring in your own station records use the [[station-data-viewer]], and to turn temperature and humidity into the moisture quantities behind them use the [[psychrometric-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors produced **ERA5-FWI-SN**: daily Fire Weather Index System values for Canada from 1950 to 2024 on the ERA5 grid (0.25°, about 31 km), with every cell's weather taken at its own solar noon. They compare it with the usual approach, a single UTC hour for the whole country, and validate both against about 650 stations in the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System for 2015 to 2024.

Why the hour matters is simple geometry. One degree of longitude is about **4 minutes** of solar time, and the equation of time adds up to ±16 minutes. Canada covers six time zones, so solar noon differs by about **104 minutes** between Havre-Saint-Pierre on the Gulf of St Lawrence and Thunder Bay on Lake Superior; even inside the eastern daylight zone, 1,900 km wide, the lag reaches 68 minutes. Fix the UTC hour and you are reading mid-morning weather in the west and mid-afternoon weather in the east.

The results: peak monthly mean FWI of **18, 26 and 29** in June, July and August by the solar-noon method against 17, 25 and 28 by the UTC method, with the difference reaching **+3.4 in July west of Alberta** and +2.6 in August west of Saskatchewan, and going slightly negative, about **−0.6**, in the east. Validation correlations are much the same either way (0.70 to 0.86, median RMSE just above 0.6), so this is a bias question, not an accuracy contest.

## Checked with the tools

We cannot recompute ERA5 in a browser. What we can do is **rebuild the mechanism** and see whether the FWI equations really move by the amounts the paper reports. Everything below is a **simulation with our own weather**, clearly labelled, run through the same Van Wagner equations the tool uses.

**Step 1: the start-up codes and the severity rating match exactly.** The paper starts each fire season from FFMC 85, DMC 6 and DC 15. Those are the tool's own default start-up values, so nothing needs changing. The paper also gives the Daily Severity Rating as 0.0272 × FWI^1.77; the tool's `dsr` is that formula, and it turns the paper's peak monthly means of 18, 26 and 29 into severity ratings of **4.53, 8.69 and 10.54**. A monthly mean FWI that climbs 61 % from June to August more than doubles the severity rating, because the exponent is 1.77.

**Step 2: how much does the hour matter?** We generated 92 summer days (June to August) of daily mean temperature, humidity, wind and rain with a fixed random seed, then gave every day a standard shape that peaks at 15:00 local solar time: ±7 °C on temperature, ∓20 points on relative humidity and ±3 km/h on wind (**all our assumptions**). Reading that same summer at each hour of the day and running the full FWI series each time gives Figure 1. Mean FWI climbs steeply through the morning, from **1.8** if the weather is read at 08:00 to **8.6** at solar noon and a peak of **12.1** at 15:00, then falls away again as the air cools.

**Step 3: west and east, from one UTC hour.** Take a single 18:00 UTC field. At 114° W, in western Alberta, that is **96 minutes before** solar noon; at 63.6° W, on the Quebec coast, it is **106 minutes after**. Running our summer at those two hours and at solar noon:

| Reading taken at | Mean FWI over the 92 days | Solar noon minus this |
|---|---|---|
| Solar noon (12:00 solar) | 8.60 | – |
| 18:00 UTC at 114° W (10:24 solar) | 5.41 | **+3.18** |
| 18:00 UTC at 63.6° W (13:46 solar) | 11.43 | **−2.83** |

Month by month the western difference is **+3.10 in June, +3.39 in July and +3.06 in August**. The paper's largest July difference, west of Alberta, is **+3.4**. The sign pattern is the paper's too: positive in the west, where a fixed UTC hour reads the cool, damp morning, and negative in the east, where it reads the hot, dry afternoon. **Step 4: the effect grows with the danger.** Rerunning the same simulation with a drought summer (warmer, drier, less rain, mean FWI 21.5 instead of 8.6) raises the western difference to **+6.31** and the eastern one to −5.41. That is why the paper's map shows its biggest positive differences in the dry interior west rather than evenly across the country: the index is a product of moisture codes and a spread index, so a fixed offset in the input weather matters more when everything is already dry.

| Check | Paper | Our simulation |
|---|---|---|
| Solar noon spread across Canada | about 104 min between two named places | 96 min west and 106 min east of an 18:00 UTC field |
| Largest July difference, west | +3.4 | +3.39 (mean FWI 8.6 summer) |
| August difference, west | +2.6 | +3.06 |
| Difference in the east | about −0.6 | −2.83 (our fixed hour is probably later than theirs) |
| Daily Severity Rating from peak monthly FWI | formula given | 4.53, 8.69, 10.54 for FWI 18, 26, 29 |
| Start-up codes | FFMC 85, DMC 6, DC 15 | the tool's defaults, unchanged |

Our eastern difference is four times the paper's. That is the honest limit of this exercise: the paper does not say which UTC hour its comparison uses, and a product built for Canada would choose an hour closer to local noon in the populated east than our 18:00. The western magnitude, where the geometry forces a large offset whatever hour you pick, lands on the paper's number.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/fire-weather-index-solar-noon-sampling-hour-canada.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fwiSeries` (the whole FWI System day by day: FFMC, DMC, DC, ISI, BUI, FWI and DSR, with the day-length factors) and `dsr` from `src/lib/sci/fwi.ts`, the maths behind the [[fire-weather-index]] tool.

From the paper: the start-up codes, the DSR formula, the 4 minutes per degree of longitude, the two longitudes and the monthly mean FWI values we convert to severity ratings. **Our assumptions**: the 92 days of daily mean weather (fixed seed), the shape of the day and its amplitudes, the choice of 18:00 UTC as the fixed hour, and the drought variant. None of it is ERA5 data, a station record or a fire danger rating for anywhere.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/fire-weather-index.mjs`, which pins the tool against xclim 1.0, the Python port of the Canadian Forest Service's own `cffdrs` code:

- All seven outputs over a made-up 150-day series with a long drought and two 60 mm rain days must match xclim day by day, worst relative difference under 2 × 10⁻⁴.
- On the tool's 30-day example the last day must give FFMC 84.821901, DMC 11.875516, DC 50.940257, ISI 7.247944, BUI 15.005556 and FWI 9.38879, with a peak FWI of 15.4516465 and a mean DSR of 0.7145806.
- Edge cases: FFMC never leaves 0 to 101, BUI is 0 when both its codes are 0, and DSR is exactly 0.0272 × FWI^1.77.
- New with this article: from the paper's start-up codes, one day of 20.6 °C, 36 % humidity, 9.7 km/h wind and 3.1 mm of rain must give FFMC 77.3982219, ISI 1.4630010 and FWI 0.7540320; the same day read 96 minutes earlier (18.2 °C, 43 %, 8.6 km/h) must give FFMC 73.3580548 and FWI 0.5439865, and 106 minutes later (22.3 °C, 31 %, 10.4 km/h) FFMC 80.2129486 and FWI 1.1992482.

## Check it yourself

On the [[fire-weather-index|Fire Weather Index tool]]:

1. In *Start-up codes*, leave *FFMC on the day before* **85**, *DMC on the day before* **6** and *DC on the day before* **15**: the paper's values are already the defaults.
2. In *Daily noon weather*, clear the box and paste three days of our solar-noon weather, one row per day as temperature, humidity, wind and rain: **20.6, 36, 9.7, 3.1**; **22.1, 33, 10.5, 0**; **23.4, 29, 12.0, 0**.
3. Set *Month, if rows have no date* to **July**.
4. The table shows FFMC climbing 77.4, 88.4, 91.4 and FWI 0.75, 6.45, **11.69**: three dry days after light rain are enough to turn the fine fuels.
5. Now replace the three rows with the same days read 96 minutes earlier, the way a fixed UTC hour reads them in the west: **18.2, 43, 8.6, 3.1**; **19.7, 40, 9.4, 0**; **21.0, 35, 10.9, 0**. Every code comes out lower: FFMC 73.4, 85.3, 89.4 and FWI 0.54, 3.72, **8.25**, so the last day's FWI is about 30 % smaller for the same weather, just read earlier.
6. Press **Download every day (CSV)** on each run and subtract the two columns to see the gap build up as the moisture codes drift apart.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check what sampling hour a gridded FWI product used**, by running your own station weather at several hours and seeing which matches the grid.
- **Put a number on a bias** before trusting a trend: run a season twice, an hour apart, and compare the means.
- **Convert FWI to Daily Severity Rating** correctly, remembering the 1.77 exponent, so severity-weighted season totals are comparable.
- **Teach the system**: step through a drying spell and watch FFMC respond in hours, DMC in days and DC in months.
- **Sanity-check a reported seasonal mean**: the paper's national average is about 5 and provincial averages 2 to 10, which is a useful scale for any new series.

## The fine print

- Our weather is **simulated**. It is not ERA5, not a station record, and the figures are not a fire danger rating for any place.
- The paper does not state which UTC hour its comparison uses, so our 18:00 UTC is an assumption. It sets how large our east-west contrast is.
- We used a single shape for every day, peaking at 15:00. Real diurnal cycles vary with cloud, front passages and coastal breezes, and the FWI System's own calibration assumes noon readings stand in for mid-afternoon peak conditions.
- We ignored the equation of time (±16 minutes) and the overwintering of the Drought Code, both of which the paper handles properly.
- The FWI System was built for Canadian conifer forests. Index values elsewhere need local calibration before they mean anything operationally.
