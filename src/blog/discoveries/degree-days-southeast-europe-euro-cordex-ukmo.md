**Degree days** are a simple way to turn weather into energy demand. A heating degree day counts how far, and for how long, the air was colder than a chosen **base temperature**; a cooling degree day counts how far it was warmer than another base. Add them up over a month and you get a number that tracks how much fuel a building needs to stay warm, or how much electricity its air conditioning will use. A new study from Bulgaria's National Institute of Meteorology and Hydrology maps both across Southeast Europe for today and for the end of this century. This article shows how the [[degree-days]] calculator, which now has the same **UK Met Office** method the study uses, lets anyone check the numbers; for the climate type of a place see [[koppen-climate]], and for crops and pests [[insect-degree-days]].

## The paper in brief

The authors took 19 combinations of global and regional climate models from EURO-CORDEX, the European branch of a worldwide downscaling project, on a grid about 12 km wide over 35-50° N and 15-30° E. They compared the models for 1976-2005 with ERA5-Land, a detailed reconstruction of past weather, and then ran them forward to 2066-2095 under a middle (RCP4.5) and a high (RCP8.5) emissions path.

Heating degree days use a base of **15.5 °C**, cooling degree days **22 °C**. Each day is put into one of four **categories** by where the base falls between the day's minimum, mean and maximum (wholly cold, mostly cold, mostly warm, wholly warm), and each category has its own formula. This is the UK Met Office (UKMO) method.

Today, January needs about **400 to 500** heating degree days on the plains and up to 700 in the Carpathians. July and August give about **75 to 90** cooling degree days in the hottest lowlands: the lower Danube, the Thracian valley and the Greek and Turkish coasts. By 2066-2095, winter heating falls by up to about 70 degree days a month under RCP4.5 and 120-125 under RCP8.5, while cooling grows by a larger share, and the cooling season gets longer.

## Checked with the tools

The Degree days calculator had four ways of drawing a day (mean, modified average, single sine, single triangle). The paper uses the UKMO four-case formulas, so we **added "UK Met Office"** to the tool's *Method* list, with tests (below). Here is what it does on one day from 12 °C to 24 °C, heating base 15.5 °C:

- The mean, 18 °C, is above the base, but the night dips below it. That is the "mostly warm" case, and UKMO counts a quarter of the gap from the minimum: (15.5 − 12) ÷ 4 = **0.875** heating degree days.
- The single sine method, which draws the day as a smooth wave, gives **0.828**. The mean method gives **0**, because it only looks at the 18 °C average.

Figure 1 runs the same comparison for cooling at the paper's 22 °C base over a range of days. On days that cross the base, the mean method misses the warm afternoons; UKMO and the sine stay within about 0.4 degree days of each other.

**Are the paper's numbers sensible?** We built a **made-up year** for a lowland site in Southeast Europe, from our own rough monthly normals (−1 °C in January, 24 °C in July) with a day-to-day wobble, and ran it through the tool at the paper's bases. It is a simulation, not the paper's data, but it shows whether the published ranges hang together.

| Check | Paper | Our tool result (made-up year) |
|---|---|---|
| January heating, plains (base 15.5 °C) | about 400-500 | 492 (January mean −0.4 °C) |
| July cooling, hot lowlands (base 22 °C) | about 75-90 | 97 by UKMO, 100 by sine, 58 by the mean method |
| January heating, every day 2.5 °C warmer | falls by up to 70 (RCP4.5), 120-125 (RCP8.5) | falls by 78, to 415 |
| July cooling, every day 2.5 °C warmer | rises by tens of degree days | rises by 57, to 154 |
| Year: cooling against heating | cooling changes most, in relative terms | heating −24 %, cooling +77 % |

A January near −0.4 °C, about right for the lower Danube plain, gives a heating total inside the paper's 400-500 range. July lands just above the paper's 75-90, so our made-up summer is a little hot. The pattern of change matches: warming takes a fixed number of degree days off winter, which is a modest share of a large total, but adds a similar number to summer, which is a big share of a small one (Figure 2).

The mean method would have given only 106 cooling degree days for the whole year, against 258 by UKMO: on a 22 °C base, most cooling happens on afternoons of days whose average stays below it. That is why the method matters for cooling far more than for heating.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/degree-days-southeast-europe-euro-cordex-ukmo.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `heating`, `cooling`, `degreeDays` and `monthly` from `src/lib/sci/degreedays.ts`, the maths behind the [[degree-days]] calculator, with the new `ukmo` method (functions `ukmoAbove` and `ukmoBelow`).

Where the inputs come from: the bases (15.5 °C and 22 °C), the method and the ranges in the table are from the paper. The monthly normals, daily ranges and the 2.5 °C warming are **our assumptions** for a made-up lowland site; the day-to-day wobble comes from a fixed pseudo-random sequence, so the script gives the same year every time. Nothing here is EURO-CORDEX model output.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci-tools/degree-days.mjs` (run by `tests/sci.mjs`) checks the tool. For the new method it works one day through each of the four cases by hand from the Met Office / CIBSE TM41 formulas:

- A day from −4 °C to 6 °C with a 15.5 °C base gives **14.5** heating degree days (the whole day is cold).
- A day from 12 °C to 24 °C gives **0.875** heating and, at a 22 °C base, **0.5** cooling degree days.
- A day from 16 °C to 30 °C at a 22 °C base gives **2.5** cooling degree days; one from 23 °C to 33 °C gives **6**.
- Heating minus cooling at one base must equal the base minus the day's mean, as it does for the sine and triangle methods.

The older methods are checked against an independent Python script that samples each day's curve at 200,000 points; for example, a day from 10 °C to 30 °C at an 18 °C base must give 2.247 heating and 4.247 cooling degree days by the sine.

## Check it yourself

On the [[degree-days]] calculator:

1. In *Date, minimum and maximum, one day per row*, paste three days:
   `2025-07-01,16,30`, `2025-07-02,12,24` and `2025-07-03,23,33` (one per line).
2. Set *Method* to **UK Met Office**.
3. Type **15.5** in *Heating base* and **22** in *Cooling base*. Leave *Growing base* at 10 and *Growing upper cutoff* at 30.
4. The result reads **Heating 0.9, cooling 9.0, growing 38.3 degree days (°C)**: the cool second day adds the 0.875 of heating, and the three days add 2.5, 0.5 and 6 of cooling.
5. Switch *Method* to **Mean**: cooling drops to **7.0** and heating to **0.0**, because the mean method ignores the cool night and the part-day above 22 °C.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn station or model temperatures into heating and cooling demand** with the paper's bases and method, then download the months as CSV with the [[degree-days]] calculator.
- **Compare methods** on your own data before choosing one, especially for cooling, where the mean method can miss half the total.
- **Sanity-check a published map** by running one typical year for a grid cell and seeing whether its totals sit in the published range.
- **Test a warming scenario** by adding a fixed number of degrees to every day and comparing the totals, as we did above.
- **Teach** why heating falls a little and cooling rises a lot, in relative terms, as a climate warms.

## The fine print

- Our year is made up from rough normals; real years vary a great deal, and the paper averages 30 of them across 19 models.
- Adding the same 2.5 °C to every day is a crude warming; models change winters and summers, nights and days, by different amounts.
- Degree days stand in for energy use only roughly: insulation, sunshine, wind and people's habits matter too, and the right base differs between buildings.
