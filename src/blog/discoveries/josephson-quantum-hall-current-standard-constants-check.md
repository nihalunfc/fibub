Since 2019 the ampere is *defined* by fixing the value of the elementary charge, but turning that definition into a real current in a laboratory is hard. One practical route uses Ohm's law with two quantum effects: a voltage from a **Josephson junction array** (microwaves make a superconducting junction give steps of voltage fixed by frequency alone) divided by a resistance from the **quantum Hall effect** (a resistance fixed by nature, h/e²). A 2025 Brazilian paper describes this chain for the Inmetro current standard. We used the [[physical-constants]] page and a small script to recompute the numbers the chain rests on; the [[dimensional-analysis]] checker and the [[uncertainty-calculator]] are the tools for the next checks, units and error budgets.

## The paper in brief

The authors explain the three ingredients and the way they combine. A Josephson voltage is V = n f / K_J, where f is the microwave frequency, n a whole number (the step) and K_J = 2e/h the Josephson constant. A quantum Hall resistance is R_H = R_K / i, where R_K = h/e² is the von Klitzing constant and i is a whole number, the plateau. A **cryogenic current comparator**, a superconducting transformer with two windings of N_H = 16 and N_S = 2065 turns, scales that resistance to a convenient standard resistor, such as 100 Ω, and then I = V/R. They calibrate a high-end multimeter calibrator in ranges of 200 µA, 2.2 mA and 22 mA with standard resistors of 10 kΩ, 100 Ω and 10 Ω, and report relative uncertainties between 0.5 and 4.6 µA/A (parts in a million).

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from exact SI constants**. The paper's measurement tables are not recomputed; they come from a lab, not from a formula.

**Step 1: the constants in the paper.** From the exact values h and e, the Josephson constant is K_J = 2e/h = **483 597.848 417 GHz/V** (4.835 978 484 17 × 10¹⁴ Hz/V) and the von Klitzing constant is R_K = h/e² = **25 812.807 459 3045 Ω**. The paper prints R_K as 25 812.807 459 3045 Ω: it agrees with the exact value to 6 × 10⁻¹⁶, i.e. every printed digit. It prints K_J as 483 597 848.416 984 **GHz/V**, which is exactly **1000 times** the true value in those units: the digits are right, but they are in MHz/V (483 597 848.417 MHz/V), not GHz/V. That is a unit slip in the paper's typesetting; the numbers that follow in the paper do not seem to depend on it.

The [[physical-constants]] page lists both: *Josephson constant* K_J = 483 597.8484… × 10⁹ Hz/V and *von Klitzing constant* R_K = 25 812.807 45… Ω. The "…" means the page shows the first ten digits of an exact value: K_J is 3.5 × 10⁻¹¹ below the exact value and R_K 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ below it, plenty for any lab claim shorter than a primary standard. The page's flux quantum (2.067 833 848 × 10⁻¹⁵ Wb) and conductance quantum (7.748 091 729 × 10⁻⁵ S) agree with h/2e and 2e²/h to 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ and 1 × 10⁻¹⁰, and K_J × R_K = 2/e to 15 digits.

**Step 2: how many junctions make a volt.** One voltage step per junction is f/K_J: **41.3567 µV** at 20 GHz and **155.0875 µV** at 75 GHz, the two drive frequencies in the paper. So 10 V, the programmable-voltage-standard limit the paper quotes, needs **241 799** junctions at 20 GHz or **64 480** at 75 GHz in the first step; 1 V needs 24 180 or 6 448. The three voltages the paper's ranges imply (1 V across 10 kΩ at 100 µA, 0.2 V across 100 Ω at 2 mA and 0.1 V across 10 Ω at 10 mA) need 24 180, 4 836 and 2 418 junctions at 20 GHz.

**Step 3: which resistor the windings imply.** The paper quotes the two winding numbers but not the plateau. The ratio of turns is 2065/16 = 129.0625. Dividing the quantum Hall resistance down by that ratio gives:

| Plateau i | R_K / i | After the 16:2065 comparator |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 25 812.8075 Ω | **200.0024 Ω** |
| 2 | 12 906.4037 Ω | **100.0012 Ω** |
| 3 | 8 604.2692 Ω | 66.6675 Ω |
| 4 | 6 453.2019 Ω | 50.0006 Ω |

Only the **second plateau** lands next to the 100 Ω standard resistor the paper uses, **12 parts per million** above it, so our reading (**our assumption**, not the paper's text) is that the comparator takes the i = 2 plateau at 12 906.4 Ω down to 100 Ω. The 12 ppm offset is not an error: the resistor is simply measured against the quantum value, and the offset is its calibrated deviation.

**Step 4: a current counted in electrons per second.** Put the pieces together for one junction step on the second plateau at 20 GHz: V = 41.3567 µV across the comparator-scaled 100.0012 Ω gives **0.41356 µA**. The same number follows from the formula I = n i f e N_S / (2 N_H) = 0.41356 µA, which has no h in it at all: the current is a frequency times the elementary charge times whole numbers and a ratio of turns, which is the whole point of a quantum current standard.

## How we made the graphs

The script is `scripts/blog-figures/josephson-quantum-hall-current-standard-constants-check.mjs`. It reads h, e, K_J, R_K, the flux quantum and the conductance quantum from `src/lib/sci/phconstants.ts` (CODATA 2022 edition), the data behind the [[physical-constants]] page, and computes K_J = 2e/h and R_K = h/e² from the exact h and e. The two drive frequencies, the printed K_J and R_K, the 10 V limit and the winding numbers come from the paper. The plateau choice i = 2 is **our assumption**. Nothing is fitted or simulated.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/physical-constants.mjs`, which compares each constant with the physics linking it to the others:

- the exact SI defining values (c, h, e, k, N_A, the caesium frequency and the luminous efficacy);
- ε₀ μ₀ c² = 1, α = e²/(4π ε₀ ħ c) and the Bohr radius, Rydberg constant and Hartree energy from α and the electron mass;
- relations among the derived electromagnetic constants.
- New with this article: K_J = 2e/h and R_K = h/e² from the exact h and e must match the page to 1 × 10⁻⁹, K_J × R_K = 2/e must hold, and the paper's printed R_K must agree with h/e² to 1 × 10⁻¹⁵.

## Check it yourself

On the [[physical-constants]] page:

1. Type **josephson** in *Search*. The page lists *Josephson constant* K_J = 483 597.8484… × 10⁹ Hz V⁻¹ (483 597.8484 GHz/V, not 483 597 848 GHz/V).
2. Type **klitzing**: *von Klitzing constant* R_K = 25 812.807 45… Ω.
3. Divide R_K by 2 (12 906.4037 Ω), then multiply by 16 and divide by 2065: **100.0012 Ω**.
4. Divide 20 × 10⁹ Hz by K_J (4.835978484 × 10¹⁴ Hz/V): **41.3567 µV**, the voltage of one step at 20 GHz.
5. Divide the voltage by the resistance: **0.41356 µA**. Press *Copy* next to the elementary charge to see the same current as f e (20 × 10⁹ × 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.2044 nA) times 129.0625.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a printed constant before reusing it.** A factor 1000 hiding in a unit is a one-minute catch with the constants page (physical constants).
- **Size a Josephson array for a target voltage** at any drive frequency (physical constants).
- **Trace a unit through a formula** such as I = f e N_S/N_H to confirm it comes out in amperes (dimensional analysis).
- **Build an uncertainty budget** for a quantum current standard from the voltage, resistance and comparator contributions (uncertainty calculator).
- **Teach the 2019 SI**: show students that K_J and R_K are now exact numbers, not measurements.

## The fine print

- The paper is a description of an operating principle. We recomputed its constants and a few consequences, not its calibration tables. Its relative errors for the calibrator (reaching 174 µA/A at the largest, against stated uncertainties of at most 7.2 µA/A, as the extraction we read lists them) are larger than its uncertainties, which the paper's table shows but, in the text we could read, does not explain; check the original before drawing conclusions.
- The plateau i = 2 is our inference from the winding numbers; the paper does not state it.
- The paper's Josephson junction count and the 10 V limit are quoted from its text; the junction counts here assume one voltage step (n = 1) per junction, while real arrays can use higher steps.
- The physical-constants page shows 10 digits of exact values and ends them with "…"; use h and e for full precision.
