In mid-November 2026, Voyager 1 became the first human-made object to sit one light-day from Earth: a command sent from a Deep Space Network dish now takes a full day to arrive, and the reply another day to come back. What the spacecraft still sends home, nearly fifty years after launch, includes one of the most direct measurements of interstellar space we have: the density of its free electrons. A 2024 paper by the Voyager plasma wave team shows that density stopped rising in 2020 and has held at about **0.147 electrons per cubic centimetre** out to 161.4 au. The [[plasma-parameters]] calculator turns that one number into the radio tone Voyager hears and the kind of plasma it is flying through, and the [[link-budget-calculator]] shows why one light-day costs the radio link so little. For how charged particles move in that thin, magnetised gas, see [[particle-drifts]].

## The paper in brief

Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the edge of the Sun's bubble of solar wind, in **2012**, and Voyager 2 followed in **2018**. Neither carries a working instrument that measures interstellar plasma directly, so the density comes from radio. A plasma rings at its own **electron plasma frequency**, which depends only on the electron density. When a shock from the Sun passes, the plasma oscillates loudly at that frequency; between shocks, the thermal motion of the electrons makes a faint, steady hum there, called the **quasi-thermal noise line**. Voyager 1's wideband receiver still resolves that line, so the team can read the density off the frequency.

The paper reports densities out to **161.4 au** (an au is the Earth-Sun distance). Inside the interstellar medium the density had been climbing with distance, with jumps at shocks and pressure fronts. Since a pressure pulse seen in **2020, around day 146, at about 149 au**, it has stayed close to **0.147 cm⁻³** on observations up to 2023. The author's reading is that Voyager 1 has reached a broad density peak, and possibly a new regime of the medium just outside the heliosphere.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated density**, plus three **assumptions of ours** where the paper's abstract gives nothing: a temperature of 7,000 K, the usual value for the local interstellar cloud; a magnetic field of 0.5 nT, the size Voyager 1's magnetometer has reported out there; and protons as the ions.

**Step 1: the tone behind the number.** The tool gives an electron plasma frequency of **3.44 kHz** for 0.147 cm⁻³. That is the frequency Voyager's receiver has to find. It does not depend on the temperature or field at all, so this part of the check uses only the paper's number. Running it backwards is just as useful: a line at 3.00 kHz would mean 0.112 cm⁻³, and one at 2.60 kHz would mean 0.084 cm⁻³. Because the frequency goes as the square root of the density, a 10 % rise in density moves the line by only about 5 %, which is why a receiver with fine frequency resolution matters so much here.

**Step 2: what kind of plasma this is.** With our 7,000 K, the **Debye length is 15.1 m**: beyond that distance, the electric field of any one charge is screened out by its neighbours. Voyager's 10 m antennas are comparable to it, which is exactly the condition that makes quasi-thermal noise measurable at all. A Debye sphere holds about **2.1 × 10⁹ electrons**, so this is a textbook ideal plasma. The electron mean free path is about **0.10 au**, about 15 million km, so for anything smaller than that, the medium is collisionless. Try 30,000 K instead, a temperature some models give for the compressed gas just beyond the heliopause: the plasma frequency stays at 3.44 kHz, the Debye length doubles to 31.2 m, and the mean free path grows to 1.7 au.

**Step 3: how the field shapes it.** At 0.5 nT, an electron circles a field line in **3.7 km** and a proton in about **158 km**, tiny next to the distances Voyager covers in a day (about 1.5 million km). The plasma beta, gas pressure over magnetic pressure, is **0.29**: the field matters as much as the gas, so shocks from the Sun travel as magnetised waves at an Alfvén speed of **28.5 km/s**. That fits the picture the paper draws, of density jumps that arrive with jumps in the field.

**Step 4: the radio link at one light-day.** The paper's farthest density, at 161.4 au, came from signals that took **22.4 hours** to reach Earth; at 149 au it was **20.7 hours**. One light-day is **173.1 au**. Going from 149 au to one light-day raises the free-space loss at Voyager's 8.4 GHz downlink from **317.9 dB** to **319.2 dB**, only **1.3 dB** more. Distance enters a link budget through its logarithm, so the hard part was the first 1 au (274.4 dB of loss); the last 24 au cost about as much as a slightly dirty dish.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/voyager-1-interstellar-electron-density-0-147-plasma-frequency.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `plasma()` from `src/lib/sci/phplasma.ts`, the maths behind the [[plasma-parameters]] calculator, for the plasma frequency, Debye length, Debye number, gyroradii, mean free path, beta and Alfvén speed, and `fspl()` from `src/lib/sci/linkbudget.ts`, the maths behind the [[link-budget-calculator]], for the free-space path loss. The light time is the distance divided by the speed of light, using the same constant as the link budget maths.

The density (0.147 cm⁻³) and the distances (149 and 161.4 au) come from the paper's abstract. The 7,000 K temperature, the 0.5 nT field and the choice of protons are **our assumptions**, and none of them changes the plasma frequency. The 8.4 GHz downlink and the date of the one-light-day milestone come from NASA.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/plasma-parameters.mjs`, which pins this maths against the NRL Plasma Formulary and an independent SciPy script:

- A plasma of 10²⁰ m⁻³ must give an electron plasma frequency of 89.8 GHz, the formulary's 8.98 × 10³ √n Hz with n in cm⁻³, and a Debye length of 7.43 × 10⁻⁵ m at 10 keV.
- The same plasma at 5 T must give the formulary's electron cyclotron frequency of 140 GHz and the SciPy Alfvén speed of 7.739 × 10⁶ m/s.
- New with this article: Voyager 1's 0.147 cm⁻³ at 7,000 K and 0.5 nT must give a plasma frequency of 3.4425 kHz, a Debye length of 15.06 m and a beta of 0.2856.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Read a density off a radio spectrum**: type a density until the plasma frequency matches the line you see, or use fₚₑ ∝ √nₑ directly, as the Voyager team does with every quasi-thermal noise detection.
- **Check whether an antenna can see quasi-thermal noise**: compare the Debye length the [[plasma-parameters]] tool gives with the antenna length; the method needs them to be of similar size or the antenna longer.
- **Decide whether a model needs collisions**: the electron mean free path of about 0.1 au says that for structures smaller than that, interstellar plasma behaves as collisionless.
- **Plan a deep-space link**: the [[link-budget-calculator]] shows that a probe twice as far away loses only 6 dB, and what dish, power or data rate makes that up.
- **Teach plasma physics with a real spacecraft**: one density, one tone, and the same page gives every other scale; [[particle-drifts]] then shows the gyration at 0.5 nT.

## The fine print

- We could not read the journal's article page from here. Every value from the paper comes from its abstract, and the licence and absence of corrections from the Crossref record.
- The temperature and field are our assumptions. They change the Debye length, gyroradii and beta, but not the plasma frequency, which depends on density alone.
- The tool's formulas are classical and assume a single temperature; the real medium may hold a hotter population of electrons, which shifts the quasi-thermal noise line's shape but not its frequency.
- The path loss is free-space loss only. A full budget for Voyager also needs its transmitter power, both antenna gains and the receiver noise, which the [[link-budget-calculator]] takes as inputs.
- Light times here are from the paper's distances to the Sun; the distance from Earth changes by up to 1 au over the year.
