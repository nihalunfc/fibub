Nigeria's Jos Plateau is built of Jurassic "Younger Granite" ring complexes, the rocks that made the country a tin and niobium producer. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Earth Science* reprocesses the regional aeromagnetic grid over that province and places mineralised structures **100 to 300 m below the surface**, with magnetic anomalies of a few nT up to about 66 nT. A depth from a magnetic map is never measured directly: it is read off the shape of an anomaly, so it is worth asking what shape such a body would actually make. That is what a forward model is for, and this article runs the paper's own numbers through the [[magnetic-anomaly]] modeller. For the same ground from other angles, try the [[gravity-corrections|gravity survey corrections]] and the [[ves-inversion|VES inversion]].

## The paper in brief

The authors take the national aeromagnetic grid over the Younger Granite region: flight lines **500 m apart**, tie lines 5,000 m apart, flown at **80 m nominal** height (the paper also quotes a 120 m average), in blocks of 55 by 55 km. They remove the main field, then apply the usual chain of enhancements: reduction to the magnetic equator, a Butterworth band-pass, the analytic signal, source parameter imaging (SPI), Euler deconvolution and a 3D inversion.

The results they report are: magnetic highs above about **66 nT** and lows below about **−5.3 nT**, with mixed anomalies of **+7 to +66 nT** and **−1 to −22 nT** over three target areas; SPI depths of **83.4 to 110 m** and **92 to 167 m** for a shallow magnetic layer and **174 to 450 m** for a deeper one; Euler solutions with structural index 0 (the index for a thin sheet) clustering at **100 to 300 m**, which they read as dykes; and a 3D inversion whose mineralised bodies sit mostly between **100 and 500 m**, with susceptibilities kept below **0.004 SI**.

## Checked with the tools

We cannot refit their grid: the maps are images and the readings are not published. What the tool can do is the other direction, which is just as useful: **take the bodies the paper proposes and compute the anomaly they must make.** If the modelled anomaly is far smaller than the one measured, or far narrower than the flight-line spacing, the interpretation needs more than the data can give.

The model is a vertical **thin dyke**, 30 m thick and 2,000 m down dip, at a range of depths, in a main field of **33,500 nT with an inclination of 0°** — the Jos Plateau lies within a degree or two of the magnetic dip equator, and the paper works with an idealised reduction to the magnetic equator. The field strength, the dyke's thickness and the 0.02 SI susceptibility of a magnetite-bearing granite are **our assumptions**; the depths, the sensor height and the anomaly sizes are the paper's.

**Step 1: near the magnetic equator a dyke reads as a low, not a high.** With the field almost horizontal, the induced magnetisation lies across the dyke, and the total-field anomaly over the body is negative with only a whisper of a high on the flanks (Figure 1). Our 30 m dyke gives a low of **−13.1 nT** with its top 100 m down, **−8.6 nT** at 200 m and **−6.3 nT** at 300 m. That sits squarely inside the paper's reported −1 to −22 nT band, so the negative anomalies need nothing unusual.

**Step 2: the 66 nT highs cannot come from a thin dyke.** The anomaly is strictly proportional to susceptibility and to thickness, so the arithmetic is easy. To reach 22 nT at 150 m depth, our 30 m dyke needs **χ = 0.042 SI**, ten times the 0.004 SI ceiling used in the paper's own 3D inversion, or a thickness of **63 m** at 0.02 SI. To reach 66 nT it needs **χ = 0.126 SI** or a thickness of **190 m** — no longer a dyke. Swap the dyke for a **400 by 400 m plug** (a ring-complex stock) running from 150 m down to 2,000 m, and the same 0.02 SI susceptibility gives a peak-to-trough anomaly of **43.4 nT**, rising to **56.9 nT** with the top at 100 m. The paper's largest anomalies therefore point to wide, plug-like bodies, while the thin-sheet Euler solutions can only own the small ones.

**Step 3: the flight lines are too far apart for the shallow class.** The width of the modelled low, measured at half its depth, is **1.6 to 1.8 times the distance from the sensor to the top of the body** — a handy rule in itself, since it turns round to *depth below the sensor ≈ 0.57 × width*. For a top 100 m down and a sensor 120 m up that width is **399 m**; at 200 m depth it is 559 m and at 450 m it is 923 m (Figure 2). With flight lines **500 m apart**, an anomaly from the shallow 83 to 167 m class is sampled by one line, sometimes two. Depths for that class are therefore along-line estimates with no control across line, and the 174 to 450 m class is the one the survey geometry can really support.

**Step 4: the flight height matters more than it looks.** Flying at 120 m instead of 80 m weakens our 150 m dyke from **−12.5 nT to −10.4 nT**, a **16 % loss**, because the sensor-to-body distance grows from 230 to 270 m. A paper that quotes both heights is quoting two different amplitude scales, and any susceptibility taken from amplitude alone inherits that 16 %.

**Step 5: remanence rearranges the picture.** Younger Granite magnetite can carry a frozen-in magnetisation. Give the same 150 m dyke a remanence of 2 A/m at an inclination of 60° (**our assumption**) and the induced magnetisation of 0.533 A/m is swamped: the Koenigsberger ratio is **Q = 3.75**, the low deepens to **−38.5 nT** and a high of **+11.4 nT** appears 117 m to one side. Remanence is the simplest way to get strong, lopsided, mixed-sign anomalies out of modest bodies, and it is the one thing a susceptibility-only inversion cannot see.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/jos-plateau-younger-granite-dyke-depth-magnetic-anomaly.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `profile`, `magnetisation` and `koenigsberger` from `src/lib/sci/magnetic.ts`, the maths behind the [[magnetic-anomaly]] modeller: the thin dyke is a closed-form integral of a sheet of two-dimensional dipoles, and the plug is the surface-pole solution for a uniformly magnetised prism.

From the paper: the depths (100, 150, 200, 300 and 450 m), the sensor heights (80 and 120 m), the 500 m flight-line spacing, the anomaly sizes (22 and 66 nT) and the 0.004 SI susceptibility ceiling. **Our assumptions:** the main field (33,500 nT, inclination 0°, declination 0°), the dyke (30 m thick, vertical, 2,000 m down dip), the plug (400 by 400 m, top to 2,000 m), the 0.02 SI susceptibility and the remanence (2 A/m at 60°). Widths are read off a profile sampled every metre from −1,000 to +1,000 m. Nothing here is the paper's own data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/magnetic-anomaly.mjs`, which pins all four bodies against independent NumPy and SciPy models in a field of 50,000 nT at inclination 60°, declination 10°, with a profile azimuth of 30°:

- the **sphere** must match a dipole potential differentiated by central differences to within **1e-6 nT** (values such as 46.1430 nT at the station 0 m);
- the **thin dyke** must match point dipoles integrated over strike and down dip by nested quadrature to within **1e-4 nT** (81.3840 nT at 45 m);
- the **prism** must match Gauss-Legendre sums of point dipoles to within **1e-6 nT** (753.4137 nT at 0 m), and a 1 m wide, very long, very deep prism must equal a vertical thin dyke to 0.1 %;
- physics checks: a sphere at the magnetic pole is symmetric with its peak at 2μ₀m/(4πz³), and a small cube far away equals a dipole of the same volume to 0.1 %.
- **New with this article:** the dyke and plug models above, sampled as the tool samples them, must give lows of **−10.44 nT** (30 m dyke), **−21.92 nT** (63 m dyke) and **−37.68 nT** (400 m plug) with a high of **+5.74 nT**.

## Check it yourself

1. In *The Earth's field*, set *Field strength F (nT)* to **33500**, *Inclination I (°, down +)* to **0**, *Declination D (°, east +)* to **0**, *Profile direction (° from north)* to **0** and *Sensor height (m)* to **120**. Set *Profile from (m)* **-800** *to (m)* **800**.

Switch *Shape* to **vertical prism (3D box)** with *Width along profile (m)* **400**, *Length across profile (m)* **400**, *Depth to top (m)* **150** and *Depth to bottom (m)* **2000**: the low is **−37.7 nT**, the high **+5.7 nT** and *Peak to trough* **43.4 nT**. ## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a depth estimate before you publish it.** Model the body you think you have, measure the width of the modelled anomaly, and compare it with your station or line spacing: with the [[magnetic-anomaly]] modeller that takes a minute.
- **Decide a survey's line spacing from the target.** Width at half amplitude is about 1.7 times the sensor-to-top distance, so a 100 m target needs lines a few hundred metres apart, not 500 m.
- **Turn an amplitude into a susceptibility, honestly.** The anomaly is linear in χ and in thickness, so one modelled profile gives the whole family; quote the thickness you assumed.
- **Test whether remanence is needed.** If no plausible susceptibility reaches the measured amplitude, remanence or a wider body is the answer; the modeller gives you the Koenigsberger ratio as you type.
- **Compare methods on one line.** Model the magnetic profile, then the gravity with the [[gravity-corrections]] tool and the layering with the [[ves-inversion]] tool, and draw the result with the [[cross-section-profile]] tool.

## The fine print

- Our anomalies are **modelled**, not measured. The paper's grid and maps are not published as numbers, so no fit to its data is possible.
- The main field, the body sizes and the susceptibility are our assumptions; the paper does not state its field inclination, so our "reads as a low" result follows from the magnetic equator assumption. Reduced-to-pole or reduced-to-equator maps change the sign convention.
- The tool models a body with uniform magnetisation and a flat ground surface. Real ring complexes are neither.
- Euler deconvolution and SPI depths carry their own window and window-size uncertainties that no forward model can repair.
