An optical trap is a spring made of light, and before it can measure a force it has to be calibrated: you need its stiffness in piconewtons per nanometre. The standard way is to watch a trapped bead jiggle for a few seconds and fit the power spectrum of its position, which is a Lorentzian whose **corner frequency** gives the stiffness. A study in *Micromachines* asks whether you can do it from a **single trapping event** instead, by tracking a bead for about two milliseconds as it falls into the trap, and reports roughly 10 % precision per fall. This article converts the paper's corner frequencies into stiffnesses and asks how long an ordinary equilibrium record has to be to match that precision, using the [[optical-tweezers|optical tweezers calibration tool]]. For the trapping focus itself see the [[gaussian-beam|Gaussian beam calculator]], and for a closer look at any spectrum the [[fft-workbench]].

## The paper in brief

The authors trap 2 µm polystyrene beads with a 1064 nm laser and record what happens in the first couple of milliseconds of a trapping event, when the bead is still falling in. From that short trajectory they reconstruct the restoring force directly (the FORMA approach) and get the trap's relaxation rate &omega;_c = **7834 ± 794 s⁻¹**, against **6942 ± 870 s⁻¹** from the usual equilibrium power spectrum of the same trap, which they quote as about **118 pN µm⁻¹**. Over five seconds their equilibrium estimate wanders between about 6900 and 7600 s⁻¹.

Their claims about precision are the interesting part: a single fall is within about 10 % of the true value 95 % of the time, the relative standard deviation across falls is below 6 % in experiment and 5 % in simulation, and a discretisation correction improves the recovered rate by about 25 %, giving 0.98 ± 0.08 of the true value from 2 ms sequences.

## Checked with the tools

The paper's raw traces are not published, so our records are **simulated** with the tool's own exact discrete Ornstein-Uhlenbeck steps for a trapped bead, in a trap set to the paper's corner frequency. Nothing here is the paper's data.

**Step 1: from the paper's corner frequency to a stiffness.** The tool's Stokes drag for a 1 µm-radius bead in water at 25 °C (0.89 mPa·s, **our assumption**: the paper states neither the temperature nor the viscosity) is &gamma; = 6&pi;&eta;r = **1.6776 × 10⁻⁸ N·s/m**. The paper's &omega;_c = 6942 s⁻¹ is a corner frequency of f_c = &omega;_c/2&pi; = **1,104.9 Hz**, so

k = &gamma; &omega;_c = **116.5 pN µm⁻¹**,

against the paper's "about 118". The 1.3 % gap is a viscosity choice, not a disagreement: 0.902 mPa·s, water at about 24.5 °C, gives exactly 118. Their quoted ±870 s⁻¹ is ±14.6 pN µm⁻¹, and their faster FORMA rate of 7834 s⁻¹ corresponds to **131.4 pN µm⁻¹**, 12.8 % stiffer, which is within the two uncertainties but worth noticing.

| Check | Paper | Optical tweezers tool |
|---|---|---|
| Drag on the bead | not stated | 1.6776 × 10⁻⁸ N·s/m (2 µm bead, water at 25 °C) |
| Corner frequency, equilibrium | &omega;_c 6942 ± 870 s⁻¹ | f_c 1,104.9 Hz |
| Stiffness, equilibrium | about 118 pN µm⁻¹ | 116.5 pN µm⁻¹ (118.0 at 0.902 mPa·s) |
| Stiffness, falling particle | &omega;_c 7834 ± 794 s⁻¹ | 131.4 pN µm⁻¹ |
| Expected diffusion kT/&gamma; | not stated | 0.245 µm²/s |

**Step 2: what a one-second equilibrium record gives.** Simulating one second at 100 kHz in that trap and running the tool's pipeline (8,192-point segments, four bins averaged, aliased Lorentzian from 50 Hz to 20 kHz) returns f_c = **1,111.9 ± 21.4 Hz**, a stiffness of **117.2 pN µm⁻¹**, &chi;²/dof = 1.23, and an equipartition cross-check of **115.1 pN µm⁻¹** from a position scatter of 6.0 nm rms. So a second of data is worth about 2 %, well inside the paper's ±12.5 % on the equilibrium value.

**Step 3: how long does the spectrum need to match a 2 ms fall?** We repeated the whole pipeline on 40 independent records at each of five lengths and measured the scatter of the fitted corner frequency:

| Record length | Segments averaged | Mean f_c | Scatter | Bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 ms | 3 × 512 | 1,151 Hz | 15.0 % | +4.2 % |
| 50 ms | 2 × 2,048 | 1,139 Hz | 10.7 % | +3.1 % |
| 200 ms | 2 × 8,192 | 1,115 Hz | 6.2 % | +0.9 % |
| 1 s | 12 × 8,192 | 1,112 Hz | 1.8 % | +0.7 % |
| 5 s | 61 × 8,192 | 1,106 Hz | 0.7 % | +0.1 % |

So the paper's 10 % per single fall is what a power spectrum achieves from about **50 ms** of equilibrium data, and their sub-6 % repeat scatter needs about **200 ms**. A 2 ms event is therefore worth roughly **25 to 100 times** its own length in equilibrium recording: a real speed-up, and the reason the approach matters when a trap's stiffness has to be measured once per bead rather than once per day.

The same table shows something the paper does not discuss: short spectra are biased **high** (+4.2 % at 20 ms), so hurrying the standard method makes a trap look stiffer than it is, which is the opposite direction to the roughly 10 % underestimate they warn about for equilibrium calibration.

**Step 4: do not skip the aliasing correction.** The paper is careful to fit an *aliased* Lorentzian, the exact spectrum of a sampled signal. We checked what that is worth. At 100 kHz the plain Lorentzian reads 1,166.7 Hz against the aliased 1,111.9 Hz, 4.9 % high. Sample the same trap at 5 kHz, a frame rate a fast camera might give, and the plain fit returns **1,663.5 Hz** against a true 1,104.9: **51 % high**, which is a 51 % error in the stiffness. The aliased model gets 1,096.7 Hz, 0.7 % low.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/optical-tweezers-corner-frequency-fast-calibration.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `drag` (Stokes drag with Faxén's correction), `simulateBead` (exact discrete Ornstein-Uhlenbeck steps), `psd` and `block` (the averaged periodogram), `fitPsd` (the analytical start plus Levenberg-Marquardt with Berg-Sørensen weights), `lorentz`, `lorentzAliased` and `equipartition` from `src/lib/sci/phtweezers.ts`, the maths behind the [[optical-tweezers|optical tweezers calibration tool]].

The corner frequencies, the bead diameter and the precision figures are from the paper. The water viscosity (0.89 mPa·s), the temperature (25 °C), the 100 kHz sample rate, the record lengths and the 40 random seeds are **our assumptions**, and every record is **simulated**.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/optical-tweezers.mjs`, which pins this tool against SciPy:

- the averaged periodogram against `scipy.signal.welch` (boxcar window, no overlap) on the same record, including the segment count and bin frequencies;
- the aliased and plain Lorentzian fits against `scipy.optimize.curve_fit` with the same reweighting: f_c = 379.993524 Hz and D = 491,613.14 nm²/s for the aliased fit on the tool's example record;
- equipartition against a NumPy variance, 205.9344 nm², giving 1.99889 × 10⁻⁵ N/m;
- that the fit recovers the simulated trap within two standard errors, with &chi;²/dof below 1.5.
- New with this article: a trap at the paper's corner frequency must give a drag of 1.6776 × 10⁻⁸ N·s/m and a stiffness of 116.5 pN µm⁻¹ for a 2 µm bead in water at 25 °C, and a simulated one-second record at 100 kHz must return f_c = 1,111.9 Hz with the aliased model and 1,166.7 Hz without it.

## Check it yourself

On the [[optical-tweezers|optical tweezers calibration]] page:

1. Press **Use the example**. It loads a simulated record of a 1 µm bead sampled at 10 kHz, and reads *Trap stiffness* **0.02003 pN/nm** at a *Corner frequency fc* of **380.0 ± 7.2 Hz**, with *Drag coefficient γ* **8.388 × 10⁻⁹ N·s/m** and an equipartition value of 0.01999 pN/nm.
2. Change *Model* to **Plain Lorentzian**. The corner frequency jumps to **467.0 Hz** and the stiffness to **0.02461 pN/nm**, 22.9 % too stiff, because at 10 kHz the aliasing is not negligible. Change it back.
3. Type *Bead diameter (µm)* **2**: the drag doubles to **1.6776 × 10⁻⁸ N·s/m** and the stiffness to **0.04005 pN/nm**, while the corner frequency does not move. That drag is the one the paper's bead has.
4. Multiply it by the paper's &omega;_c: k = 2&pi;&gamma;f_c with f_c = 1,104.9 Hz gives **0.1165 pN/nm**, the 116.5 pN µm⁻¹ above.
5. Set *Fit to (Hz)* to **500**, well below the corner, and watch the standard error on f_c grow: the corner has to be inside the fit range.
6. Type a *Height of the bead centre above the surface (µm)* of **1.5** for the 2 µm bead: Faxén's correction raises the drag, and the stiffness with it, by about a third.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Convert any published corner frequency into a stiffness** (and back) with the drag the tool works out from the bead, the liquid and the height above the glass.
- **Decide how long to record.** The scatter table above is reproducible for your own trap: set the stiffness, the sample rate and the length, and read off the precision.
- **Check a camera-rate calibration.** At a few kHz the aliasing correction is worth tens of percent, far more than most other error sources.
- **Cross-check with equipartition**, which fails differently (it is spoiled by drift and noise rather than by bandwidth), so agreement between the two is meaningful.
- **Teach the fluctuation-dissipation theorem** with something tangible: kT/&gamma; is the diffusion the fit must return if the positions are calibrated.

## The fine print

- Our records are **simulated**; the paper's traces are not published. The simulation is an ideal trapped bead: no drift, no detector noise, no hydrodynamic memory, no laser fluctuation, so our scatter figures are a best case.
- The paper's method (force reconstruction from a 2 ms fall) is **not** the tool's method (a Lorentzian fitted to an equilibrium spectrum). We compare precision at equal information, not the algorithms themselves, and a 2 ms record is far too short for the tool's spectral route.
- The temperature and viscosity are ours, so our 116.5 pN µm⁻¹ carries that choice; the paper's own number is about 118.
- The paper is from 2021, so the fast-calibration literature has moved on since; the arithmetic of corner frequency to stiffness has not.
