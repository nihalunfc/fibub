Copper-67 is one of the most wanted radionuclides in cancer research: it emits beta particles that travel about 3 mm in tissue and gamma rays you can image, and its chemistry is the same as that of copper-64, the PET tracer, so the same molecule can be used to find a tumour and then treat it. The problem has always been making it. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Nuclear Medicine* makes both isotopes by firing deuterons at a carbon target, using the neutrons that come off to irradiate zinc oxide, and then separating the copper from the zinc by boiling the zinc away. Every number in that route has a clock attached, and the [[decay-calculator]] is the tool that reads the clock. For the chains that feed and follow these nuclides, see the [[decay-chain]] simulator, and for the counting statistics behind activities this small, the [[counting-statistics]] helper.

## The paper in brief

Deuterons at 41 and 52 MeV hit a 10 mm carbon target; the neutrons produced go on into a small pellet of enriched zinc oxide. Fast neutrons knock a proton out of zinc-68 or zinc-67 to leave copper-67, and zinc-64 gives copper-64. The great advantage is that the unwanted companions are few: a 3 minute irradiation of 0.363 g of enriched zinc-68 oxide gave **1.23 kBq of copper-67** at the end of irradiation with only **2.9 Bq of zinc-65**, and the authors' calculations sit within 20 % of every measured value.

Then the chemistry. The zinc is removed by **sublimation** — zinc boils at 907 °C, copper at 2,562 °C, so heating under vacuum takes 99 % of the zinc away and leaves the copper behind — followed by a resin column. Recovery is 94 % for the enriched material, and the two stages take **about four hours each**. The final product carries at most 0.01 % zinc-65 relative to copper-64.

## Checked with the tools

Every calculation below uses the Decay calculator's own maths with the recommended half-lives: copper-67 61.83 h, copper-64 12.701 h, zinc-65 243.93 d.

**Step 1: the paper's rounded half-lives.** The tables give half-lives in days to two decimal places. For the long-lived nuclides that is harmless, but for the short ones it is not:

| Nuclide | Paper's value | In hours | Recommended | Off by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cu-67 | 2.58 d | 61.92 h | 61.83 h | +0.1 % |
| Cu-64 | 0.53 d | 12.72 h | 12.701 h | +0.1 % |
| Zn-69m | 0.57 d | 13.68 h | 13.76 h | −0.6 % |
| Ni-65 | 0.11 d | 2.64 h | 2.5175 h | **+4.9 %** |

A 4.9 % error in a half-life of two and a half hours matters: correcting a one-day-old nickel-65 measurement back to the end of irradiation gives 0.00183 instead of 0.00135 of the original, a **36 % difference**. Nothing in the paper's conclusions turns on nickel-65, which it only calculates, but it is a good reminder to quote short half-lives in their own units rather than in days.

So the paper's 1.23 kBq of copper-67 is **1.12 kBq** when the chemistry finishes, or **1.06 kBq** once its 94 % chemical recovery is applied too: decay costs 9 %, the chemistry 6 %. For copper-64 the balance flips — the same eight hours throw away **35 %** of it, three times what any of their chemical steps lose, which is a quantitative argument for the shorter route (the sublimation alone took 20 min for the enriched sample) when copper-64 is the product. Their 6.61 kBq of copper-64 would be down to **4.27 kBq**.

**Step 3: the impurity grows by itself.** Zinc-65 has a half-life of 244 days, so in any sample it stays put while the copper decays. The ratio therefore **doubles every copper-67 half-life**, 61.83 h, and every 12.7 h in a copper-64 product. Starting from the paper's un-separated end-of-irradiation values at 52 MeV, zinc-65 is 0.236 % of the copper-67 activity at time zero and **1.52 % seven days later**.

The tool's *When will it fall to* box answers the practical version of this directly: a product that leaves the lab at the paper's 0.01 % zinc-65 limit reaches 1 % after **84 h** if the product is copper-64, but only after **411 h (17 days)** if it is copper-67. That gap is the shelf life, and it is set by the product's half-life, not by the impurity's.

**Step 4: how carrier-free is it?** The Decay calculator's *Specific activity* card gives the carrier-free figures: **28,000 TBq per gram** for copper-67 (a molar activity of 1.875 × 10¹⁸ Bq/mol, mean life 89.2 h) and **143,000 TBq per gram** for copper-64. The paper's product is not carrier-free: it calculates 0.13 atoms of stable copper-65 and 0.004 of copper-63 per atom of copper-67 at 52 MeV. Diluted by those, the molar activity becomes **1.654 × 10¹⁸ Bq/mol, or 88.2 % of carrier-free** — and at 40 MeV, where the stable-copper ratios are 0.05 and 0.005, **94.8 %**. Both are remarkably high for a neutron route, and that, not the raw yield, is what makes the method interesting for labelling antibodies.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/copper-67-64-accelerator-production-decay-losses.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `decay` (activity after a time interval), `timeTo` (how long until an activity falls to a given level), `specific` (decay constant, mean life, carrier-free specific and molar activity) and `halfLifeS` from `src/lib/sci/decay.ts`, the maths behind the Decay calculator.

From the paper: the end-of-irradiation activities, the half-lives as it prints them, the 8 h separation time, the 94 % chemical recovery, the 0.01 % zinc-65 limit and the stable-copper ratios. Half-lives for the calculations are the recommended NNDC/DDEP values already in the tool. **Our assumptions**: that the separation takes the full 8 h the paper describes, that nothing is lost except by decay and the stated chemistry, and that the stable-copper ratios apply to the separated product as calculated.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` pins the decay maths against hand-checkable values, and the new `tests/sci-tools/decay-calculator.mjs` adds this paper's numbers:

- 370 MBq of fluorine-18 (109.77 min) must read 173.43 MBq two hours later, and correcting that back must return 370 MBq.
- One half-life must leave exactly half of anything, to one part in 10¹².
- New with this article: with the recommended half-lives, 8 h of decay must leave 0.914220 of a copper-67 activity and 0.646234 of a copper-64 one; 1.23 kBq of copper-67 must become 1124.49 Bq and 6.61 kBq of copper-64 must become 4271.6 Bq; a 100-fold rise in an impurity ratio must take 84.38 h against copper-64 and 410.78 h against copper-67; and the carrier-free specific activity of copper-67 must be 2.799 × 10¹⁶ Bq/g with a mean life of 89.20 h.

## Check it yourself

On the [[decay-calculator]] tool page, in the *Decay correction* card:

2. Set *Reference date and time* to **2026-11-24 08:00** and *Wanted date and time* to **2026-11-24 16:00**, the eight hours of sublimation and column work.
3. It reads about **1.12 kBq**, with *Fraction left* **0.9142**, *Half-lives passed* **0.129** and *Decay constant λ* about **3.11 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹**.
4. Now pick *Nuclide* **Cu-64: half-life 12.701 h (PET)** and type **6.61** kBq with the same two times: **4.27 kBq**, *Fraction left* **0.6462**. The same wait costs copper-64 five times as much as copper-67.

## The fine print

- The paper's activities are **tracer scale** (kilobecquerels from microamp beams). It argues for a 100 microamp upgrade; we have not scaled its yields, only applied decay to what it measured.
- The eight hours is the paper's own description of its two-stage process; a real production run would overlap steps.
- We treat the zinc-65 to copper ratio as changing only by decay. In the real product the chemistry has already removed almost all the zinc, and the paper's 0.01 % is its limit at the end of separation.
- Carrier-free specific activity uses the mass number as the molar mass, which is the usual approximation and good to well under a per cent here.
- This is laboratory and teaching arithmetic. It is not dosimetry, and it says nothing about what any of these activities would do in a patient.
