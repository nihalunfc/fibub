A regression table has more internal structure than it looks. Once you know the sample size, the number of predictors and R², the standard error of each standardised coefficient is fixed by one more number: its **variance inflation factor** (VIF), the factor by which overlapping predictors widen that coefficient's uncertainty. So a paper that prints both standard errors and VIFs has printed the same quantity twice, and the two versions have to match. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Education* on friendship quality and optimism among 450 Jordanian students prints both, and they do not match. This article shows the check with the [[regression-workbench]], which reports coefficients, standard errors and VIFs side by side; the [[hypothesis-tests]] page and the [[effect-size-converter]] handle the rest of the arithmetic.

## The paper in brief

450 undergraduates at a Jordanian university filled in a friendship-quality questionnaire with six dimensions (stimulating companionship, help, intimacy, reliable alliance, self-validation, emotional security) and the revised Life Orientation Test for optimism. The analysis is a two-step **hierarchical regression**: gender and academic year first, then the six friendship dimensions.

Step one explains 4.6 % of the variance in optimism. Adding the friendship dimensions takes R² to **0.333**, an increase of 28.7 points, with F change (6, 439) = 31.481. In the final model only one dimension is significant: **reliable alliance** (the sense that a friend will be there), with B = 0.455, standard error 0.032 and a standardised coefficient of **0.730**, t = 14.302. The other five dimensions have standardised coefficients between -0.010 and 0.054 and p values from 0.29 to 0.84. A separate collinearity table reports variance inflation factors from **3.102 to 4.983** and a Durbin-Watson statistic of 1.892.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from numbers printed in the paper**. We do not have the students' responses, and we do not need them: the model-fit statistics and the coefficient uncertainties are determined by the summary values.

**Step 1: the model fit is sound.** From R² = 0.333 with 10 predictors and 450 cases, the adjusted R² must be **0.3178** (paper: 0.318) and the overall F must be **21.92** (paper: 21.910). From the two R² values, the F for the added block must be (0.287 / 6) / (0.667 / 439) = **31.48** (paper: 31.481), p = 2 x 10⁻³¹. The one small miss is block 1: R² = 0.046 gives F(4, 445) = 5.364 rather than 5.418, which is what a rounded R² of 0.0464 would give. So the headline, that friendship quality adds about 29 % of the variance in optimism, checks out exactly.

**Step 2: the standard errors.** For standardised coefficients the algebra is short:

> var(β_j) = (1 − R²) / (N − k − 1) × VIF_j

with N = 450 cases, k = 10 predictors and R² = 0.333, so the square root of the first part is **0.03898**. A coefficient with no collinearity at all (VIF = 1) would therefore have a standard error of 0.039, and one with VIF = 5 a standard error of 0.087.

The paper reports unstandardised coefficients and their standard errors, plus the standardised coefficients. Since β = B × SD(x) / SD(y), the standard error of β is SE(B) × β / B, which needs no extra information. For reliable alliance that is 0.032 × (0.730 / 0.455) = **0.0513**, and indeed 0.730 / 0.0513 = 14.2, the paper's own t. Run the same arithmetic on all six dimensions and the standard errors sit between 0.048 and 0.055, which correspond to variance inflation factors of **1.5 to 2.0**, not the 3.1 to 5.0 of the collinearity table (Figure 1).

Two readings are possible. Either the collinearity table was computed on a different model (six dimensions only, without the gender and year dummies) and does not describe the reported fit, or the reported standard errors are too small by about 70 %. The first is the kinder explanation, but it does not rescue the numbers: a six-predictor model with those VIFs gives standard errors near 0.089, further still from 0.051.

**Step 3: does it change the conclusion?** No, and that is worth saying plainly. Widening every interval to match the printed VIFs leaves reliable alliance at t = 8.4 (95 % interval 0.56 to 0.90) and the other five dimensions well inside zero (Figure 2). The paper's substantive claim survives its own bookkeeping error.

**Step 4: the identity, demonstrated on data.** To show that this is the tool's own arithmetic and not a formula of ours, the script builds a dataset of 450 rows with six correlated predictors (one common factor with a loading of 0.78 plus independent noise, and one dominant predictor; **our simulation**, not the paper's data), then fits it with the same `ols` and `vif` functions the [[regression-workbench]] uses. The fit has R² = 0.401, VIFs of 3.84 to 4.27, and standard errors of 0.072 to 0.076 for the standardised coefficients. Each of those standard errors equals √((1 − R²) / (N − k − 1)) × √VIF, here 0.0368 × √VIF, to four decimal places. The tool's example data do the same: in the plant-growth fit, fertiliser has VIF 1.147 and a standardised standard error of 0.0798, and 0.07454 × √1.147 = 0.0798 exactly.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/friendship-quality-optimism-hierarchical-regression-collinearity-vif.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `readTable`, `design`, `ols` and `vif` from `src/lib/sci/regression.ts`, the maths behind the [[regression-workbench]], and `fSf` and `tInv` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the F tail probabilities and the t multiplier for 95 % intervals.

Inputs from the paper: N = 450, the two R² values, the six dimensions' B, SE(B) and β, and the six printed VIFs. Our own additions are labelled: the simulated 450-row dataset in step 4 (common-factor loading 0.78, dominant coefficient 0.74), which exists only to show the tool reproducing the identity.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/regression-workbench.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the tool against statsmodels 0.15 on three example data sets:

- Every coefficient, standard error, t, p and confidence interval of the four-predictor plant-growth fit (including the soil indicator variables) must match statsmodels' OLS output, together with R², adjusted R², F, the log-likelihood, leverage, studentised residuals and Cook's distance.
- The variance inflation factors must match `statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence.variance_inflation_factor` for each predictor of the same fit, which is the quantity this article leans on.
- The logistic example must match statsmodels' Logit: coefficients, z statistics, odds ratios, GLM leverage and predicted probabilities with intervals.
- A badly conditioned degree-5 polynomial fit is checked against 60-digit mpmath arithmetic, so the QR solver does not quietly lose precision.
- New with this article: the plant-growth fit must give R² = 0.8555 on 26 residual degrees of freedom with fertiliser's VIF at 1.147, and every predictor's standardised standard error must equal √((1 − R²) / df) × √VIF to twelve decimal places, which is the identity used above.

## Check it yourself

You can watch the standard error and the VIF move together on the [[regression-workbench]] page, without any data of your own:

1. In *Data*, set *Example* to **Plant growth: height from fertiliser, water, light and soil (multiple regression)**.
2. In *Model*, leave *Type* on **Linear (one or more x)**, keep *Response (y)* as **height_cm** and tick all four predictors under *Predictors (x)*.
3. In the *Coefficients* table you should see **fertiliser_g** at 1.833 with SE **0.2245** and VIF **1.147**, and above it R² = **0.8555** on **26** residual degrees of freedom.
4. Work out √((1 − 0.8555) / 26) = **0.07454** and multiply by √1.147: **0.0798**. That is fertiliser's standard error once height and fertiliser are put on standard-deviation units, and it is what the tool gives. Light hours (VIF 1.089) comes out at 0.0778 the same way.
5. Now untick **water_ml** and refit. Fertiliser's VIF falls slightly, to 1.139, but its standard error *rises*, from 0.2245 to 0.3147, because R² has dropped from 0.856 to 0.703. Both parts of the identity matter: collinearity and leftover noise.
6. For the paper's numbers, no tool is needed: 0.032 × (0.730 / 0.455) = 0.0513, and (0.0513 / 0.03898)² = **1.73**. That is the variance inflation factor its standard error implies.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published regression table in a minute.** Sample size, R², the number of predictors and one standard error are enough to recover the implied VIF; if the paper prints VIFs too, they must agree.
- **Check an added block.** The F for a change in R² needs only the two R² values and the degrees of freedom, so "our block added 29 % of the variance" can always be verified.
- **See what collinearity really costs.** Refit with and without a near-duplicate predictor in the [[regression-workbench]] and watch the intervals, rather than quoting a VIF cut-off of 5 or 10 from memory.
- **Report coefficients that can be checked.** Printing B, SE(B), β and the VIFs from the same model, with N and R², lets any reader redo the arithmetic, as we have here.
- **Decide what to do about six overlapping subscales.** When dimensions of one questionnaire correlate strongly, the honest options are a total score, one dimension chosen in advance, or a model that keeps all of them with intervals wide enough to say so.

## The fine print

- We have no access to the students' responses. Every number here is derived from the paper's printed summaries, so a typo in the paper propagates into our check (that is the point of the check).
- SE(β) = SE(B) × β / B is exact, but it inherits the rounding of the printed B and β. For reliable alliance the printed values round to a t of 14.22 against the paper's 14.302, consistent with B = 0.4577 before rounding.
- The identity assumes ordinary least squares with an intercept and the standardised variables of the same model. If the collinearity table belongs to a different model, as may be the case here, the mismatch is in the reporting rather than in the fit.
- The simulated dataset in step 4 is ours, built only to show the tool obeying the identity. It is not an attempt to reproduce the study's data, and its R² (0.40) is not the paper's.
- Cross-sectional data cannot say whether reliable friends make students optimistic or optimistic students keep reliable friends.
