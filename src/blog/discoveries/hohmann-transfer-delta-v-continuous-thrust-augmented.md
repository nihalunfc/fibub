The **Hohmann transfer** is the textbook way to move a spacecraft between two circular orbits: one burn to stretch the orbit into an ellipse that touches the target orbit, half an orbit of coasting, and a second burn to round it off. A 2025 paper in *Aerospace* asks what happens if the spacecraft also has a small engine that can push gently for the whole coast, as an electric thruster does. It calls this the **augmented Hohmann transfer** and finds that, for the same flight time, the two big burns can be made smaller. fibub's tools cannot model the low-thrust arc, so this article does the part they can: it rechecks the classical Hohmann costs the paper starts from with the [[orbit-transfer-calculator]], and turns them into fuel with the [[rocket-equation]]. For transfers between real planets on real dates, use the [[porkchop-plot]].

## The paper in brief

A rocket burn is measured by its **delta-v** (Δv), the change in speed it gives. Delta-v is the currency of spaceflight: the rocket equation turns it into fuel. A Hohmann transfer between orbits of radius r and ρ × r (ρ, "rho", is the **orbit ratio**) needs two burns, and its total Δv, divided by the starting circular speed √(μ/r), depends only on ρ. Here μ is the central body's gravitational parameter, its mass times the gravitational constant.

The paper keeps the two burns at the start and end of the half-ellipse, but adds a thrust arc between them whose push is of fixed size and can point in any direction. By optimising that direction, it finds how much smaller the two impulsive burns can be for the same orbit ratio, the same flight time and the same half-turn round the central body. It shows the method for Earth–Venus and Earth–Mars transfers round the Sun and for raising an Earth orbit, and notes that it suits **hybrid propulsion**: a chemical engine for the burns and an electric one for the arc. As baselines it states that doubling the orbit radius by Hohmann costs about **28 %** of the starting circular speed, and halving it about **40 %**.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** of the classical (Hohmann) part with the tool's maths. The paper's main result, the saving from the thrust arc, needs an optimal-control solver and is **not** checked here.

**Step 1: the two stated percentages.** In dimensionless units (μ = 1, starting radius 1), the tool gives, for doubling the radius (ρ = 2), a first burn of 0.1547 and a second of 0.1298, a total of **0.2845**: 28.45 % of the starting speed. For halving (ρ = 0.5), 0.1835 and 0.2188, a total of **0.4023**: 40.23 %. Both match the paper's "about 28 %" and "about 40 %" (Figure 1). Going inwards costs more for the same factor in radius, because the orbits are faster there.

**Step 2: the same shape at any scale.** Because the share depends only on ρ, the same 28.45 % holds round any planet. Raising a 300 km Earth orbit (radius 6678 km, circular speed 7.726 km/s) to twice its radius costs 1.195 + 1.002 = **2.198 km/s**, and 2.198 ÷ 7.726 = 0.2845. The curve keeps rising beyond the paper's range and peaks at a ratio of **15.58**, at 53.6 %; above that, a three-burn bi-elliptic transfer is always cheaper, which the tool also reports.

**Step 3: the flight time.** A Hohmann coast is half the transfer ellipse: t = π √(((1+ρ)/2)³) in units of the starting orbit's period divided by 2π (Figure 2). It is 2.041 for halving and 5.771 for doubling. Round the Sun, that is **146.1 days** to Venus and **258.9 days** to Mars. These are the fixed flight times the augmented transfer is compared against.

**Step 4: the paper's planet examples, classical part.** Starting from Earth's circular speed round the Sun (29.785 km/s), the Hohmann transfer to Mars costs 2.945 + 2.649 = **5.594 km/s** (18.8 % of that speed) and to Venus 2.496 + 2.707 = **5.203 km/s** (17.5 %). These are heliocentric burns, measured from Earth's orbit, not from a parking orbit; leaving Earth's gravity well adds more.

**Step 5: why trimming the burns matters.** The rocket equation turns the 2.198 km/s Earth-orbit raise into **1.014 kg of propellant per kg of spacecraft** at an engine Isp (exhaust speed in seconds) of 320 s, typical for a storable chemical engine, but only **0.150 kg** at 1600 s, typical for an electric thruster (**our examples**). The fuel grows exponentially with Δv, so every bit taken off the chemical burns by an electric arc saves more than it seems.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/hohmann-transfer-delta-v-continuous-thrust-augmented.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `hohmann` and `propellantFor` from `src/lib/sci/orbit.ts`, the maths behind the [[orbit-transfer-calculator]] and the [[rocket-equation]], with μ = 1 for the dimensionless curves and the tool's own values for the Sun, Earth, Venus and Mars.

The 28 % and 40 % come from the paper's text, and the ratio range 0.5 to 2 from its charts. The 300 km starting orbit and the two engine Isp values are **our examples**. No number here comes from the paper's tables or its optimised augmented transfers.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks the transfer maths against textbook worked examples:

- From a 300 km orbit (radius 6678 km) to geostationary orbit (42,164 km), the burns must be 2.426 and 1.467 km/s and the coast 5.27 hours.
- Earth to Mars must take 259 days, with Mars 44.3° ahead at departure and a launch window every 780 days.
- At an orbit ratio of 20, a bi-elliptic transfer must beat Hohmann by 0.0703 km/s.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/orbit-transfer-calculator.mjs`: in dimensionless form the tool must match the closed-form Hohmann formula, giving 0.284457 for ρ = 2 and 0.402283 for ρ = 0.5, flight times of π(1.5)^1.5 and π(0.75)^1.5, and a peak cost of 0.536258 near ρ = 15.58.

## Check it yourself

On the [[orbit-transfer-calculator]]:

1. Set *Around* to **Earth** and *Orbit height measured from* to **the surface (altitude)**.
2. Type **300** in *Start altitude (km)* and **6978.137** in *Target altitude (km)*: that doubles the orbit radius from 6678.137 km to 13,356.274 km.
3. The headline reads **2.198 km/s in two burns, 83.1 min apart**. *First burn* shows **1.195 km/s**, speeding up from **7.726 km/s** circular speed, and *Second burn* **1.002 km/s**. Divide 2.198 by 7.726: **0.2845**, the paper's 28 %.
4. Set *Around* to **Sun** and press **Earth to Mars**: **5.594 km/s in two burns, 258.9 days apart**. Press **Earth to Venus**: **5.203 km/s**, 146.1 days.
5. Open *More options*, type **1000** in *Spacecraft mass after the burns (kg)* and **320** in *Engine Isp (seconds)* for the Earth case: about **1014 kg** of propellant. Try **1600** to see the electric-thruster saving.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Get the classical baseline** for any new transfer scheme in seconds, in km/s or as a share of orbital speed, with the [[orbit-transfer-calculator]].
- **Turn a Δv saving into kilograms** of propellant at a given Isp with the [[rocket-equation]], to judge whether a few per cent matters for a mission.
- **Check a paper's dimensionless curves** at a few ratios before trusting a plot read by eye.
- **Find real launch dates** for the planet cases with the [[porkchop-plot]], where planets move on real, slightly elliptic orbits.
- **Teach** why going inwards costs more than going outwards, and where Hohmann stops being the cheapest two-orbit transfer.

## The fine print

- We check only the classical Hohmann part. The augmented transfer's optimal thrust direction and its savings come from an optimisation that no fibub tool performs.
- The tool assumes circular, coplanar orbits and instant burns. Real planet orbits are slightly elliptic and tilted, so real Earth–Mars costs change from one launch window to the next.
- The planet distances are the tool's rounded values, so the days and km/s may differ slightly from other sources.
