When a coal mine and its power station close, the jobs that go are not only the ones at the mine. The mine's suppliers lose orders, their suppliers lose orders, and the money the wages used to buy stops circulating. An **input-output table**, which records what every sector of an economy buys from every other sector, is the standard way to add all of that up, and a 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Sustainability* builds one for Muğla, the Turkish province where three lignite power stations sit beside their own mines, to ask what phasing coal out would do to the province. This article shows that the paper's own published results contain enough information to rebuild the model behind them: from six numbers per sector, the [[leontief-multipliers]] reproduces every output, value-added and employment effect it reports, and shows which of its conclusions were built into the table rather than found in it. For the regionalising step before it, see the [[location-quotient-shift-share]]; for the matrix algebra underneath, the [[matrix-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors had no regional input-output table for Muğla, so they built one. They took Turkey's 2012 national table from TURKSTAT, updated the flows to 2022 with a modified RAS procedure, split mining into coal and other mining and electricity into five sources (coal, other thermal, hydro, renewables, other renewables) to reach 68 sectors, and then scaled the national coefficients down to the province with **location quotients** worked out from 2022 establishment and employment counts. Employment and greenhouse-gas satellites came from EUROSTAT for 2022.

With that table they removed the province's final demand for coal mining (TRY 263.8 million) and coal-fired electricity (TRY 629.9 million) and moved the whole TRY 893.7 million to renewable electricity. Their headline numbers: the swap raises output by TRY 2.00 billion against TRY 1.68 billion lost, keeps value added about level, and avoids some 5,600 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent, but employment does not keep up. They then test three spending scenarios (more renewables, a circular economy, a place-based mix of farming, fishing and tourism) and find the circular-economy and place-based packages create far more jobs per lira than renewables do.

## Checked with the tools

The regional table itself is in the paper's supplementary files, not its text, so we did not feed the tool the authors' 68-sector matrix. We did something more useful: **we recovered the part of the model that matters from the published results**. For each sector the paper gives the final-demand shock, the total output effect and the own-sector output effect. Dividing the last two by the first gives, exactly, the sector's column of the **Leontief inverse** condensed to two entries: how much output the sector itself must make per lira of final demand, and how much the rest of the economy must make.

**Step 1: the multipliers check out, and two of them are identical.** The column sums come to 1.01769, 2.23306 and 2.23306, which round to the 1.02, 2.23 and 2.23 the paper prints. The coal-mining figure is remarkable in itself: a multiplier of 1.018 means the mine buys almost nothing from inside the province, so closing it has almost no knock-on effect on local suppliers. Its own-sector entry is 1.00115, barely above the 1.0 that a sector with no inputs at all would have, and the value added implied by the table is 97.9 % of its output. That is the location-quotient scaling at work: it has stripped the mine's supply chain out of the region.

The other two multipliers are not just both "2.23". Renewable electricity's total output effect divided by coal electricity's is 1.418751, and the ratio of their demand shocks is 1.418750. The two columns of the Leontief inverse have, to six figures, **the same sum**, and both sectors have the same own value-added coefficient, 0.33408. In this table renewable electricity and coal-fired electricity are the same sector as far as output and value added are concerned; they were split out of one electricity row and kept the same input recipe apart from the fuel. So the paper's finding that moving demand from coal power to renewables preserves output and value added is largely a property of how the table was built, not a result about renewables. Where the two sectors really differ is their diagonal (1.26748 for coal power, 1.14807 for renewables) and their jobs.

**Step 2: value added should equal the demand shock, and nearly does.** In any consistent input-output table the value added generated by a shock equals the shock itself: every lira of final demand ends up as somebody's income. The test is `Σ v_i L_ij = 1`, where `v_i` is value added per unit of output. The paper's numbers give 0.99097 for coal mining and 1.00383 for both electricity sectors, so its value-added effects are 0.9 % too small and 0.4 % too large. That is the accounting slack left by updating, splitting and rescaling the table, and it is small enough to be reassuring. It also pins the value-added coefficient: a consistent model with the paper's Leontief column and its rest-of-economy coefficient must have an own coefficient of 0.33106 for coal power, against the 0.33408 the table implies.

**Step 3: the jobs are the whole story.** Per million lira of final demand, coal mining supports 1.52 jobs and renewable electricity 1.53, almost exactly the same. But coal mining puts 1.49 of its 1.52 jobs inside the sector, while renewable electricity puts 0.10 of its 1.53 inside it: nineteen jobs in twenty are somewhere else in the economy. Coal-fired electricity is the job-rich one, at 3.10 per million lira. Added up over the paper's shocks, the coal sectors lose 2,353 jobs and renewables gain 1,364, a net loss of **989 jobs**, 42 % of what went.

**Step 4: rebuilding a table the tool can run.** Two entries of the Leontief inverse and a value-added coefficient are enough to rebuild a two-sector table, the sector and "the rest of the economy", that reproduces the paper's whole column. The algebra is short: with `a12` the share of the rest of the economy's output bought from the sector, the rest follows as `a11 = 1 − (1 + a12·L_rj)/L_jj`, and `a12` can be anything below `(L_jj − 1)/L_rj` without changing a single result. For coal power we took `a12 = 0.10` (**our choice**; anything from 0 to 0.277 works, and the table below is identical across that range). Fed into the tool's `model`, `impact` and `satellite` functions, the rebuilt table gives back the paper's column to the lira.

| Check | Paper's Table 2 (coal-fired electricity) | Rebuilt two-sector model in the tool |
|---|---|---|
| Output multiplier | 2.23 | 2.23306 |
| Own-sector output effect | 798,413 | 798,413 |
| Other-sector output effect | 608,238 | 608,238 |
| Total output effect | 1,406,651 | 1,406,651 |
| Total employment effect | 1,952 | 1,951.9 |
| Total value-added effect | 632,331 | 629,922 (= the demand shock, as the identity forces) |
| Type I employment multiplier | not given (own-to-total ratio 1.477) | 1.871 |

The last two rows are the interesting ones. The model cannot reproduce 632,331 because it must return exactly the 629,922 of the shock; the 0.4 % gap is the slack of Step 2. And the tool's Type I employment multiplier, 1.871, is not the paper's own-to-total ratio of 1.477: Type I divides the economy-wide jobs per lira of final demand by the **direct** jobs per lira of the sector's own output, while the paper's own-sector figure already includes the sector buying from itself, which is the diagonal 1.267. One number divided by the other is 1.476. Both are right; they answer different questions, and papers often print one and call it the other.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/coal-phase-out-regional-output-multipliers-leontief-inverse.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `model`, `impact`, `satellite` and `productive` from `src/lib/sci/leontief.ts`, the maths behind the [[leontief-multipliers]]: `model` builds the technical coefficients and inverts I − A by Gauss-Jordan elimination, `impact` multiplies the inverse by a change in final demand, `satellite` turns a per-sector total such as jobs into direct, total and Type I multipliers, and `productive` checks that no column of A sums to 1 or more.

Every input is one of the twenty-one numbers in the three columns of the paper's Table 2. The only quantity we chose ourselves is `a12`, the rest of the economy's purchases from the sector per unit of its own output, which the article shows changes nothing: the sensitivity run in the script sweeps it from 0 to 0.27 and the output effects stay at 798,413 and 608,238. The final demand of the rest of the economy (TRY 3 billion) only sets the size of the rebuilt table, not any multiplier.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/leontief-multipliers.mjs`, which pins the model against NumPy:

- On a made-up three-sector table, the Leontief inverse must match `numpy.linalg.inv` entry by entry, the output multipliers must be 1.8551, 1.9890 and 1.7903, and the income Type I multipliers 1.8502, 2.2291 and 1.6203.
- A random five-sector table must give the same inverse as NumPy to one part in a billion, and a singular I − A must be refused rather than returning nonsense.
- New with this article: the rebuilt two-sector coal-power table must give an output multiplier of 2.23306, a Leontief column of 1.26748 and 0.96558, output effects of 798,413 and 608,238 for a shock of 629,922, and an employment effect of 1,952 jobs.

## Check it yourself

On the [[leontief-multipliers]], in the *Input-output table* card:

1. Replace the contents of *Flows between sectors* with these three lines (tab or comma separated), which are the rebuilt coal-power table in TRY thousand:
   `From \ To, Coal power, Rest of the economy, Final demand`
   `Coal power, 180807, 530057, 629922`
   `Rest of the economy, 716093, 1584479, 3000000`
2. Replace the second box, *totals per sector to turn into multipliers*, with one line: `Jobs, 2220, 5490`.
3. Set *Money unit* to `TRY thousand`.
4. The *Leontief inverse* table shows **1.2675** for coal power on itself and **0.9656** for the rest of the economy, with a column sum (the output multiplier) of **2.2331**. The *Type I multipliers* table shows jobs: direct **0.001656** per unit of output, total **0.003099** per unit of final demand, Type I **1.871**.
5. In *Effect of a change in final demand*, type **629922** for coal power and **0** for the rest. The impact table shows an output change of **798,413** for coal power, **608,238** for the rest and **1,406,651** in total, with a jobs change of about **1,322**, **630** and **1,952** — the paper's Table 2 column, to the lira and to the job.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published input-output result** without the table: divide the own-sector and total effects by the shock, check the column sum against the printed multiplier, and check `Σ v_i L_ij = 1` to see how much accounting slack the table carries.
- **Rebuild a two-sector condensation** of someone else's model, as here, so a reader can run the shock themselves and vary it.
- **Separate the table from the finding**: when two sectors share a multiplier to five figures, as coal and renewable electricity do here, the comparison is about how the sectors were split, and the honest difference is in the satellites.
- **Convert between multiplier conventions** with the Type I column, so an own-sector ratio is never quoted as a Type I multiplier.
- **Plan a regionalisation** with the [[location-quotient-shift-share]], then test how much the scaling changed a sector's supply chain: a multiplier near 1.0, as coal mining has here, says the region's chain has been scaled away.

## The fine print

- We did not use the paper's 68-sector table, which is in its supplementary files. Everything here is recovered from the three columns of its Table 2.
- The two-sector condensation reproduces the shocked sector's column exactly, but it cannot tell you which other sectors the indirect output lands in; for that you need the full table.
- `a12 = 0.10` (and 0.03 and 0.05 for the other two sectors) is **our choice**, inside the range that keeps every flow positive. It changes none of the results reported here.
- Input-output models assume fixed recipes, spare capacity and fixed prices. A coal phase-out is a large, slow shock, so treat the numbers as an accounting upper bound, not a forecast.
- The paper's employment and emission satellites are national EUROSTAT figures scaled to the province, so the jobs per lira are averages for Turkey, not measured Muğla jobs.
