Show someone a screen of faces, all the same except one, and ask whether an odd one is there. The time they take grows with the number of faces, and the growth per face — the **search slope** — is the standard measure of how hard the search is. The oldest model of searching says attention checks items one at a time and stops when it finds the target, which makes a trial with no target take twice as long per item as one with a target: you have to check everything before you can say "no". A 2025 paper in *Vision* measured both slopes, for four kinds of facial difference, in 87 people, and got ratios of 1.27 to 1.64, never 2. This article uses the visual search task in the [[memory-attention-tasks]] to show what those ratios imply, and how many trials it takes before the number means anything. For the d-prime side of the same data see the [[signal-detection-calculator]], and for congruency tasks the [[reaction-time-experiments]].

## The paper in brief

Ninety-nine adults did four short search tests in a counterbalanced order: find the face that differs in **identity**, in **expression**, in **ethnicity** or in **gender**, among 2, 4 or 6 faces, 54 trials per test, of which 36 contained an odd face ("different") and 18 did not ("same"). Eighty-seven took part in the reported analyses.

Identity was hardest on every measure: 73 % correct, d-prime 1.43, a mean response time of 3,311 ms and a set-size effect of 334 ms per face. Gender was easiest to judge (88 % correct, d-prime 2.55) but not the flattest search; ethnicity was the flattest, at 169 ms per face. The authors' main structural result is in their Table 2: the **same** trials had a steeper set-size effect than the **different** trials in all four tests, but by a factor of only 1.27 to 1.64, and they test each factor against 1.5 rather than against 2.

## Checked with the tools

The tool's `line` function fits a least-squares line of reaction time against set size and reports its slope with a standard error; `searchSummary` does it separately for target-present and target-absent trials and divides one slope by the other. Everything below is that arithmetic, applied either to the paper's printed numbers or to trials we simulated from a stated model.

**Step 1: the four ratios are right.** 199 ÷ 147 = 1.3537, 333 ÷ 230 = 1.4478, 363 ÷ 221 = 1.6425 and 400 ÷ 315 = 1.2698, which round to the paper's 1.35, 1.45, 1.64 and 1.27.

**Step 2: the pooled slope confirms the 1:2 trial split.** The paper also prints one overall set-size effect per test, fitted to all 54 trials. A line through pooled trials is the trial-weighted average of the lines through each kind, so with 18 same and 36 different trials the overall effect should be one-third of the same slope plus two-thirds of the different slope. That gives 164.3, 264.3, 268.3 and 343.3 against the printed 169, 266, 271 and 334. Turning it round, the weight on the same trials that each test's three printed numbers imply is 0.42, 0.35, 0.35 and 0.22, and those average to **0.337** against the design's 18 ÷ 54 = 0.333. The three printed slopes of each test are the same fit seen from three angles, and they agree.

**Step 3: rebuilding the picture from two numbers.** With equal trials at set sizes 2, 4 and 6, the mean reaction time of a test *is* its reaction time at four faces, so the mean RT and the set-size effect fix the whole line. Identity runs from 2,643 ms at two faces to 3,979 ms at six; ethnicity, the flattest, from 2,031 to 2,707.

**Step 4: a serial search is ruled out, a half-serial one is not.** Taking the published standard deviations across the 87 participants, the standard error of each mean slope is the SD over the square root of 87, and the ratio of two means has a standard error of about `ratio × √((se₁/m₁)² + (se₂/m₂)²)`. That gives:

| Test | Same slope (ms/face) | Different slope | Ratio | 95 % interval for the ratio | Rules out 2:1? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ethnicity | 199 ± 178 | 147 ± 145 | 1.354 | 0.98 to 1.73 | yes |
| Expression | 333 ± 219 | 230 ± 147 | 1.448 | 1.17 to 1.73 | yes |
| Gender | 363 ± 156 | 221 ± 106 | 1.643 | 1.42 to 1.87 | yes |
| Identity | 400 ± 318 | 315 ± 229 | 1.270 | 0.98 to 1.56 | yes |

All four intervals exclude 2, so the strictly serial, self-terminating account is out. None of them excludes 1.5, which is where our arithmetic and the paper part company: the paper rejects 1.5 for identity with z = −3.16, p = 0.001. It can, because it compares **each participant's own pair of slopes** and then tests the log ratios, a paired test; our interval treats the two means as independent and throws away the pairing. The lesson is general: a ratio computed from two published means and SDs is an upper bound on the uncertainty of a within-subject ratio, useful for ruling a value out and no good for ruling one in.

**Step 5: what the ratio says people did.** If checking one face takes `t` milliseconds and attention stops at the odd face, a present trial checks a random 1 to n of the n faces, so its slope is `t/2`; an absent trial that checks every face has slope `t`. Read the published ratios through that model (**our model**, not the paper's) and the absent trials behaved as if observers checked only a fraction of the faces before answering "same": **0.64** for identity, 0.68 for ethnicity, 0.72 for expression and 0.82 for gender. People quit early, most on the hardest test, which fits the accuracies: identity was answered correctly only 73 % of the time.

**Step 6: how many trials the ratio needs.** This is where the tool earns its place. We simulated single observers under the model of Step 5, with the paper's design (36 present and 18 absent trials split over three set sizes) and 600 ms of trial-to-trial scatter (**our assumption**, typical for two-to-three-second searches), and let `searchSummary` fit them. Averaged over 200 simulated observers, the fitted slopes come back at **315.9** and **395.8** ms per face against the model's 315 and 400: the tool recovers what was put in. But one observer at a time, the measured ratio has a median of 1.26 and a middle 95 % spread of **0.58 to 3.17**. The algebra agrees: with 12 present trials at each set size the standard error of the present slope is 600 ÷ √96 = 61 ms per face, and with 6 absent trials each it is 87 ms per face. A single 54-trial session cannot tell 1.27 from 2, or from 0.7. The paper's 87 participants are not generosity; they are the minimum the design needs.

## How we made the graphs

Both figures and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/face-visual-search-absent-present-slope-ratio.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `line` and `searchSummary` from `src/lib/sci/cogtasks.ts`, the maths behind the visual search task in the [[memory-attention-tasks]]: `line` is the least-squares fit with the standard error of its slope, `searchSummary` keeps correct trials inside the reaction-time window, fits present and absent separately and divides the slopes.

The inputs are the paper's four set-size effects with standard errors, the same/different effects with standard deviations from its Table 2, the mean reaction times, accuracies, trial counts and set sizes. Our own additions, all labelled above: the serial self-terminating model of Step 5, the 600 ms of trial scatter in Step 6, and the reconstruction in Step 3, which assumes the trials really were split equally across the three set sizes, as the paper says they were. The simulated sessions are simulations, not the paper's data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/memory-attention-tasks.mjs`, which pins the fits against SciPy and NumPy:

- On a made-up 48-trial search session, the present-trial fit must give a slope of 23.21512 ms per item, an intercept of 434.3953, r² of 0.953344 and a standard error of 1.148374, exactly as `scipy.stats.linregress` does, and the absent fit 39.32398 with r² 0.983808.
- Trials with reaction times outside the window, and incorrect trials, must be dropped before fitting (22 of 24 present trials survive in that case), and a session with only one set size must return no line at all rather than a slope of infinity.
- Simple reaction times must give mean 327.2857, median 312 and SD 38.71999 as NumPy does, with anticipations and false starts counted separately.
- New with this article: a noise-free session built from the identity numbers (630 ms to check one face, and an absent trial checking 400/630 of the faces) must give a present slope of exactly **315** and an absent slope of **400** ms per face, a ratio of 1.26984, with the absent points exactly on their line. The article's own script adds noise and shows the mean of 200 simulated observers coming back to 315.9 and 395.8.

## Check it yourself

The visual search task runs live, so this one is a replication rather than a recalculation. On the [[memory-attention-tasks]]:

1. Press **Visual search** in the *Choose a task* row.
2. Set *Search for* to **A T among Ls (hard)**, which is the serial end of the scale, like the paper's identity test.
3. Put `2, 4, 6` in *Set sizes* and **9** in *Trials per size, present and absent*: 54 trials, the paper's number.
4. Press **Start** and answer as fast as you can without guessing. It takes about three minutes.
5. The results card gives *Present: slope, intercept*, *Absent: slope, intercept*, each r², and *Absent ÷ present slope*. Expect a present slope of some tens of milliseconds per item for letters (faces are an order of magnitude slower) and a ratio anywhere between about 0.5 and 3: Step 6 explains why. Run it four times and average, or switch *Search for* to **An O among Xs (easy)** and watch both slopes collapse towards zero, which is what "pops out" means.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recompute a published slope ratio** and put an interval on it from the printed SDs, which immediately shows whether a paper's data can separate 1.5 from 2 at all.
- **Check that a pooled slope matches the sub-slopes** it was fitted alongside, as in Step 2: it is a free consistency test of any search table.
- **Size a search experiment** by simulating sessions and reading the standard error the tool reports, instead of guessing at trial numbers.
- **Teach the serial-versus-parallel argument** with a live task, three set sizes and the class's own slopes, with the easy condition as the control.
- **Turn a ratio into a quitting rule**, as in Step 5, which turns an abstract number into a statement about what the observer did.

## The fine print

- The paper's per-set-size reaction times are in its figure, not its text, so Figure 1 here is **our reconstruction** from the mean RT and the set-size effect, valid because the trials were spread equally over the set sizes.
- Our 95 % intervals in Step 4 treat the same and different slopes as independent. They are not: the same people produced both, which is why the paper's paired test is stronger.
- The "quitting fraction" of Step 5 follows from one specific model of search. Other models (parallel comparison with a decision threshold, or a mix) fit a ratio below 2 just as well.
- The simulated sessions use normal scatter around a straight line. Real reaction times are skewed, which makes a single session's slope slightly noisier still.
- The paper's own text is inconsistent about how many participants were analysed (87 or 80); we used 87, which is what its reported degrees of freedom imply. With 80 the intervals in Step 4 widen by about 4 %.
- Browser timing adds a few to tens of milliseconds of its own, so the tool's absolute slopes are for comparing people on one device, not for publishing against laboratory values.
