A polysaccharide is never one molecule. What a paper reports as "the" molar mass is a set of averages over a whole population of chains, and the gaps between those averages say more about the sample than any one of them. A 2026 paper in *Molecules* isolates a water-soluble fructan from the roots of *Cyathula officinalis*, shows it is a **branched levan** with a β-(2→6) fructofuranosyl backbone, and reports four averages from size-exclusion chromatography with light scattering: Mw 7.491 kDa, Mn 7.2 kDa, Mp 7.401 kDa and Mz 7.865 kDa, with a dispersity of 1.04. This article rebuilds that distribution with the [[polymer-molar-mass]] tool and asks what the four numbers imply together. The [[calibration-curve]] fits the standards a conventional SEC run needs, and the [[molar-mass-calculator]] gives the residue mass behind the degree of polymerisation.

## The paper in brief

The authors extracted and purified a fraction they call CoPS (94.8 % total carbohydrate) and attacked its structure from four directions: sugar composition by ion chromatography (fructose 95.60 mol %, glucose 4.40 mol %), linkage analysis by methylation and GC–MS, NMR, and molar mass by SEC with a multi-angle light-scattering detector and a refractive index detector. Light scattering gives absolute masses, so no column calibration is involved; they state dn/dc as 0.141 mL g⁻¹.

The structure they settle on is a β-(2→6)-linked fructofuranosyl backbone carrying β-(2→1)-linked fructofuranosyl branches at the 1,6-disubstituted residues, with an α-D-Glcp-(1→2)-β-D-Fruf unit as the initiating end: in other words a short, branched levan of the kind built on sucrose. The biology comes next: in a scratch assay the residual area at 12 h was 48.10 % with the polysaccharide gel against 70.13 % for the control, and in a 6 mm mouse excision wound the residual area on day 14 was 8.70 ± 1.20 % against 24.83 ± 1.51 %.

Our interest is the molar mass block, because four averages of the same distribution cannot be independent, and a tool can say whether they hang together.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the dispersity.** Dispersity is Mw/Mn by definition. The paper's numbers give **7,491 / 7,200 = 1.0404**, which rounds to the stated 1.04. That is the easy one, and it passes.

**Step 2: rebuild the distribution.** A dispersity of 1.04 is very narrow for a plant polysaccharide. We built a log-normal mass distribution carrying exactly the paper's Mw and dispersity, wrote it out as a size-exclusion peak on a straight-line calibration of our own (log₁₀M = 7.5 − 0.15 t, standards from 1 to 100 kDa; **our choice**, since the paper used light scattering and needed no calibration), and then handed that peak to the tool as if it were a chromatogram. It integrates 397 slices and reports:

| Average | Paper | Log-normal with Mw 7,491 and Đ 1.04 | Tool, read back from the simulated peak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mn | 7,200 | 7,202.9 | 7,202.9 |
| Mw | 7,491 | 7,491 | 7,491.0 |
| Mp | 7,401 | 7,345.5 | 7,362.1 |
| Mz | 7,865 | 7,790.6 | 7,790.6 |
| Đ | 1.04 | 1.0400 | 1.0400 |

Two readings of that table. First, the round trip works: the tool recovers Mn, Mw and the dispersity to better than 0.1 % from a peak it has never seen the parameters of, which is the real test of the slice integration and the calibration derivative. Second, the paper's Mz and Mp are both a little **above** the log-normal values, by 0.9 % and 0.8 %. For a log-normal distribution Mz/Mw equals the dispersity exactly; the paper's ratio is **1.0499**, not 1.0404. The difference is small but it is in the direction that means something: a slight excess of longer chains, a high-mass shoulder too small to shift Mw.

**Step 3: three averages pin two chain lengths.** Mn, Mw and Mz are three constraints, so a mixture of just two chain lengths has exactly enough freedom to match all three. Searching for that pair gives **77.8 % by mass at 6,597 g mol⁻¹ and 22.2 % at 10,624 g mol⁻¹**, which the tool's list mode turns back into Mn 7,203, Mw 7,491, Mz 7,865 and Đ 1.040: every one of the paper's numbers. That mixture is of course not what is in the bottle, but it is a useful picture of what the four averages are saying, namely roughly one chain in ten about 60 % longer than the rest.

**Step 4: the degree of polymerisation, and a cross-check.** With an anhydrofructose residue of 162.14 g mol⁻¹ the tool reports a number-average degree of polymerisation of **44.4** residues from Mn (46.2 if you use Mw instead). Now bring in the composition: a levan built on sucrose carries one glucose per chain, so a 44-residue chain should be about 1/44 = 2.3 mol % glucose. The paper measures **4.40 mol %**, which on 44-residue chains works out at **1.95 glucose units per chain**, and which taken on its own would imply chains of only 22.7 residues (Mn ≈ 3,685). Both readings can be true at once if some free or non-chain glucose rides along in the fraction, and the 94.8 % carbohydrate figure leaves room for that. It is the kind of arithmetic worth doing before quoting a DP: the molar mass and the sugar composition are two independent measurements of the same chain length. (The methylation shares are harder to square with a 44-residue chain still: 25.99 % terminal fructose plus 10.46 % terminal glucose against 11.64 % branch points would mean about 16 chain ends for 5 branch points, where a single branched molecule needs one more end than it has branch points. Incomplete methylation over-counts terminal residues, so we would not read a chain length off those shares.)

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/branched-levan-fructan-molar-mass-dispersity-degree-of-polymerisation.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `averages`, `polyfit`, `analyseTrace` and `exampleTrace` from `src/lib/sci/polymer.ts`, the maths behind the [[polymer-molar-mass]] tool, and `molarMass` with `parseFormula` from `src/lib/sci/chem.ts` for the 162.141 g mol⁻¹ residue.

Inputs from the paper: the four averages, the dispersity, and the fructose and glucose shares. **Our choices:** the log-normal shape, the calibration line and its 1 to 100 kDa standards, and the two-component mixture found by search. The chromatogram in Figure 1 is **simulated**; the paper's own trace is not in our hands.

## How we test the maths

The polymer maths is pinned in `tests/sci.mjs` on every change to fibub:

- Textbook mixtures: equal numbers of 10 kDa and 20 kDa chains must give Mn 15,000, Mw 16,666.667 and Mz 18,000; equal *masses* of the same two must give Mn 13,333.333 and Mw 15,000.
- A single chain length must give a dispersity of exactly 1.
- A log-normal trace with Mw 100,000 and Đ 2, read through a cubic calibration, must come back with Mn 50,000 (±50), Mw 100,000 (±200), Đ 2.000 (±0.005) and Mp = Mw/√Đ = 70,711.

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/polymer-molar-mass.mjs` pins this paper's case: the simulated 7.5 kDa peak must read back as Mn 7,202.9, Mw 7,491.0, Mz 7,790.6 and Đ 1.0400; the two-component mixture must reproduce all three of the paper's averages; the repeat unit C₆H₁₀O₅ must weigh 162.141 g mol⁻¹ and give a degree of polymerisation of 44.42 residues.

## Check it yourself

On the [[polymer-molar-mass]] page:

1. Press **List of chains**.
2. In *Molar mass (g/mol) and amount, one pair per line*, clear the example and type two lines: **6597, 0.778** and **10624, 0.222**.
3. Set *Amount is* to **mass (or weight fraction)**.
4. Put **C6H10O5** in *Repeat unit formula (optional, for degree of polymerisation)*.
5. The headline reads **Mn 7,203 g/mol, Mw 7,491 g/mol, Đ 1.04**, with *Mz* **7,865 g/mol**, *Dispersity Đ = Mw/Mn* **1.040** and *Degree of polymerisation (DPn)* **44.42 (repeat unit C₆H₁₀O₅, 162.14 g/mol)**: the paper's three averages at once. (*Mp* just names the heavier-weighted of the two entries, 6,597 g/mol, since a two-line list has no real peak.)
6. Now change the second line's amount to **0** and watch the dispersity fall to 1.000: that is what a truly single-sized chain looks like, and it is why no real polysaccharide reaches it.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check the averages in a paper against each other.** Mw/Mn must equal the quoted dispersity, and for a log-normal sample Mz/Mw equals it too; a gap means a tail, with [[polymer-molar-mass]].
- **Turn a GPC or SEC trace into averages** in the browser, with your own standards, a straight-line, quadratic or cubic calibration and a visible integration window.
- **Cross-check a degree of polymerisation** against the sugar or end-group composition, as in step 4, before quoting a chain length.
- **Fit the calibration itself** with the [[calibration-curve]] when the standards and the samples were run on different days.

## The fine print

- The paper measured absolute masses by light scattering. Our Figure 1 peak is a **simulation** with a calibration of our own, used to test the tool's read-back, not a reconstruction of the paper's chromatogram.
- Mz is the most sensitive average to a high-mass tail and the least certain experimentally; a 0.9 % difference from log-normal is suggestive, not proof.
- A branched polymer's size-exclusion behaviour differs from a linear one of the same mass. That does not affect light-scattering masses, but it does affect any conventional calibration, including ours.
- The degree of polymerisation uses an anhydrohexose residue of 162.14 g mol⁻¹ and ignores the one water and one terminal glucose per chain, which shift it by about 0.3 residues.
- We read the paper's molar masses in g mol⁻¹ as printed (7.491 kDa as 7,491). Its wound-healing results are quoted here only as context; we have not checked them.
