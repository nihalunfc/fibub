Frequency doubling in a resonant cavity lives or dies on one number: how tightly the light is squeezed inside the crystal. Too loose and nothing converts; too tight and the beam diverges inside the crystal and the phase matching falls apart. A 2025 paper in *Photonics* turns 840 nm into 420 nm with 77 % efficiency in a bow-tie ring cavity, and says its design waist is about 106 µm with a Rayleigh length of 42 mm. That number is not a measurement; it follows from the mirrors. This article rebuilds it with the [[gaussian-beam]] calculator, from the mirror radius, the mirror spacing and the round-trip length alone. For the fibre that usually delivers such a beam and the mirror coatings that make the cavity resonate, see the [[fibre-optic-calculator]] and the [[thin-film-coating]] simulator.

## The paper in brief

Rubidium Rydberg experiments need a few hundred milliwatts of light near 420 nm, and no laser diode of that power and linewidth is easy to come by. The authors take 840 nm light from a titanium-sapphire laser, lock it to an ultra-low-expansion reference cavity, and send it into a **bow-tie ring cavity** containing a 10 mm type-0 PPKTP crystal, a periodically poled crystal whose 3.925 µm poling period lets 840 nm light convert to 420 nm when the crystal is held near 56 °C.

The cavity has four mirrors: two flat ones 31 mm apart, and two curved ones of 150 mm radius of curvature. The curved pair is 210 mm apart, and the whole ring measures 490 mm. The input coupler passes 5 % of the 840 nm light and the other three mirrors reflect more than 99.95 %, which the authors say gives a finesse of 62.8 on paper and about 55 once the crystal is in. With about 800 mW going in they get 620 mW of blue out, a **77.7 % conversion efficiency**, and up to 870 mW at higher input, with an output beam quality M² of 1.017.

For the cavity design they quote a **waist of about 106 µm** inside the crystal and a **Rayleigh length of 42 mm**, 4.2 times the crystal length.

## Checked with the tools

Two separate checks follow. The first is arithmetic on the paper's own waist. The second is harder and more useful: a **recomputation of the waist from the cavity geometry**, which never uses the 106 µm at all.

**Step 1: does 106 µm give 42 mm?** The Rayleigh range of a Gaussian beam is z_R = πw₀²/λ. For w₀ = 106 µm at 840 nm the tool returns **42.02 mm**, matching the paper's 42 mm, and a half-angle divergence of **2.52 mrad**. Across the 10 mm crystal, from −5 mm to +5 mm, the beam radius rises only from 106.00 µm to **106.75 µm**, a change of **0.71 %**. That is the point of choosing z_R ≈ 4 × L: through the crystal the beam is effectively a parallel pencil, so every slice sees the same intensity and the same phase matching.

**Step 2: what the mirrors actually demand.** A ring cavity only supports a beam that reproduces itself after one trip round. Unfold the ring onto a straight rail that starts at the tight waist midway between the two curved mirrors, passes M3 at 105 mm and M4 at 385 mm (each a concave mirror of radius 150 mm, so a lens of focal length 75 mm) and closes at 490 mm. The tool's ABCD matrix for that rail is

A = −0.30667, B = −39.200 mm, C = 0.023111 /mm, D = −0.30667,

and because A = D the self-reproducing beam has z_R = |B| / √(1 − ((A+D)/2)²). That gives **z_R = 41.19 mm** and a waist of **104.94 µm** at 840 nm: within **1 %** of the 106 µm the paper quotes, from nothing but 150 mm, 210 mm and 490 mm.

Treating the crystal as 10 mm of glass of index 1.84 rather than air shortens the optical path a little and moves the answer to **103.9 µm** and 40.4 mm (**our refinement**, since the paper does not say whether its design figure includes the crystal). Feeding the measured output quality M² = 1.017 in as a beam-quality factor raises the waist to **105.8 µm**, because a real beam of the same curvature is slightly fatter than an ideal one.

**Step 3: how fussy is the spacing?** Figure 1 sweeps the curved-mirror spacing with everything else fixed. The waist climbs smoothly from 67 µm at 160 mm to 174 µm at 295 mm, and the cavity goes unstable outside roughly 150 to 300 mm. The spot on the curved mirrors barely moves, staying between 286 and 297 µm from 170 mm all the way to 296 mm and growing only at the tight edge (325 µm at 160 mm). At the paper's 210 mm the slope is about **0.5 µm of waist per millimetre of spacing**, so a 2 mm alignment error moves the waist by 1 %: comfortable. The paper mentions that its own optimisation put the best spacing nearer 230 mm, which the tool says would give a **115.5 µm** waist and a 49.9 mm Rayleigh length, still a loose, crystal-friendly focus. Both choices sit on the flat part of the curve, which is exactly where a doubling cavity should be built.

**Step 4: the rest of the ring.** Figure 2 follows the beam all the way round. It reaches **287 µm** on each curved mirror, which matters for coating damage and for how accurately the mirrors must be figured, and passes through a second, looser waist of **154 µm** halfway along the long arm, with a Rayleigh range of 89 mm. That long-arm waist is where a doubling cavity's input mode-matching lenses aim, and where an electro-optic modulator or an aperture would go.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/doubling-cavity-beam-waist-rayleigh-range-840-nm-ppktp.mjs` in fibub's public code. It uses four functions from `src/lib/sci/phgauss.ts`, the maths behind the [[gaussian-beam]] calculator:

- `parseElements`, which reads the rail lines `mirror 105 150` and `mirror 385 150` (position in mm, radius of curvature in mm; a concave mirror of radius R acts as a lens of focal length R/2);
- `systemMatrix`, which multiplies the free-space and mirror matrices into the round-trip ABCD;
- `propagate`, which carries the complex beam parameter q through the rail and returns one segment per gap, giving the waist and Rayleigh range in each arm;
- `rayleigh`, `divergence` and `radiusAt` for the single-beam numbers.

The only inputs from the paper are the wavelength (840 nm), the mirror radius (150 mm), the curved-mirror spacing (210 mm) and the round-trip length (490 mm). The eigenmode condition z_R = |B|/√(1 − ((A+D)/2)²) at a plane where A = D is standard resonator theory, applied to the tool's own matrix. The crystal index of 1.84 and the decision to place the tight waist exactly midway between the curved mirrors are **our assumptions**; the midpoint follows from the ring's symmetry about that arm, and the paper's own figure shows the crystal between the curved mirrors.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/gaussian-beam.mjs` on every change to fibub. It checks the ABCD machinery against an independent NumPy calculation and against Self's (1983) Gaussian lens formula:

- a three-lens rail (f = 50, 150, 250 mm at 200, 400 and 600 mm) must give the system matrix A = 1.8, B = 146.666667 mm, C = 0.012 /mm, D = 1.533333, with determinant 1;
- a 0.5 mm waist at 633 nm through that rail must end with its waist at 850.57034647717 mm and a radius of 0.03340501880585591 mm, matching NumPy to eight digits, and 0.9019386103116326 mm at the end of the rail;
- a concave mirror of radius 500 mm must behave exactly as a 250 mm lens, and a 0.5 mm waist at 633 nm must have a Rayleigh range of 1240.755 mm and a divergence of 0.40298 mrad.
- New with this article: the paper's ring, entered as `mirror 105 150` and `mirror 385 150` on a 490 mm rail, must give A = D = −46/150 and B = −39.2 mm, and the eigenmode must come out at z_R = 41.186 mm and w₀ = 104.94 µm at 840 nm, with the beam closing on itself (the final waist back at 490 mm, the same size as the first).

## Check it yourself

On the [[gaussian-beam]] page, in the *Beam* card:

1. Set *Wavelength (nm)* to **840**, *Beam quality M²* to **1**, *Waist radius w₀ (mm)* to **0.10494**, *Waist position (mm)* to **0**, *Rail length (mm)* to **490** and *Look at position (mm)* to **105**.
2. Clear the *Lenses and mirrors on the rail* box and type two lines: **mirror 105 150 M3** and **mirror 385 150 M4**.
3. In the results, *ABCD matrix, 0 to end* reads **A −0.3067, B −39.2 mm, C 0.02311 /mm, D −0.3067**.
4. *Starting beam* reads **w₀ 0.105 mm, z_R 41.2 mm**, with a divergence of **2.55 mrad** and a depth of focus 2z_R of **82.4 mm**.
5. *Final beam* reads the same waist, **0.105 mm**, with its waist at **490 mm**: the beam has closed on itself, which is what makes it the cavity's own mode. *Overall magnification* is **1.000×**.
6. *At 105 mm* reads **w = 0.287 mm**: the spot on the first curved mirror.
7. Now change *Waist radius w₀ (mm)* to **0.12** and watch *Final beam* drift away from the start: only the right waist closes the ring.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Designing or checking a doubling or OPO cavity.** Type the mirror radii and spacings, read the waist and the Rayleigh range, and compare z_R with your crystal length before ordering anything. The [[gaussian-beam]] calculator does the whole job.
- **Finding the stability edges.** Sweeping one spacing, as in Figure 1, shows where the mode blows up and how steeply the waist moves with alignment: the difference between a cavity that stays locked and one that drifts.
- **Sizing mirror and crystal apertures.** The spot on each mirror, 287 µm here, sets the clear aperture, the coating damage threshold and how much of the mirror's figure error the beam sees.
- **Mode-matching the input.** The long-arm waist and its position are what an input telescope must reproduce; the same tool images a known waist through a lens pair to hit it.
- **Teaching resonator theory.** The round-trip ABCD, the (A+D)/2 stability test and the eigenmode all appear on one page with real numbers, which is far more convincing than the usual algebra.

## The fine print

Our recomputation is of the **ideal** eigenmode: thin mirrors at normal incidence, no crystal walk-off and no thermal lens. In a real bow-tie the curved mirrors are hit off-axis, so they focus more strongly in the plane of the ring than out of it; the mode is therefore slightly elliptical, with two waists a few per cent apart, and the tool's single number sits between them. The paper's 1 × 2 mm crystal aperture and the 56 °C oven also add a weak thermal lens at high power that shortens the effective focal lengths a little. None of that moves the design conclusion, which is the point worth taking away: the stated 106 µm waist is not a fitted number but a consequence of three lengths, and anyone can confirm it in a minute without the laser.
