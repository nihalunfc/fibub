**Insolation**, the sunlight arriving at the top of the atmosphere, looks like the one thing in climate science that is simply calculable: no clouds, no models, just orbital geometry. A 2026 paper in *Climate of the Past* is a reminder that the geometry is the easy part and the book-keeping is where people go wrong — which date counts as the equinox, what a "monthly mean" means when the Earth moves at different speeds along its orbit, and how to average a quantity that is zero for half the year. We reproduced the paper's warnings with the [[insolation-calculator]], which computes the same quantities in a browser. For what the sunlight then does to a planet, see the [[energy-balance-model|climate balance model]], and for lining a proxy record up against it, the [[proxy-stacker]].

## The paper in brief

The paper documents a Python library for astronomical and insolation quantities and, in doing so, lays out the traps. Its main points are about definitions rather than physics:

- The year is **not symmetric**. Earth moves fastest near perihelion in early January, so the Sun takes about **186.5 days** to go from the March equinox to the September equinox and only about **178.5 days** to come back. Equal arcs of the orbit are not equal numbers of days.
- **Calendar months are not orbital arcs.** December, January and February together are 90.25 days and June, July and August 92 days, so a "seasonal mean" built from fixed months mixes different parts of the orbit — and the mismatch grows as the orbit changes over thousands of years.
- Taking the value at the **middle** of an interval instead of integrating over it costs from **a few per cent to several tens of per cent** in low-resolution models.
- Quantities such as the **semi-precessional forcing** at low latitudes need the maxima and minima of insolation over the year, not an average.

The paper also notes that the orbital solutions themselves have limits: Berger's (1978) solution is good to about 1 Myr, Laskar's to perhaps 40 to 50 Myr.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from today's orbit** (eccentricity 0.016708, obliquity 23.439°, longitude of perihelion 102.937°, the J2000 values, with a solar constant of 1361 W/m²; the paper rounds to about 1365). Nothing is fitted and no data are involved — the question is whether the tool's maths reproduces the paper's stated numbers and warnings.

**Step 1: the unequal half-years.** The tool solves Kepler's equation exactly, so it can be asked when the Sun reaches a given true longitude. From the March equinox (longitude 0°, day 80 by the usual convention) to the September equinox (180°) it gives **186.41 days**, and back again **178.83 days**. The paper's figures are 186.5 and 178.5 days: agreement to about a third of a day, which is as good as it can be given that the paper and the tool fix the equinox on the calendar slightly differently.

**Step 2: the conventional dates are not the real ones.** Everybody writes "21 September" for the September equinox. With the March equinox pinned to day 80, the September equinox actually falls on **day 266.41**, which the tool dates as **23 September** — 2.41 days later than the convention. That matters: the daily-mean insolation at 65°N is **191.66 W/m²** on the conventional day and **181.82 W/m²** at the true equinox, a **5.4 % difference** from a two-day slip. The June solstice and March equinox conventions are much safer (under 0.8 days and under 0.01 %), and the December solstice costs 0.6 %. So the trap is specific, not general — and the tool shows which date carries it, because you can switch *Time of year by* between **Calendar day** and **True solar longitude λ** and watch the number move.

**Step 3: the mid-point averaging error, in numbers.** The paper says the cost is a few per cent to several tens of per cent. We took each calendar month, computed the true time average of the daily mean over it, and compared it with the value at the month's mid-point.

| Latitude | Worst month | Mid-month value against true mean |
|---|---|---|
| 0° | June | −0.2 % |
| 30°N | December | −0.9 % |
| 45°N | December | −1.9 % |
| 60°N | December | −6.5 % |
| 65°N | December | −23.5 % |
| 80°N | October | −65.4 % |

In the tropics the error is a rounding error. At 65°N, the latitude Milankovitch's argument hangs on, December's true monthly mean is **4.65 W/m²** and the mid-month value is **3.55**; January is 13.84 against 11.85. At 80°N, October's true mean is **11.41 W/m²** and the mid-month value **3.94**. The pattern is clear: the error explodes wherever insolation is near zero and changing fast, so it is a *polar winter and shoulder-season* problem — exactly where ice sheets live. The biggest errors in absolute terms are elsewhere, around **4 to 7 W/m²** in June and October, which is still the size of a doubling of carbon dioxide.

**Step 4: the quantities that do not have this problem.** The paper's point is that integrated quantities are the safe ones, and the tool computes the two classic ones. At 65°N today, Milankovitch's **caloric summer** — the half of the year, in time, with the most sunlight, which the tool finds by searching all possible half-years — runs from **22 March to 21 September** with a mean of **366.0 W/m²**, against **61.4 W/m²** for the caloric winter; the summer half delivers **5.775 GJ/m²** and the winter half **0.968 GJ/m²**, for an annual total of 6.743 GJ/m². Huybers' alternative, the energy received on days above a threshold, gives **4.991 GJ/m² over 143.6 days** above 275 W/m². None of these numbers depends on a calendar at all.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/insolation-calendar-averaging-errors-daily-mean-caloric-half-years.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `dailyMean`, `lambdaOfDay`, `dayOfLambda`, `dateOf`, `halfYears` (the caloric half-years and the energy above a threshold), `globalMean` and `precessionIndex` from `src/lib/sci/insolation.ts`, the maths behind the [[insolation-calculator]].

Inputs: today's orbital elements and the 365.2422-day tropical year (general astronomical facts, built into the tool), and the conventional calendar dates and the 186.5 / 178.5-day half-years from the paper's text. The month lengths used for the averaging test are the ordinary calendar ones scaled to one tropical year (**our assumption** for how a model would slice the year). The true monthly means are integrals over 2000 sub-steps.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/insolation-calculator.mjs`, which pins the insolation formulas against independent calculations:

- Thirty combinations of orbit, latitude and day must match an independent NumPy code that integrates Kepler's second law and the hour angle, and `climlab`'s Berger routine: the longitude to 10⁻⁷ degrees and the daily mean to 10⁻⁵ W/m². At 65°N on day 172, for instance, the answer must be 477.9428 W/m².
- The equator at an equinox must give exactly S₀/(π r²) and the North Pole at the June solstice exactly S₀ sin ε / r², both analytic results, and the area-weighted annual mean over all latitudes must equal S₀ / (4 √(1 − e²)) to 0.02 W/m².
- Kepler's equation must round-trip: `dayOfLambda` and `lambdaOfDay` must be inverses to within 10⁻⁸ of a day across the year, and today's orbit must give between 186 and 186.8 days from the March to the September equinox.
- New with this article: today's orbit must put the September equinox on day 266.41, and the caloric summer mean at 65°N must be 366.0 W/m² against 61.4 W/m² in winter, with 143.6 days above 275 W/m².

## Check it yourself

On the [[insolation-calculator]] page:

1. In *Orbits*, press **Today's orbit** so orbit A reads *Eccentricity e* **0.016708**, *Obliquity ε* **23.439**, *Longitude of perihelion ϖ* **102.937** and *Solar constant S₀* **1361**. Untick **Orbit B (compare)**.
2. In *Place and time*, choose *Place* **65° N (the Milankovitch latitude)**, leave *Time of year by* on **Calendar day (equinox on 21 Mar)** and type **264** in *Day of year*. The headline reads **191.7 W/m²**.
3. Now set *Time of year by* to **True solar longitude λ** and type **180**, or just press the **September equinox** button. The headline drops to **181.8 W/m²**, and the table's *Calendar day* row shows **23 Sep**: the two-day slip, and the 5.4 % it costs.

5. With *Summer energy threshold* at **275** W/m², *Days ≥ 275 W/m²* reads **143.6** and *Energy on those days* **4.991 GJ/m²**.
6. Further down, *Global annual mean* reads **340.30 W/m²** and *Precession index e sin ϖ* **0.01628**.
7. To see the averaging trap for yourself, set *Place* to **Type a latitude below**, *Latitude* **80**, and step *Day of year* through **274** to **304** (October). The daily mean falls from about 30 to near zero inside one month; no single day in it represents the month.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check an insolation number before you use it.** Papers quote "June insolation at 65°N" with at least three different meanings; the tool gives the daily mean at a longitude, the mean over a calendar month and the caloric summer mean side by side.
- **Report integrated quantities** (caloric half-years, energy above a threshold) when you are comparing orbits, because they do not depend on a calendar.
- **Compare two orbits** directly: the tool takes a second set of elements and shows the difference, including as a latitude-by-season map, which is how a glacial and an interglacial setting differ.
- **Teach the calendar problem** in one screen by switching between calendar day and true solar longitude.
- **Line a proxy record up against the forcing** with the [[proxy-stacker]] and the [[age-depth-model]], then feed the forcing into the [[energy-balance-model|climate balance model]].

## The fine print

- The tool carries **no orbital solution tables**. You type the elements, so for a past or future time you need Berger's or Laskar's values from elsewhere; the paper itself discusses how far those are trustworthy.
- Our half-year lengths differ from the paper's by up to a third of a day because the tool pins the March equinox to day 80 of a 365.2422-day year, a model convention rather than a real calendar.
- The month lengths we used for the averaging test are our own choice. A model that slices the year into equal arcs of longitude instead would show smaller errors, which is the paper's recommendation.
- Insolation here is at the top of the atmosphere, with no atmosphere, clouds or surface. It is a forcing, not a surface flux.
- We checked the quantities the tool implements. The paper's library also computes semi-precessional forcing from annual maxima and minima, which the tool does not.
