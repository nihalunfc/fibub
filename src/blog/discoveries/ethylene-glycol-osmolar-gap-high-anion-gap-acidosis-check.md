Antifreeze poisoning is one of the few emergencies where simple arithmetic beats a scanner. Ethylene glycol itself is almost harmless until the liver turns it into glycolic and oxalic acid; by then the patient has a deep metabolic acidosis and failing kidneys. A 2025 case report in the *Ukrainian Journal of Nephrology and Dialysis* describes a massive ingestion treated late with fomepizole and haemodialysis, and the patient survived. Its abstract gives four numbers: pH 7.09, bicarbonate 3.2, an anion gap of 22.5 and an **osmolar gap of 83**. This article shows how far the [[acid-base-interpreter]] can get from those four alone, including recovering the pCO₂ the report does not print. The [[osmolarity-calculator]] does the osmolality arithmetic on its own, and the [[lab-unit-converter]] turns these values between the conventional and SI units different countries report them in.

This is general education about how the numbers work, not advice about any patient's care.

## The paper in brief

The report follows one adult who drank a large amount of ethylene glycol. **Fomepizole**, the antidote, blocks the enzyme that converts ethylene glycol into its toxic acids; it works best early, and here it was started late. Haemodialysis then removed both the parent alcohol and the acids. The abstract records a pH of 7.09, a bicarbonate of 3.2 mEq/L (so, in SI units, 3.2 mmol/L), an anion gap of 22.5 and an osmotic gap of 83 mOsm/L, along with raised lactate, raised glucose and mild hypocalcaemia, which it does not quantify. The patient survived.

The journal's own article page would not open for us, so the whole of this article rests on the abstract's numbers and on the licence recorded at Crossref. We say so plainly and have invented nothing.

## Checked with the tools

The interpreter needs six values: pH, pCO₂, bicarbonate, sodium, chloride and albumin. The abstract gives two of them. So this is a **reconstruction**, and the assumptions are listed before the results:

- **pCO₂ is derived**, not assumed: the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation fixes it once pH and bicarbonate are known.
- **Sodium 140 mmol/L and albumin 4.0 g/dL** are plain normal values (**our assumption**), with **chloride set to 114.3** so that the anion gap comes out at the paper's 22.5. Sodium cancels out of the gap, so any other normal sodium gives the same answer.
- **Glucose 10 and urea 7 mmol/L** (**our assumption**; the paper reports high glucose and kidney injury without numbers), with the measured osmolality set so the osmolar gap is the paper's 83.

**Step 1: find the missing pCO₂.** Rearranging the equation the tool uses for its first consistency check, pCO₂ = HCO₃ ÷ (0.03 × 10^(pH − 6.1)) = 3.2 ÷ (0.03 × 9.77) = **10.9 mmHg**. That is a startling number: normal is 40. The patient must have been breathing deeply and fast enough to blow carbon dioxide down to about a quarter of normal. It is also a useful check in reverse: with this bicarbonate, a pCO₂ of 20 mmHg would mean a pH of 6.83, and a normal pCO₂ of 40 would mean **6.53**, which is beyond survival. Anyone reading a case report with a bicarbonate of 3 and a pH in the sevens should expect a pCO₂ near 10.

**Step 2: is the breathing response appropriate?** Winter's formula says a pure metabolic acidosis with bicarbonate 3.2 should pull pCO₂ to 1.5 × 3.2 + 8 ± 2, that is **10.8 to 14.8 mmHg**. The derived 10.9 sits at the bottom edge of that band, so the respiratory compensation is as complete as the rule allows and no separate respiratory disorder needs to be invoked. Figure 1 puts the point on the acid-base map: it sits where the pH 7.09 curve crosses Winter's line.

**Step 3: the gap, and what the gap does not explain.** With albumin normal, the anion gap needs no correction and stays at 22.5 mmol/L, which the tool calls a **high anion gap metabolic acidosis** (its threshold is 16). Then comes the step most bedside calculators skip. The **delta ratio** compares how far the gap has risen with how far the bicarbonate has fallen: (22.5 − 12) ÷ (24 − 3.2) = **0.50**. A pure high-gap acidosis sits between 0.8 and 2. At 0.50 the tool reports a **second, normal-gap (hyperchloraemic) acidosis on top of the first** — exactly what you expect in a poisoning treated with litres of saline and with kidneys that have stopped reclaiming bicarbonate. For the picture to be a pure high-gap acidosis, the gap would have had to reach **28.6** (Figure 2).

**Step 4: the osmolar gap, the number that names the poison.** Calculated osmolality is 2 × 140 + 10 + 7 = **297 mOsm/kg**; with a measured 380 the gap is the paper's **83**, against a normal of 10 or less. The tool flags unmeasured osmoles and notes that a toxic alcohol fits the high anion gap. Because ethylene glycol has a molar mass of 62.07 g/mol, each mOsm/kg of gap is about 6.2 mg/dL of it, so a gap of 83 points to roughly **515 mg/dL** in the blood. That is five times the 100 mg/dL at which guidelines start dialysis, and it matches the report's word "massive" with a number.

One honest caveat about step 1: because we derived pCO₂ from the pH and bicarbonate, the tool's own Henderson-Hasselbalch check (it recomputes a pH of 7.09) is circular and proves nothing about the paper. Everything after it — the compensation band, the gap, the delta ratio and the osmolar gap — is a real test of the reported values against each other.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ethylene-glycol-osmolar-gap-high-anion-gap-acidosis-check.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `interpretAbg` and `NORMALS` from `src/lib/sci/acidbase.ts`, the maths behind the [[acid-base-interpreter]], once for the reconstructed panel and then 61 times more for Figure 2, varying the anion gap through the chloride while holding bicarbonate at 3.2.

The constant-pH curves in Figure 1 are the tool's own Henderson-Hasselbalch relation, rearranged for bicarbonate; Winter's line is the formula the tool applies in its compensation step. The pH, bicarbonate, anion gap and osmolar gap come from the paper's abstract. The sodium, chloride, albumin, glucose and urea are **our reconstruction**, as set out above, and the normal values (pCO₂ 40, HCO₃ 24, gap 12, raised above 16, albumin 4 g/dL, osmolar gap 10) are the tool's editable defaults.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/acid-base-interpreter.mjs`, which pins the interpreter against worked textbook cases:

- A classic diabetic ketoacidosis panel must come back as a high anion gap metabolic acidosis with appropriate Winter's compensation.
- A pCO₂ of 60 mmHg with bicarbonate 26 must be called an **acute** respiratory acidosis, and the same pCO₂ with bicarbonate 31 a **chronic** one, from the 1-per-10 and 3.5-per-10 rules.
- With albumin 2 g/dL, a measured gap of 12 must correct to 17 and the delta ratio of 0.63 must report both a high-gap and a normal-gap acidosis together.
- New with this article: the reconstructed panel must give a derived pCO₂ of 10.9 mmHg from pH 7.09 and bicarbonate 3.2, a Winter's band of 10.8 to 14.8 with the verdict "appropriate", an anion gap of 22.5, a delta ratio of 0.505 and an osmolar gap of 83, and the delta ratio must only reach 0.8 once the gap passes 28.6.

## Check it yourself

On the [[acid-base-interpreter]] page, in the *Blood gas and electrolytes* card:

1. Type *pH* **7.09**, *pCO2* **10.9** (leave the *pCO2 unit* on mmHg) and *HCO3 (mmol/L)* **3.2**.
2. Type *Sodium (mmol/L)* **140**, *Chloride (mmol/L)* **114.3** and *Albumin* **4** (leave the unit on g/dL).
3. In the *Optional* fields, type *Glucose* **10** and *Urea* **7** in mmol/L, and *Measured osmolality (mOsm/kg)* **380**. Leave *Lactate* empty: the paper gives no value.
4. The *Interpretation* line should name a **high anion gap metabolic acidosis**, with a **normal anion gap metabolic acidosis (additional)** and a **raised osmolar gap**.
5. Read the *Step by step* list: Winter's formula **10.8 to 14.8 mmHg**, "within the range: appropriate respiratory compensation"; anion gap **22.5**; delta ratio **0.50**; osmolar gap **83.0**.
6. Now change *pCO2* to **20** and watch the compensation step turn against you: the measured value is above the expected band, so the tool adds a respiratory acidosis. That is why deriving the pCO₂ in step 1 mattered.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Fill a gap in a published panel**: when a case report prints pH and bicarbonate but no pCO₂, the Henderson-Hasselbalch step recovers it, and absurd combinations show up at once.
- **Audit case reports and teaching sets**: a reported pH, pCO₂ and bicarbonate that disagree by more than 0.05 pH units point to a typo or a unit mix-up before anything else.
- **Separate two acidoses in a dataset**: the delta ratio turns one bicarbonate and one gap into a statement about how many processes are running, useful when coding a cohort of poisonings.
- **Screen for toxic alcohols**: a raised osmolar gap beside a raised anion gap is the fingerprint; the tool gives the calculated osmolality so the gap is yours to interpret.
- **Teach compensation as a band, not a number**, with the [[osmolarity-calculator]] and the [[kidney-function]] eGFR tool alongside for the renal side of the same case.

## The fine print

- Only the paper's **abstract** was available to us, so sodium, chloride, albumin, glucose, urea and the measured osmolality are our own consistent reconstruction, not the patient's values. The gap and the delta ratio depend on the reported gap, not on our sodium.
- The derived pCO₂ assumes a pK of 6.1 and a carbon dioxide solubility of 0.03 mmol/L per mmHg, the standard values the tool uses; both shift slightly with temperature and albumin.
- The paper reports raised lactate without a number. Some of the 22.5 gap is lactate rather than glycolate, which the delta ratio cannot separate.
- The 6.2 mg/dL per mOsm conversion assumes the gap is all ethylene glycol. Ethanol, often taken with it, adds to the gap at 4.6 mg/dL per mOsm and would make our 515 mg/dL an overestimate.
- This is one patient. Nothing here is a treatment threshold; dialysis and antidote decisions follow local poisons-centre guidance.
