Patulin is a mycotoxin that turns up in apple juice, and an aptamer that binds it would make a cheap test. A 2025 paper in *One Health Advances* selected one, PTL-1, and measured how tightly it binds twice: a dissociation constant (Kd) of **12.5 µM** by isothermal titration calorimetry and **18.4 µM** by a thioflavin-T fluorescence assay. Two methods, a 47 % difference, no comment. This article uses the [[binding-curve-fitter]] to ask a question any reader can ask of any affinity measurement: given the concentrations the paper worked over, could those two numbers ever have been told apart? For the same fitting problem in enzymes see [[enzyme-kinetics-fitter]], and for a dose-response curve, [[dose-response]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ran 20 rounds of selection against patulin, a small molecule of just 154 Da, which is a hard target: there is little surface for a DNA strand to grip. The winning sequence, PTL-1, was characterised by calorimetry (Kd 12.5 µM) and by a label-free fluorescence assay in which thioflavin-T reports a change in the aptamer's fold (Kd 18.4 µM). It was selective against nine other compounds, and in fruit juice the assay detected patulin from **0.2 µM** with a working range of **0.2 to 20 µM**.

Only the abstract was available to us, so this article uses nothing but the figures it states. Everything we compute is **simulated** from those values; none of the paper's own measurements are reproduced here.

## Checked with the tools

The whole analysis rests on one fact about the one-site binding curve: at a ligand concentration equal to Kd the target is half bound, and the curve only flattens at about **10 × Kd**. The paper's top concentration, 20 µM, is 1.1 × its own fluorescence Kd. That is where the trouble starts.

**Step 1: how much of the curve the assay actually sees.** With Kd = 18.4 µM, the fraction of aptamer bound is **52.1 %** at the top of the range and **1.1 %** at the detection limit of 0.2 µM. Reaching 90 % of sites would need 9 × Kd = **166 µM**, and 99 % would need **1,822 µM**. The assay therefore works on the rising, nearly straight part of the curve, where the signal is proportional to concentration (good for a test) but the curve's shape carries almost no information about where it would level off (bad for a Kd).

**Step 2: the two reported Kd values are the same curve.** Over 0.2 to 20 µM, the Kd 12.5 and Kd 18.4 curves never differ by more than **9.6 percentage points** of saturation, and only in the middle of the range (Figure 1). A fluorescence assay with a few per cent of noise cannot resolve that.

To show what a reader can do, we simulated nine measurements across the paper's own range from the Kd 18.4 curve with 3 % noise (**our assumption**) and fitted them with the tool's one-site model. It returns **Kd 28.5 ± 8.9 µM** and **Bmax 1.38 ± 0.29**, with r² = 0.987. The fit is excellent and the answer is wrong by 55 %: an r² near 1 says nothing about whether Kd is pinned down. The tool itself flags it, reporting that the highest ligand is only 0.7 × the fitted Kd, and that Bmax needs ligand up to about 10 × Kd.

**Step 3: repeat the experiment 300 times.** Redrawing the noise 300 times and refitting gives the honest picture. With the titration stopping at 20 µM, the recovered Kd has a median of **18.2 µM** (so the fit is unbiased on average) but a 10th-to-90th-percentile range of **12.5 to 28.4 µM**. The lower end is exactly the paper's calorimetry value. In other words, a fluorescence titration over this range would report "12.5 µM" about one time in ten with no change in the aptamer at all. Extending the titration to 200 µM narrows the range to **16.0 to 21.0 µM** (Figure 2).

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/patulin-dna-aptamer-dissociation-constant-titration-range.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitBinding` with the `one` site model, and `scatchard`, from `src/lib/sci/binding.ts`, the maths behind the [[binding-curve-fitter]].

From the paper we take only the two Kd values (12.5 and 18.4 µM), the detection limit (0.2 µM) and the working range (0.2 to 20 µM). **Our assumptions** are: a one-site model with no cooperativity; a signal that runs from 0 to 1 with Bmax = 1; normal noise of 3 % of full signal; nine concentrations spaced across the range; and, for Figure 2, concentrations spaced geometrically from 0.2 µM to each top value. The random draws use a fixed seed, so the script reproduces the same numbers every time. Nothing here is the paper's data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` pins this fitter, and `tests/sci-tools/binding-curve-fitter.mjs` adds the checks for this article:

- On exact one-site, one-site-plus-non-specific, two-site and Hill data, the fit must recover every parameter it was given to within 0.01 % (for example Bmax 100 and Kd 3).
- The tight-binding model must recover y0 = 0.1, ΔY = 0.8 and Kd = 0.05 from total-ligand data, and the exact quadratic must give (3 − √5)/2 bound when receptor, ligand and Kd are all equal.
- The Scatchard transform must be exact on perfect one-site data (Kd 3, Bmax 100), and Cheng-Prusoff must turn an IC50 of 40 with 2 nM of a labelled ligand of Kd 2.5 nM into Ki = 40/1.8.
- New with this article: a noiseless one-site curve with Kd 18.4 over 0.2 to 20 µM must give back Kd 18.4 and Bmax 1 to within 0.1 %; the fraction bound must be 0.52083 at 20 µM and 0.010753 at 0.2 µM; and the nine simulated points in "Check it yourself" must fit Kd 28.46 ± 8.93 and Bmax 1.3811, with the Scatchard line giving Kd 11.42 at r² 0.3579.

## Check it yourself

On the [[binding-curve-fitter]], in the *Binding data* card:

1. Clear the example box and paste these two columns, our simulated titration (concentration in µM, signal as a fraction of full binding):
   `0.2 0.0121`, `0.5 0.0623`, `1 0.0494`, `2 0.1077`, `5 0.1727`, `8 0.3229`, `10 0.3403`, `15 0.4997`, `20 0.5596` — one pair per line.
2. Set *Concentration unit* to **µM** and *Binding or signal unit* to **fraction**.
3. Set *Model* to **One site** (or leave it on *Best supported by the data*).
4. It reads **Kd 28.5 µM** (standard error 8.9) and **Bmax 1.38** (0.29), with a fit quality r² of **0.987**.
5. Read the facts underneath: *Ligand for 50 %, 90 % and 99 % of sites* shows **28.5, 256 and 2.82 × 10³ µM**, and *Ligand range* warns that the highest ligand is only **0.7 × Kd**, where Bmax needs about 10 × Kd. *Scatchard line (for comparison)* gives **Kd 11.4 µM** and is marked as curved.
6. Now delete the last two rows (15 and 20 µM) and watch Kd and its error jump again: the end of a titration is where the Kd lives.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Judge a published Kd in a minute**: divide the highest concentration used by the reported Kd. Below about 5, treat the Kd as a lower bound with a wide error, whatever the r².
- **Reconcile two methods** before claiming they disagree: simulate each method's own concentration range and see whether the gap is bigger than the fit's own spread.
- **Design the titration before running it**: choose the top concentration from the Kd you expect, aiming for 10 × Kd, and check what standard error that design will give.
- **Report the right numbers**: Kd with its standard error, the concentration range, the model, and whether Bmax was fitted or fixed. The tool prints all of them.
- **Keep an assay's working range separate from its affinity**: a sensor that works at 1 % occupancy can be excellent, and its Kd still badly determined.

## The fine print

- Only the paper's abstract was available to us, so we could not check its fitting method, its replicate count or its own error bars. Our simulation shows what its stated range allows, not what it did.
- The paper's two methods measure different things: calorimetry follows heat released as patulin binds, fluorescence follows a change in the aptamer's fold reported by a dye. A real difference between them is possible; our point is only that this range cannot demonstrate one.
- Our simulation assumes one independent site, no ligand depletion and a signal proportional to occupancy. If the aptamer folds cooperatively, the Hill model on the same page is the one to use.
- The 3 % noise is an assumption. Lower noise narrows the spread in Figure 2 but does not change the shape of the problem: short titrations cannot see where a curve flattens.
- Nothing here is food-safety advice. Limits for patulin in juice are set by regulators; check the official source.
