On the evening of 8 March 2022 a fireball crossed the sky over Oman for 3.2 seconds. Cameras of a new fireball network filmed it from several places, which let a team work out where it fell and where it came from in the Solar System. Eleven months later, searchers found two small stones, 13.85 g and 8.21 g, in the desert. Al-Khadhaf is the first meteorite in Oman to be seen falling and then recovered. This article shows how the [[orbit-elements]] tool lets anyone check that the published orbit really leads to Earth on that night.

## The paper in brief

The paper combines the camera data with a study of the stones. The meteoroid hit the air at **14.0 km/s**, weighed about **4 kg** and came in steeply, at 68° to the ground. It started to glow at 67.6 km and was last seen at 30.2 km, slowed to 7.4 km/s. Only about 50 g survived. The stones are an **ordinary chondrite of type H5–6**, the most common kind of stony meteorite. Their exposure to cosmic rays says the rock has been a small body in space for about **8.6 million years**.

From the shape and timing of the fireball, the team worked back to the orbit round the Sun: **a = 1.72 au, e = 0.45, i = 4.36°**. Here *a* is the semi-major axis (half the long width of the ellipse; 1 au is Earth's distance from the Sun), *e* the eccentricity (0 is a circle) and *i* the tilt against Earth's orbit.

Why it matters: only a few dozen meteorites worldwide have a known orbit. Each one ties a type of rock in the lab to a place in the asteroid belt.

## Checked with the tools

We gave the Orbit elements tool's maths the published orbit, with the two angles the abstract leaves out (from the fireball network's public event page), and the Sun as the central body. Then we asked three questions: does this orbit meet Earth's orbit, on 8 March, at 14 km/s?

**Step 1: the shape of the orbit.** The tool turns *a* and *e* into the nearest and farthest points from the Sun: perihelion **0.945 au**, just inside Earth's orbit, and aphelion **2.50 au**, in the inner asteroid belt (Figure 1). One lap takes **2.26 years**. This is an Apollo-type orbit: it crosses Earth's path.

**Step 2: is the meteoroid where Earth is?** The orbit is tilted by 4.36°, so it can only meet Earth's orbit where it crosses Earth's plane, at one of its two nodes. On 8 March, Earth was at heliocentric longitude 167.9°, which is the ascending node (347.97°) plus 180°: the descending node. At that node the meteoroid's true anomaly (its angle from perihelion) is 180° − 212.25° = **−32.25°**, and the tool puts it **0.9930 au** from the Sun. Earth was 0.9927 au from the Sun. The two positions are about **100,000 km** apart, less than a third of the way to the Moon. That is as close as rounded orbital elements and a simple model of Earth's orbit can show; the elements pass the test.

**Step 3: the entry speed.** At that point the tool gives the meteoroid a speed of **35.66 km/s** round the Sun. Earth was moving at 30.00 km/s, almost the same way. The difference, **8.71 km/s**, is the speed with which the meteoroid approached Earth. Earth's gravity then speeds it up: √(8.71² + 11.19²) = **14.18 km/s** at the top of the air. The paper measured **14.0 km/s**. Over the orbit's stated uncertainty (*a* ± 0.021 au, *e* ± 0.007) our figure ranges from 13.93 to 14.45 km/s, so the paper's speed sits inside it.

**Step 4: the direction.** The same speed difference, turned into sky coordinates, gives the point the fireball came from: **RA 138.9°, Dec +34.7°**, in the constellation Lynx. The fireball network's corrected radiant is RA 138.8°, Dec +34.7°, within 0.1°.

**Step 5: where it came from.** The aphelion, 2.499 au, lies right at the **3:1 resonance** with Jupiter, where a body goes round the Sun three times for each Jupiter orbit. By Kepler's third law that is at 5.203 × (1/3)^(2/3) = **2.501 au**. Jupiter's regular tugs there stretch orbits until they cross Earth's; it is one of the main routes by which pieces of the asteroid belt reach us (Figure 2). This is our observation from the numbers, not a claim made in the abstract.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/al-khadhaf-meteorite-fall-orbit-oman.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls the tool's own functions from `src/lib/sci/orbitel.ts`, the maths behind the [[orbit-elements]] tool:

- `pFromA`, `toState` and `info` turn the elements into a position, a velocity, the perihelion, aphelion and period.
- `meanToAnomaly` and `anomalyToNu` (Kepler's equation) place Earth and Mars on their orbits on the night of the fall.
- `propagate` and `track` draw the orbit and run it backwards one lap.

Where the inputs come from: *a*, *e*, *i*, the entry speed and the date are the paper's (with the network page's extra decimals); the two angles, the uncertainties and the radiant are from the Global Fireball Observatory's public event page; Earth's and Mars's orbits are JPL's mean planetary elements (for the Earth-Moon centre of mass, which is up to 4,700 km from Earth's centre); Earth's escape speed is a standard value. We used pure two-body motion: the planets' pulls on the meteoroid are left out.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci-tools/orbit-elements.mjs` checks the Orbit elements maths against published and independent answers:

- Vallado's worked example (*Fundamentals of Astrodynamics*, Example 2-5) must come back as p = 11,067.798 km, e = 0.832853, i = 87.8691°, Ω = 227.898° and ν = 92.335°.
- Kepler's equation must match SciPy's root finder: for mean anomaly 0.3 rad and e = 0.95, E = 1.17745010.
- Propagating orbits for 1 to 5 hours must match a numerical integration of the two-body equations (SciPy, DOP853) to within 0.0000001 km.

## Check it yourself

On the [[orbit-elements]] tool:

1. Set *Body* to **Sun**. The *μ = GM* box fills in itself.
2. Under **Elements**, leave *Size given as* on *Semi-major axis a (km)* and type **257607533** in *Size (km)* (1.722 au in km).
3. Type **0.451** in *Eccentricity e*, **4.36** in *Inclination i (°)*, **347.97** in *Ascending node Ω (°)* and **212.25** in *Argument of periapsis ω (°)*.
4. Leave *Position given as* on *True anomaly ν (°)* and type **-32.25** in *Position*.
5. Read the results: *Periapsis* about **141,400,000 km** (0.945 au), *Apoapsis* about **373,800,000 km** (2.50 au), *Period* about **825 days** (2.26 years), and *Radius and speed now* about **148,550,000 km, 35.66 km/s**: one Earth–Sun distance, at the speed used in Step 3.

The tool does not know where Earth is, so Steps 2 to 4 (the comparison with Earth's position and speed) need the script, or Earth's elements typed in the same way.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published fireball orbit**: does it meet Earth's orbit on the date, at the right distance from the Sun?
- **Turn elements into a state vector** (position and velocity) to start your own integration, or the other way round.
- **Find perihelion, aphelion and period** for a near-Earth asteroid or meteoroid from its catalogue elements.
- **Run an orbit back in time** with the propagation table to see where a body was before a close approach (two-body only).
- **Teach** how a few camera frames of a fireball lead to a place in the asteroid belt.

## The fine print

- The two angles (ω and Ω), the uncertainties and the radiant come from the fireball network's public event page, not the paper's abstract; the other values are the paper's.
- Our checks use two-body motion and mean planetary orbits. Close to Earth, the real path bends in Earth's gravity; the √(v² + 11.19²) step handles the speed but not the small bend in direction (which the network's "corrected" radiant removes).
- The link to the 3:1 resonance is our reading of the aphelion; the paper's abstract does not make it.
- The entry energy, about 400 megajoules (4 kg at 14 km/s, roughly 90 kg of TNT), was spent high in the air; nothing like a crater formed.
