A car's hydrogen tank is filled to **70 MPa**, about 700 times atmospheric pressure. At that squeeze hydrogen is nothing like an ideal gas: its molecules get in each other's way, so the same mass needs far more room than the ideal gas law predicts. Filling models have to pick an **equation of state** to describe this, and a 2025 paper in *Applied Sciences* shows the choice is not a detail: swapping one equation for another moved the computed tank pressure by up to a third. This article recomputes hydrogen at tank conditions with the [[real-gas-calculator]] and reproduces the size of that error. For the easier cases, start with the [[gas-law-calculator]], and for heavier petroleum gases see the [[pvt-correlations]].

## The paper in brief

The authors built a lumped-parameter model of a hydrogen tank being refuelled: a zero-dimensional model of the gas inside coupled to a one-dimensional model of heat flowing through the tank wall. They checked the model against experiments, then ran it with several equations of state in turn: **van der Waals**, the standard modifications of it (**Redlich-Kwong**, **Soave-Redlich-Kwong**, **Peng-Robinson**), and a polynomial equation they fitted to NIST reference data themselves.

Using the NIST-based pressure as the benchmark, their fitted polynomial was off by **0.30 %**, Redlich-Kwong by **1.83 %** and plain van der Waals by **17.90 %**. In a second case with a hotter inlet and a faster fill, the van der Waals error grew to **34.57 %**: the higher the pressure, the worse it gets. They also report that the choice of equation barely changed the computed *temperature* while changing the computed *pressure* a lot, and that among the three modifications Redlich-Kwong reproduced the Joule-Thomson inversion curve best.

That is a useful warning, but it is buried in a coupled simulation. The obvious question for anyone else is: how much of that error is the equation itself, with no tank, no wall and no fill rate? That part anyone can check in a browser.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation**, not a simulation of their tank. We take hydrogen as the Real gas calculator ships it (critical point 33.2 K and 13.0 bar, acentric factor −0.216, molar mass 2.016 g/mol: rounded general values, not the paper's) at **15 °C**, our choice of a filling-station temperature, and ask each equation what it says.

**Step 1: the equations fan out with pressure.** Figure 1 plots the compressibility factor **Z = PV/(RT)**, which is 1 for an ideal gas and tells you directly how much more room the real gas needs. At 70 MPa the five answers are Z = 1 (ideal), **1.679** (van der Waals), **1.505** (Redlich-Kwong), **1.484** (Soave-Redlich-Kwong) and **1.396** (Peng-Robinson). So the ideal gas law would let you believe a 70 MPa tank holds about 50 % more hydrogen than it really does, and the equations disagree with each other by up to 20 %.

**Step 2: the same gas, the same density, five pressures.** A tank has a fixed volume and a fixed mass of hydrogen in it, so the right comparison is at fixed **molar volume**: all the equations describe the same gas, and the question is what pressure they report. We take the molar volume Redlich-Kwong gives at each pressure (51.51 cm³/mol at 70 MPa, against 34.23 cm³/mol for an ideal gas) and ask the others for their pressure at that same density. Redlich-Kwong is our reference because the paper found it the closest cubic to NIST, within 1.83 %.

Van der Waals then reads **5.7 % high at 30 MPa, 18.2 % high at 60 MPa and 23.8 % high at 70 MPa**, crossing the paper's 17.90 % at about **59 MPa** and its 34.57 % at about **86 MPa** (Figure 2). The ideal gas law is worse and in the other direction: 33.5 % low at 70 MPa. Soave-Redlich-Kwong stays within 2.2 % of Redlich-Kwong everywhere, and Peng-Robinson drifts to about 10 % low.

**Step 3: one real tank.** A 5 kg hydrogen tank of 128 litres (a typical size for a car; **our numbers**) holds 2,480 mol at a molar volume of 51.61 cm³/mol. At 15 °C the tool's *Gas in a container* card gives **69.78 MPa** from Redlich-Kwong, which is a full tank, **68.30** from Soave-Redlich-Kwong, **62.45** from Peng-Robinson, **86.29** from van der Waals and **46.42** from the ideal gas law. Read as a safety margin, van der Waals would have you believe the same tank is 16 MPa over its rating, and the ideal gas law that it is 23 MPa under.

|---|---|---|
| van der Waals pressure error | 17.90 % (first case) | +18.2 % against Redlich-Kwong at 60 MPa, 15 °C |
| van der Waals error at higher pressure | 34.57 % (second case) | +34.6 % reached at about 86 MPa |

| Error grows with pressure | stated | +5.7 % at 30 MPa to +23.8 % at 70 MPa for van der Waals |
| Ideal gas law | not reported | −33.5 % at 70 MPa, 15 °C |

Two honest caveats. First, our reference is Redlich-Kwong, not NIST, because nothing about the user leaves the browser and the tool carries no reference tables; the paper's own figure of 1.83 % is what lets us use it. Second, the paper's errors come out of a coupled fill simulation at its own conditions, while ours are the bare thermodynamics at 15 °C. That the two agree to about a percentage point is the point: the error is the equation, not the plumbing.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/hydrogen-tank-equation-of-state-pressure-error.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `zCurve`, `solveTP`, `pressure` and `containerPressures` from `src/lib/sci/phrealgas.ts`, the maths behind the [[real-gas-calculator]]. `solveTP` builds the cubic in Z for the chosen equation, takes its real roots and keeps the stable one (the lowest fugacity coefficient); `pressure` runs the equation the other way, from temperature and molar volume.

Where the numbers come from: the three error percentages and the ranking of the equations are **from the paper's text**; hydrogen's critical constants and acentric factor are the tool's own rounded values; the 15 °C temperature, the 5 kg / 128 L tank and the choice of Redlich-Kwong as the reference are **our assumptions**.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/real-gas-calculator.mjs`, which pins all four cubics against an independent NumPy/SciPy solution (polynomial roots in volume, fugacity from the residual Helmholtz energy, saturation pressure by Brent's method):

- Carbon dioxide at 300 K and 50 bar must give Z = 0.7314551466 (van der Waals), 0.6974474900 (Redlich-Kwong) and 0.6711773034 (Peng-Robinson), to eight significant figures, with matching ln φ.
- The Peng-Robinson saturation pressure of carbon dioxide at 280 K must be 4,162,542.39 Pa, and no saturation point may be found above the critical temperature.
- On the van der Waals critical isotherm, Z at the critical point must come out at 3/8, the equation's own exact value.
- New with this article: hydrogen at 288.15 K and 70 MPa must give Z = 1.0000, 1.6789, 1.5049, 1.4842 and 1.3959 for the five equations in order, and 5 kg of hydrogen in a 128-litre tank at 288.15 K must give 46.42, 86.29, 69.78, 68.30 and 62.45 MPa.

## Check it yourself

On the [[real-gas-calculator]]:

1. In *Gas*, choose **Hydrogen (H₂)**. The critical constants fill in: Tc **33.2** K, Pc **13** bar, ω **−0.216**, molar mass **2.016** g/mol.
2. Set *Temperature* to **15** and *Temperature unit* to **°C**. Set *Pressure* to **70** and *Pressure unit* to **MPa**.
3. The headline reads **Z = 1.396 (Peng-Robinson): 140 % of the ideal-gas volume**, with a molar volume of **0.04778 L/mol** and a density of **42.2 kg/m³** (an ideal gas would be 0.03423 L/mol).
4. In the table *Every equation at these conditions*, read Z down the column: **1** (ideal gas), **1.679** (van der Waals), **1.505** (Redlich-Kwong), **1.484** (Soave-Redlich-Kwong), **1.396** (Peng-Robinson). Hydrogen needs 68 % more room than an ideal gas by one equation and 40 % more by another.
5. In *Gas in a container*, type *Amount of gas* **5** with *Amount unit* **kg**, and *Container volume* **128** with *Volume unit* **litres**. The table gives **46.42, 86.29, 69.78, 68.30 and 62.45 MPa** for the five equations, and the line above it says an ideal gas would give 46.42 MPa.
6. Change *Container volume* to **160** litres and watch every pressure fall: Redlich-Kwong to **50.36 MPa**, van der Waals to **57.16**. The gap narrows because the gas is less squeezed.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a model's equation of state** before trusting its pressures: run your state point through all five equations and see how much of your result is physics and how much is the closure you picked.
- **Size a vessel or a fill**: the *Gas in a container* card turns a mass and a volume into the pressure each equation predicts, which is the number a relief valve cares about.
- **Teach where the ideal gas law stops working**: hydrogen at 70 MPa is a 50 % error, and students can see it in one chart.
- **Compare fuels on the same footing**: methane, nitrogen, carbon dioxide and helium are in the same list, so a compressed-gas comparison takes a minute.
- **Report the equation, not just the number**: if your paper gives a tank pressure, say which equation produced it, as this one does.

## The fine print

- Our reference is Redlich-Kwong, not NIST data: the tool is offline and carries no reference tables. The paper's 1.83 % figure is what makes that reference usable.
- The paper's errors come from a coupled fill simulation with heat flow into the tank wall; ours are the equations alone at one temperature. The agreement is in size, not in setup.
- Cubic equations are mean-field models. Near the critical point, or in metastable states, none of them should be trusted, as the paper says itself.
- Hydrogen's critical temperature is only 33 K, so at room temperature it is far above critical and the acentric factor (−0.216) is well outside the range Soave and Peng-Robinson were fitted for. That is part of why the cubics disagree so much here.
- We did not test the paper's own fitted polynomial equation, which is not in the tool.
