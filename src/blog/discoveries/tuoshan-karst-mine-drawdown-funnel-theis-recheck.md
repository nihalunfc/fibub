Dewatering a mine in karst is a race between the pump and the sinkholes. A 2026 paper in *Applied Sciences* builds a seven-layer groundwater model of the Tuoshan mine field in central Hunan, China, and predicts how the water table falls while the mine pumps and recovers after it closes. The headline numbers are easy to read and easy to check: **200 m³ an hour**, a central drawdown of **4.3 m after one year**, **1.9 to 2.2 m within the first week**, and a drawdown funnel about **1.2 km across**. This article puts them through the [[well-field-drawdown]] tool, which adds up Theis drawdowns for any number of wells, and finds that the funnel's own shape fixes the transmissivity far more tightly than the paper's single-well permeability does. For the measurements behind those numbers, see the [[pump-test-analyser]] and the [[darcy-flow-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The mine works an orebody in karstified Carboniferous dolomite (unit C2d) beneath a thin Quaternary silty-clay cover, 2.0 to 14.0 m thick, in an area with 170 recorded karst collapse points. The authors take a hydraulic conductivity of **0.042 m/s** for the dolomite from a single-well test at borehole ZK87, ten times that for water-conducting faults and 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ m/s for the sandstone that confines the aquifer from below, and calibrate a numerical seepage model on 2014 water levels (RMSE 0.32 m, Nash-Sutcliffe 0.85), validating it on 2015 to 2016 (RMSE 0.41 m, NSE 0.81).

With the mine pumping 200 m³/h, the model gives water levels that fall 1.9 to 2.2 m in a week, reach a central drawdown of 4.3 m after a year and then creep down by about 0.8 m a year; the funnel is about 1.2 km across while pumping and shrinks to about 0.5 km five years after it stops, recovering some 0.3 m a year. The paper also notes that rain above 100 mm a day lifts water levels by 2.8 to 3.1 m in two or three days — a strongly recharged system.

## Checked with the tools

A numerical model has hundreds of parameters, but a drawdown funnel is a simple shape, and the classical Theis solution behind the [[well-field-drawdown]] tool gives three checks that need nothing but the numbers above. We treat the mine as one well pumping **4,800 m³ a day** with an effective drainage radius of **10 m** (**our assumption**), and convert a conductivity into a transmissivity with an aquifer thickness of **50 m** (**our assumption**, the paper does not give one).

**Step 1: the funnel's shape fixes the transmissivity, and storage does not come into it.** Once the drawdown cone is well developed, the Theis drawdown falls off as the logarithm of distance, so the *difference* between two radii tends to Q ÷ (2πT) × ln(r₂ ÷ r₁) — the storage coefficient drops out (exactly in the small-u limit; the tool's full Theis solution gives a difference 4 % smaller at the extreme storage we use below). Going from **4.3 m at the mine to about 0.1 m at 600 m** therefore gives

**T = 4800 × ln(600 ÷ 10) ÷ (2π × 4.2) = 745 m² a day**,

which for a 50 m aquifer is a hydraulic conductivity of **1.7 × 10⁻⁴ m/s** (4.3 × 10⁻⁴ for a 20 m aquifer, 8.6 × 10⁻⁵ for 100 m).

**Step 2: the published permeability is 244 times too large to make the published drawdown.** The paper's 0.042 m/s over 50 m is a transmissivity of **1.8 × 10⁵ m² a day**. Put that in the tool with a storage coefficient of 0.02 and pump 4,800 m³ a day for a year: the drawdown at the mine is **3.8 cm**, and **2.1 cm** at 600 m. That is a hundred times less than the model reports, and the cone would be hundreds of kilometres wide. Both numbers cannot describe the same block of rock: 0.042 m/s is a conduit value from one borehole, while the drawdown describes the effective transmissivity of the whole fractured mass, including the parts that barely flow.

| Check | Paper | Tool gives |
|---|---|---|
| Transmissivity behind a 4.3 m / 600 m funnel | not stated (K = 0.042 m/s at ZK87) | **745 m² a day**, i.e. K ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ m/s for a 50 m aquifer |
| Drawdown after a year with K = 0.042 m/s | 4.3 m at the centre | **0.038 m** at the mine, 0.021 m at 600 m |
| Radius of the 0.5 m drawdown contour at one year | funnel about 1.2 km across (600 m radius) | **545 m** |
| Radius of the 0.1 m contour at one year | — | **1,079 m** |
| Drawdown in the first week | 1.9 to 2.2 m | **2.44 m** (54.7 % of the one-year value; any smaller storage gives a larger share) |

**Step 3: "a 1.2 km funnel" is a statement about a contour, not about an edge.** With T = 745 m² a day the modelled cone reaches **0.5 m of drawdown at 545 m** after a year — pleasingly close to the mapped 600 m radius — while the **0.1 m** contour is already at **1,079 m** and the cone is still measurable far beyond that (Figure 1). A funnel has no edge; it has whatever contour someone chose to draw. Anyone comparing two studies' funnel widths has to compare contours at the same level.

**Step 4: the drawdown settles faster in the paper than Theis allows.** The paper's model gets about **2.05 m in the first week and 4.3 m in the first year** — the week is 48 % of the year. In Theis, that fraction is at its smallest when storage is at its largest; with the physically extreme value S = 1 it is **54.7 %**, rising to 61.7 % at S = 0.2 and 68.7 % at S = 0.02. The paper's model therefore stabilises faster than any Theis curve can, which is exactly what recharge does — and the paper's own rainfall response, 2.8 to 3.1 m in two or three days, says recharge here is strong. Theis has none, so it keeps falling: **5.29 m by five years** and no sign of stopping (Figure 2), whereas the paper's model recovers 0.3 m a year once pumping stops. The lesson is a practical one: in a well-recharged karst aquifer, a Theis extrapolation is an upper bound on long-term drawdown, not a prediction.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/tuoshan-karst-mine-drawdown-funnel-theis-recheck.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `drawdown` from `src/lib/sci/wellfield.ts`, which sums `theis` from `src/lib/sci/pumptest.ts` over the wells — the maths behind the [[well-field-drawdown]] tool. Contour radii are found by bisection on the same function.

From the paper: the pumping rate (200 m³/h), the drawdowns (1.9 to 2.2 m in a week, 4.3 m in a year), the funnel width (about 1.2 km) and the conductivity (0.042 m/s). **Our assumptions:** the 10 m effective drainage radius, the 50 m aquifer thickness, the 0.1 m drawdown at the mapped funnel edge, the storage values (1, 0.2, 0.02, 0.002) and the choice to read the mine as a single well. Nothing here is the paper's own model output.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks this maths from two directions:

- The **well function** W(u) itself: W(1) must equal 0.21938393439552 to 1e-12, the small-u series must match −γ − ln u + u − u²/4, and W(2.5) must match a fine numerical integral of e⁻ˣ/x to 1e-9.
- **Theis and its fits**: drawdowns generated with T = 250 m²/day and S = 2 × 10⁻⁴ must be fitted back to within 1e-6 of those values, Cooper-Jacob must recover T within 2 % on exact data, and the distance-drawdown line must return T and S exactly.
- **Superposition and boundaries** in `wellfield.ts`: two wells must add exactly, a well that starts later must behave as a Theis curve with its own clock, the drawdown on a constant-head line must be exactly zero, and there must be no gradient across a no-flow line.
- **New with this article**, in `tests/sci-tools/well-field-drawdown.mjs`: pumping 4,800 m³/day for a year with T = 745 m²/day and S = 1 must give 4.47 m at 10 m and 0.428 m at 600 m; the drawdown difference between those radii must approach Q ÷ (2πT) × ln(60) as storage falls, within 0.1 % at S = 0.002 and 4 % at S = 1; and the one-week share of the one-year drawdown must rise as storage falls.

## Check it yourself

On the [[well-field-drawdown]] page, in the *Well field* card:

1. Set *Transmissivity T (m²/day)* to **745**, *Storativity S* to **1** and *Well radius (m)* to **10**.
2. In the wells box (*Name, x (east), y (north), pumping rate and the day it starts*) leave one line: **mine, 0, 0, 4800, 0**.
3. Leave *Straight boundary* on **None**. Set *Map at day* to **365**, *Observation point x (m)* to **10** and *Observation point y (m)* to **0**.
4. The headline reads **Drawdown at (10, 0) on day 365: 4.47 m** — the paper's 4.3 m, near enough.
5. Change *Observation point x (m)* to **600**: it reads **0.428 m**. The funnel edge is still dropping half a metre of head.
6. Change *Map at day* to **7**: the drawdown at (10, 0) is **2.44 m**; at day **1825** it is **5.29 m**.
7. Now set *Transmissivity T (m²/day)* to **181400** (the paper's 0.042 m/s over 50 m) and *Storativity S* to **0.02**: day 365 at (10, 0) gives **0.0382 m**. That is the mismatch, in one reading.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a dewatering model in five minutes.** Any reported funnel with a central drawdown and a mapped radius fixes a transmissivity by hand; if it disagrees with the model's input conductivity, say why, with the [[well-field-drawdown]] tool.
- **Report funnels as contours.** Give the drawdown level your funnel outline follows, then the width means something to the next reader.
- **Bound long-term drawdown.** Theis with no recharge is the pessimistic case; run it alongside your model and quote both.
- **Plan an observation network.** The tool's map and time series show where and when a drawdown of a given size will be measurable, so monitoring boreholes go where the signal is.
- **Separate conduit from matrix.** A single-well test in karst measures the conduit; a regional funnel measures the mass. Compare the two with the [[pump-test-analyser]] and the [[slug-test-analyser]] before trusting either.

## The fine print

- Our drawdowns are **computed from the paper's stated numbers**, not fitted to its model output, which is not published as data.
- Theis assumes one uniform, confined, infinite aquifer of constant thickness, no recharge and instant release of water from storage. Karst is none of those things; that is the point of the comparison, not a flaw hidden in it.
- The mine's effective drainage radius (10 m) and the aquifer thickness (50 m) are our assumptions. The transmissivity in step 1 does not depend on either, but the conductivity in steps 1 and 2 does: double the thickness and halve the conductivity.
- S = 1 is not a real storage coefficient. We use it only as the limit that makes Theis as slow as it can possibly be.
- The paper raises the pumping rate to 250 m³/h in October 2016; we use 200 m³/h throughout, as its drawdown figures do.
