Galileo and others turned telescopes on the Sun around 1611, and sunspots became famous. But could anyone have seen a spot earlier? A 2025 paper in the journal *Histories* tests that by doing it: the author watched the Sun with his naked eye, with pinholes (the same idea as the "meridian lines" built into old churches) and with a small telescope projecting onto a wall, and compares what he saw with historical records. His photos and measurements come with plenty of numbers, and nearly all of them are geometry that can be checked: how much of the Sun the Moon covered during a partial eclipse, how wide a pinhole image gets, and how big a spot has to be. This article rechecks them with the [[sky-events-calendar|astronomy calendar]] and the [[sun-position-calculator|Sun position calculator]], and the eclipse numbers can be compared in the [[eclipse-finder]].

## The paper in brief

The paper asks whether sunspots could have been noticed before telescopes. The author's answer: big ones, yes, by pinhole or even the naked eye when the Sun is dimmed by haze or low in the sky, but such sightings would have been rare, scattered and without much effect on how people understood the Sun. He supports that with his own 2025 observations, among them the big sunspot group AR 4079 in early May 2025, about 150 by 50 arcseconds including its penumbra and covering 1250 millionths of the Sun's hemisphere (MH). He also reads old art and records, from a pinhole meridian line in Florence (1475) to the Spörer minimum, the stretch of very few spots between about 1460 and 1550, as clues to whether spots could have been seen.

Most of what can be tested with tools is the geometry. The paper shows a partial solar eclipse from Locarno on 29 March 2025 (Figure 11), a projected Sun image 105 cm wide from a "20 × 60" telescope on a wall 5.4 m away on 9 May 2025, a pinhole image formed by gaps in a plane tree, and the 220 mm Sun image from the pinhole of the Clementine Gnomon, a meridian line in a Rome church.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from numbers printed in the paper**. We did not measure any of the photographs. We used the Sun and Moon positions behind the [[sky-events-calendar|Sky events calendar]], and the Sun's distance, altitude and noon from the [[sun-position-calculator|Sun position calculator]]'s maths.

**Step 1: the eclipse.** From the IRSOL observatory in Locarno, the calendar lists a **partial solar eclipse on 29 March 2025 with maximum at 11:05:19 UT**, 7 minutes after the New Moon (10:58:24 UT), and **11 % of the Sun covered**, with the Sun 47° high. Why does the paper say 21 %? Because the paper's figure is the **magnitude**, the share of the Sun's *diameter* that the Moon covers: from the same positions the magnitude at maximum is **0.2097**, the paper's 21 %. The area covered (the obscuration) is only 11.3 %, the calendar's 11 %. They are different measures of the same eclipse.

**Step 2: the "15.2 %".** The caption says the eclipse "reached 21 % of magnitude 23 minutes before" the photo, and the text says it was visible "at 15.2 %" through cloud. Those two statements seem to clash, but they do not. Figure 1 shows magnitude falling after maximum: it is **0.150 at 23 minutes later**, and reaches exactly 0.152 after **22.5 minutes**. The photo was taken on the way out, about 11:28 UT, when the magnitude had dropped to about 15 %. So the two numbers are consistent: one is the maximum, the other the value at about the time of the photo (we infer the photo time from the paper's 23 minutes, and say so in the fine print). The area covered at that moment was about 6.9 %.

**Step 3: the telescope projection.** On 9 May 2025 the Sun's angular width was **31.67 arcminutes**. A projected image 105 cm wide at 540 cm from the eyepiece then implies a magnification of 105 / (540 × 0.009212) = **21.1×**, in line with the "20 × 60" label of the telescope (nominal magnifications are rounded, and projection distance is measured from the eyepiece, not from the focus).

**Step 4: pinhole images.** A pinhole image of the Sun is the Sun's angular width times the distance, plus the size of the hole. With a plane-tree gap of about 58 mm and a distance of about 30 m, that is **275 mm** (point hole) to **333 mm** (hole included), and the paper measured about **300 mm**: right between the two, as expected for an image with soft edges. Figure 2 plots both lines against distance. For the Clementine Gnomon in Rome, take the pinhole height as 20.3 m (a public value, our assumption). The Sun at Rome's noon on 30 May 2025 stands **69.9° high**, so the ray travels **21.6 m** and the image should be **198 mm** wide for a point hole, **223 mm** with the 25 mm hole. The paper measured **220 mm**. In Florence, a pinhole 90 m up with the Sun 69.7° high at the summer solstice gives a ray of 96 m and an image **878 mm** across and **937 mm** long (it stretches along the meridian line), against "about 1 m" in the paper.

**Step 5: how big is a spot?** A spot of area A millionths of the hemisphere (MH), seen face-on, has an equal-area width of 2·√(2A × 10⁻⁶) times the Sun's radius. For the 100 MH visibility limit, that is **26.9 arcseconds**, just under the eye's resolution of about 30. For AR 4079's 1250 MH it is **95 arcseconds**. The paper's own 150 × 50 arcsecond ellipse works out to about **1040 MH**, 17 % less than the 1250 MH quoted, which is what you would expect for a spot group that is not an exact ellipse and is seen at an angle. So the visible-limit story holds together: a spot has to be several times the eye's resolution to show, and the pinhole's own blur of one thousandth of its height (about 200 arcseconds) smears small spots out.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/pinhole-sun-images-partial-eclipse-locarno-2025-sunspot-visibility-geometry.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `monthEvents` from `src/lib/astro/sky.ts` (the Sky events calendar's list of Moon quarters, apsides and local solar eclipses, found with astronomy-engine), and `sunAltAz` and `sunDay` from `src/lib/astro/sun.ts` (the Sun position calculator's altitude, distance and solar noon). The eclipse curves in Figure 1 use the same Sun and Moon positions; magnitude is the overlap in diameters and obscuration the overlap area of two discs, which is our extension of the calendar's single "% covered" figure.

Inputs: the dates, the hole sizes, the distances, the image widths, the magnification label and the sunspot sizes are printed in the paper. **Our assumptions:** the site coordinates, the Clementine pinhole height of 20.3 m and the pinhole distance of 30 m for the tree (the paper says "about"). The pinhole formula (width = distance × the Sun's angle + hole) and the spot formula are our geometry, not tool functions.

## How we test the maths

Every change runs `tests/sci-tools/sky-events-calendar.mjs`, new with this article. It checks that Locarno's partial eclipse of 29 March 2025 is at 11:05:19 UT with 11 % covered, 6.9 minutes after the New Moon; that the magnitude is 0.2097 at maximum and 0.1497 after 23 minutes; that the May 2025 first quarter (4 May, 13:52 UT) and perigee (26 May, 359,014 km) are right; that the Sun's noon altitude at Rome and Florence agrees with 90° − latitude + declination; and that the Sun's width on 9 May 2025 is 31.67′, giving a magnification of 21.1.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[sky-events-calendar|astronomy calendar]]. In the place box press **Change** and type latitude **46.1775**, longitude **8.7878** and height **360**.
2. Use **‹ and ›** to go to **March 2025**. In *Events this month*, find **New Moon** and **Partial solar eclipse** on 29 March, the eclipse about 7 minutes after the New Moon, with **11% of the Sun covered**. Times are shown in your chosen time zone (the page default is yours; set *Times in* to UTC for 10:58 and 11:05).
3. Go to **May 2025**. Read **First quarter** on 4 May (13:52 UT) and **Moon at perigee (closest)** on 26 May, **359,014 km away**.
4. Open the [[sun-position-calculator]], set the place to Rome (41.9035, 12.4955) and the date to 30 May 2025: the *Solar noon* row reads about **69.9° high**. Change the place to latitude 43.7731 and the date to 21 June 2025 for Florence: the same row reads **69.7° high**.
5. On paper, divide: pinhole height ÷ sin(noon altitude) × 0.0092 gives the image width in the same unit; for 20.3 m and 69.9° that is about 0.20 m.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a pinhole or projection demonstration**: the width of the Sun's image on a screen is distance × 0.0092 plus the hole size, and the calendar gives the Sun's height at the time.
- **Read an eclipse report correctly**: check whether a quoted percentage is magnitude (diameter) or obscuration (area); the numbers differ by a factor of about 2 near maximum.
- **Predict a partial eclipse for a given place**: the calendar gives the time and area covered, the [[eclipse-finder]] the contact times and magnitude.
- **Teach historical astronomy**: meridian lines, pinhole cameras and the Sun's size are a quantitative way into how early observers saw the Sun.
- **Judge sunspot visibility claims**: convert spot areas in MH to arcseconds, as in step 5, and compare them with the eye's resolution or the blur of a pinhole.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation of printed numbers**, not a test of the paper's main argument, which is historical: whether sunspots could have been seen before the telescope. That needs records, not geometry, and no fibub tool tests it.
- The paper does not give the photo's time for the eclipse, so the 22.5-minute match is an inference from two printed numbers (21 % at maximum, 15.2 % through cloud). The 23 minutes in the caption supports it but we cannot confirm the exact time.
- Sites are assumed: the paper gives no coordinates for Locarno beyond "IRSOL", and no height for the Clementine pinhole; our 20.3 m is a public value from elsewhere.
- The paper contains a few small inconsistencies (for example two different dates for the Clementine Gnomon photograph and "20 × 600" in one caption), which we left alone.
- The sunspot formulas ignore foreshortening and the exact shape, so they are rough: a few tens of per cent.
