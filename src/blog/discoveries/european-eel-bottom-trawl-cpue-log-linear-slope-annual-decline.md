The European eel is critically endangered, yet its fishery data are patchy. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Fish Science* sidestepped the fishery by using **fishery-independent trawl surveys**, standard tows run by research vessels in the Baltic, Kattegat, Skagerrak and North Sea. In seven surveys the number of eels caught per trawling hour fell by about 0.10 a year. This article shows how to turn such a slope into a plain percentage with the [[cpue-trend]] calculator, and how long a series must be before the trend is clear. For growth of a declining stock see [[population-growth-models]]; for a general trend test, the [[trend-analyser]].

## The paper in brief

The author took catch per unit effort, **CPUE** (eels per trawl hour), from seven surveys that run from 1985 or 1991 to 2023 or 2024. For each survey he fitted a zero-inflated negative binomial mixed model with year and longitude as fixed effects and depth as a random effect, once for all eels and once for eels of 50 cm or longer. The year coefficient is the yearly change. For all surveys pooled it was **−0.104**; for individual surveys it ranged from −0.069 to −0.333, and only the North Sea's first-quarter survey showed no decline (+0.001, not significant). The paper concludes there is no sign of recovery despite conservation measures.

The paper does not say which link function the model used. For a negative binomial model the default is the logarithm, so a coefficient b means the expected CPUE is multiplied by exp(b) each year. We use that reading throughout, as **our assumption**.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: slopes to percentages.** With a log link, a slope of −0.10371 a year is a change of 100 × (e^−0.10371 − 1) = **−9.85 % a year**. The other surveys come out as: BITS Q1 −7.31 %, BITS Q4 −11.47 %, BTS −13.93 %, DYFS −7.96 %, NS-IBTS Q1 +0.10 %, NS-IBTS Q3 −6.71 % and SNS **−28.36 %** a year. The coefficient looks small, but compounding is brutal: at −0.10371 a year the CPUE halves every **6.7 years** and falls tenfold in **22.2 years**. Over 33 years (1991 to 2023) only 3.3 % of the starting CPUE would remain; over the 39 years of the longest surveys, 1.8 %. SNS's −0.3335 means a drop to 3.6 % in just 10 years.

**Step 2: can the tool recover such a slope?** We cannot use the paper's data, which is only in figures. Instead we **simulated** 33 years (1991 to 2023) of 40 hauls a year, each with a random effort of 0.4 to 0.8 hours, a spread of 1.0 in the logarithm of CPUE and a true slope of −0.10371 (the number of hauls, the spread and the starting 8 eels per hour are **our assumptions**). The tool's log-linear model gives a standardised index that falls from 8.9 to 0.37 eels an hour and a trend of **−9.51 % a year** (95 % interval −10.11 to −8.90), within about a third of a percentage point of the true −9.85 %. The tool uses a lognormal model, not the paper's negative binomial, so this tests the conversion and the trend fit, not the paper's statistics.

**Step 3: how long a series is needed?** Using only the last years of the same simulated series, the estimate is wildly uncertain with a short run: 5 years give −4.6 % with an interval from −21.3 to +15.8 %, which includes no change at all. Eight years give −11.2 % (−17.7 to −4.3); twelve years −9.5 % (−12.4 to −6.5); twenty years −8.8 % (−10.1 to −7.5). This is why a short window can look flat while the long series clearly falls.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/european-eel-bottom-trawl-cpue-log-linear-slope-annual-decline.mjs` calls `cpueIndex` from `src/lib/sci/cpue.ts`, the maths of the [[cpue-trend]] calculator: a lognormal model of CPUE with a free effect for each year, an index with 95 % intervals, and a straight line through the logged index for the yearly trend. The slopes and years are from the paper. The log link, the 40 hauls a year, the effort range, the spread of 1.0 and the starting CPUE are **our assumptions**, and the data are **simulated**.

## How we test the maths

On every change, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/cpue-trend.mjs`, which compares the index with statsmodels on made-up data. New with this article:

- A noise-free series falling at −0.10371 a year must give a trend of −9.8513 % a year with no interval width.
- The same for the steepest slope (−0.3335, giving −28.3588 %) and the flat one (+0.000995, giving +0.0995 %).
- The halving time of 6.68 years and the tenfold time of 22.2 years for the pooled slope.

## Check it yourself

On the [[cpue-trend]] page:

1. Clear the box *One row per trip, set or haul* and paste ten rows, two for each year: **2019, 8, 1**; **2019, 8, 1**; **2020, 7.21, 1**; **2020, 7.21, 1**; **2021, 6.5, 1**; **2021, 6.5, 1**; **2022, 5.86, 1**; **2022, 5.86, 1**; **2023, 5.28, 1**; **2023, 5.28, 1** (year, catch, effort in hours).
2. Leave *Zero catches* on **Leave them out of the model** and tick no factors.
3. The yearly trend should read about **−9.86 % a year**, because we rounded the catches to two decimals (the exact curve gives −9.85).
4. Change the last year's catch to **8** and see the trend soften to about −2 % with an interval that spans zero: a single rebound year in a short series moves the answer a lot.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Converting a published slope** into a percentage, a half-life or a time to a tenfold fall, for a briefing or a table.
- **Fitting survey indices** of your own species from haul-level catch and effort, with year, area or vessel as factors.
- **Planning a monitoring series**: simulate the decline you want to detect and see how many years you need.
- **Sanity-checking** a "no trend" result by looking at the width of the interval, as in Step 3.
- **Teaching** compounding: why −0.10 a year is not a 10 % loss over ten years, and why logarithms turn declines into straight lines.

## The fine print

- We assumed a log link; if the paper's coefficients were on another scale, the percentages would change.
- Our simulation uses a lognormal model with no zero catches, unlike the paper's zero-inflated negative binomial; the fit shows the conversion works, not that the paper's statistics are right.
- The paper's survey years come from its figure captions and are ambiguous in a few places (for instance the end year of one survey).
- Fewer eels caught per hour in a survey indicates a declining local abundance, but surveys do not cover the whole stock or the ocean life stages.
