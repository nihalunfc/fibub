Lung-protective ventilation is prescribed in millilitres per kilogram, but not of the patient's real weight. It uses **predicted body weight**, a number worked out from height and sex alone, because lungs grow with the skeleton and not with body fat. That makes one measurement, height, the hinge of the whole prescription. A 2026 cohort study in the *Journal of Intensive Care* followed 798 adults with ARDS over 11 years and found that women received 7.6 mL per kg of predicted weight while men received 6.8, and that each extra centimetre of recorded height came with 0.034 mL/kg less. This article rebuilds that arithmetic with the [[body-measures]] calculator, whose ideal-weight functions are the same Devine formula intensive care uses, and shows that both of the paper's coefficients fall out of the formula itself. Related tools: the [[energy-needs]] calculator uses the same height-and-sex reasoning for calories, and the [[lab-unit-converter]] for the laboratory side of a chart.

This is general education about how the formulas behave, not advice about anyone's treatment.

## The paper in brief

The authors pulled 11 years of ventilator records from one German intensive care unit: 798 adults coded for ARDS, 263 of them women, with 58,802 hourly observations in the tidal-volume model. Every patient's **predicted body weight** (PBW) was the Devine formula: 50 kg for a man and 45.5 kg for a woman of 5 feet, plus 2.3 kg for every inch above that.

Three findings matter here. First, the **median tidal volume was 7.6 mL/kg PBW in women and 6.8 in men**, and women were within the 8 mL/kg ceiling for 55.3 % of their ventilated hours against 76.1 % for men. Second, in the adjusted models, absolute tidal volume rose by **3.98 mL per cm** of recorded height and was **30.1 mL lower** for women. Third, the volume *per kilogram* fell by **0.0342 mL/kg for every extra cm**. The authors also report that height was self-reported for 48.4 % of patients and set by a physician, without necessarily measuring, for the rest.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the Devine formula as the tool implements it, plus the four coefficients the paper reports. No patient data is involved and nothing is simulated.

**Step 1: what the formula does.** Asking the tool for ideal body weight at a run of heights gives a straight line with two properties that drive the whole paper. Its slope is 2.3 kg per inch, which is **0.9055 kg per cm**. And the two sexes are **4.5 kg apart at every height**: at 174.1 cm (the paper's reference height) a man's PBW is 69.65 kg and a woman's 65.15 kg. That gap is worth **4.97 cm** of height, so a woman needs to be 5 cm taller than a man to be prescribed the same breath.

**Step 2: the sex coefficient is the formula's own 4.5 kg.** The cohort is 33 % women, so its average prescription is about 7.06 mL/kg (**our weighting** of the two medians) and its average PBW about 68.2 kg, giving a typical tidal volume near 482 mL. If clinicians scaled perfectly, a woman at the same height would get 4.5 kg × 7.06 mL/kg = **31.8 mL less**. The paper's adjusted model found **30.1 mL less** (95 % CI −51.9 to −8.4). The entire measured sex effect on absolute volume is the Devine offset, nothing else.

**Step 3: the height coefficient shows the scaling is only two thirds done.** For the mL/kg figure to stay constant, absolute volume would have to rise by 7.06 mL/kg × 0.9055 kg/cm = **6.40 mL per cm**. The paper measured only **3.98 mL per cm**. Put the two together with the quotient rule: the change in volume per kilogram per cm of height is 3.98 ÷ 68.2 − 482 × 0.9055 ÷ 68.2², which is **−0.0354 mL/kg per cm**. The paper's own fitted value is **−0.0342** (95 % CI −0.0498 to −0.0185). Two independent routes to the same number, and ours used nothing but the formula.

**Step 4: who crosses the ceiling.** Take the paper's fitted rule for absolute volume and divide it by the tool's PBW at each height. The mL/kg figure falls from **8.2 at 155 cm** to 7.6 at 160, 7.2 at 174 and **6.8 at 185 cm** (Figure 2). The 8 mL/kg line is crossed below about 156 cm. In this cohort 73 of the 263 women were under 165 cm against 14 of the 535 men, which is why a protocol that drifts with height lands on women. The paper's two medians, 7.6 and 6.8, are what this curve gives at a typical woman's and a typical man's height, without any difference in intent.

**Step 5: how much a wrong height costs.** Height in these records was often self-reported or eyeballed. A **5 cm** error moves PBW by **4.53 kg**, or 6.6 % of a typical PBW, which at 7 mL/kg is **32 mL** of every breath, in the wrong direction and for days. The tool also shows that the choice of formula matters less than the height: at 174.1 cm a woman's predicted weight is 65.15 kg by Devine, 63.52 by Robinson and 64.72 by Miller, a spread of 1.6 kg, about a third of what a 2 cm height error does.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/recorded-height-sex-predicted-body-weight-tidal-volume.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `ibw` and `bodyMeasures` from `src/lib/sci/body.ts`, the maths behind the [[body-measures]] calculator, for the Devine, Robinson and Miller weights at every height from 145 to 205 cm.

From the paper come the four coefficients (3.98 mL/cm, −30.1 mL, −0.0342 mL/kg/cm), the two medians (7.6 and 6.8 mL/kg PBW), the reference height of 174.1 cm, the share of women (33.0 %) and the height strata. **Our own choices**: weighting the two medians by the cohort's sex split to get an average prescription of 7.06 mL/kg, and anchoring the absolute-volume curve in Figure 2 on the resulting 482 mL, because the paper does not publish a mean tidal volume in millilitres. The 6 and 8 mL/kg lines are the usual protocol targets, not the paper's own thresholds.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/body-measures.mjs`, which pins the body measures against hand-worked values:

- A 6 ft (182.88 cm) man must have an ideal body weight of 77.6 kg by Devine, 74.8 by Robinson and 73.12 by Miller.
- A 5 ft woman must come out at exactly 45.5 kg, the Devine constant.
- Mosteller's body surface area for 180 cm and 80 kg must be exactly 2.00 m², and the WHO body mass index classes must switch at 18.5, 25, 30, 35 and 40.
- New with this article: the Devine formula must give 0.90551 kg per cm, a 4.50 kg difference between the sexes at every height, 69.65 kg for a man and 65.15 kg for a woman at 174.1 cm, and the mL/kg-per-cm slope rebuilt from the paper's coefficients must be −0.0354, inside the paper's confidence interval.

## Check it yourself

On the [[body-measures]] page, with the units on **Metric (cm, kg)**:

1. Set *Sex* to **Male**, *Height (cm)* to **174.1** and *Weight (kg)* to anything, say **80**. In the *Body weights* table, *Ideal body weight, Devine* reads **69.7 kg** (and Robinson 68.2, Miller 68.3).
2. Change *Sex* to **Female** and read it again: **65.2 kg**. That 4.5 kg is the whole of the paper's sex effect.
3. Still on **Female**, change *Height (cm)* to **179.1**: the Devine weight rises to **69.7 kg**, the same as the man at 174.1 cm. Five centimetres of height is worth a sex change in this formula.
4. Now put *Height (cm)* back to **174.1** and try **169.1**: the Devine weight falls to **60.6 kg**. A 5 cm mistake in a recorded height is 4.5 kg of predicted weight, or about 32 mL on every breath at 7 mL/kg.
5. At 155 cm, *Female*, the Devine weight is **47.9 kg**; at **150 cm** it is 43.3 kg and the table adds a note that these formulas were built for adults 152.4 cm (5 ft) and taller, so below that they are extrapolations.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Re-express any ventilation study in the same currency**: convert mL/kg to millilitres, or back, at the heights and sexes a cohort actually had.
- **Size the error budget of a protocol**: see how much of a reported difference between groups is just the Devine offset before looking for clinical causes.
- **Plan a quality audit**: work out the height below which your unit's default volumes break the 8 mL/kg ceiling, and audit those patients first.
- **Check drug dosing on the same page**: adjusted and lean body weight, which many drug doses use, come from the same height and sex, and the [[kidney-function]] eGFR tool takes it from there.
- **Teach the difference between actual, ideal, adjusted and lean weight** with one patient's numbers side by side, which is where most prescribing mistakes start.

## The fine print

- One centre, one diagnosis code, 11 years: the ventilation habits are local, and the coefficients should not be assumed elsewhere.
- Our average prescription of 7.06 mL/kg and the 482 mL anchor are reconstructions from the paper's medians and sex split, so Figure 2's height is set by us; its **slope** comes from the paper and the formula.
- The paper's coefficients are adjusted for other factors we cannot reproduce, so an exact match with our two-term calculation would be luck. Both of ours land inside its confidence intervals.
- The Devine formula is a 1974 drug-dosing rule, not a measurement of lung size. Other predicted-weight formulas give slightly different weights, as step 5 shows.
- Below about 152 cm, every ideal-weight formula here is an extrapolation, and the tool says so.
