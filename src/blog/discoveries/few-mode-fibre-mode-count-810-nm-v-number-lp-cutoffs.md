When you send light into a few-mode fibre, the fibre decides how many shapes that light can travel in. Get the number wrong and a mode-shaping experiment is trying to control patterns the fibre cannot carry, or ignoring ones it can. A 2025 paper in *Photonics* builds cylindrical vector beams at the far end of three short few-mode fibres, and describes them as supporting 6, 12 and 16 modes. Those three numbers are fixed by three things each: the numerical aperture, the core diameter and the wavelength, so anyone can check them. This article recomputes all three with the [[fibre-optic-calculator]], and finds two exact matches and one fibre balanced on a knife edge. For the beam that goes in and the polarisation state that comes out, the [[gaussian-beam]] calculator and the [[polarisation-calculator]] take the next steps.

## The paper in brief

The authors want a clean **cylindrical vector beam**, a beam whose polarisation points radially or azimuthally all the way round, at the output of an ordinary few-mode fibre. A fibre mixes the modes as the light travels, so the pattern coming out is not the one you put in. Their method measures the fibre's transmission as a matrix, takes its **singular value decomposition** (a standard way of splitting a matrix into the input patterns that survive best and what they turn into), and uses that to design a hologram on a spatial light modulator. The hologram pre-distorts the input so that the output is the vector beam they asked for.

Three fibres were used, each one metre long and coiled to a 10 cm diameter, all at 810 nm:

- fibre I: numerical aperture 0.14, core diameter 6 µm;
- fibre II: numerical aperture 0.13, core diameter 9 µm;
- fibre III: numerical aperture 0.12, core diameter 12 µm.

The paper says these carry 6, 12 and 16 modes. That count matters directly to the method: it sets how many rows and columns the transmission matrix really needs, and therefore how much of the measured matrix is signal rather than noise.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the three numerical apertures, the three core diameters and the one wavelength printed in the paper. Nothing is fitted and no measurement of ours is involved.

**Step 1: the V-number.** A step-index fibre's whole mode structure hangs on one dimensionless number,

V = 2πa·NA / λ,

with *a* the core radius, NA the numerical aperture and λ the wavelength in the same length units. At 810 nm the three fibres give **V = 3.258, 4.538 and 5.585**. Their single-mode cut-off wavelengths, the wavelengths above which only one mode survives, are **1097 nm, 1529 nm and 1881 nm**: all three fibres are genuinely multimode at 810 nm, which is why the experiment needs a hologram in the first place.

**Step 2: which mode groups are guided.** In the weak-guidance picture the modes come in **LP groups**, and each group switches on at its own value of V, given by a zero of a Bessel function: LP11 at 2.4048, LP21 and LP02 together at 3.8317, LP31 at 5.1356, LP12 at 5.5201. Each group is counted twice for its two polarisations, and groups with l ≥ 1 twice more for their two orientations (sine and cosine), so LP01 is worth 2 modes and LP11 is worth 4.

- Fibre I (V = 3.258) guides LP01 and LP11: 2 + 4 = **6 modes**. The paper says 6.
- Fibre II (V = 4.538) guides LP01, LP11, LP02 and LP21: 2 + 4 + 2 + 4 = **12 modes**. The paper says 12.
- Fibre III (V = 5.585) guides those four plus LP31 and LP12: **20 modes**. The paper says 16.

**Step 3: why the third fibre disagrees, and why it is interesting.** LP12 starts at V = 5.5201, and fibre III's V is 5.5851. That is **1.2 % above the line**. Stated the other way: LP12 stops being guided above **819.5 nm**, just **9.5 nm** beyond the wavelength used. The same count of 16 comes back if the numerical aperture is 0.1186 instead of 0.120, or if the core is 11.86 µm instead of 12 µm, both well inside ordinary manufacturing tolerance. The paper's 16 is also exactly what the textbook rule of thumb V²/2 gives for this fibre (15.6). So 16 and 20 are not a contradiction so much as a warning: **near a cut-off, a mode count is only as firm as the third digit of the numerical aperture.**

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/few-mode-fibre-mode-count-810-nm-v-number-lp-cutoffs.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls two functions from `src/lib/sci/phfibre.ts`, the maths behind the [[fibre-optic-calculator]]:

- `basics`, which returns the numerical aperture, the relative index difference, the acceptance angle, the V-number and the single-mode cut-off wavelength;
- `lpModes`, which lists every guided LP group with its cut-off V and its degeneracy, using `besselZero` to find the Bessel zeros at run time rather than from a stored table.

The numerical apertures (0.14, 0.13, 0.12), core diameters (6, 9, 12 µm) and the 810 nm wavelength come from the paper. The core index of 1.4533, fused silica near 810 nm, is **our assumption**: the tool asks for n₁ and n₂ rather than the numerical aperture, so we set n₂ = √(n₁² − NA²) to reproduce each stated NA exactly. V, the LP cut-offs and the mode counts depend only on NA, the core radius and the wavelength, so that choice does not move any of the numbers in the table; it only affects the effective indices quoted in step 4.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/fibre-optic-calculator.mjs` on every change to fibub, and it pins this maths against SciPy:

- the Bessel zeros must match `scipy.special.jn_zeros` to 1e-8: J0 at 2.40482556 and 5.52007811, J1 at 7.01558667;
- the LP mode counts must match an independent SciPy count at V = 1.95, 2.5, 3.9, 5 and 12 (1, 2, 4, 4 and 21 groups; 2, 6, 12, 12 and 76 modes), and V = 35 must give 160 groups and 618 modes against the V²/2 estimate of 612;
- the LP01 propagation constant must match `scipy.optimize.brentq` on the eigenvalue equation to 1e-9 at V = 1, 1.951, 2.4, 3 and 6.
- New with this article: the paper's three fibres, entered as numerical aperture and core diameter at 810 nm, must give V = 3.2579, 4.5379 and 5.5851 and 6, 12 and 20 modes, and fibre III's LP12 cut-off wavelength must come out at 819.5 nm.

4. Scroll to *Guided modes*. The table lists **LP01, LP11, LP02, LP21, LP31, LP12** with cut-off V values 0, 2.405, 3.832, 3.832, 5.136 and 5.520, and the note underneath reads **6 LP mode groups, 20 modes counting polarisations and orientations; V²/2 estimates 15.6**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sizing a transmission matrix.** Before measuring a fibre's transmission matrix, the mode count tells you how many independent input patterns are worth sending. The [[fibre-optic-calculator]] gives that from a data sheet in seconds.
- **Spotting a cut-off you are sitting on.** Sweeping the wavelength in the tool shows at once whether your working wavelength is within a per cent of a mode group switching on, as fibre III here is. That is the difference between a stable experiment and one that changes when the room warms up.
- **Choosing a fibre for a fixed mode count.** Mode-division multiplexing and quantum photonics often want exactly 2, 4 or 6 LP groups. Entering candidate numerical apertures and core sizes finds the combinations that land safely in the middle of a plateau in Figure 2.
- **Planning what happens after the fibre.** The beam leaving a single-mode fibre is near-Gaussian, so the mode-field diameter from this tool is the input waist for the [[gaussian-beam]] calculator when you design the collimating and imaging optics.
- **Following the polarisation.** A cylindrical vector beam is defined by its polarisation pattern; the [[polarisation-calculator]] builds the waveplates, polarisers and rotators that make and analyse one, and reports the Stokes vector at every stage.

## The fine print

The LP mode picture assumes weak guidance (a small index step) and a perfect step-index cylinder. All three fibres here have index differences under half a per cent, so weak guidance is safe, but real fibres have rounded profiles, stress birefringence and bends. A one-metre fibre coiled to 10 cm, as in the paper, will strip a mode that is only just guided, so the *useful* mode count can be lower than the ideal count even when the theory says the mode exists. Our mode counts are therefore an upper bound from the stated numbers, not a measurement. The paper's own 16 for fibre III may well come from a mode solver with the manufacturer's measured profile, or from the V²/2 estimate; either way, the useful conclusion is the same, and it is the one the tool makes visible: that fibre is sitting on the LP12 edge.
