**Hydraulic conductivity** (K) says how easily water moves through soil or rock: it is the speed of flow under a slope of one metre per metre. Measuring it needs a lab test or a pumping test, so engineers often guess it from a sieve analysis instead, using a formula built on grain size. A 2024 study in *Water* tested nine such formulas against new lab measurements on six soils from fine sand to gravel. This article shows how the [[darcy-flow-calculator]] reads d10 from a sieve curve, applies Hazen's rule and carries the K straight into a flow and travel-time estimate; the [[grain-size-stats]] tool adds sorting and skewness when you need to describe the sample too.

## The paper in brief

The team measured K for six soils in a lab rig, pumping from a well and reading the drawdown with Thiem's equation (the steady-flow formula for a well). Their values ran from **4.02 mm/s** for soil A to **8.78 mm/s** for soil F: very permeable, coarse material.

They then estimated K from each soil's grain-size curve with nine formulas: Hazen, a modified Hazen, Kozeny-Carman, Breyer, Slichter, Terzaghi, USBR, Sauerbrey and Alyamani and Şen. Most share one shape: K = (g/ν) × (a coefficient that may depend on porosity n or uniformity) × d10², where **d10** is the size that 10 % of the sample (by weight) passes, g is gravity and ν the water's kinematic viscosity. The formulas differ in the coefficient.

Their conclusion: the **modified Hazen** and **Breyer** formulas came closest to the measurements; **USBR** did worst, and Slichter and Terzaghi usually guessed too low. On a Taylor diagram (a chart that scores correlation and spread together), Sauerbrey tracked the lab values best (correlation 0.83) and plain Hazen least well (0.74).

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** or an **illustration with our own numbers**. The paper's per-soil d10 and porosity values were not in what we could read, so we did not try to redo its per-soil table.

**Step 1: what d10 do the lab values imply?** Hazen's rule, as the Darcy flow calculator uses it, is K (cm/s) = C × d10² with d10 in cm. Turned round, d10 = √(K / C). With the middle textbook coefficient C = 100, the paper's lab values need **d10 = 0.63 mm** (soil A) to **0.94 mm** (soil F): coarse sand. With C = 40 (the value for fine, silty sand) they would need 1.0 to 1.5 mm; with C = 150, 0.52 to 0.77 mm. Figure 1 shows why: K grows with the **square** of d10, so a choice of C between 40 and 150 moves the answer almost fourfold.

**Step 2: one sand, five formulas.** We made up a coarse sand sieve curve (sizes 9.5 to 0.15 mm, 1 % passing the finest sieve) and gave it to the tool's grain-size maths. It reads **d10 = 0.357 mm**, d60 = 1.61 mm, so the uniformity Cu = d60/d10 = **4.5**, and Hazen with C = 100 gives **K = 1.27 mm/s (110 m/day)**. In our script we then used the coefficient forms the paper lists, with porosity 0.35 (our assumption) and water at 20 °C (Figure 2):

| Formula | K (mm/s), our sand | K (m/day) |
|---|---|---|
| Hazen, C = 100 (in the tool) | 1.27 | 110 |
| Modified Hazen | 1.42 | 122 |
| Breyer | 1.53 | 132 |
| Kozeny-Carman | 1.05 | 90 |
| Slichter | 0.40 | 34 |

The four that share a similar coefficient land within about 50 % of each other; Slichter is about three times lower, in line with the paper finding that it tends to underestimate.

**Step 3: into a flow problem.** With the Hazen K of 110 m/day, a water-table slope of 0.5 m over 100 m and effective porosity 0.3 (all ours), Darcy's law gives a seepage velocity of **1.83 m/day**, so water takes about **55 days** to travel 100 m. Swap in the Slichter value and the same trip takes nearly six months. For a contaminant plume or a well's capture zone, the choice of formula is not a detail.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/hazen-grain-size-hydraulic-conductivity-lab-comparison.mjs` draws both charts and writes them into this page. It uses `gradation`, `hazen`, `darcy`, `waterViscosity`, `waterDensity` and `G` from `src/lib/sci/darcy.ts`, the maths behind the Darcy flow calculator. Hazen is exactly what the tool computes. The modified Hazen, Breyer, Kozeny-Carman and Slichter values are worked out **in the script only**, from the coefficient forms the paper gives; the tool does not offer them yet. From the paper: the six lab K values and the coefficient forms. Ours: the sieve curve, porosity, temperature and flow example.

## How we test the maths

`node tests/sci.mjs` runs on every change. For this tool it checks, among others:

- `tests/sci-tools/darcy-flow-calculator.mjs` (added with this article): with 6 % passing 0.3 mm and 22 % passing 0.6 mm, d10 must be **0.3 × 2^0.25 = 0.356762 mm** (interpolated on a log scale, worked by hand); Hazen with C = 100 must give **0.127279 cm/s (110.0 m/day)**; d10 = 0.634 mm must give back **4.02 mm/s**; and that K with gradient 0.005 and porosity 0.3 must move water 100 m in **54.6 days**.
- `tests/sci.mjs` (Darcy block): Hazen with d10 = 0.2 mm and C = 100 must give **0.04 cm/s**; a curve with 10 % at 0.25 mm must give d10 = 0.25 mm, Cu = 4 and Cc = 1; water viscosity at 20 °C must be **1.002 mPa·s**.

## Check it yourself

On the [[darcy-flow-calculator]]:

1. In the **Grain-size curve and Hazen K** card, paste into **Sieve size (mm), then percent passing**: `9.5 100`, `4.75 92`, `2.36 75`, `1.18 48`, `0.6 22`, `0.3 6`, `0.15 1` (one sieve per line).
2. Leave **Hazen coefficient C (K in cm/s, d10 in cm)** at `100`. The headline should read **d10 = 0.357 mm, Hazen K ≈ 110 m/day**, with Cu = 4.5.
3. In the **Darcy flow** card, set **K comes from** to *the Hazen estimate below*.
4. Type **Head drop Δh (m)** `0.5`, **Over a distance (m)** `100`, **Effective porosity ne** `0.3` and **Travel distance (m)** `100`.
5. The headline should read a **seepage velocity of 1.83 m/day**, with 100 m taking about **55 days**. Change C to `40` and see the time grow two and a half times.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-checking a reported K**: turn a lab or field K round into the d10 Hazen would need, and compare with the sieve curve (the [[darcy-flow-calculator]]).
- **Showing the spread**: run Hazen at C = 40, 100 and 150 and report the range, not one number.
- **Describing the sample**: the [[grain-size-stats]] tool gives mean size, sorting and skewness, which tell you whether a d10-only formula is even appropriate (Hazen is meant for fairly uniform sand).
- **Planning tracer tests and well spacing**: carry the estimated K into seepage velocity and travel time in the same tool.
- **Teaching**: the square in d10² is the reason a small sieving error becomes a large K error.

## A tool we could build

A grain-size K comparer that takes one sieve curve and porosity and lists Hazen, modified Hazen, Breyer, Kozeny-Carman, Slichter, Terzaghi, USBR, Sauerbrey and Alyamani and Şen side by side, with each formula's stated range of validity, would let anyone repeat the paper's comparison on their own samples.
