Manganese(III) is an awkward ion. It has four d electrons, which in an octahedral field can sit either as four unpaired spins or as two, and the arrangement it chooses then forces the octahedron itself to distort. A 2025 paper in *Next Materials* reports a Mn(III) complex built with cyclam and long-chain ester ligands that behaves as an **ionic liquid crystal**: it melts into an ordered mesophase rather than a plain liquid. From infrared and UV-visible spectra the authors assign a **distorted octahedral** geometry, and they measure a magnetic moment of **4.6 Bohr magnetons**. This article feeds those numbers into the [[crystal-field-calculator]] and asks whether the picture holds together. For what comes next, [[tanabe-sugano]] handles the full term diagram and the [[dsc-tga-analyser]] the thermal transitions the same paper reports.

## The paper in brief

The authors made a manganese-containing ionic liquid, characterised it by infrared and UV-visible spectroscopy, measured its magnetic susceptibility, and followed its thermal behaviour. Thermogravimetry puts the start of decomposition at **242 °C**, which is high for an ionic liquid crystal; differential scanning calorimetry finds two transitions near **95 °C and 115 °C**, and polarised optical microscopy shows a textured, ordered mesophase between them. The UV-visible spectrum has bands at **434 nm and 1381 nm**. The magnetic moment is **4.6 B.M.**, and the geometry is called distorted octahedral.

Two honest caveats before anything else. First, the publisher's full text is not reachable from here, so everything above comes from the paper's abstract; we have no access to its tables or figures. Second, the abstract does not say which band it treats as the d–d transition, nor does it give a pairing energy. Those are exactly the gaps a crystal field calculation can fill in.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the three numbers in the abstract** (the d-electron count, the band positions and the measured moment), plus one textbook input of our own: the pairing energy.

**Step 1: the d count.** The tool takes the metal and the oxidation state and returns group number minus charge: manganese is in group 7, so Mn(III) is **d⁴**. Nothing else in the calculation works without that.

**Step 2: the splitting each band would imply.** For a d–d band, the photon energy is the energy gap, and the tool converts a wavelength straight into wavenumbers:

- 1381 nm → **Δo = 7,241 cm⁻¹** (86.6 kJ mol⁻¹)
- 434 nm → **Δo = 23,041 cm⁻¹** (275.6 kJ mol⁻¹)

A high-spin Mn(III) octahedron has one spin-allowed transition, ⁵E_g → ⁵T₂g, whose energy is Δo itself. A splitting of 7,241 cm⁻¹ is on the weak side but entirely normal for an O/N-donor Mn(III) complex; 23,041 cm⁻¹ would be extraordinary, up with cyanides.

**Step 3: which spin state wins.** Given Δo and a pairing energy P, the tool compares every way of putting four electrons in the five d orbitals. With **P = 28,000 cm⁻¹** (the textbook figure for Mn(III); **our input**, not the paper's) and Δo = 7,241 cm⁻¹ it returns:

| Arrangement | Unpaired | Energy | Spin-only moment | Jahn–Teller |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t2g³ eg¹ | 4 | −4,345 cm⁻¹ | 4.90 B.M. | **strong** |
| t2g⁴ eg⁰ | 2 | +16,414 cm⁻¹ | 2.83 B.M. | weak |
| t2g⁴ eg⁰ (paired) | 0 | +44,414 cm⁻¹ | 0 | weak |

High spin wins by more than 20,000 cm⁻¹, and it is not close: the crossover sits at Δo = P, so low spin would need a splitting of 28,000 cm⁻¹, or 23,000 cm⁻¹ at the bottom of the pairing-energy range. Figure 1 shows the two lines crossing.

**Step 4: the measured moment agrees.** Four unpaired electrons give a spin-only moment of √(4 × 6) = **4.90 B.M.** The paper measures **4.6**, which is 93.9 % of spin-only, the usual small shortfall for a high-spin d⁴ ion. Two unpaired electrons would give 2.83 B.M., which is nowhere near. So the moment and the 1381 nm band tell the same story, and the moment on its own rules out low spin.

**Step 5: the two bands are not interchangeable.** Figure 2 makes the point that matters for anyone reading a spectrum like this one. If you took the 434 nm band as Δo, the tool would put you at 23,041 cm⁻¹, essentially on top of the crossover for the low end of the pairing-energy range (Δo = 23,000 cm⁻¹ corresponds to a band at 435 nm), and it would predict a low-spin ion with 2.83 B.M. The measured 4.6 B.M. says that cannot be right. The 434 nm band is therefore not the d–d transition: in a Mn(III) complex of this kind it is a charge-transfer or ligand-based band, and it is the one that gives the material its colour, since a 434 nm absorption looks yellow to the eye while the 1381 nm band is in the near infrared and invisible.

**Step 6: the distortion is predicted, not assumed.** The high-spin t2g³eg¹ ground state has a single electron in the two-fold degenerate e_g set, and the tool flags that as a **strong** Jahn–Teller case: the octahedron must stretch or squash along one axis. The paper's independent call of "distorted octahedral" from its spectra is exactly what crystal field theory demands for high-spin d⁴. The crystal field stabilisation energy is −0.6 Δo = **−4,345 cm⁻¹ (−52.0 kJ mol⁻¹)** before the distortion is counted.

One limit worth stating plainly: the moment alone cannot prove the geometry. If the same ion sat in a tetrahedral field, Δt would be about 4/9 of Δo (3,218 cm⁻¹ here) and the ground state e²t₂² would also have four unpaired electrons and the same 4.90 B.M. It is the band energy, the infrared evidence and the Jahn–Teller distortion that pick the octahedron, not the magnetism.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/manganese-iii-ionic-liquid-distorted-octahedral-high-spin.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `dCount`, `deltaFromNm`, `spinStates`, `spinOnly`, `configLabel`, `jahnTeller`, `CM_TO_KJ` and `seenColour` from `src/lib/sci/crystalfield.ts`, the maths behind the [[crystal-field-calculator]].

Inputs from the paper's abstract: Mn(III), the bands at 434 and 1381 nm, the measured 4.6 B.M. **Our inputs:** the pairing energy, 28,000 cm⁻¹ with 23,000 cm⁻¹ as a lower bound, from standard tables for 3d ions; and the assumption that a d–d band's energy equals Δo for high-spin d⁴, which holds for the single spin-allowed transition but ignores the splitting the Jahn–Teller distortion itself causes.

## How we test the maths

The crystal field maths is pinned in `tests/sci.mjs` against textbook results, on every change to fibub:

- The octahedral high-spin and low-spin crystal field stabilisation energies for d⁰ to d¹⁰ must reproduce the standard tables (0, −0.4, −0.8, −1.2, −0.6, 0, … Δo for high spin).
- Fe(H₂O)₆²⁺ with Δo 10,400 cm⁻¹ and P 17,600 cm⁻¹ must come out high spin, t2g⁴eg², and Fe(CN)₆⁴⁻ at Δo 33,000 cm⁻¹ low spin and diamagnetic.
- Δo for Ti(H₂O)₆³⁺ from its 493 nm band must be 20,284 cm⁻¹, or 242.6 kJ mol⁻¹.
- The Jahn–Teller test must call Cu²⁺ strong, Cr³⁺ none and high-spin Fe²⁺ weak.

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/crystal-field-calculator.mjs` pins this paper's case: Mn(III) is d⁴; the 1381 nm band gives 7,241 cm⁻¹; the ground state is t2g³eg¹ with four unpaired electrons, a CFSE of −4,345 cm⁻¹ and a strong Jahn–Teller flag; the low-spin state is 16,414 cm⁻¹ above it; and the high-to-low-spin crossover for P = 23,000 cm⁻¹ falls at a band of 435 nm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[crystal-field-calculator]] page, in the *Metal ion and field* card:

1. Set *Metal* to **Mn** and *Oxidation state* to **3**. The *d electrons* box fills in with **4**.
2. Leave *Geometry* on **Octahedral** and *Units* on **cm⁻¹**.
3. Type **1381** into *Or: absorption band (nm, for Δ)*. *Splitting Δo* becomes about **7241**, and the *From the band* line reads 7241 cm⁻¹ = 86.62 kJ/mol, with the honest warning that for a d count other than 1 or 9 the band energy is only an estimate of Δ.
4. Set *Pairing energy P* to **28000**.
5. The headline reads **High spin d⁴: t2g³ eg¹, 4 unpaired electrons, μ = 4.90 BM**, the *CFSE* line reads **−0.6Δo = −4345 cm⁻¹**, and *Jahn–Teller* says **strong distortion expected**. Switch *Units* to **kJ/mol** and the CFSE becomes −52.0.
6. Now put **434** in the band box instead. Δo jumps to about **23041** and, with the pairing energy lowered to **23000**, the answer flips to low spin with 2 unpaired electrons and 2.83 μB: the prediction the paper's measured 4.6 B.M. rejects.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a geometry call** in a synthesis paper: a measured moment plus a d–d band is enough to say whether high spin, low spin and the stated geometry agree, with the [[crystal-field-calculator]].
- **Decide which band is the d–d band** when a spectrum has several, by asking which assignment predicts the moment you measured.
- **Predict a Jahn–Teller distortion** before you refine a structure, so an unexpectedly long pair of bonds is not a surprise.
- **Convert band positions into energies** in the units a paper wants, cm⁻¹ or kJ mol⁻¹, and relate them to concentrations and extinction coefficients with the [[beer-lambert-calculator]].
- **Go further than the one-electron picture** with [[tanabe-sugano]], which handles the multiplet structure and the Racah parameters that a d⁴ ion's weaker bands need.

## The fine print

- Only the abstract of this paper was available to us. We have not seen its spectra, its susceptibility data or its structure, and we take its numbers as printed.
- The pairing energy is ours, from tables, not a measured value for this compound. The conclusion is unaffected: high spin wins for any pairing energy above about 7,250 cm⁻¹.
- Crystal field theory treats the ligands as point charges. The real splitting pattern of a Jahn–Teller distorted d⁴ ion has two or three overlapping transitions, so 7,241 cm⁻¹ is the energy of the main band, not a perfectly defined Δo.
- The spin-only formula ignores orbital contributions and any magnetic coupling between neighbours, which is the usual reason a measured moment falls a few per cent short.
