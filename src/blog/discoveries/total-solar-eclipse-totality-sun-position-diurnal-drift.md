To measure the Sun's shape you need its edge, and the only time you can see the Sun's edge sharply is during a total eclipse, when the Moon's mountains chop the last sliver into **Baily's beads**. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Astronomy and Space Sciences* describes SunSketcher, which turned thousands of ordinary phones along the path of the 8 April 2024 eclipse into a bead-timing network: 35,323 downloads, 2,145 users who sent data, about 100 GB of images, each stamped with a GPS time and position. The whole method rests on knowing exactly where the Sun was for each phone, which is what the [[sun-position-calculator]] does. Below we reproduce the paper's one-degree solar drift during totality, work out the Sun's height at two of its observers' own coordinates, and turn its "100 m on the Moon" into milliseconds. For the eclipse itself at your place, use the [[eclipse-finder]]; for the clock arithmetic, [[astro-time]].

## The paper in brief

A phone cannot resolve the solar disc, but it can record *when* each bead appears and disappears, and with the Moon's limb profile known from laser altimetry, the bead timings pin the Sun's limb. The oblateness — how much wider the Sun is across the equator than pole to pole — is a few parts in a hundred thousand, so the project needs many observers spread across the path.

The authors report a first, basic timing analysis with a standard error equivalent to about **100 m** on the Moon, and describe the precise analysis to come. They also set out the geometry a user has to live with: totality on the centreline lasted about **4 min 4 s** in the middle of the US path, up to about **265 s** near the Mexican border and about **200 s** in eastern Maine; the orientation of the solar disc's major axis relative to the centreline swung from about −18° in Texas to −6° in Maine; and during totality the Sun itself **drifts up to one degree, two solar diameters**, along its daily path. Their Table 2 publishes two example observers' own GPS fixes and image times, which is all we need.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is **recomputed from the paper's two observer records and its stated totality durations**, with the Sun position calculator's own `sunAltAz` and `sunDay`.

**Step 1: where the Sun was.** At observer 1024's recorded moment, 18:42:33.127 UT at 32.8654° N, 96.9378° W, the Sun stood **64.56° high at azimuth 187.6°** — a hand's breadth past due south. At observer 1033's 19:08:10.652 UT in Indiana it was **52.99° high at azimuth 215.5°**. The Texas observer caught totality only **13 minutes after local solar noon**, just **0.18°** below the Sun's greatest height of the day; the Indiana observer was 83 minutes past noon and nearly 5° down from the day's maximum. **Step 2: the one-degree drift, confirmed.** Take the recorded moment, step back and forward by half a totality, and measure the angle between the two solar positions on the sky (we use `separation` from the [[batch-airmass]] planner's maths, which is the same spherical formula). For the mid-path **244 s** of totality the Sun moves **1.008°**. For the longest **265 s** it moves **1.094°**. The Sun's angular diameter that day was **31.93′ = 0.532°**, so those drifts are **1.89 and 2.06 solar diameters**. The paper's "up to one degree (two solar diameters)" is exactly right, and Figure 2 shows the straight line behind it.

**Step 3: why both sites give the same drift.** The rate comes out at **0.2478° per minute** at both places, to four figures. That is not a coincidence: the Sun's apparent diurnal speed is 15° per hour times the cosine of its declination, and on 8 April 2024 the declination was **+7.60°**, so 15 × cos 7.60° = **14.868° per hour = 0.2478° per minute**. Latitude changes the Sun's height and its azimuth, but not how fast it slides along its own path. Useful to know if you are planning any timed sequence through totality: the drift budget is the same in Texas and in Maine, only the duration differs.

**Step 4: the Moon's shadow between the two phones.** The two observers' own fixes are **11.142° apart on the ground, 1239 km**, and their timestamps are **1537.5 s** apart. That makes the umbra's mean speed along the chord between them **806 m s⁻¹** — about Mach 2.4, and in the right range for the middle of a path where the shadow slows towards the centre of the track. Two rows of a table, and you have measured the speed of the Moon's shadow across a continent.

**Step 5: what a millisecond is worth.** The paper's error is quoted as a distance on the Moon, which is the natural unit for a limb profile but not for a stopwatch. At observer 1024's moment the Moon was **354,054 km** away and moving against the Sun at **0.409″ per second**. One hundred metres on that limb subtends 0.0583″, so **100 m on the Moon is 142 ms of timing** — and, the other way, **100 ms of timing is 70 m**. That is why the paper's timestamps carry milliseconds, and why GPS time rather than the phone's clock is the whole game.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/total-solar-eclipse-totality-sun-position-diurnal-drift.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `sunAltAz` and `sunDay` from `src/lib/astro/sun.ts`, the maths behind the [[sun-position-calculator]], and `separation` from `src/lib/sci/airmass.ts`, the maths behind the [[batch-airmass]] planner. Both sit on astronomy-engine, the same library the [[eclipse-finder]] uses. The Moon's distance and its motion against the Sun in Step 5 come straight from astronomy-engine, because no fibub tool reports them.

From the paper: the two observers' latitudes, longitudes, elevations and image times; the totality durations of 200, 244 and 265 s; and the 100 m limb error. **Our assumptions:** that totality is centred on each recorded image time (the paper's Table 2 calls them central images, so this is close but not exact), that the drift may be measured as the great-circle angle between the two end positions, and that 6371 km is a fair Earth radius for the ground distance. The umbra speed is the mean along the chord between two points, not the instantaneous speed at either.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` pins this maths:

- `sunDay` must land within a minute of the US Naval Observatory's sunrise and sunset for Toronto on the 2026 June solstice (05:36 and 21:03 EDT), give a day length of 15.44 h, and put the noon altitude at 90° − latitude + 23.44°.
- The equation of time must be +16.45 minutes in early November and −14.2 in mid February, within 0.1 minute, and Tromsø must show midnight sun in June and polar night in December.
- `separation`, from the same airmass module, must give 90° between the pole and the equator and exactly 1° for a 1° step along the equator; the altitudes and azimuths behind it match astropy's AltAz within 2 arcseconds.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/sun-position-calculator.mjs`: the paper's two observer records must give altitudes 64.556° and 52.989°, azimuths 187.62° and 215.54°, a drift of 1.0077° over 244 s and 1.0944° over 265 s, a rate matching 15 cos(declination) to four figures, and an umbra speed of 805.8 m s⁻¹ between the two fixes.

On the [[sun-position-calculator]], put yourself in the Texas observer's place:

1. In the place bar at the top, enter the latitude **32.8654** and longitude **−96.9378** (the paper's observer 1024), and set *Times in* to **UTC**.
2. Set *Date* to **2024-04-08** and *Time* to **18:42**.
3. The headline reads about **64.6° above the horizon, towards the south (azimuth 187.3°)**, with *Altitude* **64.57°** in the table below it.
4. Type **2** into *Object height for the shadow (m)*: the shadow is **0.952 m, pointing N (7°)** — the Sun is nearly overhead and nearly due south, which is why eclipse shadows that day were so short.
5. In *The day*, read **Solar noon 18:29, 64.7° high** and **Day length 12h 47m**. Totality came 13 minutes after noon, with the Sun 0.2° below its best.
6. Now change *Time* to **18:44** and watch *Altitude* and *Azimuth* move. Two minutes of clock is about half a degree of sky: half the Sun's width, which is the whole reason totality's four minutes cost a full degree of drift.
7. Repeat with latitude **39.6572**, longitude **−85.9603** and *Time* **19:08** for the Indiana observer: **53.01°** altitude, azimuth **215.5°**, solar noon **17:45**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a citizen-science upload** before trusting it: if the Sun's altitude and azimuth for the submitted GPS fix and timestamp disagree with the image, the metadata is wrong.
- **Budget for drift** in any timed sequence through totality or a transit: 0.248° per minute on 8 April 2024, and 15 cos(declination)° per hour on any other date.
- **Plan a bead-timing station**: the altitude tells you whether a phone on a tripod can even see the Sun over the trees, and the shadow direction tells you where to stand.
- **Turn a published limb error into a timing requirement**, as in Step 5, before choosing a camera or a clock source.
- **Pick the next path**: the paper is already aiming at the eclipses of 12 August 2026, 2 August 2027 and 22 July 2028; the [[eclipse-finder]] gives the local circumstances and the [[batch-airmass]] planner the altitude of other targets the same night.

## The fine print

- This is **geometry recomputed from published coordinates and times**. We have not touched a SunSketcher image, and nothing here measures solar oblateness.
- The paper's Table 2 times are for central images rather than exact mid-totality, so our drift is centred on a moment that may be a second or two out. At 0.248° per minute, two seconds is 0.008°, well under the rounding we quote.
- The 806 m s⁻¹ umbra speed is the straight-line average between two widely separated fixes. The real shadow follows a curve and slows towards the path's middle, so treat it as a sanity check, not a measurement.
- The paper's −18° to −6° orientation of the solar disc's major axis against the centreline depends on the shadow path, which no fibub tool computes, so that part is not checked.
- Rise, set and solar noon from `sunDay` use standard refraction. On an eclipse day they are ordinary almanac numbers: the eclipse does not move them.
