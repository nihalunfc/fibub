A **sky quality meter** is a small box that points up and reports how bright the night sky is, in magnitudes per square arcsecond. Point one at the sky for four years and you have a record of light pollution; but only a sliver of those four years is usable, because the Sun, the Moon and the clouds get in the way first. A 2025 paper in *MNRAS* goes through that arithmetic for 27 meters in the Netherlands and northern Germany, and finds the darkest sites brightening by **2.8 to 6.7 % a year**. This article rebuilds the paper's hour budget with the [[dark-sky-calendar|Dark-sky calendar]], which does the Sun-and-Moon part of exactly this job for any place on Earth. For the brightness side, see the [[light-pollution-calculator|light pollution calculator]]; for one night at a time, [[sky-tonight|sky tonight]].

## The paper in brief

The network, "Was het donker" (was it dark), runs meters from Oostkapelle on the Dutch coast at 51.6° N up to Borkum at 53.6° N, and from very dark sites (Hornhuizen, 21.77 mag arcsec⁻² in January 2020) to city sites (Rijswijk, 18.16). The authors take four years of readings, keep only the hours when the Sun was at least **18° below the horizon** and the Moon was more than **3° below** it, mask clouds with EUMETSAT satellite data, remove outliers, and fit a trend to the weekly medians.

Their headline is the trend: the dark sites are losing their darkness at a few per cent a year, and the sensors' own ageing (about 4.1 % a year, uncorrected) means the real loss is probably worse. Their second headline is how little data this leaves. Of **1,051,920** possible hourly readings, 72.4 % go to sunlight, 53.8 % of the rest to moonlight and 93.9 % of what survives to cloud: **8207 hours, 0.8 % of the time**. And the leftovers are badly spread through the year: 4697 usable hours in February to April, 88 in May to July.

Everything in that budget except the clouds is pure geometry, which means anyone can check it.

## Checked with the tools

The Dark-sky calendar splits every night of a year into 10-minute slots and labels each one day, civil, nautical or astronomical twilight, dark with the Moon up, or dark with no Moon, using the Sun's and the Moon's altitudes for the place. We ran it for 2020 to 2023 at three of the paper's sites, straight from their published coordinates.

**Step 1: the sunlight line.** At Lauwersoog (53.385° N, 6.235° E) the tool makes **27.8 %** of all hours astronomically dark over the four years, which is the other side of the paper's "72.4 % removed for sunlight". At the network's southernmost site, Oostkapelle at 51.6° N, it rises to 28.8 %; at Borkum, the northernmost, it falls to 27.7 %. The paper's single figure covers all the stations at once, and it sits inside that spread.

**Step 2: the moonlight line.** With the calendar's default rule (the Moon spoils the dark whenever it is above the horizon) **48.9 %** of those dark hours are Moon-free. The paper uses a slightly stricter rule, excluding hours when the Moon is less than 3° below the horizon, because a Moon just under the horizon still glows. Applying the same −3° rule on the same 10-minute grid gives **46.5 %**, against the paper's 46.2 %. That is as close as two independent calculations of this kind get.

| Check | Paper | Tool gives |
|---|---|---|
| Hours astronomically dark | 27.6 % (100 − 72.4) | 27.8 % at Lauwersoog, 27.7 to 28.8 % across the network's latitudes |
| Moon-free share of dark hours, Moon below −3° | 46.2 % (100 − 53.8) | 46.5 % |
| Moon-free share, Moon simply below the horizon | not used | 48.9 % |
| Moon-free dark hours as a share of all hours | 12.7 % | 12.9 % |
| Usable hours after cloud (93.9 % lost) | 8207 of 1,051,920 | not a geometry question |
| Hours Feb-Apr versus May-Jul | 4697 versus 88 | 2784 versus 123 dark hours in four years, a factor of 23 |

**Step 3: the summer hole.** The reason summer gives almost nothing is not cloud. Above about 48.5° N the Sun never reaches 18° below the horizon around midsummer, so there is no astronomical darkness at all. Figure 1 shows it plainly for 2021: the dark hours shrink from about 12 a night at midwinter to **zero from mid-May to late July**, and 307 of the 1461 nights in the four years have no dark hours whatsoever at Lauwersoog. The Moon-free line underneath swings up and down with the lunar month, which is why a single bad month can cost a trend study most of its season.

**Step 4: where the usable hours really are.** Added up month by month (Figure 2), February to April hold about **2784** astronomically dark hours in four years at Lauwersoog and May to July about **123**, a ratio of 23. The paper's own usable hours split 4697 to 88, a ratio of 53, so clouds roughly double the seasonal imbalance that geometry has already created. A network at this latitude is, in effect, a winter-and-spring instrument.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/dutch-sky-quality-meter-network-moonless-astronomical-dark-hours.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `darkNight` and `yearDates` from `src/lib/astro/darkness.ts`, the maths behind the [[dark-sky-calendar|Dark-sky calendar]], and uses astronomy-engine directly for the one comparison the tool does not offer: the paper's −3° Moon rule on the same 10-minute grid.

**From the paper:** the site coordinates (its Table 1), the four-year span, the two selection rules, the 1,051,920 potential hours and the percentages removed at each stage, and the seasonal split. **Our choices:** which three sites to run (Lauwersoog, Oostkapelle, Borkum), a site height of 0 m, and the four calendar years 2020 to 2023 as the span. Nothing is fitted; nothing uses the paper's brightness readings. We cannot and do not check the cloud step or the brightening trend.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` and the per-tool files in `tests/sci-tools/` pin this maths:

- `tests/sci.mjs` checks `darkNight` against astronomy-engine's own twilight search at Toronto for 15 January and 21 June 2027: astronomical dusk within one minute, the dark hours within 0.02 h, and the Moon-free hours never more than the dark hours. London at midsummer must come out with exactly zero dark hours, and a leap year with 366 evenings.
- The twilight bands come from astronomy-engine, which `tests/sci-tools/batch-airmass.mjs` pins against astropy 8.0 to better than 2 arcseconds in altitude.

## Check it yourself

On the [[dark-sky-calendar|Dark-sky calendar]] page:

1. Open the coordinate boxes in the place row and type *Latitude* **53.385**, *Longitude* **6.235**, *Height (m)* **0**. That is Lauwersoog, one of the paper's dark sites.
2. Step the year back to **2021** with the **‹** button.
3. Leave *The Moon spoils the dark when it is* on **above the horizon**.
4. The headline reads **1,215 hours of Moon-free darkness in 2021**, and the picture shows one row per night: the black band is Moon-free darkness, and it pinches out completely around midsummer.
5. In *Month by month*, June reads **0** for both *Dark* and *Moon-free dark*, July 3.1 and 0, while December reads about **374** dark hours and **189** Moon-free. The *Nights with 4 h+* column is 0 for May, June, July and August and 19 in December.
6. Switch the Moon rule to **up and over 25 % lit** and the yearly total rises to about **1,267** hours: a thin crescent costs you almost nothing, which is worth knowing before you cancel a night.
7. Press **Every night (CSV)** to get the whole year as a table, which is the shape you want for a selection step like the paper's.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Budget a monitoring campaign before buying hardware**: how many Moon-free dark hours your latitude actually offers, and in which months, decides how long a trend takes to measure.
- **Reproduce or audit a published selection step**, as here: the sunlight and moonlight cuts are geometry and should agree to a fraction of a per cent between any two codes.
- **Choose a Moon rule on purpose.** Below-the-horizon, 3° below, or "up and more than 25 % lit" differ by a few per cent of your sample; say which one you used.
- **Plan deep imaging** around the Moon-free windows the calendar marks, and size the exposures with the [[sub-exposure-calculator|sub-exposure calculator]] using your site's own sky brightness.
- **Explain a seasonal artefact** in a brightness time series: at 53° N, summer readings are twilight readings, not dark-sky readings.

## The fine print

- The calendar's default Moon rule is "above the horizon". The paper's is 3° below, so its Moon-free share is a little smaller; we computed both.
- We ran three of the 27 sites, at 0 m height, for the four calendar years 2020 to 2023. The paper's exact window (to November 2023 for the cloud mask, data retrieved in August 2024) is slightly different.
- Nothing here checks the cloud mask, the outlier filter, the trend fit or the sensor-ageing correction, which is where the paper's scientific result lives.
- Astronomical darkness (Sun 18° down) is a convention. Many deep-sky observers work in nautical twilight, and the calendar shows those bands too.
