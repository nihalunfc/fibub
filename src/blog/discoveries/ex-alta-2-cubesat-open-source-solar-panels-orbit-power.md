Solar panels are one of the most expensive parts of a small satellite, and most CubeSat teams buy them ready-made. A 2024 conference paper from the University of Alberta's student satellite group describes panels they designed, built and tested themselves, and is releasing the designs openly. The panels flew on three satellites deployed from the International Space Station in April 2023, including the 3U **Ex-Alta 2**. This is a preprint-style conference paper, not yet peer-reviewed in a journal. This article rebuilds Ex-Alta 2's panel power, its time in Earth's shadow and its orbit-average power with the [[cubesat-power-budget]] tool, and shows why the answer moves through the year. To find the satellite's real orbit, use the [[satellite-tracker]]; to turn heights into orbital elements, the [[orbit-elements]] tool.

## The paper in brief

A **CubeSat** is built from 10 cm cubes: a 3U is three cubes long, about the size of a loaf of bread. Ex-Alta 2 carries five panels: three fixed to the body (on the zenith face, which points away from the Earth, and on the port and starboard sides) and two **deployables**, hinged wings that are folded over the sides at launch and open out level with the zenith face once in orbit. Each panel holds six high-efficiency **triple-junction** cells (three layers, each catching a different part of sunlight), 69 by 40 mm, wired in series.

The authors report a typical panel output of **14.4 V at 0.48 A** at its best operating point, 5.2 to 7.4 W per panel in orbit, and an orbit-average power from a commercial simulator (STK) of **9.6 W ± 17 %** with the wings open, but only **2.9 W** if both wings had stayed stuck. The panels deliver power for at least 60 % of any orbit. Ex-Alta 2 uses **3.8 W** in normal operation. The authors believe the panels worked as planned but give no measured on-orbit power.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** with the tool's maths from the paper's stated cell size and layout. Three inputs are **our assumptions**: the cell efficiency (30.7 %, the maker's figure), an ISS-like orbit (415 km, 51.64°), and a satellite that keeps its zenith face to the sky (nadir pointing). Losses are left out unless said.

**Step 1: one panel.** Six cells of 69 × 40 mm cover 165.6 cm². In full sunlight (1361 W/m²) at 30.7 % that is **6.92 W**. The paper's 14.4 V × 0.48 A is **6.91 W**. The stated cell size and the maker's efficiency agree with the measured panel to 0.1 %.

**Step 2: time in the shadow.** At 415 km an orbit takes **92.9 minutes**. When the Sun lies in the orbit plane (a **beta angle**, the Sun's height above the orbit plane, of 0°), the satellite spends **36.0 minutes**, 38.8 % of the orbit, in Earth's shadow, so it is in sunlight **61.2 %** of the time at worst. That matches the paper's "at least 60 % of any orbit". Above a beta angle of 69.9° there is no eclipse at all.

**Step 3: power against beta angle.** Facing the Earth, the zenith-facing panels see the Sun best at orbit noon and not at all on the night side, while the side panels only see it when the Sun is well out of the orbit plane. With the wings open, the orbit-average power is **6.61 W** at beta 0° and rises to **8.47 W** at 74° (Figure 1). With both wings stuck (our reading: their cells face outwards over the sides) it falls to **2.20 W** at beta 0°, below the satellite's 3.8 W use, and only climbs to 7.26 W at high beta.

**Step 4: the first year.** The ISS orbit's beta angle swings between about **−74.8° and +73.9°** as the orbit turns and the seasons change. Day by day, the wings-open average stays between **6.64 and 8.71 W**, a mean of **7.68 W** (Figure 2). With both wings stuck the mean is 4.12 W, but on **147 days** of the year it would fall below 3.8 W, so the satellite would need to cut its use.

**Step 5: comparing with the paper's 9.6 W.** Our year mean of 7.68 W sits just under the paper's lower bound (9.6 − 17 % = 7.97 W). The average **in sunlight only** is 11.75 W, above it. The paper's simulator likely includes things our simple model leaves out, such as sunlight reflected from the Earth (albedo) and its own attitude and orbit, so the two bracket it. For the stuck case, the paper's 2.9 W lies between our two readings: 4.12 W if the stowed wings' cells face outwards and 1.78 W if only the zenith panel works. Adding typical losses (panels at 60 °C and 90 % kept after converters, **our assumption**) lowers our wings-open mean to 6.36 W.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ex-alta-2-cubesat-open-source-solar-panels-orbit-power.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `panelPeak`, `eclipseFraction`, `period`, `betaStar` and `simulate` from `src/lib/sci/cubesatpower.ts`, the maths behind the [[cubesat-power-budget]] tool: a cylindrical Earth shadow, the Sun's direction in a nadir-pointing body frame, and the power of each face summed step by step around the orbit.

The cell size, the panel layout, the 14.4 V and 0.48 A, the 3.8 W load and the paper's simulated averages come from the paper. The 30.7 % efficiency, the 415 km and 51.64° orbit, the 5° a day drift of the orbit's node, the starting position of the node and the way the stuck wings face are **our assumptions**. The beta angle through the year uses the standard formula from the Sun's ecliptic longitude and the orbit's node.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/cubesat-power-budget.mjs`, which pins the tool against independent numpy calculations:

- The share of the orbit in shadow at 400 km must be 0.3901 at beta 0° and 0.0461 at beta 70°, matching a brute-force shadow test at 2 million points around the orbit; above 70.2° it must be zero.
- A nadir-pointing test satellite at 500 km and beta 20° must collect 25.805 Wh per orbit and reach a deepest discharge of 9.965 %, as a separate numpy simulation does.
- New with this article: six 69 × 40 mm cells at 30.7 % must give 6.919 W; 415 km must be 61.2 % sunlit at beta 0°; and the five-panel Ex-Alta 2 layout at beta 20° with a 3.8 W load must average 7.67 W with a deepest discharge of 13.3 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[cubesat-power-budget]] tool:

1. In *Orbit*, type **415** in *Altitude (km)* and **20** in *Beta angle (°)*.
2. In *Solar array*, replace the *Panels* box with: **Zenith, -Z, 165.6**; **Wing 1, -Z, 165.6**; **Wing 2, -Z, 165.6**; **Port, +Y, 165.6**; **Starboard, -Y, 165.6** (one panel per line). Keep *Attitude* on **Nadir pointing**.
3. Set *Cell efficiency (%)* to **30.7**, *Packing* to **100**, *Panel temperature (°C)* to **28**, *Degradation per year (%)* to **0** and *Power kept after converters and wiring (%)* to **100**.
4. In *Loads*, replace the box with **Load, Power (W), Nominal** on the first line and **Whole satellite, 3.8, 100** on the second.
5. The result reads **Nominal: power positive, +6.00 Wh per orbit, deepest DoD 13.3 %**, with *Eclipse* **35.3 min** and *Generation, orbit average* **7.67 W** (12.39 W average in sunlight).
6. Delete the two wing lines and change *Beta angle (°)* to **0**: the result turns to **Nominal: dod over the limit, −2.47 Wh per orbit, deepest DoD 42.2 %**, with 2.20 W of generation. A stuck pair of wings cannot carry the 3.8 W load in this season, as Figure 1 shows.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Size a student CubeSat's panels**: try layouts, attitudes and beta angles before building, with the [[cubesat-power-budget]] tool.
- **Check a vendor's or a paper's panel rating** from cell size and efficiency in one line, as in Step 1.
- **Plan for a stuck deployable**: see on which beta angles the satellite must go into a low-power mode.
- **Find the real beta angle** of a flying satellite from its orbit with the [[satellite-tracker]], then read its power off the tool.
- **Teach** why the same satellite makes more power in some weeks than others, and why "orbit average" and "average in sunlight" differ by more than a third.

## The fine print

- Our results come from a simple model: a cylindrical shadow, no Earth albedo, perfect nadir pointing and no shading between panels. The paper's 9.6 W comes from a commercial simulator with its own settings, which the paper does not list in full.
- The paper reports no measured on-orbit power, so nothing here checks the panels' real performance.
- The 30.7 % efficiency, the orbit and the stuck-wing geometry are our assumptions; the satellite's orbit also sank over its life.
- This is a conference paper on arXiv, not yet peer-reviewed in a journal.
