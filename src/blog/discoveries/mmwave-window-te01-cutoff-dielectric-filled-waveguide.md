Millimetre-wave 5G antennas in a phone do not radiate into open air. They radiate through a **window**: a slot in the metal side frame, filled with plastic (the "deco"). A 2026 paper in *IEEE Access* shows that as phones get thinner and the window gets shorter, that window stops behaving like a hole and starts behaving like a short piece of **waveguide** — a metal pipe that carries radio waves only above a **cut-off frequency**. One polarisation stays above cut-off and radiates; the other drops below it and is choked off, which the authors measured as an 8 dB difference between the two polarisations and a loss of about 30 percentage points of throughput. Every cut-off number in that story can be reproduced in a browser with the [[waveguide-modes|waveguide mode calculator]], and the related steps are covered by the [[skin-depth-calculator]] for wall and frame losses and the [[antenna-calculator]] for the radiating element itself.

## The paper in brief

The device is a vertically mounted, dual-polarised antenna-in-package module sitting behind a radiation window in the handset's metal frame. The window is **4.0 mm wide**, filled with a plastic of **relative permittivity 3**, and its **height is the thing that shrinks** when the phone gets thinner: the authors take it from 4.0 mm down to 2.8 mm.

The abstract's key physical claim is a pair of numbers: the cut-off frequency of the aperture mode that carries one polarisation rises **from 21.6 GHz to 30.9 GHz** as the height shrinks, so it crosses the **27.5 GHz** operating band. The other polarisation's mode is set by the width, which does not change, so it keeps propagating. Over the air, that showed up as an **8 dB RSRP imbalance** between polarisations and about **30 percentage points** of normalised throughput lost. Their fix is a **two-material deco**: an ordinary outer region plus a high-permittivity inner region. Full-wave simulation puts the imbalance down from **7.5 dB to below 0.5 dB**, boresight gain up by **8.3 dB**, and measurements recover about 40 percentage points of throughput at the same window height.

Only the abstract was readable for us (IEEE Xplore refuses automated reading), so everything below is built from the numbers quoted above, which are the paper's own.

## Checked with the tools

A rectangular window of width *a* and height *b*, filled with permittivity ε<sub>r</sub>, has mode cut-offs

f<sub>c</sub>(TE<sub>mn</sub>) = (c / 2√ε<sub>r</sub>) · √((m/a)² + (n/b)²)

where *c* is the speed of light in vacuum and *m*, *n* count half-waves across the width and the height. The two lowest modes are the interesting ones: **TE10** depends only on the width, **TE01** only on the height. Typing the paper's window into the waveguide calculator's maths gives:

| Window | TE10 cut-off | TE01 cut-off | At 27.5 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.0 × 4.0 mm, ε<sub>r</sub> 3 | 21.64 GHz | 21.64 GHz | both propagate |
| 4.0 × 2.8 mm, ε<sub>r</sub> 3 | 21.64 GHz | 30.91 GHz | TE10 only |

Those are the paper's 21.6 GHz and 30.9 GHz, to the three figures it quotes. The square window is a **degenerate pair**: both modes sit at exactly the same cut-off, which is the balanced case. Shrinking the height splits the pair and pushes one member through the band (Figure 1). The tool also puts the crossing point at a window height of **3.15 mm**: above that the second polarisation still works at 27.5 GHz, below it the window is an attenuator.

Below cut-off a mode does not vanish; it **decays** along the window, at √(k<sub>c</sub>² − k²) nepers per metre. At a height of 2.8 mm and 27.5 GHz the calculator gives **4.45 dB per millimetre** of window depth. That turns the paper's dB figures into a thickness: a window **1.69 mm** deep would leak exactly the 7.5 dB imbalance the paper reports, and 1.8 dB more for every extra 0.4 mm of depth. The paper does not state its window depth, so 1.69 mm is **our inference**, not a measurement; what matters is the scale — a window a couple of millimetres deep turns a mode a few GHz below cut-off into a loss of several dB, which is the size of the effect the authors measured. Figure 2 shows how fast that loss builds as the window shortens.

Finally, the fix. Cut-off scales as 1/√ε<sub>r</sub>, so raising the permittivity of the material inside the window pulls the TE01 cut-off back down. The calculator gives a clean target: at 2.8 mm the cut-off re-enters the band once ε<sub>r</sub> passes **3.79**, and reaches the 4.0 mm window's own 21.64 GHz at **ε<sub>r</sub> = 6.12** — which is just 3 × (4.0/2.8)², the permittivity that keeps *b*√ε<sub>r</sub> constant. That is exactly what a "high-permittivity inner region" has to do, and it says how high: roughly double the plastic's permittivity, not a few per cent more.

## How we made the graphs

Everything above comes from `scripts/blog-figures/mmwave-window-te01-cutoff-dielectric-filled-waveguide.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls two functions from `src/lib/sci/ph-waveguide.ts`, the maths behind the [[waveguide-modes|waveguide mode calculator]]: `rectModes(a, b, er, fmax)` for every mode's cut-off, and `propagate(mode, f, er)` for the guide wavelength, wave impedance and the evanescent decay constant below cut-off.

The inputs are the paper's: width 4.0 mm, permittivity 3, heights 4.0 and 2.8 mm, frequency 27.5 GHz, and the reported 7.5 dB imbalance. The **window depth of 1.69 mm is ours**, obtained by inverting the decay rate against that 7.5 dB. Nothing here is a fit to the paper's data; it is a recomputation of its stated geometry from first principles.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/waveguide-modes.mjs` on every change to fibub, and it pins the same functions used here against independent references:

- Bessel functions and their zeros must match SciPy to 10 decimal places (`J0(2.5) = −0.0483838`, first zero of J<sub>1</sub>′ at 1.8411838), which fixes the circular-guide cut-offs.
- The X-band guide, 22.86 × 10.16 mm, must give TE10 at exactly *c*/2*a* = 6.5573 GHz, then TE20, TE01, TE11/TM11 in that order, and filling it with ε<sub>r</sub> = 4 must halve every cut-off.
- Propagation identities must hold to 12 figures: v<sub>p</sub> v<sub>g</sub> = c², λ<sub>g</sub> = λ/√(1 − (f<sub>c</sub>/f)²) and Z<sub>TE</sub> Z<sub>TM</sub> = η²; the TE10 wall loss must match a numerical power-loss integral (0.1083 dB/m for copper at 10 GHz).
- New with this article: the paper's own window must come out right — 4.0 × 4.0 mm with ε<sub>r</sub> 3 gives a degenerate TE10/TE01 pair at 21.636 GHz, 4.0 × 2.8 mm gives 21.636 and 30.908 GHz, and the TE01 mode at 27.5 GHz decays at 4.4486 dB/mm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[waveguide-modes|waveguide mode calculator]] page:

1. Leave *Shape* on **Rectangular**. Set *Wide side a (mm)* to **4**, *Narrow side b (mm)* to **4**, *Filling εr* to **3** and *Operating frequency (GHz)* to **27.5**.
2. The modes table shows **TE01 and TE10 both at 21.636 GHz**, both "propagates". *Single-mode band* reads **21.64 GHz to 30.6 GHz** — the square window treats the two polarisations alike.
3. Now set *Narrow side b (mm)* to **2.8**. TE10 stays at 21.636 GHz, and **TE01 moves to 30.908 GHz** with "evanescent" in the *At f* column and **4.45 dB/mm** in its *Loss* column. That is the choked polarisation.
4. Set *Narrow side b (mm)* to **3.147**: TE01's cut-off lands on **27.5 GHz**, the knife edge between the two behaviours.
5. Put *Narrow side b (mm)* back to **2.8** and raise *Filling εr* to **6.12**. TE01 returns to **21.6 GHz** and propagates again, and the pair is balanced once more: the paper's two-material fix, in one field.
6. Read *Guide wavelength (TE10)* (**10.2 mm** at 27.5 GHz in the 2.8 mm window) if you need the electrical length of the window itself.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Size a radiation window or slot before simulating it.** One field change tells you whether a mode is above or below cut-off, which is a minute of work instead of a full-wave sweep.
- **Decide how much permittivity a filling needs.** The ε<sub>r</sub>·b² rule above turns "use a higher-k material" into a number a materials supplier can quote against.
- **Convert cut-off margin into decibels.** The evanescent decay in dB/mm tells you whether a below-cut-off path leaks 1 dB or 20 dB through a given thickness — useful for shielding gaps as well as antennas, next to the [[skin-depth-calculator]].
- **Check a published cut-off in seconds**, as we did here, before building on someone else's geometry.
- **Teach mode degeneracy**: a square guide, then a slightly rectangular one, shows polarisation splitting better than any diagram.

## The fine print

- We could read only the paper's abstract. Its full-text geometry (window depth, deco regions, antenna design) may differ in detail from the simple rectangular guide we used.
- A radiation window is a **short, open** piece of guide, not an infinite one. Cut-off and evanescent decay are still the right physics for whether a mode gets through, but the exact radiated gain needs a full-wave solver.
- The 1.69 mm window depth is our inference from the paper's 7.5 dB, not a stated value.
- The paper's mode labels ("TE01-like") are ours too, in the usual convention where the first index counts half-waves across the wide side.
- Wall loss and dielectric loss in the plastic are ignored here; both are small next to several dB of cut-off attenuation.
