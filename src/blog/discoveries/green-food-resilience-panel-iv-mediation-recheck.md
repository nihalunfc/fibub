A regression table is a compressed claim. From a coefficient and its standard error you can rebuild the t statistic, the p value and the confidence interval — but only if you know how many degrees of freedom the author used, and with **cluster-robust standard errors** that number is not the sample size. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems* studies 31 Chinese provinces over 13 years and reports a complete set: seven coefficients with province-clustered standard errors, a two-stage least squares model with its first-stage F, and a mediation path with a bootstrap interval. We rebuilt all of it with the maths behind the [[regression-ols-iv-panel]]. Every piece of the paper's arithmetic checks out; its p values are the large-sample ones, and with 31 clusters they should be a little wider. For the related jobs, see [[difference-in-differences]] and [[hypothesis-tests]].

## The paper in brief

The outcome is **agricultural sustainable development performance**, an index the authors build from economic, environmental and social indicators with entropy weights. The explanatory variable is **green food industry resilience**, split into three capacities: adaptive (adjusting to a shock), absorptive (taking a shock without changing) and restorative (recovering afterwards). The panel is 403 province-years, with province and year fixed effects and standard errors clustered by province.

The headline results: resilience as a whole raises performance (0.341), with adaptive capacity the strongest single capacity (0.243) and absorptive capacity not significant (0.127, the paper's own p = 0.163). **Export competitiveness** partly mediates the effect: resilience raises export competitiveness (0.298), which in turn raises performance (0.278), leaving a direct effect of 0.258. The authors call the mediated share "approximately one-quarter". Because resilience could itself respond to performance, they also instrument it with **historical disaster frequency**, getting 0.398 with a first-stage F of 29.7 and a Hausman p of 0.078.

## Checked with the tools

The [[regression-ols-iv-panel]] fits OLS, two-stage least squares and panel fixed effects, and reports each coefficient's t, p and interval. Here we use the same functions on the paper's own printed numbers, so nothing below depends on data we do not have.

**Step 1: the t statistics and intervals.** Dividing each coefficient by its standard error gives the t values. The question is what to compare them against. The paper's stated p of 0.163 for absorptive capacity (t = 1.396) matches the **normal** reference to three decimals (0.1628). The tool's OLS mode, when you cluster, instead uses **G − 1 degrees of freedom**, 30 here, which is the usual practitioner's rule for cluster-robust inference; that gives 0.1731. In its panel mode the tool follows `linearmodels` and uses n − k − absorbed effects, which is 359 here, and that gives 0.1637, essentially the normal answer again.

None of the paper's conclusions change: the same six coefficients are significant at 5 % and the same one is not. The intervals widen by about 4 % (the 95 % multiplier goes from 1.960 to 2.042). Figure 1 shows both sets side by side.

**Step 2: the mediation arithmetic is exactly consistent.** The indirect effect is the product of the two path coefficients: 0.298 × 0.278 = **0.0828**, which the paper rounds to 0.083. Independently, total minus direct is 0.341 − 0.258 = **0.0830**. Those two routes agree to the fourth decimal, which means the three models were fitted on the same sample with the same controls — a check worth doing on any mediation table, because it fails more often than you would think. The mediated share is 0.0828 / 0.341 = **24.3 %**, so "approximately one-quarter" is fair.

The paper's interval for the indirect effect comes from a bootstrap: 0.028 to 0.147. The classical alternative, Sobel's standard error for a product, is √(b²·SE_a² + a²·SE_b²) = **0.0264**, giving z = 3.13, p = 0.0017 and an interval of **0.031 to 0.135**. The bootstrap interval is wider on the upper side, which is what you expect for a product of two estimates; the two agree well enough that neither is doing any work the other would undo. If you want to redo that bootstrap on your own data, [[bootstrap-permutation]] does percentile and BCa intervals.

**Step 3: the instrument is strong, but the price is precision.** The 2SLS coefficient, 0.398 with a standard error of 0.178, gives t = 2.236 and p = 0.025, so the paper's "significant at 5 %" is right. The first-stage F of 29.7 is comfortably above the usual threshold of 10 (with one instrument that is a first-stage t of 5.45), so this is not a weak-instrument case. But the 95 % interval is **0.049 to 0.747**, 2.4 times as wide as the fixed-effects interval, and it contains the fixed-effects estimate throughout. Combined with the Hausman p of 0.078, the honest reading is that the instrumented and uninstrumented estimates cannot be told apart, not that the true effect is 0.398.

**Step 4: the same pipeline, end to end, on a simulated panel.** None of the steps above fits anything, so we also **simulated** a panel of the paper's exact shape — 31 provinces × 13 years = 403 rows, a true slope of 0.341, province and year effects — with the residual spread tuned so that the tool's province-clustered standard error comes out at the paper's 0.074. Running it through the panel mode with two-way effects returns a slope of **0.289** (clustered SE 0.0740, t 3.91, p 0.00011, 95 % 0.144 to 0.435) on 359 degrees of freedom and 31 clusters. That is 0.7 standard errors below the 0.341 we put in: a reminder that with this much noise, a single study's point estimate is worth about ±0.15, whatever its stars say. Figure 2 shows the within-province, within-year variation the slope is fitted to.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number here come from `scripts/blog-figures/green-food-resilience-panel-iv-mediation-recheck.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls:

- `tTwoSided`, `tInv`, `normCdf` and `normInv` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the p values and intervals — the same functions the regression tool uses to turn a coefficient and a standard error into a row of its table;
- `panelFE`, `ols`, `iv2sls` and `demean` from `src/lib/sci/econometrics.ts`, the maths behind the [[regression-ols-iv-panel]], for the simulated panel, the two-way demeaning in Figure 2 and the first-stage F.

The coefficients, standard errors, sample size, cluster count, bootstrap interval, first-stage F and Hausman p are stated values from the paper. The simulated panel, the instrument, the random seeds and the two bisection calibrations (residual spread to hit SE 0.074; instrument strength to hit F = 29.7) are **our assumptions**, and the simulated data are not the paper's.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/regression-ols-iv-panel.mjs`, which pins the three estimators against `statsmodels` and `linearmodels` on fixed example data:

- OLS coefficients, classical and HC0 to HC3 standard errors, t, p and intervals must match `statsmodels` `OLS` with `use_t=True` to many decimals, and the cluster-robust version must match its `cov_type='cluster'` with the G − 1 degrees of freedom;
- `iv2sls` must match `linearmodels`' `IV2SLS` with `debiased=True`, including the first-stage F and partial R² for each endogenous variable;
- `panelFE` must match `PanelOLS` with entity and time effects, including the within R² and the count of absorbed effects.
- New with this article: the 20-row panel in "Check it yourself" must give a two-way fixed-effects slope of 0.3346 with a standard error of 0.0122, a within R² of 0.9855 on 11 degrees of freedom, and 0.3955 (0.1103) when the year effects are switched off.

1. Choose **Panel fixed effects**.
2. Set *Outcome (y)* to **performance**, *Entity (person, firm, country)* to **province** and *Time (year, wave)* to **year**. Under *Explanatory variables*, tick **resilience** only.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Re-derive any published regression row.** A coefficient and a standard error are enough for the t, the p and the interval; the tool's own reporting shows which degrees of freedom were used, which is the part papers leave out.
- **Check a mediation table for consistency**: the indirect effect must equal total minus direct, and the product of the two paths. If it does not, the three models were not fitted on the same sample.
- **Decide whether an instrument is worth it.** Run both modes on the same data in the [[regression-ols-iv-panel]] and compare the intervals, not just the point estimates; the first-stage F and its "strong (F ≥ 10)" label are printed for you.
- **Plan a panel study's size.** Simulate a panel of your intended shape, tune the noise to a plausible standard error, and see how wide the interval will be before you collect anything.
- **Report a mediation interval both ways**: Sobel's normal interval from [[hypothesis-tests]] and a bootstrap from [[bootstrap-permutation]], so a reader can see the product's skew.

## The fine print

- We never saw the paper's data. Steps 1 to 3 use only its printed coefficients and standard errors; step 4 uses **our simulation**.
- The paper's tables give stars and a few exact p values, not a p for every row, so the comparison in the table above rests on its stated p of 0.163 for absorptive capacity to identify the reference it used.
- Which degrees of freedom are "right" for cluster-robust inference is itself a judgement: G − 1 is the common conservative rule with few clusters, and 31 clusters is on the edge of where the choice matters.
- The Hausman p of 0.078 and the first-stage F of 29.7 are the paper's own; we did not recompute them, as that needs the data.
- Our simulated panel has one regressor and no controls, so its within R² is nothing like the paper's.
