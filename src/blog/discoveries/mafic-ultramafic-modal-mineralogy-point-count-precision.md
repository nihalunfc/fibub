Mafic and ultramafic rocks are the raw material for enhanced rock weathering, the idea of spreading crushed silicate rock so that it takes carbon dioxide out of the air as it dissolves. How much a rock can take up depends on which minerals it is made of and in what proportions, so a petrographer counts them: a grid of points is laid over a thin section and the mineral under each point is tallied. A 2025 paper in *Geosciences* did that for fourteen thin sections from six provinces of northern Thailand and published the modal percentages. Those percentages are counts, so they carry a countable uncertainty, and that is what the [[point-counter]] works out. For the steps on either side, turn a modal count into a three-part diagram with the [[ternary-plot-maker]] or into a normative mineralogy from a chemical analysis with the [[cipw-norm]].

## The paper in brief

The author sampled ten sites in six northern Thai provinces and cut fourteen thin sections, each 26 mm by 46 mm and 30 micrometres thick. Points were placed on a **1 mm grid** and at least **400 points** were counted per section, then normalised to 100 %. The rocks run from vesicular olivine basalt and basaltic tuff through gabbro and gabbronorite to peridotite and altered pyroxenite, with metabasites in between.

The point counts are the paper's backbone. Two Mae Chan gabbros come out quite differently: one is a **pyroxene-hornblende gabbronorite** (plagioclase 29.25, hornblende 28.25, clinopyroxene 21.25, orthopyroxene 17.75, olivine 1.0 modal %), the other a **gabbronorite** (plagioclase 54.75, clinopyroxene 17.25, opaque minerals 11.5, orthopyroxene 6.0, olivine 3.25 modal %). The four peridotite samples from Phrao carry **olivine 62 to 67.25 %** with **plagioclase 9 to 12.5 %**, and the altered pyroxenite from Huay Lao is 60 % orthopyroxene and 28 % clinopyroxene. For weathering, the olivine- and pyroxene-rich rocks matter most, because those are the minerals that release magnesium and calcium fastest.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is **recomputed from the percentages printed in the paper's text**. No measured data were used; we did not recount anything.

**Step 1: how many points were really counted?** Every value in the two gabbros, and most values in the peridotites, is a multiple of 0.25 % (29.25, 28.25, 21.25, 17.75, 11.5, 6.0, 0.75). A percentage from a count of N points can only be a multiple of 100/N. We searched N from 20 to 4000 for the smallest count that makes **all** of those values whole numbers of points: it is exactly **400**. So the quarter-percent steps are the fingerprint of a 400-point count, in line with the stated method, and 29.25 % means 117 points of 400.

The basalt percentages behave differently: 10.63, 8.71, 41.03, 12.31 and 14.13 % fit **no** count between 100 and 2000 points at the printed rounding. Those sections were measured another way (the paper also used image-analysis software) or renormalised after some category was removed. Worth knowing, because the statistics below only apply to real counts.

**Step 2: the precision of one percentage.** For a count, the standard error of a proportion *p* after *N* points is √(p(1 − p)/N), and petrographers usually quote twice it, the **Van der Plas and Tobi 2σ**, which about 95 % of repeat counts fall inside. At 400 points this is **5.0 percentage points** at 50 %, **3.0** at 10 % and **1.0** at 1 %. Figure 1 shows the curve for 400, 900 and 2000 points with the paper's own minerals on it.

**Step 3: which differences between the two gabbros are real?** Using the exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95 % interval, which stays sensible for rare minerals where the normal approximation does not:

| Mineral | Gabbronorite | Pyroxene-hornblende gabbronorite | Can they be told apart? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plagioclase | 54.75 % (49.7 to 59.7) | 29.25 % (24.8 to 34.0) | Yes, far apart |
| Orthopyroxene | 6.0 % (3.9 to 8.8) | 17.75 % (14.1 to 21.9) | Yes |
| Olivine | 3.25 % (1.7 to 5.5) | 1.0 % (0.3 to 2.5) | **No**, the ranges overlap |

So the names and the big mineralogical contrast are safe at 400 points, but the olivine difference, which is the one that matters for how fast the rock would weather, is not resolved. Olivine at these levels needs a much bigger count.

**Step 4: the 10 % boundary in the peridotites.** Rock names change at round numbers: 10 % plagioclase is the usual cut between a plain peridotite and a plagioclase-bearing one. The paper's four samples give 9 to 12.5 %. At 400 points, 9 % has an exact 95 % range of **6.4 to 12.2 %** and 12.5 % a range of **9.4 to 16.2 %**: both are consistent with 10 %, so the count cannot place these rocks on one side of the line. To get the 2σ down to ±2 percentage points at a true 10 % you need **900 points**; for ±1 point, 3600 (Figure 2). The same arithmetic at 50 % needs 400 points for ±5 and **2500** for ±2.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/mafic-ultramafic-modal-mineralogy-point-count-precision.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `classStat` (proportion, binomial standard error, 2σ and the exact Clopper-Pearson interval), `twoSigma` and `pointsNeeded` from `src/lib/sci/pointcount.ts`, the maths behind the [[point-counter]].

The modal percentages, the 1 mm grid and the "at least 400 points" are from the paper's running text. The search for the count N, the confidence intervals, the overlap test and the points-needed curves are ours. Nothing here is a measurement.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/point-counter.mjs`, which pins the counting statistics against SciPy and NumPy:

- For 40 counts, including the edge cases 0 of 10, 10 of 10 and 1 of 1, the proportion, the standard error and the exact, Wilson and normal intervals at 95 % and 99 % must equal `scipy.stats.binomtest(k, N).proportion_ci` and `scipy.stats.norm` to about 1e-14.
- The Van der Plas and Tobi line: 2σ at 50 % after 400 points must be exactly 5 %, and at 10 % after 900 points exactly 2 %.
- N = 4p(1 − p)/e²: 400 points for ±5 points at 50 %, 3600 for ±1 point at 10 %, 712 for ±3 points at 20 %.
- New with this article: the published gabbronorite count of 219 plagioclase points in 400 must give 54.75 %, a 2σ of 4.98 points and an exact 95 % range of 49.73 to 59.70 %; the peridotite's 9 % plagioclase (36 of 400) must give 6.38 to 12.24 %, so that both sides of the 10 % name boundary stay inside it.

## Check it yourself

The [[point-counter]] works as a plain tally counter with no image, which is all you need to repeat the boundary problem above. On the tool page:

1. In *One class per line: its key, then its name*, replace the text with five lines: `o olivine`, `c clinopyroxene`, `p plagioclase`, `s spinel`, `x secondary`.
2. Leave the image alone (the help line says the class buttons work as a plain tally counter) and in the *Count* card press the keys for a small peridotite-like count: **o** 13 times, **c** twice, **p** twice, **s** once and **x** twice. The heading reads *Tally: 20 counted*.
3. The *Modal composition* table now shows olivine **65 %** with *SE %* **10.67**, *2σ %* **21.33** and a 95 % range of **40.78 to 84.61**; plagioclase shows **10 %**, 2σ **13.42** and a range of **1.23 to 31.70**.
4. Set *Target 2σ (± percentage points)* to **2**. The *Points for target* column now reads **900** for plagioclase, clinopyroxene and secondary minerals, **475** for spinel and **2275** for olivine. That 900 is the number the paper's peridotites would have needed to settle the 10 % boundary.
5. Press *Results (CSV)* to keep the table, then *Clear counts* and try the same tally with one point moved between classes to see how much a single point matters at N = 20.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Put error bars on a published modal analysis** before you build an argument on it: paste the percentages as counts (percentage × N ÷ 100) and read the exact 95 % ranges.
- **Plan a count before you start**: set your target 2σ and let the tool tell you how many points the rarest mineral you care about needs. Rare phases, not common ones, set the workload.
- **Check whether two sections really differ**: if the exact intervals overlap, the difference is not established by counting alone.
- **Recover the count from a paper**, as in step 1, when the method section is vague: the step size between reported percentages gives N away.
- **Teach the binomial**: a thin section is the clearest real example of why a 1 % mineral and a 50 % mineral have completely different precisions, with the [[point-counter]]'s Van der Plas and Tobi chart on screen.

## The fine print

- We recomputed from percentages in the paper's text; we did not recount any section, and the paper's own numbers stand.
- The binomial treats points as independent. On a 1 mm grid over coarse grains (the paper's gabbro oikocrysts reach 5 mm) neighbouring points can land in the same crystal, which makes the real precision a little worse than the binomial says, not better.
- "At least 400 points" means some sections may have more; our intervals assume exactly 400, so they are slightly conservative for those.
- The 10 % plagioclase cut is the usual IUGS convention for ultramafic rock names. Check the current recommendation before renaming a rock.
- Modal percentages are by area in a section, which equals volume only if the section is a fair cut. They are not weight percentages, and enhanced-weathering potential depends on mass and grain size too.
