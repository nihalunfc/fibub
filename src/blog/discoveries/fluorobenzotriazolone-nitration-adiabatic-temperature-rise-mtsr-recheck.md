Nitrations are among the most hazardous steps in chemical manufacturing: they give off a lot of heat, and the mixtures can decompose violently if they get hot. Before a process is scaled up, safety engineers ask a simple question: **if the cooling failed in the middle of this step, how hot would the batch get?** A 2025 paper in *Molecules* answered it for the four steps that make a nitrated benzotriazolone (nitration, quenching, extraction and an alkaline wash). The answer rests on one short formula, which the [[adiabatic-temperature-rise]] tool applies, so we recompute the numbers. The heats behind them come from calorimetry; the [[thermochemistry-calculator]] works with heats of formation, and the reactor behind such a step is a job for the [[reactor-design]] calculator.

## The paper in brief

The authors measured the thermal behaviour of the raw materials and of the solution left after each step, using differential scanning calorimetry and an accelerating rate calorimeter. For each step they report the mass of the batch, its **specific heat** (how much heat raises one kilogram by one degree), the **heat released** and the resulting **adiabatic temperature rise** ΔTad: the warming if all of that heat stayed in the batch. Adding ΔTad to the process temperature gives the **maximum temperature of the synthesis reaction (MTSR)** after a total cooling failure. They compare it with **TD24**, the temperature at which a runaway decomposition of that solution would take 24 hours to reach its peak rate, and sort each step into a risk level. Nitration and quenching are rated the most dangerous; extraction and washing the least.

## Checked with the tools

Both quantities are simple: ΔTad = Q ÷ (m × cp), and MTSR = T<sub>process</sub> + ΔTad. We charged each batch into the tool as one lumped species (**our assumption**): one "mole" of the whole batch, with molar mass equal to the batch mass and a heat capacity of m × cp, reacting one-to-one to a product with the same heat capacity and a heat of formation lowered by Q. The tool's energy balance then gives exactly Q ÷ (m cp).

**What the recomputation shows.** The tables and the MTSR values reproduce to the printed digits, so the main numbers hang together. Two other printed values do not. In the discussion of quenching, the text gives 191.74 K, but the heat, mass and specific heat all give **197.14 K**, which is what the table prints and what its MTSR of 227.14 °C uses; the two sets of digits look swapped. The conclusions give 86.70 K for nitration, but every input gives **81.86 K** (which the text and table print and the MTSR uses). 86.70 K would need a heat of about 96.6 kJ or a specific heat of about 1.18 kJ/(kg K), neither of which appears in the paper. These are most likely typing slips and do not change the paper's conclusions, but they are worth knowing when someone quotes the summary.

The second chart is the safety message: after a cooling failure, **nitration reaches 109 °C, above its TD24 of 95.7 °C**, and **quenching reaches 227 °C against a TD24 of 37.9 °C**, so a failure in those two steps could start a decomposition that peaks within a day. Extraction and washing barely warm up and sit far below their TD24.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/fluorobenzotriazolone-nitration-adiabatic-temperature-rise-mtsr-recheck.mjs` imports `readSpecies`, `adiabatic`, `quickRise` and `energyRelease` from `src/lib/sci/eng-adiabatic.ts`, the maths of the Adiabatic temperature rise tool. Batch masses, specific heats, heats released, process temperatures and TD24 values come from the paper's text and tables. The lumped-batch model is our assumption. Figure 1 calls `adiabatic()` for conversions from 0 to 100 %; Figure 2 plots the tool's outlet temperature next to the paper's TD24.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/adiabatic-temperature-rise.mjs` pins the tool against an independent SciPy calculation: the methane example gives ΔrH° = −802.5 kJ/mol, an outlet of 2209.689 K for a feed at 298.15 K, and a quick estimate of 2334.87 K. New with this article, the same file checks the lumped-batch case: a 891.33 g batch with cp 1.25 kJ/(kg K) and 91.20 kJ released rises 81.855 K, the quenching batch 197.137 K, and the quick estimate equals the full balance when heat capacities are equal. It runs on every change with `node tests/sci.mjs`.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[adiabatic-temperature-rise]] tool and clear the species box.
2. Type two lines: `batch, -1, 1, 0, 891.33, 1114.1625` and `released, 1, 0, -91.2, 891.33, 1114.1625`. The columns are name, ν, feed (mol), ΔfH° (kJ/mol), M (g/mol) and cp a (J/(mol K)); the cp of 1114.1625 is 891.33 g × 1.25 J/(g K).
3. Set **Feed temperature** to 27.5 with **Temperature unit** °C, and **Conversion of the key reactant (%)** to 100.
4. You should read an adiabatic outlet of about 109.4 °C, a rise of 82 K, and "Heat released" of 91.2 kJ.
5. For quenching use `batch, -1, 1, 0, 1000, 1310` and `released, 1, 0, -258.25, 1000, 1310` with a feed of 30 °C: the rise reads 197 K, not 192.

## What researchers can use it for

- Checking a published calorimetry table: ΔTad = Q ÷ (m cp) catches mis-copied numbers in seconds, as the 191.74 and 86.70 values here show.
- Screening a new batch recipe: change the charge masses and see how the cooling-failure temperature moves against a known decomposition onset.
- Teaching process safety: the MTSR against TD24 comparison is the first step of most hazard assessments.
- Planning dilution or solvent changes: a larger thermal mass lowers ΔTad in proportion, which the quick estimate shows directly.
- Linking to a full energy balance for a gas-phase or adiabatic reactor, with the real heats of formation instead of a lumped heat.

## The fine print

This recomputes the paper's arithmetic from numbers printed in the paper; it does not repeat the calorimetry, and it says nothing about the decomposition kinetics behind TD24 or the paper's risk-matrix rankings. A lumped batch with constant specific heat is a simplification: real heat capacities change with temperature and composition. The two mismatches are findings about printed numbers, not about the experiments, and may reflect typing slips; the authors' own data would settle it. This page is education, not process-safety advice: real scale-up needs professional calorimetry and a hazard review.
