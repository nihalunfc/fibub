Black holes made in the first instant after the Big Bang, primordial black holes, are one of the few dark-matter candidates that astronomy can still test directly, and they have been in the news all month. A 2026 paper in The Astrophysical Journal asks what happens when a Sun-like star swallows one of **asteroid mass, 10¹⁷ to 10²³ grams**: the black hole settles into the star's core, eats it slowly from the inside, and once the gas it swallows starts to swirl into a disk, jets of **10⁴⁵ to 10⁵⁰ erg per second** blow the star apart **within minutes**. Two numbers in that story are pure black-hole geometry, the size of the hole and the angular momentum of its innermost stable orbit, and both come straight out of the [[schwarzschild-orbits]]. To see how the paper's Jupiter-like planet sets the capture rate, use the [[kepler-orbits]]; to place primordial black holes in cosmic history, the [[cosmology-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

Asteroid-mass primordial black holes are the last mass window where they could still make up all of the dark matter, because lighter ones would have evaporated by Hawking radiation and heavier ones would have shown up in lensing surveys. The authors follow one that is caught by a solar-type star with the help of a Jupiter-like planet. It sinks to the centre and grows by **Bondi accretion**, sucking in gas from the sphere where its gravity beats the gas's thermal motion. At first the growth is so slow that it takes billions of years and the star notices nothing.

The turning point comes when the gas that falls in carries more angular momentum than the black hole's **innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO)** can hold. Then the inflow can no longer fall straight in: it **circularizes into a disk**, the black hole spins up to about **a = 0.8**, and the disk launches winds and relativistic jets. Using stellar-evolution models and general-relativistic simulations, the paper finds the star is destroyed within minutes, leaving a black hole of about **0.01 to 1 solar masses** and a **day-long ultraviolet and blue flash**, possibly with X-rays or a weak gamma-ray burst. Searches for such flashes, or for low-mass black holes that no dying star could make, would then test whether primordial black holes are the dark matter.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation from the paper's stated masses and criterion**, using the formulas behind the [[schwarzschild-orbits]]. The stellar models and simulations themselves are far beyond any browser tool, and we do not try to redo them.

**Step 1: "the size of an atom".** A black hole's horizon radius is 2GM/c². For 10²⁰ grams, about 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ solar masses, the tool's maths gives **1.485 × 10⁻¹⁰ m**, or 0.149 nanometres. Its diameter, 0.297 nm, is about three hydrogen atoms (a Bohr diameter is 0.106 nm). Across the whole window the horizon runs from **1.5 × 10⁻¹³ m** (smaller than an atom's electron cloud by a factor of a thousand) at 10¹⁷ g to **0.15 µm** (a small bacterium) at 10²³ g. So the headline holds for the middle of the window.

**Step 2: the Bondi radius.** Gas is captured inside R_B = 2GM/c_s², where c_s is the sound speed. Written in units of M = GM/c², that is just 2(c/c_s)², so the ratio to the horizon does not depend on the mass at all: with c_s = 600 km/s it is **249,654 horizon radii**. For 10²⁰ g that gives **3.71 × 10⁻³ cm**, which the paper rounds to **4 × 10⁻³ cm** in its Eq. 27. A black hole the size of three atoms drinks from a sphere 37 micrometres across, about half the width of a human hair.

**Step 3: when does a disk form?** The paper's test is that gas falling in from the Bondi radius must carry at least the ISCO's angular momentum. For a non-spinning black hole the tool gives that as **L = 3.4641 GM/c, which is √12**. **Our assumption** for the inflow, the usual one: the gas at R_B spins with the star at a fraction λ of break-up, averaged over a sphere, so j = (2/3)ΩR_B². For a Sun-like star the break-up rate is Ω_k = √(GM/R³) = **6.278 × 10⁻⁴ rad/s** (a 2.78-hour spin). Because R_B grows as M², j grows as M² too, while the ISCO's grows only as M, so there is a definite mass where they cross:

M_disk = 3 L c_s⁴ / (8 λ Ω_k G c)

With c_s = 600 km/s this gives **0.067 solar masses for λ = 0.1** (a star that turns once in 27.8 hours), **0.034 for λ = 0.2** and **0.017 for λ = 0.4**. That is exactly the "few per cent of a solar mass" scale in the paper's results, and well inside its range of 0.01 to 1 solar masses for what is left. The fourth power of the sound speed makes the answer touchy: 500 km/s halves it, 700 km/s nearly doubles it.

**Step 4: what spin does.** The paper finds the black hole spinning at about 0.8 when the disk forms. A spinning hole's ISCO is smaller and holds less angular momentum, down to √(4/3) GM/c for maximal spin (a textbook value, not in the tool), which would cut the threshold to **0.0225 solar masses** at λ = 0.1. Spin 0.8 sits between, so the non-spinning number from the tool is an upper bound for a given star.

**Step 5: how small the engine is.** At the λ = 0.1 threshold the tool's radii table gives a horizon of **199 m** and an ISCO of **597 m**, while the Bondi radius is about **49,700 km**, 7 % of the Sun's radius. A disk well under a kilometre across, buried at the centre of a star, is what powers jets strong enough to destroy it.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/primordial-black-hole-inside-sun-like-star-atom-sized-horizon-disk-threshold.mjs` in fibub's public code. It imports `circular()` and `M_SUN_M` from `src/lib/sci/phgr.ts`, the geodesic maths behind the [[schwarzschild-orbits]]: `circular(6)` gives the ISCO's energy (√(8/9)) and angular momentum (√12) per unit mass, and `M_SUN_M`, GM☉/c² = 1,476.625 m, turns lengths in units of M into metres for any mass. The Bondi radius and the disk threshold are two lines of algebra on top, written out in the script's comments. Figure 1 is drawn on log axes; Figure 2 for three core sound speeds because the paper's own value changes as the core is eaten.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/schwarzschild-orbits.mjs` checks the plotter's orbits against SciPy integrations to twelve figures, the ISCO's E = √(8/9) and L = √12 exactly, the marginally bound orbit at 4M, light bending and the solar length scale. With this article it gained one check: a 10²⁰ g black hole must have a horizon of 1.4852 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, and the disk threshold built from `circular(6).L` must give 0.0674 solar masses for λ = 0.1 and c_s = 600 km/s.

## Check it yourself

On the [[schwarzschild-orbits]] page:

1. Type **5.0291e-14** in *Mass (solar masses)*; that is 10²⁰ g.
2. In the radii table, *Event horizon* reads **1.485 × 10⁻¹³ km**, which is 1.485 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, and the *ISCO* is three times that.
3. Change the mass to **0.0674**, the threshold for λ = 0.1. The *Event horizon* reads **0.199 km** and the *ISCO* **0.5971 km**.
4. Set *Closest distance r_p (in M)* and *Farthest distance r_a (in M)* both to **6.0001**, just outside the ISCO. *Angular momentum per unit mass L* reads about **3.4641 M c**, the √12 in the disk formula.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a mass into sizes at once**: horizon, photon sphere, ISCO and shadow radius for anything from an asteroid-mass primordial black hole to Sgr A*.
- **Get the ISCO's energy and angular momentum** for threshold arguments like this one: disk formation, circularization, or the 5.7 % binding energy that sets an accretion disk's efficiency.
- **Check scalings in a paper**: any criterion that sets an R² angular momentum against the ISCO's gives a threshold mass, and the tool supplies the GR factor.
- **Teach why black holes are small**: the plotter draws everything to scale, so the gap between a horizon and a Bondi radius 250,000 times larger is easy to show.

## The fine print

- This is a check of the paper's scalings, not of its stellar models or simulations. The disk threshold uses **our assumption** that the infalling gas carries (2/3) of the star's spin at the Bondi radius, and a fixed sound speed; the paper follows both as the star evolves.
- The tool is for non-spinning black holes. The maximal-spin factor √(4/3) is a textbook value we typed in, not tool output.
- We could not open the journal's page from here. Values from the paper come from the abstract in its Crossref record and from the first part of the arXiv text, which we read in full up to its Eq. 27; we read the later sections only in summary and quote none of their numbers.
- Primordial black holes are not known to exist; the paper is about what we would see if they do.
