Esports players sit for hours, and their wrists do small repeated movements all the while. A 2025 study followed 32 competitive players through two long gaming sessions and used surface electromyography (EMG, the electrical signal of a muscle) to see whether their wrist extensors tired. The sign used is the **median frequency** of the EMG spectrum, which drifts downwards as a muscle fatigues. The reported drop is small: about 3.7 %, or 3.65 Hz. This article asks a practical question the paper's group averages can't answer on their own: how noisy is one median-frequency reading, and what does it take to see a change that small? For the signal-processing side see [[emg-analyser]], [[notch-filter-psd]] and [[fft-workbench]].

## The paper in brief

Thirty-two healthy men played two competitive sessions of 90 to 120 minutes each, with a **10 minute break** between them. Electrodes on the **wrist extensors** and the upper trapezius recorded EMG at **2000 Hz**, filtered from 4 to 450 Hz with a 50 Hz notch. The team computed the **median frequency (MDF)**, the frequency that splits the spectrum's power in half, from a fast Fourier transform over **10 second periods**, and the amplitude (RMS, as a percentage of a maximal voluntary contraction) over the first and last 5 minutes of each game.

The left wrist extensor's MDF fell from **99.24 to 95.59 Hz** (-3.7 %) and the right's from **97.86 to 94.60 Hz** (-3.3 %), both with p below 0.001 and eta-squared (the share of variance explained) of 0.23 and 0.28. The normalised RMS fell too, by about 16 %. The upper trapezius showed no clear change once the post-hoc tests were done. The authors read the falling MDF as progressive wrist extensor fatigue that was not recovered during the break.

## Checked with the tools

The paper gives group means, not signals, so we cannot re-run its analysis. What we can do is ask how well the [[emg-analyser]]'s own window maths could see a shift of this size in a recording like theirs. All the signals below are **made-up EMG** from the tool's built-in generator, set to the paper's 2000 Hz rate and 10 second windows; the spectrum shape is **our assumption**, a smooth one-pole shape.

**Step 1: tune the made-up muscle to the paper's numbers.** The generator has a spectral corner frequency that sets how much power sits at high frequencies. Averaging 40 independent 10 second windows per setting, the tool's median frequency rises almost in step with that corner. Bisecting to the paper's left extensor values gives a corner of **87.6 Hz for the start (MDF 99.2 Hz)** and **81.7 Hz for the end (95.6 Hz)**.

**Step 2: how noisy is one window?** We then drew 100 new independent windows at each state, measured with a 512-sample Welch segment (0.256 s) over the band 20 to 450 Hz. The windows' median frequencies scatter with a standard deviation of **2.5 Hz at the start and 2.3 Hz at the end**, against a true shift of 3.65 Hz. The mean difference of our 100 windows is 3.9 Hz, so the average is right, but a single late window reads higher than a single early one **13 % of the time**.

**Step 3: how much averaging is needed.** To bring the standard error of the difference down to a quarter of the shift, each state needs about **14 windows** of 10 seconds, or 2.3 minutes of signal. The paper analysed the first and last 5 minutes of each game, which is 30 windows per period, comfortably more than that, so a significant group result is no surprise. A single 10 second reading from one player would not have been.

**Step 4: the segment length matters as much as the effect.** The same made-up signal, measured with different Welch segment lengths, gives mean median frequencies of **96.95 Hz (256 samples), 99.24 Hz (512), 99.81 Hz (1024) and 100.18 Hz (2048)**. The choice of segment moves the reading by up to 3.2 Hz, nearly the size of the whole reported fatigue effect. Comparisons are safe only when the same settings are used throughout; the absolute value of an MDF is not comparable between papers unless the method matches.

| Check | Paper says | Tool gives |
|---|---|---|
| Left extensor MDF, start to end | 99.24 to 95.59 Hz (-3.7 %) | tuned to 99.2 and 95.6 Hz (corner 87.6 and 81.7 Hz) |
| Scatter of one 10 s window | not stated | standard deviation about 2.4 Hz |
| Windows needed per state (SE of difference at a quarter of the shift) | not stated | about 14 |
| Effect of Welch segment length on MDF | not stated | 96.95 to 100.18 Hz for 256 to 2048 samples |

The amplitude result goes the other way from the usual textbook picture, in which EMG amplitude rises with fatigue as more muscle fibres are recruited. In this seated, low-force task the normalised RMS fell by about 16 %. The tool's windows also give RMS, but we did not simulate amplitude: we have no model of how a keyboard-and-mouse task changes it.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/wrist-extensor-median-frequency-esports-emg-welch-window-detectability.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `synthEmg` (the tool's made-up EMG generator) and `fatigue`, which in turn use `welch` and `meanMedian`, all from `src/lib/sci/emg.ts`, the maths behind the [[emg-analyser]]. From the paper: the 2000 Hz sampling rate, the 10 second window, the 50 Hz notch and the left extensor's MDF at the start and end. **Ours:** the one-pole spectral shape, the 20 to 450 Hz band (the generator has a 20 Hz high-pass, so we did not use the paper's 4 Hz edge), the Welch segment of 512 samples and every seed. The signals are **simulated**, never the study's recordings.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/emg-analyser.mjs`. It pins the FFT to a direct DFT, the Welch density to a pure 100 Hz sine (total power A squared over two) and the mean and median frequency to SciPy's spectra, the moving RMS to NumPy, and the fatigue slope to `scipy.stats.linregress` (-1.419 Hz/s on the built-in example). New with this article, it also pins our 10 second windows: the average median frequency over seeds 1 to 10 must stay at 94.85 Hz for a corner of 80 Hz and 101.06 Hz for 90 Hz, and the Welch segment must keep moving the same signal from 92.85 Hz (256 samples) to 96.21 Hz (2048 samples).

## Check it yourself

On the [[emg-analyser]] page:

1. Press **Use the example** to load the 32 second made-up recording.
2. Set *Welch segment* to **256** and read the median frequency slope in the fatigue panel: about **-1.42 Hz/s**.
3. Change *Welch segment* to **128** and then **512**: the slope becomes about **-1.51** and **-1.35 Hz/s**, and the first window's median frequency moves between roughly 140 and 143 Hz. The same signal gives different numbers for different segment lengths.
4. Set *Window length (s)* to **10** (and *Move the window by (s)* to **5**) with *Sample rate (Hz)* at 1000 for the example, to see how few windows a 32 second recording leaves.
5. Press **Download windows (CSV)** to take every window's mean and median frequency into your own spreadsheet and compute the scatter yourself.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a fatigue study.** Estimate the scatter of one window from a pilot recording, then work out how many windows per time point you need for the shift you expect.
- **Report the method with the number.** Give the window length, segment length, band and notch next to every median frequency, since the same signal reads up to 3 Hz differently.
- **Check a filter chain** before comparing sessions: [[notch-filter-psd]] shows what a mains notch does to the spectrum, and [[fft-workbench]] lets you look at the full spectrum of a recording.
- **Teach why group means can be significant when single readings are not**, with the 13 % flip rate as a concrete example.
- **Follow a fatigue slope within one contraction** by sliding the tool's window across a burst and reading the trend line.

## The fine print

- Our recordings are **simulations** with a smooth one-pole spectrum; real wrist EMG has crosstalk, movement artefacts and subject variation that would add to the scatter.
- We used a 20 to 450 Hz band, not the paper's 4 to 450 Hz, and one Welch segment length; the paper's FFT settings may give a different level of noise.
- The paper's effect sizes are for 32 players averaged; the scatter between players is not in our model and is not given in the paper's text.
- This article is about the maths of a published measurement. It is general education about signal processing, not advice about posture, training or health.
