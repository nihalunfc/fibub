Proton therapy works because a proton stops. It drops most of its energy in the last few millimetres of its path, a sharp rise called the **Bragg peak**, and then deposits nothing beyond it. So the single most important number for a proton beam is how deep it goes in water. A 2025 paper in *Applied Sciences* built a GEANT4 model of the proton beams from the cyclotron at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital in Bangkok and checked it against measurements in a water tank. Here we ask a simpler question: how close does a one-line textbook formula, the Bethe-Bloch stopping power behind the [[stopping-power]] tool, get to the same measured depths? To go further, find the protons' speed and momentum with the [[relativistic-kinematics]] calculator and carry measurement errors through with the [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The hospital's cyclotron gives protons of 70 to 220 MeV. The team measured how the dose changes with depth in water at 70, 100, 150 and 220 MeV and then tuned a GEANT4 simulation (a Monte Carlo code that follows each proton and its collisions) until it matched. Two depths summarise each curve: the depth of the **Bragg peak**, and **R80**, the depth beyond the peak where the dose has fallen to 80 % of its maximum. R80 is the standard way to quote a clinical beam's range.

Measured R80 rose from **40.5 mm** at 70 MeV to **305.4 mm** at 220 MeV. The simulation matched every measured R80 within about 1.2 mm once each beam was given an **energy spread** (how much the protons' energies differ from the nominal value): 1.5 % at 70 MeV, falling to 0.5 % at 220 MeV. The authors also showed why spread matters: at 220 MeV, going from no spread to 5 % moved the simulated Bragg peak 23 mm shallower (303.4 to 280.0 mm), while R80 moved only 3 mm (306.6 to 303.4 mm). That is why physicists quote R80 and not the peak.

## Checked with the tools

The Stopping power tool works out how fast a proton loses energy at each speed (the Bethe-Bloch formula) and adds up the path until it stops. That total is the **CSDA range**, for "continuous slowing-down approximation": it ignores the random spread of individual tracks and nuclear collisions. For a mono-energetic beam, R80 sits very close to this mean range, so it is a fair comparison. This is a **recomputation** from the four energies in the paper, with the tool's default water (mean excitation energy I = 75 eV).

**How sure is that match?** The biggest unknown in the formula is the **mean excitation energy** I of water, an average binding energy of its electrons. The tool uses 75 eV, the long-standing value. The newer recommended value is 78 eV (our comparison). With 78 eV, every range comes out longer: 41.0, 77.5, 158.5 and 306.9 mm, or 0.5 to 1.5 mm past the measured R80. So the closeness of the 75 eV match is partly luck in the choice of I, and a 1 mm agreement is about as good as this simple formula can honestly claim. It is still a strong independent check that the hospital's measured ranges are sound.

What the tool does not reproduce is the paper's second finding, the effect of energy spread on the peak. That needs the full spread of proton energies and straggling, which is what the GEANT4 model is for.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/proton-beam-range-water-r80-bethe-bloch-csda-geant4-cyclotron.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `range` (the CSDA range by integrating the inverse stopping power), `stopping` (Bethe-Bloch) and `braggCurve` (energy loss against depth for one proton) from `src/lib/sci/phstop.ts`, the maths behind the [[stopping-power]] tool.

The four beam energies and the measured peak and R80 depths come from the paper's text. The water properties are the tool's defaults (Z/A 0.55509, I 75 eV, 1 g/cm³); the 78 eV run is our comparison. The curves in Figure 2 are single-proton curves, not the measured depth-dose curves.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/stopping-power.mjs`, which pins the tool against an independent Python implementation using SciPy's adaptive integration:

- The mass stopping power of 100 MeV protons in water must be 7.2904 MeV cm²/g, and their CSDA range 7.711 g/cm².
- The ranges at 10 and 250 MeV must be 0.1213 and 37.93 g/cm², and a 100 MeV proton must leave 5 g/cm² of water with 55.82 MeV (SciPy root-finding).
- New with this article: the ranges at 70, 150 and 220 MeV must stay at 40.74, 157.67 and 305.44 mm, and setting I to 78 eV must give 306.94 mm at 220 MeV.

## Check it yourself

On the [[stopping-power]] tool, in the *Particle and material* card:

1. Leave *Particle* on **Proton** and *Material* on **Water**, type **220** in *Kinetic energy* with *Energy unit* **MeV (total)**.
2. The result reads **Range 30.54 cm of water (30.54 g/cm²)**, the paper's measured R80 of 305.4 mm. *Mass stopping power* shows **4.229 MeV cm²/g** and *Speed* β = **0.5863**.
3. Type **30** in *Thickness to cross* with *Thickness unit* **cm**: the proton still has **22.97 MeV** left, about 5 mm before the end of its range.
4. Change *Kinetic energy* to **70**: the range falls to **4.074 cm**, against a measured R80 of 40.5 mm.
5. Back at 220 MeV, change *Mean excitation energy I (eV)* to **78**: the range grows to **30.69 cm**. That 1.5 mm is the size of the uncertainty in the physics itself.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a commissioning table**: a measured R80 more than a millimetre or two from the CSDA range at the same energy is worth a second look, with the [[stopping-power]] tool.
- **See how much I matters** for your phantom material: type a custom Z/A, I and density (PMMA, solid water) and compare ranges.
- **Plan a range shifter or degrader**: use *Thickness to cross* to find the energy left after a slab of PMMA or aluminium.
- **Teach the Bragg peak**: plot pristine curves for several energies and show why the dose stops so sharply, then explain straggling as the reason real peaks are wider.
- **Get the beam's speed and momentum** for magnet and detector work with the [[relativistic-kinematics]] calculator.

## The fine print

- The CSDA range is a mean path length. R80 is close to it for a narrow, single-energy beam, but the two are not the same thing, and energy spread nudges R80 by a fraction of a millimetre.
- The tool's Bethe-Bloch formula leaves out shell, density-effect and Barkas corrections. They are small at these energies, but they matter more for slow protons and heavy ions.
- The tool's rounded straggling option is not used here; its shape is rough, and the paper's spread study needs a Monte Carlo code.
- For study and checking only. Treatment planning needs validated clinical software.
