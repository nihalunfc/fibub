A daily maximum sounds simple: the highest temperature of the day. But which day? Midnight to midnight where the thermometer is, or midnight to midnight in universal time (UTC)? A station in Argentina that reports "the highest of the last 24 hours" at 12:00 UTC is describing 09:00 to 09:00 local time, and the label on the record can end up a day out. A 2026 paper in *Earth System Science Data* builds a global daily temperature dataset on a strict 00:00 to 24:00 UTC day and measures how much the old habits mattered. We show the same effect with a simulated station and the maths of the [[station-data-viewer]], so that you can test your own logger file. For a home log, the [[weather-log]] gives the daily mean as (max + min) / 2 in the same way, and monthly climate diagrams come from the [[walter-lieth]] tool.

## The paper in brief

The authors start from hourly and synoptic reports in the Integrated Surface Database, split the 6, 12 and 24 hour maxima and minima into smaller windows, and recombine them into daily values for a 00:00 to 24:00 UTC day. That raises the number of daily Tmax values by 64 per cent and Tmin by 45 per cent, and the dataset covers about 17,000 sites over 1981 to 2024. Daily average temperature is taken as the mean of four 6-hourly values. They compare it with the widely used GSOD summaries and find that GSOD Tmax is on average about 0.3 degrees warmer, Tmin 0.3 degrees cooler and Tave 0.1 degrees cooler. One cause is that GSOD counts a value taken exactly at a UTC day boundary in both days, which adds +0.28 degrees to Tmax and -0.85 degrees to Tmin across millions of records. At Villa Reynolds, Argentina, in 2022, the GSOD Tmax was 1.6 degrees too warm; the 12:00 UTC "24 hour maximum" is the 09:00 local report of the previous day. After shifting the date forward by a day the gap fell to 0.1 degrees and R squared rose from 0.87 to 0.99.

## Checked with the tools

We cannot use the station data, so we built a station that behaves like Villa Reynolds. **Our assumptions:** a station on UTC-3, hourly temperature for 120 days, a diurnal cycle with maximum at 15:00 and minimum at 06:00 local time and a sharp afternoon peak, and slowly changing weather (a random series whose day-to-day correlation is 0.93, the value that gives R squared = 0.87 for a one-day mismatch: 0.93 squared is 0.87). We then asked the Weather station tool's summary maths for the min, mean and max of every calendar day under different labelling.

**Step 1: the window shifted by hours.** If the file's clock is UTC, the tool's days are UTC days. For our station the UTC day ends at 21:00 local, when the temperature is falling slowly, so the maxima are the same as for the local day, and the minima differ by only 0.09 degrees rms. A UTC boundary does little harm when it falls at a quiet hour; it is large when it falls near the daily extreme (a boundary at dawn splits the minimum).

**Step 2: the window shifted by a day.** The 09:00 to 09:00 local window contains the previous afternoon, so its maximum is the maximum of the previous local day. If you label the window with the date it ends on (the synoptic habit), then compared with the true local day the daily Tmax has an rms difference of 1.54 degrees and R squared of 0.892 in our simulation, close to the paper's 0.87 by our choice of weather correlation. The Tmin is worse: rms 2.19 degrees, because the window's minimum is the next morning's. Label the same window with the date it started on and the Tmax is the local day's Tmax exactly (rms 0.00 degrees, R squared 1.000), while the Tmin still differs by 1.50 degrees rms because the window contains the following dawn, not the day's own. So a date shift can repair Tmax but not Tmin.

**Step 3: what is the daily mean?** The same hourly data give three different "daily means". With our sharp afternoon peak, (Tmax + Tmin) / 2 is 1.04 degrees warmer than the mean of the 24 hourly values, because the day spends more hours cool than warm. The mean of four values at 00, 06, 12 and 18 local time is 0.36 degrees cooler (rms 0.36 degrees) in our cycle; this one depends on the shape of the cycle, and for a pure cosine the 24-hour mean, the four-value mean and (max + min) / 2 are all equal. The paper's Tave difference of 0.1 degrees between datasets is of the same order as these definition effects. Any trend or comparison needs one definition throughout.

| Quantity | Paper says | Our simulation gives |
|---|---|---|
| Window one day late, Tmax | R squared 0.87 before the date shift | R squared 0.892 (we tuned the weather correlation to 0.93) |
| After the forward date shift, Tmax | gap 0.1 degrees, R squared 0.99 | gap 0.00 degrees, R squared 1.000 (no sensor error in ours) |
| Window one day late, Tmin | not given | rms 2.19 degrees; still 1.50 degrees after the shift |
| UTC against local day | boundary matters at some stations | Tmax identical; Tmin rms 0.09 degrees at UTC-3 |
| Daily mean definitions | Tave: mean of four 6-hourly values | (max + min) / 2 is +1.04 degrees; four-value mean -0.36 degrees (our cycle) |

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/daily-tmax-tmin-24-hour-window-utc-boundary-bias-station-data-viewer.mjs` builds hourly records with times in UTC and calls `summarise(records, 'day', step)` from `src/lib/sci/station.ts`, the daily summary maths of the [[station-data-viewer]]. To change the window it shifts all times by a whole number of hours before summarising, which is what you do to a file by editing its clock. The 00:00 to 24:00 UTC day, the UTC-3 offset of the report, the one-day mismatch and R squared of 0.87 come from the paper's text. The temperature cycle, the weather series, its seed, the 120 days and the 12 degree swing are **our assumptions**. No figure or table from the paper is used.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/station-data-viewer.mjs` on every change; it compares calendar-day and calendar-month summaries, gap detection and wind-rose binning with pandas and the windrose package. For this article we added a check that a cosine day (mean 10, amplitude 5, maximum at 15:00) gives a daily max of 15, min of 5 and mean of 10, that shifting its clock by nine hours moves the day boundary so that a 24-record day is still 24 records, and that the two small files in "Check it yourself" give the values below.

## Check it yourself

Save this as `a.csv` (two days of 6-hourly readings):

```
time,temperature
2026-03-01 00:00,8
2026-03-01 06:00,6
2026-03-01 12:00,14
2026-03-01 18:00,12
2026-03-02 00:00,9
2026-03-02 06:00,7
2026-03-02 12:00,16
2026-03-02 18:00,13
```

1. On the [[station-data-viewer]] page, press the open button and choose the file. Keep *Dates are written* on **Year first**.
2. In the daily summary (*By day*), 1 March should show a maximum of **14.0**, a minimum of **6.0** and a mean of **10.0** with all four records; 2 March **16.0**, **7.0** and **11.3** (11.25 before the page rounds to one decimal).
3. Now save a second copy as `b.csv` with every time moved nine hours later (09:00, 15:00, 21:00, then 03:00 the next day, and so on, starting 2026-03-01 09:00). The same eight numbers now give 1 March (three records, mean **9.3**, 75 per cent complete), 2 March (maximum **16.0**, minimum **7.0**, mean **11.0**) and a lone record on 3 March. The numbers did not change; the clock did.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Checking a logger or an old record:** find out whether its day starts at midnight, 09:00 or in UTC, and test how much that moves Tmax and Tmin with the summary table.
- **Merging two data sources:** convert both to the same window and mean definition before computing anomalies.
- **Teaching:** show with one CSV why "the daily maximum" needs a stated 24-hour window.
- **Trend work:** a change of observing time, a well-known cause of fake trends, is easy to mimic by shifting a file's clock.

## The fine print

- Our station is a simulation with a smooth cycle and no noise, so the date shift fixes Tmax perfectly; real data have sensor noise, missed reports and rain days that break the pattern.
- The mean-definition biases depend on the shape of the cycle; yours will differ, so test with your own logger data.
- The tool uses the date and time as written; it does not convert time zones. Convert the clock yourself first.
- We use the paper's examples only as stated in its text; its global comparisons rest on millions of reports that we do not reproduce.
