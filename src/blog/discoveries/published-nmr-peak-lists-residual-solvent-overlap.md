A new compound's claim to exist rests on a few lines of an experimental section: a proton NMR list, a mass, perhaps an elemental analysis. Readers skim those lines, but they are data, and they can be checked. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Chemistry* reports new **sulfoximine and sulfonimidamide analogues of bumetanide** as inhibitors of the NKCC1 chloride transporter, with its best compound reaching an EC₅₀ of 150 nM. This article runs the paper's published ¹H lists through the [[nmr-impurity-checker]] and asks a question the paper does not: which of those signals sit where a residual solvent, water or a column solvent would be, and when is a match worth anything? Pair it with the [[ir-band-finder]] for the functional groups and the [[isotope-pattern-calculator]] for the mass.

## The paper in brief

Bumetanide is a loop diuretic that also blocks NKCC1 in the brain, which makes it interesting for conditions linked to chloride handling in neurons, but it carries a carboxylic acid that keeps it out of the brain. The authors replace that acid with bioisosteres and replace the sulfonamide with a **sulfoximine** or a **sulfonimidamide**, then measure how well each compound blocks NKCC1. Aryl substitution at the sulfonamide position helps most: compound **BA-83** reaches an EC₅₀ of **150 nM**, better than bumetanide, and the potent compounds are selective for NKCC1 over the related NKCC2 and KCC2 transporters at 2 µM. Removing the carboxylic acid at the other position generally costs activity.

Each new compound is backed by a ¹H NMR list in **DMSO-d₆ at 400 MHz**. Two of them were fully available to us, for **BA-01** and **BA-13**; they are our test cases.

## Checked with the tools

The checker holds **72 proton signals** for DMSO-d₆: the solvent's own residual peak, water in it, and 45 common laboratory impurities from ethyl acetate and hexane to silicone grease. It parses a journal-style list (dropping multiplicities and coupling constants in brackets), then does two things: for each peak it lists every known signal within a tolerance, and for each impurity it counts **how many of that compound's signals are present**. The second is the part that matters.

**Step 1: what the published lists contain.** Parsing BA-01's list gives 16 values (each multiplet's two limits counted separately) and BA-13's the same. At the tool's default tolerance of ±0.03 ppm, **9 of BA-01's 16** and **6 of BA-13's 16** values fall within reach of something in the table. Not one impurity has all of its signals present. Neither list reports anything at the **residual DMSO (2.50 ppm)** or **water (3.35 ppm)** positions, as Figure 1 shows: both authors cleaned those out, which is normal practice and the reason you cannot judge dryness from a published list.

**Step 2: the one real warning.** BA-01's methyl singlet at **2.45 ppm (s, 3H)** is **0.05 ppm from the residual DMSO multiplet**: at 400 MHz that is only 20 Hz, so this methyl sits on the shoulder of the solvent peak. Widen the tolerance to ±0.05 and the checker pairs them. The practical consequence is for anyone repeating the work: that methyl cannot be integrated reliably in DMSO-d₆, and a wet or concentrated sample will bury it. It is also 0.00 ppm from triethylamine's CH₂, a reagent common in sulfonamide chemistry, so if a later batch shows extra intensity there, triethylamine is the first thing to rule out (look for its CH₃ at 0.95 ppm, which neither list has).

**Step 3: how much a single match is worth, counted properly.** The 72 signals are not spread thinly. With a ±0.03 ppm window around each, they cover **33 % of the 0 to 10 ppm range**; at ±0.1 ppm they cover **70 %**. So a 16-peak list should expect about **5.3 chance matches at ±0.03** and **11 at ±0.1** even if the sample is perfectly clean. BA-01's 9 and 16 are in that territory. Figure 2 plots both curves. That is why the tool ranks by *how many of an impurity's signals are found*, and why the pyridine, propane and ethanol that appear in the ranked list with one signal each should be ignored.

**Step 4: what it looks like when there really is something.** Take BA-01's values and add the peaks a raw spectrum would show before tidying: residual DMSO at 2.50, water at 3.33, and a trace of ethyl acetate at 4.03, 1.99 and 1.17 (**our own example**, not the paper's data). Now the checker reports **all three ethyl acetate signals present** and names it first, together with the residual solvent and water. That is the difference between a coincidence and a real impurity: a whole set of lines, in the right places, in the right relative sizes.

The same run also shows a trap. The checker also reports "all signals of methanol present", because methanol's CH₃ at 3.15 is near the compound's own CH₂ quartet at 3.12 and its OH at 4.0 is near ethyl acetate's CH₂ at 4.03. Two signals are a weak test; look for a third line, or check the integrals, before believing a two-peak compound.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/published-nmr-peak-lists-residual-solvent-overlap.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `parsePeaks`, `signals` and `match` from `src/lib/sci/nmr.ts`, the maths behind the [[nmr-impurity-checker]]. The chance-match curve is worked out in the same script: the union of the ±tolerance bands around all 72 signals, divided by the 10 ppm window, times the number of peaks in the list.

From the paper: the two ¹H lists, the solvent and the spectrometer frequency. **Ours:** the simulated "untidied" list in step 4, the 0 to 10 ppm window used for the chance calculation, and the choice of tolerances.

**A small bug, found and fixed while writing this.** The parser read a published multiplet range such as "7.55–7.42 (m, 3H)" as 7.55 and **minus** 7.42, because the dash became a minus sign. Since journal lists are full of such ranges, that quietly threw away half the peaks. `parsePeaks` now turns a dash between two digits into a separator, so the range gives both limits, and a check pins it.

## How we test the maths

New with this article, `tests/sci-tools/nmr-impurity-checker.mjs` runs inside `tests/sci.mjs` on every change:

- the DMSO-d₆ table has 72 proton signals, its residual peak at 2.50 and water at 3.35, and CDCl₃'s residual peak is at 7.25 with water at 1.55;
- a journal-style line is parsed to peaks only: `δ 7.26 (d, J = 8.0 Hz, 2H), 3.80 (s, 3H)` gives 7.26 and 3.80, integrals and coupling constants dropped;
- the fixed range case: BA-01's published list gives all 16 values, every one positive, starting 7.78, 7.55, 7.42;
- matching is symmetric and respects the tolerance: at ±0.03 ppm, 9 of BA-01's 16 values match something and no impurity is complete; the 2.45 methyl pairs with residual DMSO only once the tolerance reaches ±0.05;
- with ethyl acetate's three signals added, ethyl acetate comes back 3 of 3 and is ranked first.

## Check it yourself

On the [[nmr-impurity-checker]] page, in the *Check a peak list* card:

1. Set *Solvent* to **DMSO-d₆**, *Nucleus* to **¹H**, and leave *Tolerance (± ppm)* at **0.03**.
2. Paste BA-01's published list into the peaks box, brackets and all: `7.78 (d, J = 2.0 Hz, 1H), 7.55–7.42 (m, 3H), 7.28 (dd, J = 8.7, 7.3 Hz, 2H), 7.09–6.98 (m, 1H), 6.93–6.84 (m, 2H), 5.30 (t, J = 5.7 Hz, 1H), 3.12 (q, J = 6.6 Hz, 2H), 2.45 (s, 3H), 1.49–1.33 (m, 2H), 1.21–1.05 (m, 2H), 0.79 (t, J = 7.3 Hz, 3H)`.
3. The headline reads **9 of 16 peaks match a solvent or impurity signal; possible: ethane, pyridine, propane**. In *Likely impurities*, nothing is complete: pyridine shows 2 of 3, everything else 1 of 2 or 1 of 3. In *Peak by peak*, seven values come back **no match: likely your compound**.
4. Change *Tolerance* to **0.05**. Now **10 of 16** match, the list grows to 15 compounds, and 2.45 picks up **DMSO-d₆ (residual solvent) 2.50** beside triethylamine's CH₂. Watch how little the extra 0.02 ppm buys you and how much noise it adds.
5. Back at **0.03**, add the peaks a raw spectrum would have: paste `4.03, 3.33, 2.50, 1.99, 1.17` on a new line. The headline becomes **14 of 21 peaks match ...; all signals of ethyl acetate, methanol are present**, and ethyl acetate tops the table with 3 of 3.
6. In the second card, *Impurity table for this solvent*, type **3.3** in *Find* to see everything that sits near the water peak in DMSO-d₆, so you can tell water from a glycol or a dioxane trace.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit your own experimental section** before submission: paste each list, and check that nothing you assigned to the compound sits on a residual solvent or water peak.
- **Spot a leftover solvent properly**, by whether *all* of its signals are there, not by one coincidence; the tool lists the missing ones to look for.
- **Pick the right solvent for a crowded region**: a methyl at 2.45 ppm is unreadable in DMSO-d₆ but clear in CDCl₃, where the residual peak is at 7.25.
- **Set a defensible tolerance**: ±0.02 to ±0.03 ppm for ¹H; above ±0.08 the table covers so much of the spectrum that a match means nothing.
- **Reuse a published list as a reference** when you repeat a synthesis: any new peak that the checker names as a known impurity is probably work-up, not a new product.

## The fine print

- We check the **characterisation data**, not the biology. Nothing here tests the EC₅₀ values, the selectivity or the paper's conclusions about NKCC1.
- Only two full ¹H lists were available to us; the article page served part of the experimental section, so later compounds are not covered.
- Impurity positions are approximate (rounded to 0.05 ppm) and real peaks move with concentration, temperature and water content. OH and NH signals move a lot, so a miss there means little.
- A peak list is not a spectrum. Without integrals and line shapes the checker cannot tell a 1 % impurity from a 10 % one, and a published list has usually had its solvent peaks removed on purpose.
- The "untidied" list in step 4 is **our own construction** to show the tool working, not data from the paper.
