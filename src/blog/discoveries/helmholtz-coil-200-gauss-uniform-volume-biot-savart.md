Treating food or biomass with a steady magnetic field only means anything if the field is the same everywhere in the sample. A 2025 paper in *AgriEngineering* builds a coil device for exactly that job — a uniform field over a 20 × 20 × 10 cm tray of fermenting biomass — and reports a measured **142.77 gauss** with a spread of only **±1.32 %**. The interesting part for anyone designing such a rig is the geometry: how big must the coils be, and how many turns, before a given volume is uniform? That is a Biot-Savart calculation anyone can run in a browser with the [[coil-field-designer]], and the companion steps are covered by the [[field-line-plotter]] for the picture and the [[magnetic-circuit]] solver if iron gets involved.

## The paper in brief

The authors wanted 200 gauss (20 mT) spread evenly through a sample space of 20 × 20 × 10 cm, from a supply of 300 V and 8 A, keeping the current density in the wire under 5 A/mm² so the coils survive without cooling. They simulated four arrangements before building one:

- a **ring-coil stack**: eight coils of 135 turns, in a column 20 cm high and 15 cm across, at 4.3 A. Their simulation put 200 G in a central region about 7 × 10 cm, falling to roughly 50 G outside it;
- a **double-square** pair (two 441-turn square coils, 13 cm apart, 12 A), a **triangular** three-coil set and a **hexagonal** six-coil set, each uniform over a larger box.

The built rig is rated 12 A and was run at 70 % of that, 8.4 A. Measured at 27 points, it averaged **142.77 G** (error 1.88 G) with a maximum deviation of **±1.32 %**, against about 140 G predicted. The frame mattered too: steel distorted the field, while an aluminium-profile frame kept 95.2 % homogeneity.

## Checked with the tools

The coil designer works out the field of any set of coaxial circular coils exactly, from the closed form of the Biot-Savart integral for a circular loop. Three things in the paper can be checked against it.

**1. Does the eight-coil stack really make 200 gauss?** Taking the paper's stack as eight 135-turn coils of 7.5 cm radius, evenly spread over 20 cm, each at 4.3 A, the tool gives **213.8 G at the centre** — 7 % above the paper's "about 200 G", which is as close as a thin-loop model should get to a finite-element model of real windings. The field stays above 200 G within **±4.96 cm** of the centre along the axis and out to **6.4 cm** off the axis, and falls to **50 G at 16.6 cm**. The paper describes a 200 G region "about 7 × 10 cm" falling to about 50 G outside: the 10 cm length and the 50 G fall-off both come out of the recomputation (Figure 1).

**2. The measured field scales as it should.** 142.77 G at 8.4 A is 17.0 G per amp, so the full 12 A gives **204.0 G** — the design target, reached by a straight-line extrapolation, and 200 G exactly at **11.77 A**. Nothing saturates, because there is no iron: field and current stay proportional.

**3. How big would a plain Helmholtz pair have to be?** This is where the paper's choice of a multi-coil design is justified in numbers. The uniform region of a coil set scales with the coil radius: the tool finds the sphere that stays within 1 % of the centre field is about **0.31 R** for a Helmholtz pair and **0.43 R** for a Maxwell three-coil set (Figure 2). The paper's 20 × 20 × 10 cm sample space has a half-diagonal of 15.0 cm, so to hold the whole tray to 1 % you need

| Coil set | Radius needed | Turns per coil at 12 A for 200 G | Wire | Uniform sphere |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Helmholtz pair | 48 cm | 890 | 5.4 km | radius 15.0 cm |
| Maxwell three-coil | 35 cm | 495 (centre coil) | 2.3 km | radius 15.0 cm |

A two-coil rig would have to be nearly a metre across and swallow five kilometres of wire; the three-coil version does the same job at 35 cm radius with **56 % less wire**. That is the same conclusion the paper reaches experimentally when it moves from two square coils to three- and six-coil sets, and the tool reaches it in a few seconds (Figure 3).

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/helmholtz-coil-200-gauss-uniform-volume-biot-savart.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `loops`, `fieldAt`, `uniformSphere`, `preset`, `helmholtzCentre` and `wireLength` from `src/lib/sci/ph-coil.ts`, the maths behind the [[coil-field-designer]]. `fieldAt` adds up the exact loop field (complete elliptic integrals K and E by the arithmetic-geometric mean), `uniformSphere` grows a sphere around the centre until the **field vector** anywhere on it differs by more than the tolerance, and `preset` builds the classical Helmholtz and Maxwell geometries.

Inputs from the paper: the eight coils, 135 turns, 4.3 A, 7.5 cm radius, 20 cm of stack, the 20 × 20 × 10 cm sample space, the 200 G target, 12 A maximum and the 142.77 G at 8.4 A. **Our assumptions:** that the eight coils are evenly spaced over the full 20 cm and are circular (the paper's later sets are square), and that "15 cm in diameter" is the coil diameter, so R = 7.5 cm. Round coils of the same size give a slightly stronger and rounder field than square ones; the point here is the scale, not the last per cent.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/coil-field-designer.mjs` on every change to fibub. It pins these same functions against independent references:

- K(m) and E(m) must match SciPy's `ellipk` and `ellipe` to 13 figures; three off-axis loop fields must match a direct numerical Biot-Savart integral to nine figures; and on the axis the loop must give the textbook μ₀NIa²/2(a²+z²)^1.5 exactly.
- A 10 cm, 100-turn Helmholtz pair at 1 A must give **0.8992 mT** at its centre, with zero gradient there, a 1 % half-length of **31.4 mm** (that is the 0.31 R used above) and a 0.1 % half-length of 17.3 mm, each pinned against SciPy's root finder.
- The Maxwell three-coil set's deviation must be −2.01 × 10⁻⁶ at 0.1 R and its uniform length at least 1.5 times the pair's; the anti-Helmholtz pair must give zero field and the closed-form gradient at its centre; and ∇·B must vanish off the axis.
- New with this article: the paper's eight-coil stack (eight 135-turn coils of 7.5 cm radius spread over 20 cm at 4.3 A) must give 213.8 G at the centre and fall to 50 G at 16.63 cm, and a 48 cm Helmholtz pair of 890 turns at 12 A must give 200.1 G uniform to 1 % in a sphere of radius 15.0 cm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[coil-field-designer]] page:

1. Set *Coil set* to **Custom (type the coils)** and paste the paper's stack into the box, one coil per line (radius cm, position cm, turns, current A):
   **7.5, -10, 135, 4.3** / **7.5, -7.14, 135, 4.3** / **7.5, -4.29, 135, 4.3** / **7.5, -1.43, 135, 4.3** / **7.5, 1.43, 135, 4.3** / **7.5, 4.29, 135, 4.3** / **7.5, 7.14, 135, 4.3** / **7.5, 10, 135, 4.3**.
2. The headline reads **B = 21.38 mT (213.8 gauss) at the centre**, and *Uniform to 1 % in a sphere* reads **radius 2.03 cm**: strong but tight, exactly the trade-off the paper ran into.
3. Set *Probe z (cm along the axis)* to **5** and read **19.98 mT**, "−6.56 % from the centre" — the edge of the paper's 200 G region.
4. Now switch *Coil set* to **Helmholtz pair (uniform field)**, with *Coil radius R (cm)* **48**, *Turns per coil N* **890** and *Current I (A)* **12**. You get **20.01 mT (200.1 gauss)**, *Uniform to 1 % in a sphere* **radius 15 cm**, and *Wire length* **5.368 km**.
5. Change *Coil set* to **Maxwell three-coil (more uniform)** with *Coil radius R (cm)* **35** and *Turns per coil N* **495**: **19.99 mT**, the same 15 cm uniform sphere, and *Wire length* **2.349 km**.
6. Put **20** in *Target field at the centre (mT, optional)* to have the tool read back the current any coil set needs for 200 gauss.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Size a coil rig before buying wire.** The uniform sphere is 0.31 R for two coils and 0.43 R for three; work backwards from the sample you must treat.
- **Decide between two coils and three.** Same uniformity, a third less radius and half the copper, as the table above shows.
- **Check a reported field.** Centre field, ampere-turns and current are all proportional with no iron, so one number fixes the rest.
- **Place the probe honestly.** The tool's probe field gives the percentage difference from the centre at any point, which is what a homogeneity claim like "±1.32 %" has to be measured against.
- **Plan a treatment dose.** With field per amp known, a schedule like "40 G for 4 hours" becomes a current setting, not a power percentage.

## The fine print

- The paper's built rig uses **square** coils; the tool models **circular** coaxial coils. We use it on the paper's own ring-coil arrangement, where circles are the right shape, and for sizing the classical round alternatives.
- The 20 cm stack spacing and the 7.5 cm radius are read from the paper's description; exact winding widths and depths are not stated, and a real winding spreads the field slightly.
- Our Helmholtz and Maxwell designs ignore wire gauge, voltage and heating; at 890 turns and 12 A you also have to check the current density and the supply, which the tool reports but which we did not optimise.
- The paper's magnetic-treatment results for fermentation are a biology claim, not something a field calculator can check. Only the coil physics is checked here.
- Iron or steel nearby changes everything, as the paper's own steel-frame experiment shows; the thin-loop model assumes no magnetic material at all.
