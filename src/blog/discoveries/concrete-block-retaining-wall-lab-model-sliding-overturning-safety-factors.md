Retaining walls fail in three classic ways: they slide forward, tip over about their toe, or press their base into soft ground. A 2021 conference paper from Indonesia built a small glass-sided test box, stacked concrete blocks without mortar into a wall 40 cm high, backfilled it with dry sand and worked out the safety factors by hand. The wall came out below the usual targets, and the sand behind it did move in the tests. The paper prints enough numbers (sand strength, block weight, thrust, moments) that the whole hand calculation can be redone in a browser with the [[retaining-wall-check|retaining wall stability tool]], which is what this article does. The ground under a wall is a job for [[bearing-capacity]], and a slope above or below it for [[slope-stability]].

## The paper in brief

The authors tested a model wall of 11 concrete blocks, each 9.9 cm long and 5 cm square in section, with no mortar, reinforcement or connectors, in a box 40 cm long, 10 cm wide and 80 cm high. The backfill was clean sand (about 96 % sand by sieve analysis) with **no cohesion** and a friction angle of **31.7°**. From these they worked out an active earth pressure coefficient of **0.311** and an active thrust of **365.74** (the paper does not give units; we read them as grams of force per centimetre of wall length).

They then compared the wall with two targets: a **sliding** factor of at least **1.5** and an **overturning** factor of at least **2.5**. They report **1.05** and **2.35**, plus an overall factor of 1.16 from a slice method, and conclude that the wall needs bigger dimensions. They also fit a curved, S-shaped failure line to the sand surface they observed. This article checks the first part, the hand-calculated safety factors.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the thrust is reproduced exactly.** The Rankine coefficient for a friction angle of 31.699° is `tan²(45° − 15.85°)` = **0.31108**, which the tool gives and the paper rounds to 0.311. With the paper's own rounded coefficient, unit weight 1.47 g/cm³ and height 40 cm, the thrust `½ Ka γ H²` is **365.74**, the paper's number to the last digit. With the unrounded coefficient it is 365.83, 0.03 % higher. The overturning moment is the thrust times H/3: 365.74 × 13.33 = 4,876, against the paper's 4,875.26.

**Step 2: the sliding factor follows from one friction coefficient.** Sliding resistance is the base friction coefficient times the vertical load. Working backwards from the paper's 1.05, 990.75 and 365.74 gives a coefficient of **0.388**. That is `tan(21.13°)`, which is two thirds of the sand's friction angle, a common rule of thumb for soil on a rough base. We use that value (**our assumption**, since the paper states no coefficient), and it returns a sliding factor of 1.047.

**Step 3: what the paper's vertical load means.** A vertical load of 990.75 on a block wall of unit weight 2.20 g/cm³ and height 40 cm corresponds to a plain rectangular wall **11.26 cm** wide. The tool, run for that rectangle, gives sliding **1.047** (the paper: 1.05) and overturning **1.143**. The paper's overturning factor is **2.35**, double ours. Its resisting moment, 11,469, divided by its vertical load gives a lever arm of 11.6 cm from the toe, which is more than the whole width of the wall, so the paper's resisting moment cannot come from the weight of an 11 cm wall alone. The paper does not say what else was counted (the weight of sand over the blocks, or a different toe point, are possibilities we cannot check). Whatever the cause, **a plain 11 cm wall passes neither target**, and on the paper's own numbers the sliding factor is the one furthest short.

**Step 4: how wide would it have to be?** The tool can search widths. A plain rectangular block wall needs about **16.1 cm** to reach a sliding factor of 1.5 and about **16.6 cm** to reach an overturning factor of 2.5, so roughly **17 cm** passes both (sliding 1.55, overturning 2.51 at 16.7 cm). That is one and a half to two blocks laid lengthways instead of one. Figure 1 shows both curves: sliding grows in proportion to width, while overturning grows with its square.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/concrete-block-retaining-wall-lab-model-sliding-overturning-safety-factors.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `rankineKa()` and `retainingWall()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-retwall.ts`, the maths behind the [[retaining-wall-check|retaining wall stability tool]]: Rankine pressure, the thrust and its moment, and the sliding and overturning factors per metre of wall.

The friction angle, unit weights, wall height, targets and the paper's reported results come from the paper. The model scale is handled by converting g/cm³ to kN/m³ (× 9.80665) and cm to m. The base friction coefficient (tan of two thirds of φ), the level and dry backfill and the zero base thickness (a plain rectangular wall with no footing) are **our assumptions**. The 11.26 cm equivalent width comes from dividing the paper's vertical load by block weight and height.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/retaining-wall-check.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the tool against an independent Python calculation (a trial-wedge maximisation for Coulomb coefficients, areas and centroids by the shoelace formula):

- The Rankine coefficient at φ = 30° must be exactly 1/3 and Coulomb with zero wall friction must equal it.
- A made-up cantilever wall must give sliding 1.5813 and overturning 3.8557, and a gravity wall with no base projections must give 1.7240 and 2.9740, to nine figures.
- New with this article: Ka at 31.699° must be 0.31108, the thrust with the paper's rounded values must be 365.74, and the 11.26 cm and 16.7 cm block walls must give sliding 1.0463 and 1.5518 and overturning 1.1431 and 2.5145.

## Check it yourself

On the [[retaining-wall-check|retaining wall stability tool]]:

1. Set *Earth pressure* to **Rankine**. Type *Height H, base underside to top (m)* **0.4**, *Stem thickness at top (m)* **0.1126** and *Stem thickness at bottom (m)* **0.1126**.
2. Set *Base thickness (m)*, *Toe length (m)* and *Heel length (m)* all to **0**.
3. Type *Wall unit weight (kN/m³)* **21.57**, *Soil unit weight (kN/m³)* **14.42**, *Friction angle φ (°)* **31.699**, *Backfill slope β (°)* **0**, *Surcharge on the backfill (kPa)* **0** and *Base friction coefficient μ* **0.3865**.
4. You should see an active coefficient of **0.3111**, a sliding factor of about **1.05** and an overturning factor of about **1.14**.
5. Now set both stem thicknesses to **0.167**. Sliding becomes about **1.55** and overturning about **2.51**: both of the paper's targets are met.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a hand calculation** in a minute: reproduce the thrust and each moment, and see which term does not match before trusting a safety factor.
- **Size a model wall before building it:** sweep width or backfill friction angle and choose a model that will stand or fail on purpose, as the experiment requires.
- **Teach the two failure modes:** sliding grows with width, overturning with its square, so a wide, low wall and a tall, thin one fail differently.
- **Test the sand first** with [[soil-classification]], because the friction angle drives everything here.
- **Check the ground under the base** with [[bearing-capacity]] once the wall passes sliding and overturning.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated numbers, not a re-test of the wall. We never saw the blocks or the sand.
- The paper gives its forces without units, and its text and tables disagree in places (the model wall is 40 cm high but 11 blocks of 5 cm would stand 55 cm; the sand fraction is 96.067 % in one place and 96.096 % in another). We took the 40 cm height, which fits the thrust and moment it reports.
- The paper's overturning factor of 2.35 could not be reproduced from a plain 11.26 cm rectangle. We do not know what extra resisting moment the authors counted, so this article does not say their value is wrong, only that it needs more weight or lever arm than the wall's blocks alone.
- A dry-stacked block wall and the tool's solid wall are not identical: real blocks can slip against one another, which the tool does not model.
- Targets such as 1.5 and 2.5 are conventions; local codes govern real walls. Check the official source, and use a qualified engineer for anything built.
