Automatic design is spreading into mechanical CAD: describe the duty, let a search algorithm pick the numbers, and get a model back. A 2026 paper in *Machines* does exactly that for a gearbox, using an evolutionary algorithm for the gear pair and a multi-objective genetic algorithm for the bearings, all driven from inside the CAD system. Its worked case is a one-stage box taking **70 kW at 330 rpm** with a ratio of **4**, and the algorithm answers: **19 and 76 teeth, module 1.94 rounded to 2 mm, 58 mm wide**. This article checks that answer with the [[gear-train-calculator|Gear trains tool]], which does the same arithmetic in a browser in no time at all. For what comes after the gears, see [[shaft-bearing-life]] and [[bolt-torque-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The paper's point is the workflow, not the gearbox: a parametric CAD model, an evolutionary algorithm that dimensions the gear pair against the duty, then a genetic algorithm that picks bearings from a catalogue with several objectives at once. The search was allowed tooth counts from **17 to 150** and face widths from **10 to 100 mm**, with a lower limit of 0.3 mm on the module. For the case study it returned a single-stage pair of 19 and 76 teeth, said the optimum module was 1.94 mm and rounded it to 2 mm ("a 3 % change does not play a significant role in a preliminary design"), set the gear width at 58 mm, and went on to choose an SKF bearing.

## Checked with the tools

The Gear trains tool takes an input speed, an input torque, an optional module, and a list of meshes with their tooth counts, type and efficiency. It returns the ratio, the speed, torque and direction of every shaft, and each mesh's centre distance. The paper gives power, not torque, so the first step is one division: 70 kW at 330 rpm is **2025.6 N·m** (the [[unit-converter|engineering unit converter]] does it if you prefer).

| Check | Paper | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Ratio from 19 and 76 teeth | 4 ("index ratio") | **4.000** exactly |
| Output speed | not stated | 82.5 rpm, reversed |
| Output torque, lossless | not stated | 8102.4 N·m |
| Output torque at 98 % mesh efficiency | not stated | 7940.4 N·m |
| Centre distance at module 2 | not stated | 95.0 mm (92.15 mm at 1.94) |
| Pitch diameters | not stated | 38 and 152 mm |

So the kinematics check out: 76 ÷ 19 is exactly 4, and the rounding of the module from 1.94 to 2 mm moves the centre distance from 92.15 to 95.0 mm, a 3 % change as the paper says.

**What the search space really looks like.** The tool's ratio finder asks for a target ratio and the tooth-count limits and lists the pairs that come closest. For a ratio of exactly 4 between 17 and 150 teeth there are **21** pairs that hit it dead on — 17/68, 18/72, 19/76, and so on up to 37/148 — so the algorithm was not choosing between good and bad ratios at all. It was choosing a size. Figure 1 draws that choice: the centre distance of every exact pair at three modules.

Why 19 and not 17, the smallest allowed? Because 17 is the classical limit below which a 20° full-depth pinion starts to undercut, and designers keep a tooth or two in hand. Why not 30, which would share the load over more teeth? Because the box grows: 30/120 at module 2 needs a 150 mm centre distance instead of 95 mm.

**Where the numbers start to argue.** The tool reports the torque, and the torque makes the module look small. At 2025.6 N·m on a 38 mm pitch circle, the tangential force on the tooth is **106.6 kN**, which over the 58 mm face width is **1838 N per millimetre** of tooth. Put that into the Lewis bending equation with the standard form factor for 19 teeth (about 0.314) and the tooth stress comes out near **2900 MPa** — an order of magnitude past what any gear steel will take. For a 200 MPa allowable, the same duty needs a module of about **7.6 mm** and a centre distance near **363 mm** rather than 95 mm; even a bold 400 MPa still asks for 5.4 mm. Figure 2 shows the curve. The pitch-line speed, for the record, is a gentle 0.66 m/s, so this is not a dynamic-load effect.

That strength check is **ours, not the tool's** (the Gear trains tool does kinematics, torques and geometry, not tooth stress), and we cannot see the paper's own strength constraint. But it is the kind of check that takes thirty seconds and would catch a units slip or a missing constraint in an automated pipeline before it reaches a CAD model.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/gearbox-tooth-numbers-ratio-four-automatic-dimensioning.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `train` (shaft speeds, torques and directions through a list of meshes), `centreDistance` and `findRatios` (the exhaustive search for tooth pairs hitting a target ratio) from `src/lib/sci/eng-gear.ts`, the maths behind the [[gear-train-calculator|Gear trains tool]].

From the paper: 70 kW, 330 rpm, the ratio of 4, the 19 and 76 teeth, the modules 1.94 and 2 mm, the 58 mm width and the 17-to-150 tooth limits. **Our assumptions:** the 98 % mesh efficiency in the torque row, and in Figure 2 the Lewis form factor of 0.314 and the 200 MPa allowable bending stress. Nothing here is simulated — it is all closed-form arithmetic on the paper's own numbers.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/gear-train-calculator.mjs`, which pins this arithmetic:

- A three-mesh compound train must give the product of its tooth ratios, with the torque rising by the same factor and the direction flipping once per external mesh; an internal mesh must not flip it.
- The belt formulas must agree both ways: a belt length worked out from a centre distance must give that centre distance back.
- The planetary panel must satisfy Willis's equation and the sun, ring and carrier torques must sum to zero.
- New with this article: 19 and 76 teeth at 330 rpm and 2025.6 N·m must give exactly a ratio of 4, 82.5 rpm and 8102.4 N·m with a reversed output, 7940.4 N·m at 98 % efficiency, a 95 mm centre distance at module 2, and the ratio search between 17 and 150 teeth must return 17/68 first and 21 exact pairs in all.

## Check it yourself

On the [[gear-train-calculator|Gear trains tool]] page:

1. Set *Input speed (rpm)* to **330**, *Input torque (N·m)* to **2025.6** and *Module (mm, optional)* to **2**.
2. In the first mesh, set *Driver teeth* to **19**, *Driven teeth* to **76**, *Type* to **External** and *Efficiency (%)* to **100**.
3. The shaft table shows the output at **82.5 rpm** and **8102.4 N·m**, turning the other way, with a ratio of **4** and a centre distance of **95 mm**.
4. Change *Efficiency (%)* to **98**: the output torque falls to **7940.4 N·m**. Change *Module* to **1.94**: the centre distance becomes **92.15 mm**.
5. In the ratio finder, put **4** in *Ratio I want*, choose **1 pair**, set *Fewest teeth on any gear* to **17** and *Most teeth on any gear* to **150**. It lists five exact hits beginning 17/68, 18/72, 19/76 — the paper's pair is the third.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit an optimiser's output** in seconds: ratio, shaft speeds, torques and centre distance from the tooth counts, before trusting a generated CAD model.
- **See the whole design space** for a target ratio inside tooth-count limits, which turns "the algorithm chose 19/76" into "19/76 is one of 21 exact options, and here is what each costs in size".
- **Convert duty to load.** The torque the tool reports is the input every strength, shaft and bearing check needs; take it on to [[shaft-bearing-life]].
- **Teach gear trains** with a real published case rather than a textbook exercise: the ratio is exact, the sizes are plausible, and the module invites an argument.
- **Check compound alternatives**: the same finder, set to two pairs, offers split-ratio options that keep the gears small.

## The fine print

- The paper states the module, tooth counts, width, power, speed and ratio in its text; its tables are not reproduced here, and we could not read its own strength constraint or its bearing results in full.
- The output speeds, torques and centre distances are not in the paper. They follow from its numbers; nothing is being contradicted by them.
- The Lewis check is a first-order screen with a constant form factor, no dynamic, size or load-distribution factors and no surface-durability check. Treat it as a smell test, not a verdict: a real design uses ISO 6336 or AGMA.
- The tool models a mesh with one efficiency number and does not know about lubrication, mounting stiffness or tooth profile modification.
