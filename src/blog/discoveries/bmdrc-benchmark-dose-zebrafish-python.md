When a laboratory exposes hundreds of embryos to a chemical at several concentrations and counts how many come out malformed, the question is: at what concentration does the risk start to matter? The modern answer is a **benchmark dose**: the dose at which the chance of the effect rises by a chosen amount, usually 10 %, over the background. A 2025 paper describes **bmdrc**, a Python package that does this for whole screens at once, following US EPA rules. If you only have one chemical's counts in front of you, fibub's [[benchmark-dose|BMD calculator]] does the same job in a browser; for continuous measurements such as growth or signal, see the [[dose-response]].

## The paper in brief

bmdrc takes the kind of data a zebrafish screen produces, where each plate well is scored yes or no for a set of body and behaviour phenotypes, and turns it into dose-response curves. It applies the filters the EPA and statisticians recommend (dropping endpoints with too much response in the untreated controls, or with too few concentrations showing anything), fits a family of curves, picks the best-fitting one automatically, and writes out a report. For proportional (yes/no) endpoints the package offers gamma, log-logistic, log-probit, logistic, multistage, probit, quantal linear and Weibull curves; for continuous endpoints it offers asymptotic regression, exponential, Gompertz, Hill, Michaelis-Menten, power and Weibull. The package is open source and has fed a public chemical-information portal.

The useful point for anyone reading such a paper is that the benchmark dose is not one number. It depends on which curve you fit, what benchmark response you ask for, and how you set the lower confidence limit. That is exactly what a small tool can show.

## Checked with the tools

We cannot refit the paper's screening data here, and we do not reproduce any of it. Instead we show what the same class of analysis does, on **our own made-up screen**: seven concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 µM), 32 embryos at each, with 1, 1, 2, 4, 9, 19 and 29 affected. Everything below is computed by the [[benchmark-dose]]'s own maths, at a benchmark response of **10 % extra risk**, with the multistage degree set to 2.

| Model | BMD10 (µM) | BMDL10 (µM) | BMD/BMDL | p (fit) | AIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Log-logistic | 4.65 | 2.94 | 1.58 | 0.96 | 164.66 |
| Weibull | 3.94 | 2.47 | 1.59 | 1.00 | 164.15 |
| Gamma | 4.17 | 2.54 | 1.64 | 1.00 | 164.11 |
| Multistage (degree 2) | 3.65 | 2.24 | 1.63 | 0.97 | 164.59 |
| Probit | 5.51 | 4.58 | 1.20 | 0.53 | 166.17 |

All five models fit these counts acceptably (goodness-of-fit p well above 0.1). The tool marks **Gamma** as the pick, because it has the lowest AIC among the adequate fits, which is the rule the EPA's software uses and the kind of automatic choice bmdrc makes for every endpoint in a screen. But notice the spread: the benchmark dose runs from 3.65 to 5.51 µM depending on the model, a factor of 1.5, while the *lower limits* run from 2.24 to 4.58 µM. A screen that reports a single BMD per endpoint hides that spread; reporting the BMDL, as regulators ask, is the safer habit.

**How far below the data can you read?** We refitted the same counts at five benchmark responses. At 10 % extra risk the three shapes agree within about 1.3 times. At 1 % they disagree by more than three times (0.43 µM for the multistage curve against 1.53 µM for the log-logistic), because a 1 % response is far below anything these 32-embryo groups could have measured, and the answer then comes from the model's shape rather than the data. That is the practical reason the 10 % default exists for yes/no endpoints.

**What the lower limit is.** The BMDL here is not a standard error times two. It is the lowest dose whose profile log-likelihood stays within χ²₁(0.90)/2 = 1.353 of the best fit: the whole model is refitted with the benchmark dose held at each trial value. That is Crump's method, the one the EPA's guidance describes, and it is what gives the asymmetric gap between BMD and BMDL in the table above.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/bmdrc-benchmark-dose-zebrafish-python.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitAll`, `fitModel` and `checkGroups` from `src/lib/sci/bmd.ts`, the maths behind the [[benchmark-dose]]: maximum likelihood by Nelder-Mead on bounded transforms, with the EPA's default restrictions (log-logistic slope ≥ 1, Weibull power ≥ 1, gamma power between 1 and 18, multistage coefficients ≥ 0), and the BMDL by profile likelihood.

Where the inputs come from: the list of curve families and the automatic model choice are from the paper and the package's documentation. The 10 % extra-risk default, the parameter restrictions and the AIC-with-adequate-fit rule are from the EPA's 2012 guidance. **The seven dose groups are our own invention**, chosen to look like a plate dilution series; no number in them is taken from the paper or from any published table.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/benchmark-dose.mjs` and, new with this article, `tests/sci-tools/benchmark-dose-screen.mjs`:

- The five models fitted to a standard five-group data set must match an independent SciPy implementation (L-BFGS-B likelihood, SLSQP profile with the BMD as a constraint): log-likelihoods to 1e-7, for example Weibull at −104.7152 with BMD 34.834 and BMDL 22.320, and the lowest-AIC pick must be Weibull.
- For every model, the extra risk at the fitted BMD must come out at exactly the benchmark response (10 %) to 1e-9, which is the definition the BMD has to satisfy.
- A quantal-linear (degree 1 multistage) fit must satisfy BMD = −ln(1 − BMR) / β₁ in closed form, and fits that land on a parameter bound must be counted with the right number of free parameters.
- New: this article's screen must give gamma BMD10 4.17 µM and BMDL10 2.54 µM, Weibull 3.94 and 2.47, log-logistic 4.65 and 2.94, with gamma picked, and the models must spread more than threefold at a 1 % benchmark response while staying within 1.3 times at 10 %.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published benchmark dose**: type the paper's dose groups, fit all five models, and see whether the reported BMD sits inside the spread or at one edge of it.
- **Decide what to report**: compare BMD and BMDL across models before choosing, instead of accepting one automatic pick, which is what large screens like the one bmdrc serves have to do.
- **Design the next experiment**: raise the group size in the tool and watch the BMD/BMDL ratio shrink; it tells you how many animals or wells buy how much certainty.
- **Teach the idea**: show a class why a 1 % benchmark response is a model extrapolation and a 10 % one is nearly a measurement.
- **Handle continuous endpoints** such as length, signal or activity with the [[dose-response]] instead, where the response is a measured number rather than a count.

## The fine print

- The screen in Figures 1 and 2 is ours. None of the paper's data, tables or figures is reproduced here.
