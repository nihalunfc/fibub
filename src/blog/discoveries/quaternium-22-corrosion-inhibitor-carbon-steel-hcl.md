Steel is cleaned and descaled with hydrochloric acid in many factories, and the acid eats the steel too. A **corrosion inhibitor** is a chemical added in tiny amounts that sticks to the metal and slows the attack. A team in Egypt tested **Quaternium-22**, a water-soluble ingredient better known from hair conditioners, and found it blocks about **97 %** of the corrosion of carbon steel in strong acid. This article shows how the [[tafel-corrosion-rate]] tool turns a polarisation curve into a corrosion current and a rate in millimetres per year; the [[impedance-fitter]] does the same job for impedance (EIS) spectra, the paper's second method.

## The paper in brief

The team put ASTM A35 carbon steel in **1.0 M hydrochloric acid** with 0, 41, 69, 138 and 277 µmol/L of Quaternium-22 (Q-22), at 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C. They measured weight loss, polarisation curves (current against potential, swept slowly through the corrosion potential) and impedance spectra, and backed them with computer models of how the molecule sits on iron.

The best result was **97.21 % inhibition** at 277 µmol/L and 20 °C. Q-22 slowed both the metal-dissolving (anodic) and hydrogen-making (cathodic) reactions, but mostly the anodic one, so it is called a **mixed-type** inhibitor. Its coverage of the surface followed the **Langmuir isotherm** (one layer of molecules, each site either full or empty), and the free energy of adsorption, **−37.4 to −39.8 kJ/mol**, sits between the values usually read as physical sticking (about −20) and chemical bonding (about −40). Protection fell as the acid warmed, which the authors put down to molecules letting go.

## Checked with the tools

The paper's tables of Tafel slopes and currents were not in the text we could read, so we did not redo them. Everything below is either a **simulation** built around the paper's one headline number, or a **recomputation** from its stated ΔG°ads range. We say which.

**Step 1: what does 97.21 % mean in current?** Inhibition efficiency from polarisation is IE = 1 − icorr(with inhibitor) / icorr(blank), where **icorr** is the corrosion current density. So 97.21 % means the inhibited steel carries **2.79 %** of the blank's current, about one thirty-sixth.

**Step 2: a simulated test.** We made up a blank curve typical of steel in 1 M HCl (Ecorr −470 mV against a saturated calomel electrode, icorr **400 µA/cm²**, Tafel slopes 70 and 120 mV per decade) and an inhibited one with **11.16 µA/cm²**, Ecorr shifted 18 mV positive (well under the 85 mV that would make it purely anodic) and slightly steeper slopes, then added 4 % noise (Figure 1). The Tafel tool's maths gave:

| Simulated curve | Tafel lines (80 to 200 mV) | Wagner-Traud fit | Rate, ASTM G102 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blank | 393 µA/cm² | 400 µA/cm² | 4.6 mm/year |
| 277 µmol/L Q-22 | 10.9 µA/cm² | 11.2 µA/cm² | 0.13 mm/year |
| Efficiency | 97.2 % | 97.2 % | |

A steel wall that would lose 4.6 mm a year in the bare acid loses about **0.13 mm a year** with the inhibitor. The straight-line Tafel method reads both currents a little low, because the opposite reaction still adds 1 to 3 % of the current at 80 mV from Ecorr; the full Wagner-Traud fit, which the tool runs as well, has no such bias. Because both currents are read low by a similar amount, the efficiency barely moves. The polarisation resistance (the slope of potential against current right at Ecorr) went from **47 to about 2,000 Ω·cm²**, which with Stern-Geary also gives 97.2 %.

**Step 3: what the adsorption energy says.** The Langmuir constant follows from ΔG°ads as K = (1/55.5) × e^(−ΔG°/RT), where 55.5 mol/L is the concentration of water. Taken at 20 °C (a simplification: the paper's range spans its four temperatures), −39.8 kJ/mol gives **K = 2.2 × 10⁵ L/mol** and −37.4 kJ/mol gives **8.3 × 10⁴ L/mol**. At 277 µmol/L those predict a surface coverage θ = Kc/(1 + Kc) of **98.4 %** and **95.8 %**, bracketing the measured 97.21 % (Figure 2). So the headline efficiency and the reported adsorption energies agree with each other.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/quaternium-22-corrosion-inhibitor-carbon-steel-hcl.mjs` draws both charts and writes them into this page. Figure 1 uses `bv` (the Wagner-Traud equation), `signed`, `tafel`, `fitBV`, `polarisationResistance`, `sternGeary` and `rateMmY` from `src/lib/sci/corrosion.ts`, the maths of the Tafel corrosion rate tool, with iron's equivalent weight and density from the same file. Figure 2 is the Langmuir formula worked out in the script itself; fibub has no adsorption-isotherm tool yet. From the paper: 97.21 % and the ΔG°ads range. Ours: every other number on the simulated curves (Ecorr, blank icorr, slopes, noise), and 20 °C for Figure 2.

## How we test the maths

`node tests/sci.mjs` runs on every change. For this tool:

- `tests/sci.mjs` (corrosion block): the tool's example curve must give Ecorr **−650 mV**, icorr **10 µA/cm²** and slopes **60 and 120 mV/decade**; ASTM G102 must turn **1 µA/cm²** on iron into **0.0116 mm/year**; the Stern-Geary constant for 60 and 120 mV slopes must be **0.017372 V**.
- `tests/sci-tools/tafel-corrosion-rate.mjs` (added with this article): exact blank and inhibited curves must read **394.3** and **10.85 µA/cm²** with straight Tafel lines and **400** and **11.16 µA/cm²** with the Wagner-Traud fit, an efficiency of **97.21 %**; G102 must give **4.640 mm/year** for 400 µA/cm² on carbon steel (worked by hand).

## Check it yourself

On the [[tafel-corrosion-rate]] tool:

1. Clear **Potential and current, one pair per line** and paste these 21 pairs (potential in mV, current in µA/cm², one pair per line): `-652 -385.5`, `-632 -270.5`, `-612 -189.7`, `-592 -133`, `-572 -93.05`, `-552 -64.86`, `-532 -44.75`, `-512 -30.1`, `-492 -18.89`, `-472 -9.412`, `-452 0`, `-432 11.35`, `-412 27.49`, `-392 52.84`, `-372 94.76`, `-352 165.6`, `-332 286.7`, `-312 494.2`, `-292 850.5`, `-272 1463`, `-252 2515`. This is our exact inhibited curve.
2. Set **Potential unit** to *mV* and **Current unit** to *µA/cm²*.
3. Leave **Tafel range from (mV)** at `80`, **to (mV from Ecorr)** at `200`, and **Metal** on iron or carbon steel (equivalent weight 27.92, density 7.87).
4. The headline should read **icorr 11.2 µA/cm²: about 0.129 mm per year**, and the table shows the Tafel-line row slightly lower, near 10.8 µA/cm².
5. Divide by a blank of 400 µA/cm²: 1 − 11.16/400 = **97.2 %** inhibition.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Inhibitor screening**: run the blank and each concentration through the [[tafel-corrosion-rate]] tool and get icorr, slopes and mm/year for each in a minute, with the Wagner-Traud fit as a check on the straight-line reading.
- **Cross-checking efficiencies**: compare IE from Tafel, from polarisation resistance (Stern-Geary) and from charge-transfer resistance fitted to EIS spectra with the [[impedance-fitter]]; they should agree within a few percent.
- **Weight-loss coupons**: the same tool's weight-loss card turns mass lost, area and hours into mm/year for the gravimetric side of a study.
- **Reviewing a paper**: check that a reported efficiency and ΔG°ads are consistent through the Langmuir relation, as above.
- **Teaching**: shows students why corrosion currents are read on a log scale and why the Tafel range starts well away from Ecorr.

## A tool we could build

An adsorption isotherm fitter that takes surface coverage (or efficiency) against inhibitor concentration at several temperatures, compares Langmuir, Temkin and Frumkin, and gives K, ΔG°ads, ΔH°ads and ΔS°ads with errors, would complete the inhibitor workflow.
