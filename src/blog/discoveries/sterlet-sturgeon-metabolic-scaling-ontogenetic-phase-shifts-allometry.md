How fast an animal burns oxygen depends on its size, and across species the rate tends to rise with body mass to the power **3/4** (Kleiber's rule). Within one species growing up, the exponent can differ, and a 2025 paper in *Hydrobiology* shows why one line through all the data can mislead: young sterlet sturgeon pass through distinct growth phases, and each phase has its own metabolic level. This article uses the [[allometry-calculator]] to recompute the paper's exponent tests and to show, on simulated fish, how stacking three phases with an exponent near 0.61 produces a single line near 0.77. To get oxygen rates from chamber readings first, use the [[respirometry-calculator]]; for body mass from length, the [[fish-length-weight]] tool.

## The paper in brief

The **sterlet** (*Acipenser ruthenus*) is a small sturgeon farmed for caviar and restocked into European rivers. The author measured the oxygen consumption of single fish at 20 °C in closed chambers, from 3-day-old larvae of 0.02 g to 64-day-old juveniles of 2.6 g, and fitted the **allometric equation** R = a M^b, where R is oxygen use (µL O₂ per fish per minute), M is body mass in grams, b is the **scaling exponent** and a is the rate at 1 g. On log axes this is a straight line with slope b.

The data break into phases at about **0.25 g and 0.65 g**, matching changes in the fish's development. Within each phase the exponent is similar, about **0.54 to 0.64**, and a common within-phase exponent of **0.608 ± 0.061** fits all three. But the level a jumps between phases, from **2.17 to 2.38 to 3.77**: a fish in the third phase uses 1.74 times the oxygen a first-phase fish of the same mass would. Fitted as one line, all fish give **0.763**, and a between-phase fit gives **0.791**. The newest larvae, before the first phase, scale close to **isometry** (b = 1, oxygen use rising in step with mass).

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the paper's tests, recomputed.** The paper tests each exponent against 1 with a t test. The tool's test is t = (b − b₀) ÷ SE with n − 2 degrees of freedom. Feeding it the stated exponents, standard errors and fish numbers reproduces the paper's p values to within rounding of the inputs, and adds the test against 3/4 that the paper does not report:

| Fit | b ± SEM (paper) | p against 1: paper | p against 1: tool | p against 3/4: tool |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 (5 fish) | 0.638 ± 0.077 | 0.0185 | 0.0182 | 0.242 |
| Phase 2 (10 fish) | 0.540 ± 0.096 | 0.00135 | 0.00137 | 0.0602 |
| Phase 3 (14 fish) | 0.609 ± 0.106 | 0.00304 | 0.0031 | 0.208 |
| All fish (30) | 0.763 ± 0.031 | 2.16 × 10⁻⁸ | 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ | 0.678 |

So no single phase can be told apart from 3/4 with so few fish (the 95 % limits are ±0.22 to ±0.25), and all fish together sit right on 3/4. Read alone, the pooled fit would look like a textbook Kleiber result.

**Step 2: why pooling steepens the slope.** We **simulated** fish from the paper's own numbers: each phase's stated mass range and fish count, masses spread evenly on a log scale, the common exponent 0.608, the three phase constants and a random scatter matching the paper's residual mean square (a log₁₀ standard deviation of 0.063). Without any scatter, one line through all 29 simulated fish has a slope of **0.771**, though every phase has exactly 0.608. The step up in level from phase to phase is read as extra slope. With scatter (Figure 1), the tool fits 0.462, 0.520 and 0.560 to the three phases and **0.746 ± 0.029** to all fish.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/sterlet-sturgeon-metabolic-scaling-ontogenetic-phase-shifts-allometry.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitAllometry` and `testExponent` from `src/lib/sci/allomfit.ts`, the maths behind the [[allometry-calculator]], and `tTwoSided` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the p values from stated exponents.

The phase mass ranges, fish numbers, exponents, standard errors, p values, phase constants and residual mean square come from the paper. The even spacing of masses, the random scatter and the seed are **our assumptions**; the simulated fish are not the paper's measurements.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/allometry-calculator.mjs`, which pins the fit against SciPy's `linregress` on a made-up 14-species example:

- The exponent must be 0.74149 with a standard error of 0.017376 and 95 % limits of 0.70364 to 0.77935, and a must be 3.5318.
- The test against 3/4 must give t = −0.4895 and p = 0.6333; against 2/3, p = 0.00102.
- An exact power law, Y = 2 M^0.25, must come back as a = 2 and b = 0.25.
- New with this article: three phases with exponent 0.608 and constants 2.17, 2.38 and 3.77 over the paper's mass ranges, fitted as one line, must give b = 0.7709, and a phase exponent of 0.540 ± 0.096 from 10 fish must test against 1 at p = 0.00137.

## Check it yourself

On the [[allometry-calculator]]:

1. In *Body mass M and the trait Y, one species or individual per row*, paste our 14 simulated third-phase fish (mass in g, then oxygen use), one pair per line: **0.6438, 2.386**; **0.7176, 3.466**; **0.7998, 3.116**; **0.8914, 3.019**; **0.9935, 4.412**; **1.107, 4.749**; **1.234, 4.784**; **1.376, 3.121**; **1.533, 3.878**; **1.709, 5.016**; **1.904, 5.611**; **2.123, 4.684**; **2.366, 5.473**; **2.637, 7.56**.
2. Set *Mass unit* to **g** and *Y name and unit* to **oxygen use (µL O₂/min)**. Leave *Confidence* on **95 %** and *Test b against* on **0.75**.
3. *Exponent b* reads **0.5598 ± 0.11**, with 95 % limits **0.3204 to 0.7993**; *Coefficient a* is **3.613**, close to the paper's 3.77.
4. *b against 0.75* shows **t = −1.73, p = 0.11**: 3/4 is inside the limits.
5. Change *Test b against* to **1**: **p = 0.0017**, so the phase is clearly not isometric, as in the paper.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Recheck a paper's exponent tests** from b, its standard error and n, against 1, 3/4, 2/3 or any value, with the [[allometry-calculator]].
- **Look for phase shifts before pooling**: fit each life stage separately and compare their constants; a rising a with a flat b means pooling will inflate the slope.
- **Plan sample sizes**: with 5 to 14 animals per stage, limits of ±0.2 or more are normal; simulate first to see what n you need.
- **Turn chamber readings into rates** with the [[respirometry-calculator]], and lengths into masses with the [[fish-length-weight]] tool, before fitting.
- **Teach the 3/4 debate**: one dataset gives 0.61 or 0.77 depending on whether development stages are kept apart.

## The fine print

- Our fish are **simulated** from the paper's summary numbers; real masses are not evenly spaced, and the paper's own fits differ from ours by chance.
- The paper gives the number of fish as 33 in its methods, 29 in a figure and 30 in a table; we used 5, 10 and 14 per phase (29) for the simulation and n = 30 for the all-fish test, as stated beside each value.
- The tool fits ordinary least squares on log₁₀ values with one line; the paper's common within-phase slope comes from a covariance analysis, which the tool does not do in one step.
- Rates are per fish at 20 °C; temperature, activity and feeding all shift both a and b.
