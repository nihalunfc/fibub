Microwave ceramics are usually sold on a high dielectric constant, because a high εr shrinks the circuit. For an **antenna** the trade runs the other way: a low εr keeps more of the field in the air, which is where the radiation has to come from. A 2025 paper in *Crystals* makes a calcium borate, Ca₃(BO₃)₂, with an **ultra-low εr of 5.95** and a good quality factor, then simulates a **14.97 GHz microstrip patch** on it, reporting a footprint of 3.75 × 5.4 × 1.4 mm and 7.15 dBi of gain. A patch is one of the few antennas whose size follows from closed-form formulas, so the claim can be checked on a phone with the [[antenna-calculator]]. For what to do with the feed afterwards, see the [[smith-chart]]; for the ceramic's other life as a waveguide filler, see [[waveguide-modes]].

## The paper in brief

The authors sintered Ca₃(BO₃)₂ (CBO) under 800 MPa and measured its microwave properties: **εr = 5.95**, a quality factor **Q × f = 11,836 GHz**, and a temperature coefficient of resonant frequency **τf = −39.89 ppm/°C**. An εr near 6 is low for a dense ceramic; most microwave ceramics sit between 20 and 40, and only polymers and foams go much lower.

They then simulated a rectangular microstrip patch on a 1.4 mm board of the material, quoting **dimensions of 3.75 × 5.4 × 1.4 mm**, a resonance at **14.97 GHz**, a return loss of **−25.5 dB** and a peak gain of **7.15 dBi** broadside. The argument is that a low-εr, low-loss ceramic is the right substrate for millimetre-wave 5G patches.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from the paper's stated permittivity, thickness and frequency** with the transmission-line model, the standard closed form for a rectangular patch. We do not re-run their electromagnetic simulation. **Our reading** of the quoted dimensions is that 5.4 mm is the patch width, 3.75 mm the resonant length and 1.4 mm the substrate thickness; no other arrangement gives a patch anywhere near 15 GHz on this ceramic.

**Step 1: the width lands almost exactly.** For good radiation efficiency the model takes W = c/(2f)·√(2/(εr + 1)). At 14.97 GHz with εr 5.95 that is **5.371 mm**, against the paper's **5.4 mm**: **0.5 % apart**. Read the other way, a 5.4 mm width is the textbook width for 14.89 GHz. The paper's board was clearly sized with this formula, or something equivalent to it.

**Step 2: the length is 7 % longer than the thin-substrate formula wants.** The model's resonant length is L = c/(2f√εeff) − 2ΔL, with an **effective permittivity of 4.693** (part of the field runs in the air above the board) and a **fringing extension ΔL of 0.574 mm** at each radiating edge. That gives **3.473 mm**, while the paper has **3.75 mm**, 7.4 % longer. Turned around, the model says a 3.75 mm patch on this ceramic resonates at **14.06 GHz**, about 6 % below the simulated 14.97 GHz.

That gap is expected, and it is worth knowing which way it goes. The substrate here is **1.4 mm on a 20.03 mm free-space wavelength**: h = 0.070 λ₀, and h = 0.15 λg inside the dielectric. The transmission-line model with Hammerstad's fringing is a thin-substrate approximation; above roughly h = 0.05 λ₀ it underestimates the fringing fields, so it asks for a shorter patch than a full-wave simulation does. fibub's tool refuses the calculation altogether above h = λ/10 (2.00 mm here), which is a reminder rather than a guarantee: at 0.07 λ₀ you are already in the band where a few per cent of error is normal. Anyone designing on this ceramic should take the closed form as a starting point and expect to tune down by a few per cent, which is exactly the difference the paper's own numbers show.

**Step 3: what the low permittivity is actually buying.** The tool makes the trade-off concrete. At the same 14.97 GHz and 1.4 mm thickness, the patch area is **18.7 mm² on this εr 5.95 ceramic**, but only **3.97 mm² on a conventional εr 25 microwave ceramic**: the high-εr board is 4.7 times smaller. The low-εr patch pays for its size with a much lower effective permittivity (4.69 against 17.5), which means more of the field in the air, and with a far friendlier feed: the **edge resistance is 369 Ω here against 1,209 Ω at εr 25**, so the inset feed that matches 50 Ω needs a notch **1.32 mm** deep rather than a much deeper and lossier one. Those are the mechanisms behind the paper's headline claim, in numbers a reader can reproduce.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/low-permittivity-ceramic-patch-antenna-transmission-line-model.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `patch()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-antenna.ts`, the maths behind the [[antenna-calculator]]: the width formula, Hammerstad's effective permittivity and fringing extension, the resonant length, the radiating-slot conductance G1 and mutual conductance G12 (both numerical integrals, with a Bessel J₀ inside G12), the edge resistance and the inset depth that matches a 50 Ω line. The frequency that fits a given dimension is found by bisection on the tool's own output.

From the paper: εr 5.95, the 1.4 mm thickness, the 14.97 GHz resonance and the 3.75 × 5.4 mm footprint. **Our own choices**: reading those dimensions as length × width, the εr 2.2 to 25 range in Figure 1, and the 50 Ω feed.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/antenna-calculator.mjs`, which pins this maths against Balanis's worked examples and an independent SciPy implementation:

- Balanis's design example, a patch at 10 GHz on εr 2.2 and h 0.1588 cm, must come back as W = 1.186 cm, εeff = 1.972 and L = 0.906 cm, the book's own numbers.
- The radiating-slot conductance and the inset depth must match SciPy's numerical integration of the same integrals, and the Bessel function J₀ must match `scipy.special.j0`.
- New with this article: the paper's ceramic, 14.97 GHz with εr 5.95 and h 1.4 mm, must give W = 5.3714 mm, L = 3.4734 mm, εeff = 4.6932, ΔL = 0.57430 mm, an edge resistance of 369.33 Ω and a 50 Ω inset depth of 1.3201 mm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[antenna-calculator]] page:

1. Press **Patch** in the antenna-type row. The frequency unit switches itself to **GHz**.
2. *Frequency* **14.97**, *Substrate relative permittivity εr* **5.95**, *Substrate thickness h (mm)* **1.4**, *Feed line impedance for the inset (Ω)* **50**.
3. The headline reads **Patch 5.371 mm wide × 3.473 mm long**. In the facts below: *Effective permittivity εeff* **4.693**, *Fringing extension ΔL* **0.5743 mm**, *Edge resistance* **369.3 Ω**, *Inset depth for 50 Ω* **1.32 mm**, *Ground plane at least* **13.77 mm × 11.87 mm**.
4. Change *Frequency* to **14.06** and the length becomes **3.75 mm**, the paper's own dimension.
5. Put *Substrate relative permittivity εr* up to **25**, a normal microwave ceramic, and watch the patch fall to **2.777 × 1.431 mm** while the edge resistance climbs to **1209 Ω**: smaller, but much harder to feed and with less of its field in the air.
6. Type *Substrate thickness h (mm)* **2.5** and the tool refuses the calculation: above λ/10 (2.003 mm at this frequency) the model is no longer a fair approximation.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published patch** in a minute: type the stated εr, thickness and frequency and see whether the quoted footprint is the textbook one, as we did here.
- **Report what the closed form predicts** alongside a full-wave simulation, so readers can see how much of the final size came from tuning.
- **Choose a substrate for a new ceramic**: sweep εr and watch the patch area, the effective permittivity and the edge resistance move together, instead of arguing about size alone.
- **Plan the feed before the board**: the edge resistance and inset depth say whether a 50 Ω line can be matched directly or needs a quarter-wave section, which the [[smith-chart]] then handles.
- **Teach the limits of a model**: the h/λ ratio and the refusal above λ/10 make the approximation's boundary visible, which is hard to show with a simulator.

## The fine print

- The paper gives one combined dimension string (3.75 × 5.4 × 1.4 mm) and does not label which number is which, nor the feed type or position. Our reading is stated above; if the 3.75 mm is something else, the length comparison changes.
- The transmission-line model gives sizes, not gain or return loss. The paper's 7.15 dBi and −25.5 dB cannot be checked this way; a 7 dBi broadside gain is, however, in the normal range for a single patch on a thick, low-εr substrate.
- We used the paper's εr at its working frequency as stated. Permittivity drifts with frequency and temperature (the paper's own τf is −39.89 ppm/°C), which moves the resonance by far more than our 0.5 % width agreement suggests is achievable in a real board.
- Nothing here is a measurement of the ceramic. The dielectric data are the paper's.
