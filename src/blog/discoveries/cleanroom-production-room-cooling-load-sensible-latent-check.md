Rapid diagnostic tests go soft if the air they are packed in is damp, so the room they are made in is held under 30 % relative humidity. A 2026 conference paper designs the air conditioning for one such room — 20.54 m² of floor, 2.8 m to the ceiling — then measures what it actually achieved over a month. The design numbers it prints are enough to rebuild the whole load from scratch, which is what the [[hvac-room-load]] tool does, and the answer is not what most people expect: for this room the moisture matters more than the heat. The air-state arithmetic behind it belongs to [[psychrometric-calculator]], and the wall and ceiling part to [[heat-conduction]].

## The paper in brief

A user requirement specification set three targets: air **under 26 °C**, **under 30 % RH**, and **6 to 20 air changes per hour**. The authors sized the plant against those, installed it, and then monitored the empty room for a month.

Their design numbers: a **room cooling load of 30,436 Btu/h**, a separate dehumidifier drawing **600 m³/h** of process air and removing **4.314 kg of water per hour**, whose regeneration heater adds **9000 W**, giving a **total thermal load of 61,162 Btu/h** and a selected cooling unit of **85,846 Btu/h**. The room holds four operators and four listed machines — a cutting machine (200 W), an assembly roller (100 W), a heat sealer (500 W) and a microwave (900 W).

Their verification: **19.93 air changes per hour** from a measured total supply of **1146 m³/h**, with the room sitting at **21 to 25.2 °C and 20 to 28 % RH** over the month. Both are inside the specification, which is the paper's headline claim.

## Checked with the tools

Three of the paper's chains of numbers can be rebuilt exactly, and the fourth — the room load itself — can be reconstructed once we say out loud what the paper leaves unsaid.

**Step 1: the airflow and the air changes are self-consistent.** Air changes per hour times room volume gives the supply airflow. With 57.5 m³: 6 × 57.5 = **345 m³/h** and 20 × 57.5 = **1150 m³/h**, exactly the range the paper derives. Going the other way, the measured 1146 m³/h divided by 57.5 m³ is **19.930 h⁻¹**, the paper's 19.93. That is 318.3 L/s, the number the tool's *Ventilation outdoor air (L/s)* box wants.

**Step 2: the psychrometrics are right to three figures.** The paper dries air from 23 °C and 60 % RH down to 25 % RH. Running the same two states through the maths behind [[psychrometric-calculator]]:

| Quantity | Paper | Tool's maths |
|---|---|---|
| Saturation vapour pressure at 23 °C | about 28.1 hPa | 28.104 hPa |
| Absolute humidity at 23 °C, 60 % RH | 12.33 g/m³ | 12.337 g/m³ |
| Absolute humidity at 23 °C, 25 % RH | 5.14 g/m³ | 5.141 g/m³ |
| Difference | 7.19 g/m³ | 7.197 g/m³ |
| Water removed at 600 m³/h | 4.314 kg/h | 7.19 × 600 ÷ 1000 = **4.314 kg/h** |

The moisture duty is an exact product of the paper's own two numbers. Turning it into power: 4.314 kg/h at a latent heat of about 2544 kJ/kg at 23 °C is **3.05 kW, or 10,400 Btu/h** of latent load that the room's cooling coil never has to see, because the dehumidifier takes it.

**Step 3: the total load is the room load plus the heater, and nothing else.** The 9000 W regeneration heater is **30,709 Btu/h** (the paper rounds it to 30,726). Added to the 30,436 Btu/h room load that gives **61,145 Btu/h** against the paper's stated 61,162 — a difference of 17 Btu/h, or 0.03 %, which is rounding. So the "total thermal load" is literally those two terms, and the 85,846 Btu/h unit is **40.4 %** above it, the margin the paper quotes. Worth knowing, because it means the heater the dehumidifier needs is as big a load as the whole production room.

**Step 4: how much of that 1146 m³/h can be outdoor air?** Here the paper is silent, and this is where the tool earns its keep. We built the room in [[hvac-room-load]]: 20.54 m² of floor, 2.8 m high, held at 25 °C and 25 % RH, with the four machines (1700 W), four people at 75 W sensible and 55 W latent each, 10 W/m² of lighting, and a light insulated shell against a 32 °C, 70 % RH outdoor design state. The envelope, the outdoor state and the people and lighting figures are **our assumptions**; the room size, the airflow and the machine powers are the paper's. Then we swept the outdoor-air share of the supply from nothing to everything.

If all 1146 m³/h were outdoor air, the load would be **67,600 Btu/h** — more than twice what the paper reports, with 51,000 Btu/h of it latent. The paper's 30,436 Btu/h is matched at about **36 to 37 % outdoor air**, roughly **420 m³/h or 118 L/s**, the rest recirculated. At that point the tool splits the load as **11,400 Btu/h sensible and 19,400 Btu/h latent**, a sensible heat ratio of **0.37**: nearly two thirds of the duty is drying the air. For comparison, the machines contribute 5800 Btu/h and the whole envelope only about 800.

That is the engineering lesson of the paper restated in numbers it does not print. A room like this is a **dehumidification problem wearing a cooling problem's clothes**, which is exactly why the authors bought a separate 4.5 kg/h dehumidifier rather than a larger chiller.

**Step 5: what the humidity target costs.** Holding the same room at 30 % RH instead of 25 % drops the load from 30,700 to **29,600 Btu/h**; letting it drift to a normal office 50 % drops it to **25,000**. Figure 2 shows the curve. The sensible part barely moves; all of the change is latent.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/cleanroom-production-room-cooling-load-sensible-latent-check.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `roomLoad` from `src/lib/sci/hvacload.ts` (fabric U·A·ΔT, outdoor air sensible and latent from the humidity ratios, people, lighting and equipment), the maths behind [[hvac-room-load]], and `pws` and `air` from `src/lib/sci/psychro.ts`, the maths behind [[psychrometric-calculator]].

From the paper: the floor area, ceiling height and volume, the 6 to 20 air changes, the measured 1146 m³/h, the room and total loads, the heater power, the dehumidifier's 600 m³/h and 4.314 kg/h, the two air states and the four machine powers. **Our assumptions:** the 32 °C and 70 % RH outdoor design state, the atmospheric pressure at 700 m, the partition and ceiling areas and U-values (0.5 and 0.4 W/m²K), 75 W sensible and 55 W latent per person, 10 W/m² of lighting, a 25 °C room and no safety margin. Step 4 is a **reconstruction**, not the paper's calculation.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/hvac-room-load.mjs` runs inside `tests/sci.mjs` on every change to fibub:

- A made-up room's fabric load must equal the hand sum of U·A·ΔT term by term, and its solar gain the hand product of area, SHGC and irradiance.

## Check it yourself

On the [[hvac-room-load]] tool page:

1. In *Room and design conditions*, set *Floor area (m²)* to **20.54**, *Ceiling height (m)* to **2.8** and *Altitude (m above sea level)* to **700**.
2. Set *Cooling: inside (°C)* to **25**, *Cooling: inside humidity (% RH)* to **25**, *Cooling: outside design (°C)* to **32** and *Cooling: outside humidity (% RH)* to **70**. Leave the heating row alone; this room never needs heat.
3. In *Walls, windows and roof*, replace the example with two lines: **Partition walls and door, 50.74, 0.5** and **Ceiling, 20.54, 0.4**.
4. In *Outdoor air and internal gains*, set *Infiltration (air changes per hour)* to **0**, *Ventilation outdoor air (L/s)* to **117.8**, *People* to **4**, *Equipment (W)* to **1700**, *Lighting (W/m² of floor)* to **10** and *Safety margin (%)* to **0**.
5. *Peak loads* shows a cooling total near **9.0 kW (30,700 Btu/h)**, split about **11,400 Btu/h sensible and 19,400 Btu/h latent**, with a sensible heat ratio of **0.37**. The breakdown table puts *Outdoor air* at about 21,600 Btu/h of that and *Equipment* at 5800.
6. Now type **318.3** into *Ventilation outdoor air (L/s)* — the full measured supply as outdoor air — and the total jumps past **67,000 Btu/h**. Put it back to 117.8 and raise *Cooling: inside humidity (% RH)* to **50** to watch the load fall to about **25,000**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Back out an unstated assumption.** A paper that gives a load but not its outdoor-air fraction can be inverted, as in Step 4, by sweeping the one unknown until the published number appears.
- **Check an air-change specification against a real airflow** before trusting it: volume times air changes, both ways, in one line.
- **See whether a room is sensible- or latent-driven** from the sensible heat ratio, which decides between a bigger coil and a separate dehumidifier.
- **Price a humidity target** for a clean or controlled space by sweeping the setpoint, as in Figure 2, before it is written into a specification.
- **Sanity-check a layered wall or ceiling U-value** with [[heat-conduction]], then feed it straight into the element list here.

## The fine print

- Step 4 is **our reconstruction**, not the paper's calculation. The paper does not publish its envelope, its outdoor design state, its people or lighting gains, or its outdoor-air fraction, so the 36 to 37 % is the share that reproduces its stated load **under our assumptions**, and a different envelope would move it.
- The tool is a steady-state estimate: no thermal mass, no time lag, no hour-by-hour sun, no diversity. The paper used a commercial hourly program, so small differences are expected.
- Cleanroom air changes are driven by particle removal and room pressurisation, not comfort. Treating the whole supply as outdoor air, as in Step 4's right-hand edge, is a worst case, not a design.
- The paper's own text is inconsistent in places (the dehumidifier's start state is quoted at 23 °C while the room runs nearer 25 °C), so the moisture duty in Step 2 is an upper figure.
- For study and planning. A real controlled-environment room needs a qualified engineer and a validated calculation.
