Every environmental or biomonitoring dataset has results the instrument could not see: "<0.124 µg/g" means the element is somewhere between nothing and the detection limit, and nobody knows where. A 2026 paper in *Toxics* measured barium, strontium and vanadium in the hair of 120 children and 97 adolescents in an urban-industrial area of Spain, and had to report medians and 95th percentiles when **38 %, 23 % and 74 %** of the results were below the limit. The authors did the right thing and used censored-data statistics rather than filling in half the limit. This article puts their published figures through the [[non-detects]] tool, shows which of them a log-normal distribution can actually carry, and measures how far the usual shortcut would have taken them. For the limit itself, see the [[calibration-curve]]; for how many samples you need, the [[sample-size]].

## The paper in brief

Hair was collected from children aged 6 to 9 and adolescents aged 13 to 16 across four zones, and analysed for barium and strontium (markers of local geology and dust) and vanadium (a marker of heavy oil burning and traffic), with topsoil vanadium measured at the same places.

Detection limits in hair were **0.124 µg/g** for barium in children and 0.153 in adolescents, 0.187 and 0.434 for strontium, and 0.02 and 0.011 for vanadium. The share of results below those limits was large: **38.3 % of children's barium**, 23.3 % of strontium and **74.2 % of vanadium**. Children's medians were 0.193 µg/g for barium (95th percentile 0.629), 0.412 for strontium (2.155) and 0.003 for vanadium (0.428).

The statistical rules the authors set out matter as much as the numbers: **no substitution**; **Kaplan–Meier** when less than half the results are censored; **regression on order statistics (ROS) or maximum likelihood** from 50 to 80 %; and above 80 %, report only detection frequency and the upper tail. Barium and strontium differed strongly between zones (p below 1 × 10⁻⁸ with a censored-data test), vanadium did not (p = 0.162). An earlier version of the vanadium data had used half the limit as a filler, and the authors replaced it.

## Checked with the tools

Two different checks follow. The first uses only the paper's published numbers; the second is a **simulation**, with no data from the paper in it.

**Step 1: do the three published facts fit one distribution?** For each element the paper gives a median, a 95th percentile and a share below the limit. A log-normal distribution has only two free numbers, so any two of those three fix the third. Matching the median and the 95th percentile for **children's barium** gives a spread (σ on the log scale) of 0.718, which puts only **26.9 %** below 0.124 µg/g, not 38.3 %. Matching the median and the 38.3 % instead needs σ = 1.487, which would push the 95th percentile up to **2.23 µg/g** rather than 0.629. Figure 1 draws both.

Children's barium is the only group where the three facts pull apart like that. Strontium in children comes out at 21.6 % against a reported 23.3 %, adolescents' barium at 30.0 % against 35.1 %, and **vanadium in children at 73.5 % against a reported 74.2 %** — almost exact. That pattern makes sense: the vanadium summary came from a fitted, log-normal-based method (ROS or maximum likelihood, by the authors' own 50 to 80 % rule), so it is internally log-normal by construction, while the barium summary came from Kaplan–Meier, which follows the data's own shape and owes nothing to a log-normal. The mismatch is not an error in the paper; it is evidence that children's hair barium really is not log-normal, and so a vote in favour of the distribution-free method they used.

- **Substituting zero** loses 9.5 % of the mean at 30 % censoring and **41 %** at 70 %.
- **Substituting half the limit** happens to look good here, within 3 % everywhere, which is why the habit survives. It is luck: the right filler depends on the spread, and this is a mild one.
- **Substituting the full limit** gains 6 % at 30 % censoring and 37 % at 70 %.
- **Kaplan–Meier** follows exactly the same line as the full limit. That is not a bug: with a single detection limit and no detected value below it, the lowest point in the dataset *is* the limit, and Kaplan–Meier's restricted mean has to place the censored mass there. It is the reason Helsel recommends it for data with **several** limits, and it is why the authors switched to other methods above 50 % censoring.
- **ROS** and **maximum likelihood** stay within about 1 % of the truth all the way to 70 % censoring, because they borrow the shape of the detected values. Of course, that shape has to be right.

**Step 3: the median that is not a measurement.** Children's vanadium has 74.2 % of results below 0.02 µg/g and a reported median of **0.003 µg/g**, a sixth of the limit. We simulated the same situation (85 of 120 censored) and asked the tool for a median: Kaplan–Meier returns **nothing at all**, because 71 % of its probability mass sits below the lowest limit and the tool refuses to invent a percentile there. Maximum likelihood returns 0.004 µg/g and ROS 0.0034. Those are extrapolations from the shape of the top quarter of the data, which is exactly what the paper's own rule says to do between 50 and 80 % censoring, and exactly why it also says to lean on detection frequency at that point. A median of 0.003 µg/g with a limit of 0.02 is a model's opinion, not an observation, and the paper is right to treat the vanadium zone comparison (p = 0.162) as the weaker of its results.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/hair-trace-metals-below-detection-limit.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `kaplanMeier`, `ros`, `mleLognormal`, `substitute` and `percentile` from `src/lib/sci/censored.ts`, the maths behind the [[non-detects]] tool, with `normCdf` and `normInv` from `src/lib/sci/stats.ts` for the log-normal fits.

From the paper: the detection limits, the censored shares, the medians, interquartile ranges and 95th percentiles quoted above. **Ours**: the 120-result sample size is the paper's, but the simulated values, the random seed, and the assumption that the underlying distribution is log-normal are all **our assumptions**, used to measure the methods against a known truth. Figure 2 contains none of the paper's data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` pins the non-detect methods against Python:

- On a made-up 16-result set with two limits, the Kaplan–Meier mean must be 2.48125 and its standard error 0.603003, as statsmodels' `SurvfuncRight` on the flipped data with a Greenwood variance gives; the median must be 1.5 and the 90th percentile 6.2.
- ROS must return a mean of 2.428836 and a standard deviation of 2.527843, matching statsmodels' `impute_ros`, and the censored log-normal maximum likelihood must give μ = 0.488877 and σ = 0.903271 with a log-likelihood of −18.415341, matching SciPy.
- When the lowest value in the set is itself a non-detect, the 25th percentile must come back as "not available" rather than as a number.
- New with this article: our 24-result barium set below must give a Kaplan–Meier mean of 0.3120 µg/g with a standard error of 0.0719, a ROS mean of 0.2897 and a maximum-likelihood mean of 0.2932, and the 85-of-120 vanadium set must return no Kaplan–Meier median at all.

2. Set *Unit* to **µg/g**. The *Summary* card reads a **Kaplan–Meier mean of 0.312 µg/g**, a standard error of **0.0719**, a median of **0.164** and an upper 95 % confidence limit of the mean of **0.435 µg/g**.
3. In *Every method*, compare the rows: **ROS mean 0.2897**, **maximum likelihood 0.2932**, **half the limit 0.2887**, **zero 0.2655**. The spread between methods, 0.047 µg/g, is about two thirds of one standard error here: with 37.5 % non-detects the method matters less than the sample size does.
4. The 95th percentile differs much more: **0.626 µg/g** from Kaplan–Meier (the largest detected value it can reach) against **1.53** from ROS, which extrapolates a log-normal tail. Upper percentiles are the first thing to go wrong with censored data.
5. Put **0.5** in *Guideline value (optional)*: the tool counts **3 of 24** results above it. Because the detection limit (0.124) is well below the guideline, every non-detect is safely under it, and the count is exact.
6. Now change every `<0.124` to `0.062` (half the limit). The Kaplan–Meier mean falls from 0.312 to **0.2887**, every method in the table reads the same number, and the non-detect count drops to zero: substitution has thrown away the one thing the tool needed to know.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report a censored dataset properly** in the browser: Kaplan–Meier, ROS and maximum likelihood side by side, with the substitution results shown only for comparison.
- **Check a published summary for internal consistency**, as in step 1: a median, an upper percentile and a censored share must fit together.
- **Decide which method to use** from the censored share, following the rule this paper sets out, and see with your own data how much the choice moves the answer.
- **Get an exposure-point concentration**: the upper 95 % confidence limit of the Kaplan–Meier mean is what risk assessments usually ask for.
- **Lower the limit instead.** If more than half your results are censored, no statistic will rescue them; improve the method with the [[calibration-curve]] or collect more sample.

## The fine print

- We did not re-analyse the paper's data. Step 1 uses only its published summary figures, and steps 2 and 3 are simulations.
- A log-normal is a convention, not a law. Our consistency check shows where the published figures depart from it, which is information about the data, not a mistake in the paper.
- Kaplan–Meier's restricted mean cannot fall below the lowest value in the dataset, so with one detection limit it behaves like substituting the full limit. Several limits break that tie.
- Hair concentrations depend on washing, dyeing and external dust as well as on intake, so nothing here says anything about any child's exposure or health.
- The paper's zone comparisons use censored-data tests we do not reproduce; our checks are about the summary statistics only.
