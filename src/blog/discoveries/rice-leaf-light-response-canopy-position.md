A rice plant is not one leaf. The top leaves sit in full sun, while the leaves lower down are shaded and older, and crop models that treat every leaf alike get the field's photosynthesis wrong. A 2025 paper in *Agronomy* measured **light-response curves**, how fast a leaf takes in carbon dioxide as the light rises, on rice leaves from the top of the plant down to the seventh leaf, and compared four common models for describing them. This article shows how the [[photosynthesis-curve-fitter]] fits the most widely used of those models and reads off the numbers the paper reports. For the next step, scale leaves to the plant with the [[leaf-area-analyser]] and [[plant-growth-analysis]].

## The paper in brief

The authors grew japonica rice in Jiangsu, China, and in mid-August clamped leaves into a gas-exchange chamber held at 30 °C and 400 µmol mol⁻¹ CO₂ (about today's air). They stepped the light down through 19 levels from 2000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (full summer sun) to darkness and recorded **net photosynthesis**, the carbon a leaf fixes minus what it breathes out. They then fitted four models: the rectangular hyperbola, the **non-rectangular hyperbola** (which adds a curvature θ, between 0 and 1, for how sharply the curve bends into its plateau), an exponential model and a modified rectangular hyperbola.

The headline findings: photosynthesis peaks in the **second leaf from the top** (a net maximum of 35.92 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and falls to **16.00** in the seventh. The **apparent quantum efficiency**, the carbon fixed per photon in dim light, stays high (0.054 to 0.056) in the top three leaves, and dark respiration falls from about 0.9 to 0.46 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ down the canopy. The authors find the modified rectangular hyperbola tracks the bend and plateau best; the plain rectangular hyperbola overshoots the maximum.

## Checked with the tools

The paper gives its fitted values in figures, not tables, so we do not have the measured curves. Instead we **simulated** two leaves from the top-of-range values in the text, using the non-rectangular hyperbola with a curvature of 0.7 (a typical value; **our assumption**, as the paper does not state one), the paper's 19 light steps and a random scatter of 0.3 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Then we fitted them with the tool's maths.

**Step 1: the shape of the curves.** The second leaf needs a gross maximum (Amax, before respiration) of 42.2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ to reach a net 35.9 at 2000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹; the seventh leaf needs 17.8 to reach 16.0. Figure 1 shows the two simulated curves and the fits: the upper leaf is still climbing at full sun, while the lower one has nearly levelled off by 1000.

**Step 2: what the fitter recovers.** From the scattered points it finds, for the second leaf, Amax 41.2 ± 0.6, quantum yield φ 0.0547 ± 0.0017, curvature 0.75 ± 0.03 and dark respiration 0.79 ± 0.17 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹; for the seventh leaf, 17.7, 0.0371, 0.74 and 0.44. All are within about one standard error of the values we put in. Dark respiration is the loosest: only the 0 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ point measures it directly, which is why the paper's own respiration values differ between models.

**Step 3: the light compensation point**, the light at which a leaf just breaks even. The fitter puts it at **16.1 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for the second leaf** and **12.0 for the seventh** (Figure 2). The upper leaf's value sits at the top of the paper's range of 13.3 to 16.3. A quick check anyone can do: dark respiration divided by quantum yield, 0.90 ÷ 0.0563 = 16.0, gives nearly the same number, because the curve is almost a straight line in dim light.

**Step 4: the light saturation point is a matter of definition.** The paper reports 1673 to 1867 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. For our second-leaf curve, the light at which net photosynthesis reaches 75 % of its maximum is **1090**; for 90 %, **2544**, beyond the highest light measured; for 95 %, nearly 4900. For the seventh leaf the same three give 675, 1576 and 3015. A "saturation point" means little without its rule, and on a curve that bends gently (θ 0.7) it moves a long way with the rule. The paper's values most likely come from a different model and definition, so compare like with like.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/rice-leaf-light-response-canopy-position.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `nrhGross` (the non-rectangular hyperbola) and `fitLight` (the least-squares fit, with the compensation and saturation points) from `src/lib/sci/photosynth.ts`, the maths behind the [[photosynthesis-curve-fitter]].

The 19 light steps, the net maxima, quantum efficiencies and dark respiration values come from the paper's text. The curvature of 0.7, the 0.3 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ scatter and the random draws are **our assumptions**. The curves are **simulated**, not the paper's measurements.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/photosynthesis-curve-fitter.mjs`, which pins the fitter against SciPy:

- On a made-up 12-point curve, the four-parameter fit must give Amax 21.812, φ 0.05223, θ 0.7892 and Rd 1.553, as SciPy's `curve_fit` does, with matching standard errors.
- The same curve's light compensation point must be 30.23 and its 90 % saturation point 1159 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, found by root-finding in SciPy.
- New with this article: the exact seventh-leaf rice curve, rounded to 0.01, must give back Amax 17.80, φ 0.0385, θ 0.70 and Rd 0.46, a compensation point of 12.0 and a 90 % saturation point of 1576 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Fit your own gas-exchange curves** in the browser, with standard errors, and compare leaves, varieties or treatments on the same model.
- **Report saturation points with their rule** (75, 85, 90 or 95 % of maximum), so other people can compare them.
- **Check a paper's compensation point** quickly as dark respiration over quantum yield, then refit properly.
- **Plan a light-curve protocol**: simulate a curve, drop light steps and see which parameters lose precision (dark respiration needs low-light points).
- **Feed canopy models**: fit leaves at several heights, then scale up with leaf area from the [[leaf-area-analyser]].

## The fine print

- Our curves are **simulated** from values in the paper's text; the measured points are only in its figures.
- We used only the non-rectangular hyperbola. The paper also fits three other models, and its parameters differ a little between them.
- The curvature of 0.7 is our assumption; a sharper bend would give a lower saturation point.
- The paper gives the quantum efficiency's units in two ways; we read it as mol CO₂ per mol photons.
