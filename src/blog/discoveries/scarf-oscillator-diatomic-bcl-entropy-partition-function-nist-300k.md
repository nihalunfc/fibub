How much entropy does a diatomic gas carry, and why does its heat capacity creep up as it gets hot? Statistical mechanics answers both from one list: the molecule's energy levels. A 2023 paper in *Frontiers in Physics* wrote down a new model for how a diatomic molecule vibrates and then used it to predict the thermodynamic functions of three halogen-containing molecules (BCl, BrF and BrCl) between 300 and 6000 K. One of its tables prints 41 vibrational levels for BCl, which is all the [[partition-function]] tool needs for the vibrational part. Adding a rotor and translation gives the full entropy, so we check the paper's own thermodynamics against its own levels. For the levels themselves see [[diatomic-spectroscopy]], and for heats of reaction use the [[thermochemistry-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors start from a mathematical potential called the scarf oscillator, tune its parameters to each molecule's known bond energy, bond length and vibrational frequency, and solve for the vibrational energy levels. They then sum over the levels to get the vibrational partition function, and from it the entropy, reduced enthalpy, Gibbs energy and heat capacity at constant pressure between 300 and 6000 K. They compare each with NIST reference values and report average deviations of a fraction of a per cent for entropy and about 2 % for heat capacity. For BCl they print the 41 vibrational term values (counted from the bottom of the potential well), the bond length of 1.7159 angstrom, and the model and NIST numbers at the two ends of the range.

## Checked with the tools

For a gas of diatomic molecules at 0.1 MPa, the entropy is the sum of three independent parts. The first is translation, from the Sackur-Tetrode equation. The second is rotation, from levels B J(J+1) with degeneracy 2J + 1, where B follows from the bond length and the reduced mass. The third is vibration, which is a sum over the 41 levels the paper printed. The heat capacity at constant pressure is the sum of the three heat capacities plus R. We used the 11B-35Cl isotopologue and a rigid rotor, both **our assumption**, and fed the 41 printed levels (measured from the lowest one, since only differences matter) to the partition-function maths.

| Quantity | Tool + rotor + translation | NIST value (printed in paper) | Paper's model |
|---|---|---|---|
| S at 300 K (J/(mol K)) | 213.386 | 213.441 | 212.994 |
| Cp at 300 K (J/(mol K)) | 31.655 | 31.693 | 33.746 |
| S at 6000 K (J/(mol K)) | 322.317 | 323.737 | 322.508 |
| Cp at 6000 K (J/(mol K)) | 37.867 | 39.742 | 38.751 |

**What the recomputation shows.** At 300 K the entropy built from the paper's own levels is 0.06 J/(mol K) below NIST, closer than the paper's model (0.45 below). The heat capacity is closer still: 31.655 against NIST 31.693, where the paper's model value sits 2.05 above. The 300 K split is translation 156.73, rotation 55.87 and vibration 0.78 J/(mol K), and the rotational constant that results is B = 0.6838 cm⁻¹. The paper's heat-capacity error at 300 K is therefore not caused by its energy levels. It must come from how those levels are summed or combined with rotation, which the page text did not let us see. At 6000 K our numbers fall below NIST by 1.4 (entropy) and 1.9 (Cp), and below the paper's model for Cp. The reason is visible in the tool: with only 41 levels, the highest level at 27,284 cm⁻¹ still holds about 0.03 % of the molecules at 6000 K, and keeping only 21 levels would give Cp = 34.90 instead of 37.87. Levels beyond v = 40 (the paper says the upper limit for BCl is v = 102) and the coupling of rotation with vibration add the rest. So the agreement at room temperature is real, and the gap at 6000 K is a known truncation effect rather than a failure of the formulas.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/scarf-oscillator-diatomic-bcl-entropy-partition-function-nist-300k.mjs` imports `thermo`, `presets`, `UNIT`, `KB`, `NA`, `H` and `C0` from `src/lib/sci/phpartition.ts`, the maths of the Partition function explorer. The 41 levels, the bond length and the printed NIST and model values come from the paper. The isotope masses, the rigid-rotor rotation and the Sackur-Tetrode term are our assumptions (standard formulas, not taken from the paper). The vibrational and rotational parts are `thermo()` of the level lists (rotor levels from the `rotor` preset, enough of them to hold the sum); the lines are 60 temperatures from 300 to 6000 K.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/partition-function.mjs` pins the tool against an independent NumPy script: a rigid rotor with B = 10 cm⁻¹ at 300 K gives Z = 21.1876, an oscillator at 1000 K the Einstein heat capacity 0.84398 kB, and a two-level system the Schottky curve. New with this article, the same file checks the BCl numbers above: the rotor with B = 0.68379 cm⁻¹ at 300 K gives 55.874 J/(mol K), the first six printed levels give a vibrational entropy of 0.7846 J/(mol K), and the whole sum with translation gives 213.386 J/(mol K). It runs on every change with `node tests/sci.mjs`.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[partition-function]] tool, set **Energy unit** to cm⁻¹ and **Temperature (K)** to 300.
2. Under "Or make levels from a formula" choose **Kind** "Rigid rotor B·J(J+1), g = 2J+1", set **Spacing or B** to 0.68379 and **Number of levels** to 100, then press **Fill in the levels**.
3. The tool shows the entropy per mole as about 55.87 J/(mol K), and the heat capacity close to R (8.314 J/(mol K)).
4. Replace the levels by the first six BCl term values, one per line: 418.228, 1249.181, 2071.965, 2886.579, 3693.023 and 4491.297. The entropy per mole reads about 0.785 J/(mol K) and Cv about 2.55 J/(mol K).
5. Add 156.73 (translation, from the Sackur-Tetrode equation) to get the 213.4 J/(mol K) in the table.

## What researchers can use it for

- Checking a published thermodynamic table: rebuild S and Cp from the printed levels, as here, and see which column of the paper's table disagrees.
- Seeing how many levels a high-temperature calculation needs: add levels until the top one holds a negligible share, which the tool warns about.
- Splitting an entropy into translation, rotation and vibration to see which part dominates (translation, at 300 K).
- Teaching: a real molecule's levels make the step from "levels" to "heat capacity" concrete, and [[heat-capacity-fitter]] compares the result with measured data.

## The fine print

This recomputes the paper's thermodynamics from the paper's printed vibrational levels; it does not test whether the scarf potential is a good model, and it uses a rigid rotor, an ideal gas and a single isotopologue, so rotation-vibration coupling, centrifugal distortion, electronic states and natural isotope mixing are left out. The paper's own values at 300 K and 6000 K are the only ones its text showed us. Its abstract and results give slightly different average deviations for a few quantities, so we quote only its printed table values. Education, not reference data: use NIST or JANAF tables for design work.
