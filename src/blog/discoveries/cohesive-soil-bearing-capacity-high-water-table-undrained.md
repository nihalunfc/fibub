Peshawar's Warsak Road sits on soft cohesive soil with the water table close to the surface, which is awkward ground for the shallow pad footings most low-rise buildings use. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Earth Science* sampled five sites along the road, measured shear strength and consolidation in the laboratory, modelled a loaded footing in a finite element package and reported an ultimate bearing capacity for every site by two classic equations. The useful thing for anyone else is that the paper prints the soil numbers it used, so the whole calculation can be redone in a browser: this article does exactly that with the [[bearing-capacity|bearing capacity calculator]], and shows that the two "different theories" in the paper differ only by one degree of friction angle. The soil descriptions and the settlement half of the paper are jobs for the [[soil-classification]] and [[consolidation-settlement]] tools.

## The paper in brief

The authors took samples from five sites (called S1 to S5 here) at about 2 m depth, ran unconfined compression, direct shear and oedometer tests, and recorded standard penetration test blow counts. Cohesion came out between **9.94 kPa (S4)** and **20.2 kPa (S5)**, with friction angles of only about **1°**: these are clays tested as if undrained, where strength comes almost entirely from cohesion.

They then put those strengths through the Meyerhof and Vesic bearing capacity equations and reported ultimate bearing capacities from **50.88 kPa (S4)** to **103.82 kPa (S5)** for Meyerhof, and values about 5 % higher for Vesic. A finite element model gave a third set, close to the Vesic column. Separately, SPT blow counts gave allowable pressures of 19.16 to 57.60 kPa. The conclusion is a practical one: on these soils a shallow footing has little capacity to spare, and S4 is the weakest site.

The paper never states a footing width, a founding depth or a factor of safety, which is where a tool earns its keep.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the paper's own columns, reproduced.** Running the tool's Meyerhof maths for a **strip footing at the ground surface** with the friction angle set to **zero** gives back the paper's Meyerhof column to within half a per cent at every site (Figure 1). That pins down what the paper actually computed: the term `c·Nc` alone, with `Nc = π + 2 = 5.14`, no surcharge term and no shape factor.

**Step 2: the Vesic column is the same equation at 1°.** Feed the tool the paper's measured friction angles (about 1°) instead of zero and the same strip footing reproduces the Vesic column to within about 1 %. The factor rises from 5.14 to **5.39** at φ = 1.05°. So the roughly 5 % gap between the paper's two columns is not a difference between Meyerhof's and Vesic's theories at all; it is one degree of friction angle, applied in one column and dropped in the other.

**Step 3: a real footing is stronger than the paper's number.** A surface strip footing is the worst case. Put the same soils under a **1.5 m square pad with its base 2 m down** (the paper's own sampling depth; the size and depth are **our assumption**), with the water table 1 m below ground and a saturated unit weight of 20 kN/m³ (also **our assumption**), and the tool gives, for S1, a cohesion term of 142.4 kPa, a surcharge term of 27.5 kPa and an ultimate 169.9 kPa: **1.8 times** the paper's 93.5. Two things did that: Meyerhof's square-shape factor on the cohesion term, and the depth factor that grows with Df/B.

Divide by a factor of safety of 3 and the allowable pressures become 56.6, 48.7, 43.7, 35.0 and 61.9 kPa, which is close to the paper's SPT-based allowable values (57.45, 47.88, 38.30, 19.16, 57.60 kPa) at four of the five sites. The odd one out is **S4**, where the SPT route gives 19.16 kPa, about half what the strength-based route gives: worth a second look before designing on that site.

**Step 4: the water table matters less than you would think.** Moving the water table from 1 m depth up to the ground surface drops S1's ultimate capacity only from 169.9 to 162.8 kPa, about 4 %, because with φ ≈ 0 the water can only shrink the surcharge term; the `½γB·Nγ` term, the one water really punishes, is zero. On a sand the same change would cut capacity by nearly half. Figure 2 shows that, and shows the whole depth curve.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/cohesive-soil-bearing-capacity-high-water-table-undrained.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `bearing()` and `meyerhofN()` from `src/lib/sci/bearing.ts`, the maths behind the [[bearing-capacity|bearing capacity calculator]]: the Prandtl–Reissner factors, Meyerhof's shape and depth factors, the three water table cases and the allowable pressure and load.

Cohesion, friction angle, bulk density and the reported capacities come from the paper's text and tables. Unit weights were taken as bulk density × 9.81 m/s². The footing size (1.5 m square), its depth (2 m), the water table depth (1 m), the saturated unit weight (20 kN/m³) and the factor of safety (3) are **our assumptions**, because the paper gives none. Nothing here is a re-measurement: it is a recomputation from the paper's own stated strengths.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/bearing-capacity.mjs` runs on every change to fibub and pins the tool against an independent Python implementation of the same textbook formulas:

- All three sets of factors (Terzaghi, Meyerhof, Hansen) must match Python at every 5° from 0 to 50° to one part in 10¹², including the limits Nc = 5.7124 (Terzaghi) and 5.1416 (Prandtl) at φ = 0.
- 60 footings covering three methods, four shapes, three water table cases and local shear must match Python's ultimate bearing capacity to the same tolerance; Meyerhof at φ = 30° must give Nc 30.14, Nq 18.40, Nγ 15.67.
- New with this article: the S1 soil (c = 18.22 kPa) as a surface strip footing must give qu = 93.68 kPa at φ = 0 and 98.26 kPa at φ = 1.05°, and the 1.5 m square pad 2 m down with the water table at 1 m must give qu = 169.9 kPa, an allowable 56.6 kPa and an allowable load of 127 kN.

## Check it yourself

On the [[bearing-capacity|bearing capacity calculator]]:

1. Set *Method* to **Meyerhof (1963)** and *Footing shape* to **Strip (long wall footing)**.
2. Type *Width B (m)* **1**, *Depth to footing base Df (m)* **0**, *Cohesion c′ (kPa)* **18.22**, *Friction angle φ′ (°)* **0**, *Unit weight above water γ (kN/m³)* **17.27**, and clear *Water table depth below ground (m)* so it is blank. Leave *Factor of safety* at **3**.
3. The result reads **ultimate bearing capacity qu = 93.7 kPa**, allowable 31.2 kPa, load 31.2 kN/m. That is the paper's S1 Meyerhof value of 93.54 kPa.
4. Change *Friction angle φ′ (°)* to **1.05**: qu becomes **98.3 kPa**, the paper's Vesic value of 98.38 kPa.
5. Now the real footing: *Friction angle φ′* back to **0**, *Footing shape* **Square**, *Width B (m)* **1.5**, *Depth to footing base Df (m)* **2**, *Saturated unit weight γsat (kN/m³)* **20**, *Water table depth below ground (m)* **1**. You should see **qu = 170 kPa**, allowable **56.6 kPa** and a load of about **127 kN**, with the three terms printed as 142 + 27.5 + 0.
6. Set the water table to **0** and watch qu fall only to **163 kPa**: with φ = 0 the water table is almost beside the point.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published bearing capacity** in a minute: set the shape, depth and water table the paper describes and see whether its number can be reproduced. If it only comes out with φ = 0 and Df = 0, the paper reported a lower bound, not a design value.
- **Report the assumptions, not just the answer.** The tool prints the three terms separately, so a paper can say which term carried the capacity.
- **Compare theories honestly** with the side-by-side Terzaghi, Meyerhof and Hansen table, instead of comparing two equations with different inputs.
- **Classify the soil first** with the [[soil-classification]] tool and check its plasticity with [[atterberg-compaction|the Atterberg limits tool]], so an undrained analysis is used only where it belongs.
- **Follow the settlement**, which usually governs on soft clay, with [[consolidation-settlement]] and the paper's own oedometer coefficients.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation** from the paper's stated strengths, not a re-measurement. We never saw the soil.
- The paper's text and its Table 3 disagree slightly: cohesion 15.23 against 15.34 kPa at S2, and friction angles of 1.05 and 1.04° in the text against 0.85 and 1.03° in the table at S1 and S3. We used the text values, which fit its reported capacities better.
- Vesic's equation is not in the tool. The match in step 2 says only that the paper's Vesic column equals the same Prandtl–Reissner Nc evaluated at the measured friction angle, which at φ ≈ 1° the two theories share.
- The footing size, depth, water table depth, saturated unit weight and factor of safety are our assumptions; change them and the allowable pressure changes with them.
- A factor of safety of 3 on the gross pressure is a convention, not a code requirement. Local codes govern; check the official source.
