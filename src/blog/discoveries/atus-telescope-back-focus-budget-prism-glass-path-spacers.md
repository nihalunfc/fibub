Every camera on every telescope has to sit at one particular distance behind the last optical surface, and almost nobody gets there in one piece: the distance is made up of a camera body, a filter wheel, an off-axis guider, adapters and a pile of spacer rings. A 2026 paper in *PASP* tells the ten-year story of the **Astronomical Telescope of the University of Stuttgart**, and along the way it publishes the kind of numbers this arithmetic needs: three optical configurations with their flange focal distances, and the 8.52 mm that a 25 mm glass prism adds to the guider's light path. Both are things the [[backfocus-calculator|Backfocus calculator]] exists to work out, so this article checks the tool against them. For the field of view and sampling that go with those focal lengths, see the [[telescope-calculator|telescope calculator]]; for whether the focus is actually right, the [[star-fwhm-checker|star FWHM checker]].

## The paper in brief

ATUS is a 0.6 m telescope at f/8.17 with a 4899.8 mm focal length, and the paper is unusually honest about everything that had to be fixed. The mirrors arrived with the wrong spacing: the authors found the best separation at **1187.4 mm**, about 12 mm from the delivered value, which lengthened the focal length from 4740 to 4900 mm and pushed the focal plane back by roughly **100 mm**. That alone changed every adapter in the back end. They also give the two alternative configurations, with a field flattener (5236.2 mm, f/8.73) and with a focal reducer (3836.1 mm, f/6.39), and the distance from the mounting flange to the focal plane in each case: **303.5, 228.1 and 83.7 mm**.

The second half is about image quality: a pointing model good to under an arcminute for 95 % of slews, tracking drift under 10 arcseconds in five hours, and a final FWHM floor near **1.3 arcseconds** at 0.547 arcseconds per pixel. The small number we use here is in the guider description: the off-axis guider's **25 mm right-angle BK7 pick-off prism extends the optical path to its camera by 8.52 mm**.

## Checked with the tools

The Backfocus calculator does two things: it adds the glass in the path (a filter, a prism, a window) as an extra distance, and it picks the best stack from the spacers you own. We checked both against the paper.

**Step 1: the prism, to a hundredth of a millimetre.** A flat block of glass of thickness t and refractive index n moves the focus back by **t(1 − 1/n)**, because light inside the glass is slowed and the rays emerge less steeply converged. For 25 mm of N-BK7 at its catalogue index of 1.5168 the tool gives **8.5179 mm**, which rounds to the paper's **8.52 mm**. Using the index at 550 nm instead (1.5185) gives 8.5364, still 8.54 to two places, so the paper's figure pins down both the thickness and roughly the glass. Figure 1 puts the paper's prism on the tool's own curve.

The same formula settles a smaller question in the paper: its wavefront sensor uses a **2 µm pellicle** beam splitter, and 2 µm of glass shifts the focus by **0.68 µm**. That is why a pellicle is used at all. A 1 mm glass beam splitter would have moved the focus by a third of a millimetre, several times the depth of focus.

**Step 2: the three configurations as back-focus targets.** Taking the paper's flange focal distances as the target, and an example camera train of our own (camera flange-to-sensor 17.5 mm, filter wheel 20 mm, off-axis guider 25 mm, with a 3 mm filter at n = 1.5 adding 1.00 mm), the tool reports the gap to fill and the best stack from a set of rings that includes the paper's 88.9 mm ones:

| Configuration | Flange focal distance (paper) | Gap left after our 62.5 mm of parts and a 1.00 mm filter shift | Best stack from the rings | Miss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fully reflective, f/8.17 | 303.5 mm | 242.00 mm | 227.20 mm, everything we own | 14.8 mm short |
| With the field flattener, f/8.73 | 228.1 mm | 166.60 mm | 88.9 + 50 + 16.5 + 10 + 1 + 0.3 | 0.10 mm long |
| With the focal reducer, f/6.39 | 83.7 mm | 22.20 mm | 20 + 2 + 0.3 | 0.10 mm long |

That first row is the useful kind of failure: the tool says plainly that no combination of these rings reaches 242 mm, you are 14.8 mm short, go and buy a tube. The paper hit the same wall in real life and moved from 2.7-inch extension rings to Takahashi M98 tubes, partly for clear aperture and partly for length.

**Step 3: how fine your shims have to be.** A miss of 0.1 mm sounds harmless. The **critical focus zone**, the range over which a star stays acceptably sharp, is about 2λN² for a focal ratio N: at f/8.73 and 550 nm that is **0.084 mm**, and at f/6.39 only **0.045 mm** (our arithmetic, with the common rule of thumb). So the 0.1 mm miss above is outside tolerance for both the flattener and the reducer. Figure 2 sweeps the required gap in 0.1 mm steps and shows the tool's best-stack error: a ring set that stops at 0.3 mm misses by up to **0.1 mm** somewhere, while adding plain 0.1 and 0.2 mm shims brings **every** gap exactly on target.

**Step 4: the guider's own spacing.** Put the prism in as the glass, with a guide camera 17.5 mm deep and a 55 mm nominal back focus, and the tool asks for **46.02 mm** of spacers, offers **30 + 10 + 5 + 1 = 46 mm** and says it is 0.02 mm short. That is well inside the critical focus zone, and it is the calculation that stops a guide camera from being focused 8.5 mm away from the science camera, which is the trap the paper's 8.52 mm is warning about.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/atus-telescope-back-focus-budget-prism-glass-path-spacers.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `filterShift`, `readSpacers` and `solveSpacers` from `src/lib/sci/backfocus.ts`, the whole of the maths behind the [[backfocus-calculator|Backfocus calculator]]: the glass shift t(1 − 1/n) and a 0/1 knapsack over the spacers you own, which keeps the stack with the fewest pieces among equally close ones.

**From the paper:** the 25 mm BK7 prism and its 8.52 mm, the 2 µm pellicle, the three flange focal distances and focal ratios, and the 88.9 mm spacer rings. **Our inputs:** the refractive indices (catalogue values, not stated in the paper), the example camera train of 17.5 + 20 + 25 mm with a 3 mm filter, the spacer sets, and the 2λN² critical-focus-zone rule. The paper does not publish its camera, filter wheel or adapter lengths, so the budget table is an illustration built on the paper's targets, not a reconstruction of its real back end.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks this module directly:

- `filterShift` must give exactly two-thirds of a millimetre for 2 mm of n = 1.5 glass, a third of the thickness.
- `readSpacers` must read "10, 2 x3, 0.5mm" as the five lengths 10, 2, 2, 2 and 0.5.
- `solveSpacers` must land 0.03 mm from a 9.17 mm gap with that set, using only four pieces, must hit 7.3 mm exactly as 5 + 2 + 0.3, and must refuse a negative gap (parts already longer than the back focus).
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/backfocus-calculator.mjs`: 25 mm of BK7 at n = 1.5168 must shift the focus by 8.5179 mm, so that it still rounds to the paper's 8.52; a 2 µm pellicle must shift it by 0.68 µm; and the paper's three flange focal distances must come out of our example train as gaps of 242.00, 166.60 and 22.20 mm, with the 242 mm case correctly reported as 14.8 mm beyond what the rings can reach.

## Check it yourself

On the [[backfocus-calculator|Backfocus calculator]] page, for the prism:

1. Leave *Backfocus needed (mm)* at **55**.
2. In *Parts in the light path*, clear the box and type one line: `Guide camera (flange to sensor) 17.5`.
3. Set *Filter thickness (mm)* to **25** and *Filter glass index* to **1.5168**. That is the paper's prism, standing in for any glass in the path.
4. In *Spacers you have*, type `30, 20, 10, 5, 3, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.3`.
5. The result reads **Add 46.02 mm of spacers**, with *Plus the filter shift* **+8.52 mm** (the paper's number), *Best stack from your spacers* **30 + 10 + 5 + 1 = 46 mm** and *Total spacing* **63.5 mm, short by 0.02 mm**.
6. Now the flattener configuration: set *Backfocus needed* to **228.1**, the parts box back to its three lines (`Camera (flange to sensor) 17.5`, `Filter wheel 20`, `Off-axis guider 25`), *Filter thickness* to **3**, *Filter glass index* to **1.5**, and the spacers to `88.9, 50, 30, 20, 16.5, 10, 5, 3, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.3`. It asks for **166.6 mm** and gets within **0.1 mm**.
7. Change *Backfocus needed* to **303.5**, the fully reflective configuration, and the tool says the best stack is 227.2 mm: **14.8 mm short**. Add `75` to your spacer list and it lands on target.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Plan a back end before buying adapters**: add up what you have, see the gap, and find out whether your rings can reach it at all.
- **Keep a guider and a science camera at the same focus** when a pick-off prism, dichroic or window sits in one of the paths, using the same t(1 − 1/n) the paper applies to its prism.
- **Decide how fine a shim set you need** from the focal ratio: 0.1 mm rings are enough at f/8, but f/4 wants 0.05 mm and real measurement.
- **Check a published optical train** for internal consistency, as here, before copying its numbers into your own design.
- **Re-budget after an optical change.** The paper's mirror re-spacing moved the focal plane by about 100 mm; a back-focus list makes that kind of change a five-minute recalculation instead of a surprise.

## The fine print

- The numbers come from the authors' accepted version (arXiv:2602.16415); the licence and DOI are from the journal's Crossref record. We have not read the typeset version of record.
- The camera train in the budget table is ours. The paper does not give its camera, filter wheel or adapter lengths, so those rows show how the tool behaves, not what ATUS is built from.
- Refractive indices are catalogue values for N-BK7 and fused silica. The paper names BK7 but not an index or a wavelength, and glass dispersion moves the 8.52 mm by about 0.02 mm across the visible.
- The critical focus zone rule 2λN² is a rule of thumb for where a star looks sharp, not a specification; real tolerances depend on the corrector and the sensor size.
- Nothing here checks the paper's optical design, its pointing model or its image quality. The flange focal distances are treated only as back-focus targets.
