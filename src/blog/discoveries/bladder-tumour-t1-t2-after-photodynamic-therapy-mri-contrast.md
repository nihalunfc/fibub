A relaxation time is not a picture. When a paper reports that a tissue's **T1** fell from 1352 to 369 milliseconds, a radiologist still has to ask the practical question: on which scan, at which settings, would that show up, and how brightly? A 2025 study in *Biomedicines* measured T1 and T2 of bladder tissue at 1.5 tesla before and after **photodynamic therapy**, a treatment that uses a light-activated drug to kill tumour cells. This article feeds those measured numbers into the [[mri-bloch-simulator]], which steps the Bloch equations, and works out the signal each tissue would give, the settings that separate them best, and the inversion times that would make each one disappear. To carry on from there, open real scans with the [[dicom-viewer]] or compare the physics with the [[ct-reconstruction]] demo.

## The paper in brief

The authors worked on **non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer** (NMIBC), the common kind that sits in the bladder's lining. They took 50 tissue samples from 25 patients: 25 pieces of healthy wall from the resection margin and 25 pieces of tumour. The tumour samples were then treated in the laboratory with **5-aminolevulinic acid** (5-ALA) photodynamic therapy and measured again, so each tumour acts as its own control.

Mapping was done on a 1.5 T scanner with fast spin-echo sequences: for T1, twelve acquisitions with repetition times from 50 to 15,000 ms at a 3 ms echo time; for T2, eleven echo times from 10 to 260 ms at a fixed 10,000 ms repetition time. Each map was fitted pixel by pixel and averaged over a hand-drawn region.

The results are a clean ladder. **T1** fell from 1351.7 ms in healthy wall to 727.7 ms in untreated tumour and 368.9 ms after therapy. **T2** fell from 93.5 to 78.5 to 55.7 ms. The analysis of variance was much stronger for T1 (F = 184.4) than for T2 (F = 16.7), and the T2 difference between healthy wall and untreated tumour was **not** statistically significant.

## Checked with the tools

What follows is a **recomputation**: we take the paper's six mean relaxation times as given and work out what they imply for a scan. There is no image data here, and we are not rechecking the paper's fits. One assumption matters throughout: we give all three tissues the same **proton density** of 1, because the paper does not measure it (**our assumption**). So every signal below is relaxation-only, in fractions of the full magnetisation M₀.

**Step 1: what a routine T1-weighted scan would show.** The simulator's own default spin echo (TR 500 ms, TE 15 ms) gives **0.257** of M₀ for healthy wall, **0.402** for untreated tumour and **0.559** for tumour after therapy. The treated tissue is more than twice as bright as the healthy wall. The short-T1 tissue wins because it recovers most between pulses, which is exactly what "T1-weighted" means.

**Step 3: the physics explains the paper's statistics.** Look at the first row. On T1 weighting, healthy wall and untreated tumour differ by 0.188 of M₀; on T2 weighting the same pair differ by only **0.064**, about a third as much, while their measurement scatter (standard deviations of 20.3 and 20.4 ms, a quarter of the values themselves) is proportionally larger. That is precisely why the paper's T2 comparison between those two groups failed to reach significance while every T1 comparison passed. The Bloch maths and the analysis of variance agree without either knowing about the other.

**Step 4: nulling, and why it is an easy win here.** On an inversion-recovery sequence there is an inversion time **TI** at which a chosen tissue gives no signal at all. With a 2500 ms repetition time the simulator puts the null at **739 ms** for healthy wall, **481 ms** for untreated tumour and **255 ms** for treated tumour. Those are far apart, so a single inversion pulse could blank the healthy wall and leave both tumour states visible, or blank the treated tissue and show only what the therapy has not reached. For a very long TR the rule collapses to the textbook TI = T1 × ln 2 (937, 504 and 256 ms); at TR 2500 ms the healthy wall's null is 200 ms earlier than that, because it never fully recovers between inversions.

**Step 5: a sanity check on the paper's own mapping protocol.** The paper's shortest repetition time, 50 ms, leaves the healthy wall at 3.3 % of M₀ and the treated tumour at 11.3 %: enough of a spread to anchor the fit at the fast end. Its longest, 15,000 ms, is 11 times the healthy T1, so all three tissues are above 94 % recovered and the plateau is well defined. At the other end, the last echo (TE 260 ms) leaves 6.2 % of the healthy signal but only **0.9 %** of the treated tumour's, which is near the noise of most receive coils. Short of extra averaging, late echoes contribute little for the fastest-decaying tissue, which may be part of why the treated group's T2 has the smallest spread in the paper (6.1 ms) while its T2 is the least constrained by the long echoes.

**Step 6: the two ways of computing the signal agree.** The tool gives both a closed-form signal and the result of stepping the Bloch equations through repeated excitations until they settle. For all three tissues at TR 500 ms, TE 15 ms, the formula and the stepped simulation agree to five decimal places (0.25682, 0.40192, 0.55885). A difference between the two columns on the tool page would mean a timing problem in the sequence, not a tissue effect.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/bladder-tumour-t1-t2-after-photodynamic-therapy-mri-contrast.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `signalFormula`, `steadyBloch`, `nullTI` and `ernst` from `src/lib/sci/phmri.ts`, the maths behind the [[mri-bloch-simulator]].

The six relaxation times, their standard deviations, the field strength and the mapping protocol (12 repetition times, 11 echo times) come from the paper's abstract and methods. Equal proton density of 1 for all three tissues, the 10 ms echo time used for the TR sweep and the 10,000 ms repetition time used for the TE sweep are **our choices**, the last two taken from the extremes the paper itself used. The simulator is a teaching model: hard pulses exactly on resonance, perfect spoiling at the end of each repetition, and no diffusion, flow or field inhomogeneity.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/mri-bloch-simulator.mjs`, which pins the simulator against an independent numpy solver written for the tests:

- Seven sequence-and-tissue combinations must match the numpy steady state to 2 × 10⁻⁶, by the closed formula and by stepping the Bloch equations: for instance a spin echo at TR 500, TE 15 on a tissue with T1 780 ms and T2 90 ms must give 0.39198.
- The inversion time from `nullTI` must drive the inversion-recovery signal to exactly zero, and must tend to T1 × ln 2 as TR grows.
- The Ernst angle must be the flip angle that maximises the gradient-echo signal, checked half a degree either side.
- New with this article: the paper's three tissues at TR 500 ms, TE 15 ms must give 0.25682, 0.40192 and 0.55885 of M₀ by both routes; their inversion nulls at TR 2500 ms must be 739, 481 and 255 ms; and the best TR for separating healthy wall from untreated tumour at TE 10 ms must be 954 ms, with a signal difference of 0.188.

## Check it yourself

On the [[mri-bloch-simulator]] page:

1. In the *Tissues* table, overwrite the first three rows. Row 1: name **Healthy wall**, *T1 (ms)* **1351.7**, *T2 (ms)* **93.5**, *PD* **1**. Row 2: **Tumour**, **727.7**, **78.5**, **1**. Row 3: **After PDT**, **368.9**, **55.7**, **1**.
2. Leave *Preset* on **T1-weighted spin echo (TR 500, TE 15)**. The *Signal and contrast* table should read about **0.257, 0.402 and 0.559** in both signal columns, with the fourth row (the fluid-like tissue, which you can ignore) far dimmer.
3. Change *Preset* to **T2-weighted spin echo (TR 4000, TE 100)**: the order flips, and the healthy wall becomes the brightest at **0.324** against **0.278** and **0.166**. That flip is the whole idea of weighting.
4. Set *Preset* to **My own settings**, *Sequence* to **Spin echo**, *TR* to **632** and *TE* to **10**: the healthy wall and the treated tumour now differ by about **0.35** of M₀, the largest gap any spin echo can give these two tissues.
5. Set *Sequence* to **Inversion recovery**, *TR* to **2500**, *TE* to **10** and *TI* to **739**. The healthy wall's signal drops to nearly zero while the two tumour rows stay bright; the table's *Null TI* column lists **739, 481 and 255 ms**.
6. Set *Sequence* to **Spoiled gradient echo** with *TR* **30**: the *Ernst angle* column reads about **12.0°, 16.3° and 22.8°**, the flip angles that give each tissue its most signal at that repetition time.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn published relaxometry into a protocol**: any paper that reports T1 and T2 for two tissues can be converted, in a minute, into the TR and TE that separate them best.
- **Sanity-check a mapping protocol before scanning**: see whether the shortest TR and the longest TE actually bracket the relaxation times you expect, as in step 5.
- **Plan a nulling sequence**: read the inversion time that blanks a tissue at your real TR, not just the textbook T1 × ln 2.
- **Teach weighting honestly**: the same three tissues changing rank between a T1- and a T2-weighted scan makes the point far better than words.
- **Predict which comparison will be statistically weak**: divide the signal difference by the measurement scatter before you run the study, as the T2 healthy-against-tumour pair shows.

## The fine print

- The paper's samples were measured **ex vivo**, outside the body. Blood flow, breathing and bladder filling all change what an in-vivo scan would show.
- We assumed equal proton density. A real tumour may hold more or less water than healthy wall, which would shift every brightness up or down.
- The simulator ignores T2*, so its gradient-echo numbers are optimistic, and it assumes perfect pulses and perfect spoiling.
- We used the group **means** only. With standard deviations as wide as a quarter of the T1 values, individual samples overlap, so these settings are a starting point for a protocol, not a diagnostic rule.
- The article was republished with a minor typographical correction; the relaxation times we used are unaffected.
