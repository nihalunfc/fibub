Most controllers in factories are still PI or PID loops, and the hardest part of using one is choosing its gains when the process has **dead time**, a pure delay between acting and seeing the result (a long pipe, a conveyor, a slow sensor). A 2025 paper in *Processes* takes a graphical tuning rule, the **weighted geometrical center** (WGC) method, and adds an optimisation step that picks a better point inside the region of stable gains. For a simple delayed process it reports that the settling time falls from 3.39 s to 1.94 s. We rebuilt that loop in the [[pid-simulator]] and got both numbers back to the hundredth of a second. To go further, design a full state-feedback loop with [[pole-placement]], or see how noisy measurements are cleaned up before they reach a controller with the [[kalman-filter-playground]].

## The paper in brief

A PI controller sets its output from the error *e* (setpoint minus measurement) as *u* = kp·*e* + ki·∫*e* dt: a **proportional gain** kp that reacts to the error now and an **integral gain** ki that removes any error that lingers. For a given process, only some pairs (kp, ki) give a stable loop; drawn on a kp–ki plane they form a **stability region**. The WGC method takes a weighted centre of that region as the tuning, which keeps the gains well away from the edge where the loop would go unstable.

The author's change is to search around that centre for the point that minimises a cost built from the step response, so that the loop is still safely inside the region but faster. The paper shows it on three processes. In the first, a process with a gain of 1, a time constant of 1 s and a dead time of 0.5 s, written e^(−0.5s)/(s + 1), the plain WGC method gives kp 1.0539 and ki 1.1811, and the optimised version kp 0.9939 and ki 0.9311. The paper states that the optimised gains settle in **1.94 s** instead of **3.39 s**, and across its examples reduces overshoot and settling time by up to 15 % and 20 %.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation**: we simulated the same closed loop, with the same process and the paper's gains, using the maths behind the [[pid-simulator]]. Nothing was fitted. The run lasts 15 s in 2000 steps, as the tool page does, and the dead time is handled exactly (the controller's output is stored and fed back to the process 0.5 s later).

**Step 1: the two step responses.** Figure 1 shows the output after the setpoint jumps from 0 to 1. Both loops do nothing for the first 0.5 s, because of the dead time. The WGC loop then rises fast, overshoots by **11.5 %** at 2.19 s and swings back. The optimised loop rises a little more slowly but barely overshoots (**1.0 %**) and is inside the ±2 % band for good at **1.94 s**.

**Step 2: the settling times.** Settling time is the moment after which the output stays within a band around its final value; the paper uses ±2 %. The simulator gives **3.396 s** for the WGC gains and **1.942 s** for the optimised gains. The paper states 3.39 s and 1.94 s, so the main result is reproduced. With a looser ±5 % band the two times are 3.01 s and 1.79 s: the improvement does not depend on the band.

**Step 3: how safe is each loop?** A faster loop is only useful if it is still robust. The **gain margin** says how many times the loop gain could grow before it goes unstable, the **phase margin** how much extra phase lag it can take, and the **delay margin** how much more dead time it can stand. The optimised gains are not only faster, they are also safer: gain margin 3.22 against 2.88, phase margin 64.3° against 54.9°, and delay margin 1.17 s against 0.86 s. That is because the optimised point has lower gains (kp and ki both smaller), which also cuts the largest controller output from 1.65 to 1.46 times the setpoint step.

**Step 4: is 1.94 s the fastest possible?** Not quite, and the paper does not claim it is: its optimiser minimises a combined cost, not settling time alone. Figure 2 holds kp at each published value and steps ki from 0.6 to 1.4. With kp 0.9939 the shortest settling time is 1.92 s at ki 0.94, almost exactly the published point. With kp 1.0539 it is 1.80 s at ki 0.96, so the WGC's slow settling comes from its large integral gain, not from its kp. The steps in the curves are where a small bump of the response just leaves or enters the ±2 % band, which is why settling time alone is a jumpy thing to optimise.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/pi-controller-dead-time-weighted-geometrical-center-optimised-settling-time.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `simulate` (the closed loop with dead time, integrated with fourth-order Runge-Kutta while the delayed controller output is interpolated), `stepInfo` (rise time, overshoot, settling time), `margins` and `openLoopTf` (gain and phase margins) and `closedLoopPoles` (a stability check) from `src/lib/sci/ctl-pid.ts`, the maths behind the [[pid-simulator]].

The process, the four gains and the two settling times come from the paper's text. The 15 s run length, 2000 steps, the ki sweep from 0.6 to 1.4 and the ±5 % comparison are **our choices**. The paper's own optimisation cost is not recomputed here, because its weights are not needed to check the settling times.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/pid-simulator.mjs`, which checks the simulator against python-control and an independent NumPy simulation:

- A first-order process 2/(5s + 1) under PID control without dead time must match python-control's step response to 1e-7, with rise time 4.5708 s, settling time 11.7325 s and overshoot 2.0951 %.
- The same process with 1 s of dead time must match an independent NumPy simulation to 1e-4 at 2, 4 and 6 s, and a nearly unstable delayed loop must give a gain margin of 1.03263 and a phase margin of 1.3997°.
- New with this article: the paper's process e^(−0.5s)/(s + 1) must settle within ±2 % in 3.396 s with kp 1.0539, ki 1.1811 and in 1.942 s with kp 0.9939, ki 0.9311, with overshoots of 11.5 % and 1.0 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[pid-simulator]]:

1. In *Plant (the process you control)*, keep *Model* on **First order + dead time** and type *Gain K* **1**, *Time constant τ (s)* **1** and *Dead time L (s)* **0.5**.
2. In *PID controller*, type *Kp (proportional)* **1.0539**, *Ki (integral, 1/s)* **1.1811** and *Kd (derivative, s)* **0**.
3. Type **15** in *Simulate for (s)* and keep *Settling band* on **±2 %**.
4. The results read *Settling time (±2 %)* about **3.40 s**, *Overshoot* about **11.5 %**, *Gain margin* about **2.88** and *Phase margin* about **54.9°**.
5. Change *Kp* to **0.9939** and *Ki* to **0.9311**. *Settling time* drops to about **1.94 s**, *Overshoot* to about **1.0 %**, and *Delay margin* rises from about 0.861 s to about **1.17 s**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a tuning paper's table in a minute**: type the process and the published gains and compare rise time, overshoot and settling time, as we did here.
- **See the robustness behind a fast response**: read the gain, phase and delay margins next to the step response, so a faster tuning is not quietly a fragile one.
- **Compare tuning rules on one process**: the simulator's Ziegler-Nichols starting values give a baseline to set against WGC or any optimised gains.
- **Teach dead time**: raise *Dead time L* step by step with the same gains and watch the overshoot grow and the margins shrink.
- **Move on to state feedback**: for processes with several states, place the closed-loop poles directly with [[pole-placement]].

## The fine print

- We checked only the paper's first example. Its second (an integrating process under PI-PD control) and third (a higher-order process) use gains whose layout in the text we could not match with certainty, so we left them out.
- The paper's figures and tables of overshoot and rise time were not used; the overshoot and margin values here are our simulation of its stated gains.
- A different simulation step or run length can move a settling time by a few thousandths of a second, because the time is read where the response crosses the band.
- The ki sweep is our own exploration, not part of the paper.
