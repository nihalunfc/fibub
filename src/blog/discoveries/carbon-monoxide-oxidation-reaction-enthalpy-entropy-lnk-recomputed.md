After a gas explosion underground, the gas that kills is carbon monoxide, and the fastest way to clear it is to oxidise it over a catalyst at close to room temperature. A 2026 paper in *Molecules* studies that removal and starts, properly, with the thermodynamics: how much heat **2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂** gives out, which way the entropy goes, and how completely the reaction can go at 15 to 65 °C. Papers that report their standard values in full are a gift, because anyone can check them, and this one's values are a clean test of the [[thermochemistry-calculator]]: four numbers in the paper, four numbers from standard tables, agreeing to the fourth digit. The tool then adds something the paper does not need but every combustion engineer does — the temperature where the reaction turns around. For what happens next, the [[equilibrium-solver]] takes the equilibrium constant to actual compositions and the [[gas-law-calculator]] handles the pressure rise in a sealed space.

## The paper in brief

The authors study catalytic CO oxidation over hopcalite in a closed, constant-volume vessel meant to stand for a sealed-off area after an explosion, with CO at a few per cent and the temperature stepped from 15 to 65 °C. On the thermodynamic side they report, at 298.15 K and 100 kPa: **ΔrH° = −565.968 kJ/mol**, **ΔrS° = −173.006 J/(mol·K)**, **ΔrG° = −514.38 kJ/mol**, a heat-capacity difference of **−13.42 J/(mol·K)** and an equilibrium constant of **exp(207.6)**. Across the whole range the reaction stays strongly exothermic and entropy-decreasing, the total heat release peaks at 25 °C (a maximum of −135.91 J in their vessel), and **ln K falls to 180.48 at 65 °C** — still K ≈ 10⁷⁸, far above the 10⁵ they take as "goes to completion". Their kinetic result is separate and more interesting: although the thermodynamics is slightly less favourable as it warms, the **average reaction rate peaks at 45 °C**, so the best working temperature is set by kinetics, not by equilibrium.

## Checked with the tools

This is a pure **recomputation**. We typed the balanced reaction into the same maths the [[thermochemistry-calculator]] runs, which adds up standard formation values from its built-in CRC/NIST table (CO, CO₂ and O₂ are all in it) and takes ΔH° and ΔS° as constant with temperature. Nothing from the paper was used as an input except the reaction itself and, for one sensitivity test, its ΔCp.

**Step 1: the three standard values.** From the table alone the tool gives **ΔrH° = −565.96 kJ/mol**, **ΔrS° = −172.89 J/(mol·K)** and **ΔrG° = −514.40 kJ/mol**. Against the paper's −565.968, −173.006 and −514.38, the gaps are 0.008 kJ/mol, 0.12 J/(mol·K) and 0.02 kJ/mol — inside the rounding of published formation data. Per mole of CO burnt that is **−282.98 kJ**, the figure worth remembering, because it is why a few per cent of CO in a sealed space is a thermal problem as well as a toxic one.

**Step 2: the equilibrium constant, twice.** From ΔrG° the tool gives **ln K = 207.51** at 25 °C, against the paper's exp(207.6), and K ≈ 1.3 × 10⁹⁰. Carrying ΔH° and ΔS° to 65 °C gives ΔG = **−507.50 kJ/mol** and **ln K = 180.51**, against the paper's **180.48** (Figure 1). Two independent routes to the same number, each from different data, agreeing in the fourth digit, is about as strong as a check on a published thermodynamic analysis gets. Over the whole 15 to 65 °C window ln K falls from 215.4 to 180.5: a drop of 35 in the logarithm, which sounds dramatic and means nothing in practice, because 10⁷⁸ and 10⁹³ are both "complete".

**Step 3: does holding ΔH° and ΔS° constant matter?** This is the one approximation the tool makes, and the paper hands us the number needed to test it. Applying Kirchhoff's law with ΔCp = −13.42 J/(mol·K) gives ΔH = −566.50 kJ/mol and ΔS = −174.58 J/(mol·K) at 65 °C, and ΔG = **−507.46** instead of −507.50 — a difference of 0.03 kJ/mol, and **ln K = 180.49 instead of 180.51**. So over a 50-degree window the simplification costs nothing, which is the honest justification for using a room-temperature table at all. Over hundreds of degrees it would not be.

**Step 4: what the tool adds — the turnaround.** Both ΔH° and ΔS° are negative, so there is a temperature where they cancel and the reaction stops being spontaneous. The tool puts it at **3,274 K** (about 3,000 °C): above that, ΔG for 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ is positive and carbon dioxide comes apart again (Figure 2). That is not a laboratory curiosity. It is why flame measurements in hydrocarbon fires find stubborn CO at the hottest point and why adiabatic flame temperature calculations must allow CO₂ to dissociate. The paper has no need for it at 65 °C, but the same two numbers give it for free.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/carbon-monoxide-oxidation-reaction-enthalpy-entropy-lnk-recomputed.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `lookup` (the built-in formation table), `reaction` (ΔH, ΔS, ΔG and K from the stoichiometry), `dGat`, `lnK` and `crossover` from `src/lib/sci/thermo.ts`, the maths behind the [[thermochemistry-calculator]].

The only input from the paper is the balanced reaction, plus its ΔCp = −13.42 J/(mol·K) for the Kirchhoff sensitivity test in Step 3 and its two stated ln K values, drawn as the dots in Figure 1 for comparison. All the formation values are the tool's own, from the sources listed above. Figure 2 extends the calculation far past the paper's range, and the constant-ΔH, constant-ΔS assumption behind it is **our simplification**: the true crossing is a few hundred degrees away from 3,274 K once real heat capacities are used, so treat it as "about 3,000 °C", not a precise value.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` and `tests/sci-tools/thermochemistry-calculator.mjs` pin this maths against textbook thermochemistry:

- Burning methane, CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O(l), must give ΔH° = −890.57 kJ/mol, ΔS° = −242.91 J/(mol·K) and ΔG° = −818.15 kJ/mol from the same table.
- Calcining limestone, CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, must turn spontaneous at 1,118.6 K, the standard textbook answer; and a reaction with ΔH < 0 and ΔS > 0 must correctly report that it never changes sign.
- ln K from ΔG° = −5.708 kJ/mol must be 2.3027, so that K = 10.
- Every entry in the built-in table must satisfy ΔfG° = ΔfH° − TΔfS° against the elements' own entropies, which catches a typo in any one of the three columns.
- New with this article: 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ must give −565.96 kJ/mol, −172.89 J/(mol·K) and −514.40 kJ/mol, ln K must be 207.51 at 298.15 K and 180.51 at 338.15 K, and the sign change must land at 3,273.5 K.

## Check it yourself

On the [[thermochemistry-calculator]]:

1. In *Balanced reaction, with state symbols* type **2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g)** over the example. The values table fills itself in from the built-in data for all three gases.
2. Leave *Temperature (K)* at **298.15**. Read **ΔH° = −565.96 kJ/mol**, **ΔS° = −172.89 J/(mol·K)**, **ΔG° = −514.40 kJ/mol** and **ln K = 207.51**, with K about **1.3 × 10⁹⁰**.
3. Change *Temperature (K)* to **338.15** (65 °C). ΔG becomes **−507.50 kJ/mol** and ln K **180.51** — the paper's 180.48.
4. The page's *Turns over at* line reads about **3,274 K (3,000 °C)**, with the note that the reaction is spontaneous below it and not above. Set *Temperature (K)* to **3300** and watch ΔG go positive.
5. For the per-mole figure, retype the reaction as **CO(g) + 0.5O2(g) → CO2(g)**: ΔH° halves to **−282.98 kJ/mol** while ln K halves too, a useful reminder that K depends on how you write the equation.
6. Take K onwards: with K that large, the [[equilibrium-solver]] will show CO driven to essentially zero, which is the paper's point about completeness.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published thermodynamic analysis in a minute** by retyping its reaction and comparing all four standard values, as done here ([[thermochemistry-calculator]]).
- **Find the temperature where a reaction turns**, for calcination, reduction, cracking or dissociation work, straight from ΔH° and ΔS°.
- **Test whether the constant-ΔH approximation is safe** for your own temperature window, using a ΔCp from the literature, before reaching for full heat-capacity polynomials.
- **Separate thermodynamics from kinetics** in your own writing: this paper's equilibrium constant improves as it cools while its rate peaks at 45 °C, and the two statements are not in conflict.
- **Balance first, then compute**: the [[equation-balancer]] fixes the stoichiometry, and the [[gas-law-calculator]] turns moles consumed into the pressure change in a sealed vessel.

## The fine print

- Formation values differ slightly between compilations. The 0.1 J/(mol·K) gap in ΔS° between the paper and the tool is that, not an error on either side.
- The tool holds ΔH° and ΔS° constant with temperature. We showed this is harmless over the paper's 50-degree window; the 3,274 K crossing in Figure 2 uses the same assumption far outside it and is indicative only.
- Standard values assume unit activities and 1 bar. The paper's own vessel ran at up to 0.12 MPa with a compressibility factor of 0.998 to 1.002, so ideal-gas standard states are a fair description there.
- Thermodynamics says nothing about rate. A reaction with K = 10⁷⁸ can still need a catalyst, which is the whole subject of the paper.
- We did not check the paper's catalytic rate measurements, its heat-release total of −135.91 J (which depends on its vessel and loading) or its kinetic staging.
