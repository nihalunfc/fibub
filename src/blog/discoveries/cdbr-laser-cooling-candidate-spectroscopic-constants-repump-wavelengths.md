Cooling molecules to a few millionths of a degree above absolute zero opens the way to precision tests of physics and to quantum chemistry with single molecules. Lasers can slow a molecule only if it keeps returning to the same state after each photon it scatters, so researchers hunt for molecules whose ground and excited states have almost the same shape. A 2026 paper in the *International Journal of Molecular Sciences* computes the electronic states of **cadmium monobromide (CdBr)** and proposes it as such a candidate. This article rechecks the paper's main numbers with the [[diatomic-spectroscopy]] calculator, and its beam speed with the [[kinetic-theory]] explorer. To see how the same constants decide which levels are filled at a given temperature, try the [[partition-function]] explorer.

## The paper in brief

The authors used **ab initio** quantum chemistry (calculations from first principles, here multireference configuration interaction with spin–orbit coupling) to compute the potential energy curves of the low-lying states of CdBr. From the curves they read the usual **spectroscopic constants**: the equilibrium bond length Re, the vibrational constant ωe (how stiff the bond is) and the rotational constant Be (set by the bond length and the masses). For the ground state they find Re = 2.468 Å and ωe = 231.4 cm⁻¹, within 1 cm⁻¹ of the measured value.

The cooling transition they propose is between the ground state X²Σ⁺₁/₂ and the excited C²Π₁/₂ state, whose bond lengths differ by only 0.007 Å. That makes the **Franck–Condon factors** (the chance of falling back to each vibrational level) strongly diagonal, so one main laser at 349.6 nm and three repump lasers at 352.5, 355.4 and 358.3 nm could keep a molecule scattering about 35,000 photons. They estimate a Doppler cooling limit of 124 µK, a recoil limit of 0.83 µK, and a radiative lifetime of the upper state between 16 and 41 ns.

## Checked with the tools

These are **recomputations** from values stated in the paper's text. We had only the text: the paper's tables of constants and Franck–Condon factors did not come through on the page we read.

**Step 1: the bond length from the rotational constant.** The rotational constant and the bond length are tied by the reduced mass μ: B = h/(8π²cμr²). The page gives Be as 5.928; the only reading consistent with Re = 2.468 Å is **5.928 × 10⁻² cm⁻¹**. With that value the Diatomic spectroscopy calculator's maths gives 2.4698 Å for the most common isotopologue, ¹¹⁴Cd⁷⁹Br, and between 2.4517 and 2.4788 Å for the other main ones. With **standard atomic weights** (μ = 46.706 u) it gives **2.4675 Å**, the only value that rounds to the paper's 2.468 (Figure 2). So the paper's constants appear to use average masses rather than one isotopologue. The difference, 0.002 Å, is far below the accuracy of the calculation, but it matters if you compare with spectra of a single isotopologue.

**Step 2: the laser wavelengths and the ground-state spacings.** Each repump laser drives the excited level down to a higher vibrational level of the ground state, so the differences between the four laser wavenumbers are the ground state's level spacings. The wavelengths give 28,604.1, 28,368.8, 28,137.3 and 27,909.6 cm⁻¹, so spacings of **235.3, 231.5 and 227.7 cm⁻¹**. A straight-line (Birge–Sponer) fit in the calculator gives ωe = 239.1 and ωexe = 1.90 cm⁻¹. But the wavelengths are rounded to 0.1 nm, which at 350 nm is about ±4 cm⁻¹ for each laser and ±8 cm⁻¹ for each spacing. Trying every rounding corner gives ωe anywhere from 223 to 255 cm⁻¹. So the laser wavelengths are **consistent with the paper's ωe of 231.4 cm⁻¹** but too coarse to measure the anharmonicity (Figure 1). The fit's own error bars are tiny only because three rounded points happen to lie on a line.

**Step 3: the cooling limits.** The **Doppler limit**, the lowest temperature that the scattering itself allows, is T = ħΓ/(2kB), where Γ = 1/τ is the decay rate of the upper level. Across the paper's lifetime range of 16.07 to 41.35 ns this gives **238 to 92 µK**; the paper's 124 µK corresponds to a lifetime of **30.8 ns**, inside that range. The **recoil limit**, set by the kick of a single photon, is h²/(mλ²kB) = **0.815 µK** for a 192.3 u molecule at 349.6 nm, 2 % below the paper's 0.831 µK. Small differences in the mass or the wavelength used, or a slightly different convention, account for that.

**Step 4: the beam speed.** The paper starts its slowing estimate from molecules at 496 K moving at 207.2 m/s, which it calls the rms speed. The Kinetic theory explorer's maths gives an **rms speed of 253.6 m/s** for CdBr (192.318 g/mol) at 496 K, and a **most probable speed of 207.1 m/s**. So the paper's figure is the most probable speed, √(2RT/M), not the rms speed, √(3RT/M). Using the rms speed would lengthen any stopping distance worked out from it by about half, since distance grows with the square of the starting speed.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/cdbr-laser-cooling-candidate-spectroscopic-constants-repump-wavelengths.mjs` imports `bondLength`, `rotConstant`, `reducedMass`, `fitAnharmonic` and `G` from `src/lib/sci/diatomic.ts`, the maths behind the Diatomic spectroscopy calculator, and `vrms` and `vmostProbable` from `src/lib/sci/learngas.ts`, the maths behind the Kinetic theory explorer. It draws Figure 1 (the spacings from the laser wavelengths with their rounding bars and the fit) and Figure 2 (bond length against reduced mass), and prints every number in this article. The Doppler and recoil limits use the standard formulas with the exact SI constants from the same module.

Inputs from the paper: Re, ωe, Be, the four wavelengths, the lifetime range, the two limits, 496 K and 207.2 m/s. Our assumptions: that Be is in units of 10⁻² cm⁻¹, that the wavelengths are vacuum wavelengths rounded to 0.1 nm, and standard atomic weights and isotope masses.

## How we test the maths

The calculator's maths is checked in `tests/sci.mjs` and `tests/sci-tools/diatomic-spectroscopy.mjs`, run on every change:

- For each built-in molecule (HCl, CO, H₂, N₂, O₂, HF, I₂), the bond length worked out from Huber and Herzberg's Be matches their Re within 0.0015 Å.
- H³⁵Cl's fundamental is 2,885.31 cm⁻¹, its force constant 516.3 N/m, and its R(0) and P(1) lines fall at 2,905.57 and 2,864.43 cm⁻¹.
- The band-origin fit of the measured HCl fundamental and overtones gives ωe = 2,989.22 and ωexe = 51.71 cm⁻¹.
- **New with this article:** Be = 0.05928 cm⁻¹ gives 2.4675 Å with the atomic weights of Cd and Br and 2.4698 Å for ¹¹⁴Cd⁷⁹Br, and the spacings 235.3, 466.8 and 694.5 cm⁻¹ fit ωe = 239.1 and ωexe = 1.90 cm⁻¹.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[diatomic-spectroscopy|diatomic molecule spectroscopy calculator]].
2. Set **Molecule** to "My own constants". Type **Atom 1** 114Cd and **Atom 2** 79Br.
3. Type **ωe (cm⁻¹)** 231.4, **Be (cm⁻¹)** 0.05928 and, since the tool needs one, **ωexe (cm⁻¹)** 0.5 (**our assumption**; the bond length does not depend on it). The headline ends with **bond length 2.4698 Å**, and **Reduced mass** shows 46.6185 u.
4. In **Fit ωe and ωexe from band origins**, replace the example with three lines: `1 235.3`, `2 466.8`, `3 694.5`. The tool shows **ωe = 239.1 ± 0.0 cm⁻¹, ωexe = 1.90 ± 0.00 cm⁻¹**: a perfect line through rounded data, which is why the real uncertainty has to come from the rounding, as in Step 2.
5. In the [[kinetic-theory]] explorer, under **Solve my numbers**, pick "Speed of molecules: v_rms = √(3RT / M)" and type a temperature of 496 K and a molar mass of 192.318 g/mol. The answer is an rms speed of about 253.6 m/s, and the working lists the most probable speed, about 207.1 m/s: the paper's figure.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Checking a table of constants.** The Diatomic spectroscopy calculator turns Be into a bond length for any isotopologue in one step, which catches unit slips and mass conventions before a paper goes out.
- **Planning laser wavelengths.** From ωe and ωexe the calculator gives the level energies G(v), and so the spacing of the repump lasers, for any isotopologue.
- **Extracting constants from measured bands.** Paste band origins into the fit card to get ωe and ωexe, then a Morse estimate of the dissociation energy.
- **Beam and trap design.** The Kinetic theory explorer gives rms, mean and most probable speeds at any temperature, so you can state which one a slowing estimate uses.
- **Teaching.** Comparing isotopologues shows students why spectra of molecules with heavy atoms of several isotopes are crowded.

## The fine print

- This is a computational study; no CdBr molecule was cooled. The checks above test the consistency of the stated numbers, not the accuracy of the ab initio method.
- We could not see the paper's tables of constants, Franck–Condon factors or branching ratios, so we did not check the 34,573 photon cycles or the repump scheme beyond the wavelengths.
- Reading Be as 5.928 × 10⁻² cm⁻¹ is our inference from the bond length; check the paper's own table for the units.
- The Doppler and recoil formulas are the simple two-level forms. Real molecules with several ground-state sublevels usually cool less well than these limits suggest.
