The Mexican blind cavefish, a cave form of *Astyanax mexicanus* with no working eyes and little pigment, is one of the best-studied animals in evolutionary biology, yet nobody knew how many live in a given cave. A 2024 paper in *Fishes* counted them in Pachón cave without clipping a single fin: it swabbed the fish's flanks, read their DNA, and treated a genotype seen twice as a **recapture**. This article rebuilds the paper's two population estimates with the [[mark-recapture]] estimator, finds the exact formula they match, and shows how much the answer leans on just three matches. For counts that do not need individual marks, see [[distance-sampling]]; to turn a population size into a trend, use [[population-growth-models]].

## The paper in brief

**Mark-recapture** estimates the size of a population you cannot count directly. You catch and mark some animals (M), let them mix back in, catch a second sample (C) and count how many of those carry a mark (R). If the population is **closed** (no births, deaths or movement between samples), the share marked in the second sample estimates the share marked in the whole population, so N ≈ M × C / R. That simple ratio is the **Lincoln-Petersen** estimate.

The authors used a DNA "mark" instead of a physical one. They swabbed fish in the main pool of Cueva de El Pachón on two visits three days apart, genotyped each swab at 26 **microsatellite loci** (short repeated stretches of DNA that differ between individuals), and found three pairs of identical genotypes among the 29 fish sampled each time. Their estimate for the main pool is **218 fish (95 % range 40 to 395)**. Comparing 35 fish genotyped in 2019 with 55 in 2022 across the whole Pachón water system, with three found in both, gave **490 (76 to 904)**.

The paper also mapped fish along a 310 m pool in Sabinos cave by line transects (317 fish counted, about 1142 estimated, two-thirds of them in the first 50 m, under a bat roost), and set its numbers beside fin-clip estimates of 8671 and 9781 fish made by Elliott in 1971 for Yerbaniz and Pachón, whose raw counts are not given.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation from the counts in the paper's text**. We read "29 fish sampled twice" as M = 29 and C = 29, with R = 3 (**our reading**; the paper does not name its formula).

**Step 1: which formula did the paper use?** The tool gives Lincoln-Petersen and **Chapman's** corrected estimate, (M+1)(C+1)/(R+1) − 1, which removes most of the upward bias that Lincoln-Petersen has with few recaptures. Neither gives 218. But **Bailey's binomial estimator**, M(C+1)/(R+1), gives 217.5, and its usual ±1.96 standard-error interval gives 40.0 to 395.0. The whole-system counts give 490.0 and 76.1 to 903.9. Both match the paper to the fish, which also confirms our reading of the counts.

| Pachón count | Paper | Bailey (our script) | Chapman, tool | Lincoln-Petersen, tool | Tool's 95 % log-normal interval |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main pool (M 29, C 29, R 3) | 218 (40 to 395) | 217.5 (40.0 to 395.0) | 224 | 280.3 | 120 to 493 |
| Whole system (M 35, C 55, R 3) | 490 (76 to 904) | 490.0 (76.1 to 903.9) | 503 | 641.7 | 254 to 1124 |

The point estimates agree closely: Bailey and Chapman differ by 6 and 13 fish. **The intervals do not.** A symmetric "estimate ± 1.96 SE" interval goes down to 40 fish in the main pool, yet 55 different fish were actually swabbed (29 + 29 − 3). The tool's default **log-normal interval** (Chao 1987) never drops below the animals already seen and is skewed upward, as the uncertainty in a ratio really is: 120 to 493 fish. For the whole system, 87 fish were handled, against a symmetric lower limit of 76. The tool's "Normal" option gives 55 to 395 for the main pool: the same upper limit as the paper, with the lower one raised to the 55 fish seen.

**Step 2: how much rests on three matches?** Figure 1 runs the tool's maths for 29 fish per visit and every possible number of matches. One match fewer (R = 2) gives 299 fish; one more (R = 4) gives 179. The coefficient of variation (standard error ÷ estimate) is 38.9 % at R = 3. Robson and Regier's rule of thumb asks for at least **7 recaptures** before the Chapman estimate is close to unbiased, and the tool flags anything below that.

**Step 3: planning the next survey.** Figure 2 asks what a repeat visit would need, if the pool really holds about 224 fish. With 29 fish on each visit we expect only 3.8 matches and a coefficient of variation of 35 %. Swabbing 40 fish on each visit gives about 7 matches and 27 %; 60 on each gives about 16 matches and 17 %. Marking more fish on the first visit helps as much as catching more on the second.

The whole-system estimate has one more catch: three years separate its two samples, long enough for births and deaths, so the population was not closed. Treat 490 as a rough size for the fish present across both years, not a snapshot.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and the numbers in the table come from `scripts/blog-figures/mexican-blind-cavefish-pachon-mark-recapture-dna-bailey-chapman.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `twoSample` from `src/lib/sci/markrecap.ts`, the maths behind the [[mark-recapture]] estimator, for the Lincoln-Petersen and Chapman estimates, Seber's variance and the log-normal, normal and Poisson intervals. Bailey's estimator is not part of the tool; the script works it out in two lines so it can be compared with the paper.

The counts (29, 29 and 3; 35, 55 and 3) and the published estimates come from the paper's text. Reading the main-pool counts as 29 fish per visit, the 224-fish population behind Figure 2 and the expected number of matches (M × C / N) are **our assumptions**. Nothing here uses the paper's genotype data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/mark-recapture.mjs`, which pins the estimator against an independent Python implementation using SciPy:

- 120 marked, 100 caught and 18 recaptured must give Lincoln-Petersen 666.67, Chapman 642.21 with Seber variance 14157.4, and a log-normal 95 % interval of 463.60 to 942.78.
- 50, 40 and 3 must be flagged as too few recaptures, with Chapman 521.75 and a log-normal interval of 262.02 to 1166.90.
- A five-occasion Schnabel example must give ΣCM 10402, ΣR 48 and N 216.71, with Poisson limits 163.45 to 293.91.
- New with this article: the two Pachón counts must give Chapman 224 and 503, with log-normal intervals of 120.2 to 493.0 and 254.0 to 1123.5 fish.

## Check it yourself

On the [[mark-recapture]] estimator, with **Two samples** selected:

1. Type **29** in *Marked in the first sample (M)*, **29** in *Caught in the second sample (C)* and **3** in *Of those, already marked (R)*.
2. Leave *Interval* on **Log-normal (recommended)** and *Confidence* on **95 %**. The headline reads **N ≈ 224 (95 % interval 120 to 493)**.
3. The facts list shows *Lincoln-Petersen* **280.33**, *Standard error* **87.21**, *Coefficient of variation* **38.9 %** and *Animals handled* **55**, and *Recaptures* warns that fewer than 7 is unreliable.
4. Switch *Interval* to **Normal (± z × SE)**: the interval becomes **55 to 395**, held at the 55 fish seen.
5. Now type **35**, **55** and **3** for the whole system: **N ≈ 503 (95 % interval 254 to 1,124)**.
6. Try R = **2** and R = **4** with 29 and 29 to see the estimate jump to 299 and fall to 179.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Genetic and photo-ID surveys**: a matched genotype or photo is a recapture, so the same two-sample sums apply; the [[mark-recapture]] estimator gives the interval as well as the number.
- **Check which estimator a paper used**: Lincoln-Petersen, Chapman and Bailey differ most when recaptures are few, as here.
- **Report an interval that cannot fall below the animals seen**: the log-normal interval is the better choice for small R.
- **Plan sample sizes** before a field trip, as in Figure 2: aim for at least 7 recaptures.
- **Combine with transects**: where fish cannot be caught twice, a line or strip count with [[distance-sampling]] or the [[quadrat-transect-analyser]] gives density instead.

## The fine print

- We used only the counts in the paper's text. Our reading of "29 fish sampled twice" as 29 per visit is confirmed only by matching the paper's numbers.
- Bailey's estimator is meant for one marked sample followed by one recapture sample with replacement; Chapman's is the more common choice today. The two agree closely here; the intervals are what differ.
- All the estimates assume the swabbed fish mix freely through the pool and that a genotype match always means the same fish (identical genotypes in close relatives would inflate R).
- We did not recompute Elliott's 1971 estimates or the Sabinos transect totals, as their raw counts are not in the text.
