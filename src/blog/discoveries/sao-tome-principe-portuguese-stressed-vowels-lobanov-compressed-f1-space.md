Portuguese is spoken on two small islands in the Gulf of Guinea, and nobody had measured its stressed vowels properly. A 2025 paper in *Isogloss* did: it recorded twelve speakers from São Tomé and from Príncipe, measured the first two formants of every stressed vowel, and reported **eight** oral vowels where Brazilian and European Portuguese have seven. The extra one is a mid central [ə]. The authors also say the island space is **more compressed** than the mainland ones, especially along F1 (the formant that tracks how open the mouth is). Both claims can be re-examined from the numbers they print, and that is what we do here with the [[vowel-formant-chart]]. If you work on sound systems, the [[ipa-feature-matrix]] turns those eight symbols into distinctive features, and the [[pitch-contour-tracker]] handles the other half of a vowel's acoustics, its pitch.

## The paper in brief

Formants are the resonances of the vocal tract. For vowels, the first two carry most of the identity: **F1** falls as the tongue body rises (so [i] and [u] have low F1, [a] a high one), and **F2** falls as the tongue moves back and the lips round (so [i] is high, [u] low). Plot F2 against F1 with both axes reversed and you get the familiar quadrilateral that looks like the vowel chart in a textbook.

The authors recorded six speakers of São Tomé Portuguese (PST) and six of Príncipe Portuguese (PP), three men and three women in each, and measured F1 and F2 of stressed vowels in read speech. They normalised each speaker's formants with the **Lobanov** method, which replaces a value with a z-score: how many standard deviations it sits from that speaker's own mean for that formant. The point of normalising is that a short vocal tract raises every formant, so two speakers can share a vowel system and still print different hertz.

Their findings: eight stressed oral vowels, [i e ɛ ə a ɔ o u], with [ə] behaving as an allophone of /a/ in stressed syllables; and an F1-F2 space that is smaller than the Brazilian and European values they quote from an earlier cross-dialect study, with the squeeze strongest along F1.

## Checked with the tools

We took the mean F1 and F2 in hertz that the paper prints for each vowel, variety and sex, and pushed them through the same maths the [[vowel-formant-chart]] runs. Nothing here is the paper's raw measurements; we only recompute from its published means. For every comparison we use the **seven vowels all four varieties share**, so [ə] never inflates the island spaces.

**Step 1: in hertz the gap looks enormous.** Figure 1 plots the women's four spaces as the tool draws them. Joining the seven vowels into a closed outline and taking its area gives **304,111 Hz²** for São Tomé women against **689,936 Hz²** for the Brazilian reference: the island space is **44 %** of it. For the men the same sum gives 253,357 against 329,595 Hz², or 77 %.

**Step 2: most of that is scale, not shape.** Switch the tool's *Scale* control to **Lobanov z-scores** and the four outlines nearly land on top of one another (Figure 2). The normalised areas are **3.79** (São Tomé women), 3.68 (Príncipe), 3.92 (Brazilian) and 3.71 (European); across both sexes and all four varieties every area falls between **3.68 and 4.12**, a spread of about 6 %. No vowel moves more than **0.29 z** between São Tomé and Brazilian women. In other words, the hertz difference is almost entirely a uniform stretch of the whole space, which is exactly what Lobanov is built to remove, and the *relative* arrangement of the seven vowels is close to identical across the Atlantic.

**Step 3: but a real F1 squeeze does survive.** A z-score forces every speaker's spread to 1 by construction, so a normalised chart cannot answer "is this space compressed?" at all. The measure that can is the **coefficient of variation**, the standard deviation of F1 across the seven vowels divided by its mean: multiply every formant by the same factor and it does not change. Figure 3 shows it. São Tomé Portuguese is the **lowest of the four in both sexes** (0.261 for men, 0.316 for women) against 0.332 and 0.408 for Brazilian Portuguese. For F2 the picture is weaker: São Tomé men are again lowest (0.374), but among the women Príncipe (0.330) sits below São Tomé (0.375). So the paper's "compressed, especially along F1" holds up once scale is taken out, and its emphasis on F1 is the right one.

**Step 4: how much room is there for an eighth vowel?** Normalising each island variety's own eight vowels puts [ə] at z-F1 0.68, z-F2 −0.11 for São Tomé men: between [ɛ] and [a], as a mid central vowel should be. Its distance to [a] is **0.86 z** and to [ɛ] **0.94 z**, while [ɛ] and [a] are 1.47 z apart and the nearest ordinary contrast in the system, [e] versus [ɛ], is 1.11 z. So [ə] is wedged into a gap narrower than any contrast the language already keeps, which fits the authors' reading of it as an allophone of /a/ rather than a new phoneme of its own. For São Tomé women the picture differs: [ə] sits 1.50 z from [a] and 0.88 z from [ɛ], because their [a] is far more open.

**Step 5: can the tool measure these vowels off a sound file at all?** The chart's other half is a formant tracker: linear prediction on a windowed waveform, the polynomial's roots turned into frequencies and bandwidths. We made a synthetic vowel at the São Tomé male [ə] (F1 530, F2 1389 Hz, with F3 to F5 above), wrote it to a WAV and tracked it exactly as the tool does when you open a recording. It reads back **537.4 and 1398.7 Hz**, within 1.4 % and 0.7 %. The same test on [a] (625, 1350 Hz) gives 615.9 and 1344.8 Hz. That is the accuracy you can expect from this kind of measurement on a clean vowel, and it is well inside the differences the paper is arguing about.

## How we made the graphs

Everything comes from `scripts/blog-figures/sao-tome-principe-portuguese-stressed-vowels-lobanov-compressed-f1-space.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls four functions from `src/lib/sci/formant.ts`, the maths behind the [[vowel-formant-chart]]:

- `lobanov(values, groups)` for every z-score in figures 2 and 3 and in step 4;
- `synthVowel` to build the test vowel in step 5, `formantTrack` to run the linear-prediction analysis over it and `spanFormants` to take the median formants across the steady part, which is what the tool does between its *From (s)* and *To (s)* boxes.

The mean F1 and F2 values in hertz are the paper's, read from its text and tables for the two island varieties and for the Brazilian and European figures it quotes. The closed-outline area (a shoelace sum over the seven shared vowels) and the coefficient of variation are **our own summary statistics**: the paper reports neither, and it compares spaces by eye from plots. Treating each variety-and-sex group as one "speaker" for Lobanov is also **our assumption**; the paper normalised per individual speaker, whose values are not published. That choice cannot create the agreement we see in Figure 2, because it only standardises each group's printed means.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/vowel-formant-chart.mjs` on every change, pinning the tool against NumPy and SciPy on the same made-up vowel:

- the Levinson-Durbin recursion that solves for the prediction coefficients must match `scipy.linalg.solve_toeplitz` to 1e-9;
- the formants read off the polynomial's roots must match `numpy.roots`: 712.449 Hz with a 53.32 Hz bandwidth for the first one, and so on up to 4518.260 Hz;
- the whole pipeline, through a WAV round trip at both 16 and 44.1 kHz, must recover F1 to F3 of three made-up vowels to within 5 %;
- the Lobanov z-scores must match a pandas group-wise standardisation to 1e-9.
- New with this article: the eight São Tomé male means must normalise to z-F1 −1.3622 for [i], 0.6771 for [ə] and 1.5306 for [a], with [ə] 0.857 z from [a] and 0.943 z from [ɛ].

## What researchers can use it for

- **Re-use published vowel means.** Papers print tables of F1 and F2 far more often than they share recordings. Paste a table, normalise it and compare it with your own speakers on one chart.
- **Separate scale from shape before you claim a difference.** A hertz chart mixes vocal-tract length with the vowel system. Switch between *Hz* and *Lobanov z-scores* and see which of your findings survives.
- **Measure formants without installing anything.** Open a WAV, pick a steady stretch with *From (s)* and *To (s)*, set *Highest formant* for your speaker's sex and read F1 to F3 with their standard deviations across frames.
- **Check whether a new vowel has room.** Normalise the whole inventory, then measure the distance from the candidate to its neighbours against the smallest contrast the language already keeps, as in step 4.
- **Teach the vowel chart.** Students can type any published means, watch the quadrilateral appear and then watch normalisation collapse the sex difference.

## The fine print

- The paper's tables disagree with each other on a few values (the women's [u], and the men's [ɛ] and [i] F2 differ between its tables 3, 5, 6 and 7). We used the tables that carry the Brazilian and European comparison, because that is where the compression claim lives.
- The tables are labelled as hertz but the methods say the values were Lobanov-normalised. We read them as hertz, which is what their size and pattern show.
- The Brazilian and European means are not the paper's own measurements; they are quoted from a 2009 cross-dialect study with different speakers, recordings and word lists. Any comparison carries that difference too.
- Twelve speakers, three per variety and sex, is a small sample. A 6 % spread in normalised area is not evidence of sameness, only an absence of a large difference.
- Our areas and coefficients of variation are computed from means, not from individual tokens, so they say nothing about how much speakers vary inside each variety.
