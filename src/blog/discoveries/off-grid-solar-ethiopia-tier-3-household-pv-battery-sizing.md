Hundreds of millions of rural homes are far from any power line, and a small solar home system (a few panels, a battery, a charge controller and an inverter) is often the quickest way to light, a fridge and phone charging. The World Bank's **multi-tier framework** grades such access from Tier 1 (a lamp and a phone) to Tier 5; Tier 3 adds a fridge and small kitchen appliances. A 2026 paper in *Energies* designs Tier 2 and Tier 3 systems for villages in Ethiopia and gives every step of its sizing in words, so the [[battery-solar-sizing]] tool can redo each one. For the step before, estimate what a panel really yields with the [[solar-pv-yield]] tool; for a grid-tied home, simulate a battery hour by hour with [[home-battery-sizing]].

## The paper in brief

The authors compared four solar data sources for the region (annual means from 4.22 to 6.54 kWh/m²/day, and 7.30 from a 13-year PVGIS average), built hourly load profiles for Tier 2 and Tier 3 homes, and sized a 12 V system for the worst case: **4.8 peak sun hours** (the hours of full 1000 W/m² sun that give a day's energy). Their Tier 3 home uses about **1.7 kWh a day**: roughly 40 to 50 W overnight when only the fridge runs, 80 W in the early morning, 50 to 60 W through the day and 110 to 120 W in the evening.

The sizing method, in their words: multiply the demand by 1.15 to 1.2 for losses, divide by the overall system efficiency (85 %) and by the worst-case sun hours to get the array; multiply by **3 days of autonomy** (days with no sun) and divide by an **85 % maximum depth of discharge** (how much of the battery may be used) to get the bank. The headline result is a **400 Wp array with a 2 × 250 Ah battery bank**, built as **four 130 Wp modules** with a **35 to 40 A** charge controller, giving more than 60 % surplus energy over the year. They also find lithium iron phosphate batteries cheaper over the system's life for loads above 1.5 kWh a day.

## Checked with the tools

We put the paper's own numbers through the maths of the Battery solar sizing tool. This is a **recomputation** from values in the text; we did not have the paper's spreadsheets or its appliance table.

**Step 1: the load.** Adding up the paper's four load bands over 24 hours (8 h overnight, 2 h morning, 8 h day, 6 h evening) gives **1540 Wh** at the low ends, **1650 Wh** in the middle and **1760 Wh** at the high ends. The paper's 1.7 kWh design value sits inside that, and its Tier 3 range of 1.43 to 1.68 kWh is consistent. (The hours of each band are **our reading** of the text.)

**Step 2: the array.** Following the method all the way (1700 Wh × 1.15, ÷ 0.85, ÷ 4.8 h) needs **479 W**; with the 1.2 factor, **500 W**. Rounded up to 130 Wp modules, both give **four modules, 520 Wp**, exactly the paper's final setup. The stated **400 Wp** only comes out if one of the two factors is left out: 1700 × 1.15 ÷ 4.8 = **407 W**, or 1700 ÷ 0.85 ÷ 4.8 = **417 W**. So the paper's two array figures follow two different versions of its own method, and they are not the same size: 400 Wp gives **1632 Wh** a day at 4.8 h and 85 %, less than the 1700 Wh load, while 520 Wp gives **2122 Wh**, enough even with the 1.15 factor (1954 Wh).

**Step 3: the battery bank.** 1700 Wh × 3 days ÷ 0.85 is **6000 Wh, or 500 Ah at 12 V**, which is exactly 2 × 250 Ah. With the 1.15 factor carried through, the bank grows to **575 Ah** (6897 Wh) and needs a third battery. Figure 1 shows how strongly the bank depends on the usable depth of discharge: at 50 %, a common limit for long life with lead-acid batteries, the same three days need **850 Ah**. The 85 % in the design suits lithium iron phosphate, which is the chemistry the paper recommends at this load.

**Step 4: the charge controller.** The tool's rule, array watts ÷ system volts × 1.25, gives **54.2 A** for 520 Wp at 12 V (43.3 A with no margin) and **41.7 A** for 400 Wp. The paper instead divides the day's energy by 5.5 h and by 12 V, which gives **34.8 to 36.4 A**, matching its 35 to 40 A. That rule sizes the controller for the average current, not for the array's peak, so a 35 to 40 A unit would clip a 520 Wp array on clear middays. It is worth checking the module's short-circuit current before buying.

**Step 5: the surplus.** Figure 2 plots the daily energy of both arrays against sun hours. For four 130 Wp modules to make 60 % more than the 1.7 kWh load, the year must average **6.15 peak sun hours** (7.07 if the 1.15 factor is counted), inside the high end of the paper's solar estimates (6.54 and 7.30). A 400 Wp array would need **8.0 hours**, more than any of them. So the paper's "over 60 % surplus" fits the 520 Wp system it built, not the 400 Wp figure in its abstract.

## How we made the graphs

Every number and both charts come from `scripts/blog-figures/off-grid-solar-ethiopia-tier-3-household-pv-battery-sizing.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `solar()` from `src/lib/sci/batsolar.ts`, the maths behind the [[battery-solar-sizing]] tool, for the array, panel count, battery bank, layout and controller current, and for the daily energy of a given array.

The daily demand, the 1.15 and 1.2 factors, the 85 % efficiency and depth of discharge, the 4.8 and 5.5 hours, 3 days, 12 V, 130 Wp modules and 250 Ah batteries all come from the paper's text. **Our mapping** onto the tool's fields: the demand factor goes in as an inverter efficiency of 1 ÷ 1.15 (87 %), the 85 % overall efficiency as 15 % system losses, and battery charge efficiency as 100 % (already inside the 85 %). The hours we gave each load band are **our reading** of the text.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/battery-solar-sizing.mjs`, which checks the tool against an independent Python calculation of the same formulas:

- Five battery runtimes (loads in watts and in amps, Peukert exponents from 1 to 1.25), including 100 Ah at 12 V with a 60 W load at 90 % efficiency and half the capacity usable: **9.0 h** simply and **8.81 h** with Peukert's k = 1.2.
- Three off-grid systems, including the tool's example (1230 Wh/day at 4.5 sun hours, 25 % losses, 2 days at 50 %): an array of **449.9 W**, a bank of **227.8 Ah** at 24 V and a **41.7 A** controller.
- New with this article: the Tier 3 home (1700 Wh at 87 % inverter efficiency, 15 % losses, 4.8 h, 130 Wp modules) must need **478.9 W** and **four modules**, and at 100 % inverter efficiency its 3-day bank at 85 % must be **500 Ah**, exactly two 250 Ah batteries.

## Check it yourself

On the [[battery-solar-sizing]] page, in the *Off-grid solar sizing* card:

1. Replace the text in *Appliance, watts, hours per day, quantity* with one line: **Tier 3 home, 1700, 1, 1** (1700 W for 1 hour is the paper's 1700 Wh a day as a single line; ignore the "draw up to" note this gives).
2. Set *Peak sun hours per day* to **4.8**, *System losses (%)* to **15**, *Inverter efficiency (%)* to **87**, *Battery charge efficiency (%)* to **100**, *Days of autonomy* to **3** and *Usable depth of discharge (%)* to **85**.
3. Choose *System voltage* **12 V**, *Panel rating (W)* **130**, *One battery: voltage (V)* **12** and *One battery: capacity (Ah)* **250**.
4. *Solar array needed* reads **479 W**, and the headline says **4 × 130 W panels**, **3 × 12 V 250 Ah batteries** (*Battery bank* 6900 Wh, 575 Ah).
5. Now set *Inverter efficiency (%)* to **100**: the bank falls to **6000 Wh, 500 Ah** and the headline to **2** batteries, the paper's 2 × 250 Ah. The array needed drops to **417 W**, still four 130 W panels.
6. Set *Usable depth of discharge (%)* to **50** to see what a lead-acid bank would need: **850 Ah**, four batteries.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Audit a published sizing:** put a paper's demand, sun hours, efficiencies and autonomy through the [[battery-solar-sizing]] tool and see which of its numbers follow from which version of the method.
- **Compare battery chemistries:** change only the usable depth of discharge (50 % for lead-acid, 80 to 90 % for lithium iron phosphate) and read off the bank, then cost it over the years with the [[lcoe-calculator]].
- **Pick the design sun hours:** check what a panel really yields at the site and tilt with the [[solar-pv-yield]] tool before trusting a single worst-case number.
- **Teach the method:** students can rebuild a whole solar home system from a load list in a few minutes and see how each factor moves the answer.
- **Grid-tied homes:** for a home that also has the grid, simulate self-consumption hour by hour with [[home-battery-sizing]] instead.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation** from numbers in the paper's text. We did not have its appliance table or its simulation files, and its own energy simulation (behind the 60 % surplus) is more detailed than a peak-sun-hours sum.
- How the 1.15 to 1.2 factor and the 85 % efficiency combine is our reading of the method's wording; the tool's fields are a close stand-in, not the paper's spreadsheet.
