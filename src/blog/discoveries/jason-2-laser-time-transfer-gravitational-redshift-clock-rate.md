A clock higher up in Earth's gravity runs faster; a clock moving quickly runs slower. Einstein's equivalence principle fixes the size of the first effect exactly, and one way to test it is to fire laser pulses at a satellite, time them to a few picoseconds, and watch its onboard clock drift against a hydrogen maser on the ground. A 2026 paper in *The European Physical Journal C* does that with **T2L2**, the laser time-transfer payload on the ocean-altimetry satellite **Jason-2**, and gets the redshift parameter α = (−0.77 ± 3.54) × 10⁻³: agreement with general relativity to about 0.35 %. Because Jason-2 orbits low and fast, the clock arithmetic behind that test is not the familiar GPS story, and the [[gps-relativity]] explainer shows why in one chart. To follow the satellites themselves, use the [[satellite-tracker]] and [[orbit-elements]]; to draw the two effects as worldlines, the [[spacetime-diagram]].

## The paper in brief

Jason-2 flew from 2008 to 2019 in a near-circular orbit about **1335 km** up (a geocentric perigee and apogee of roughly 7715 and 7718 km), inclined 66°, at about **7.2 km/s**. T2L2 timed laser pulses between ground stations and the satellite with an overall accuracy **better than 100 ps**, a time-tagging resolution of 3 ps and a single-shot stability of 45 to 50 ps.

The authors take **47 independent passes** over the Grasse MéO station in France, about 12 hours of data in total, model the clock's own behaviour and the orbit, and fit the redshift violation parameter α in z = (1 + α)ΔU/c². They find **α = (−0.77 ± 3.54) × 10⁻³**, more than an order of magnitude better than the 1975 Maryland aircraft experiment (|α| ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻²), and then work through which error sources would have to shrink for the next mission to do better. The satellite's own oscillator is the weak link: an ultra-stable quartz at about 3.5 × 10⁻¹³ over 10 to 100 s, against 10⁻¹⁵ at 1000 s for the ground maser.

## Checked with the tools

The paper's test rests on a quantity it does not tabulate: how fast the Jason-2 clock actually ran compared with Grasse. We put the orbit into the maths behind the [[gps-relativity]] explainer — semi-major axis 7716.5 km (the mean of the quoted perigee and apogee), receiver at 43.75° N and 1320 m, which are **our values** for the Grasse site — and got:

| Quantity | Paper | Our tool result |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital speed | about 7.2 km/s | **7.187 km/s** (√(GM/a)) |
| Orbital period | not stated | **112.43 minutes** |
| Gravitational rate gain | not stated | **+10.47 µs/day** (fraction 1.212 × 10⁻¹⁰) |
| Speed rate loss | not stated | **−24.77 µs/day** |
| Net clock rate | not stated | **−14.30 µs/day** (the satellite clock *loses* time) |
| Range error if ignored | not stated | **4.29 km/day** |
| Eccentricity term, e = 0.001 | not stated | **±1.234 ns**, that is ±0.37 m of range |
| Clock precision matching α = 3.54 × 10⁻³ | α only | **4.29 × 10⁻¹³**, or 37 ns/day |

**The sign is the story.** For GPS, at 26,560 km, the same maths gives the famous **+38.55 µs/day**: the clock gains. Jason-2 is low enough that its speed wins, so its clock runs **slow by 14.3 µs a day**. The crossing point, where the two effects cancel exactly, is the **break-even radius of 9550 km** (3179 km above the mean surface) for a receiver at Grasse. **Why that matters for the test.** The gravitational part alone — the +10.47 µs/day, a fraction of 1.212 × 10⁻¹⁰ — is what the redshift parameter multiplies. The velocity part, 2.4 times larger and of the opposite sign, is *not* being tested: it has to be removed from the data with an orbit good enough that its error does not leak into α. A 0.35 % measurement of the gravitational term needs the sum of everything else known to **4.29 × 10⁻¹³** of rate, which is **429 ps of clock error over a 1000 s pass**, or 18.5 ns over the full 12 hours. Against a 100 ps link, that is a comfortable margin per pass, which is consistent with the paper's conclusion that the limit is the satellite oscillator's stability over a pass, not the laser.

**One term you cannot drop.** Jason-2's orbit is almost a circle (e ≈ 0.001), and it is tempting to treat it as one. The explainer's eccentricity correction, Δt = (2/c²)√(GMa) e sin E, still comes to an amplitude of **1.234 ns**, more than ten times the link's 100 ps accuracy and equal to 0.37 m of range (Figure 2). It averages to zero over a full orbit but not over a single pass, so for a pass-by-pass fit like this one it is a real systematic.

**Is the first-order formula good enough?** The explainer also carries the exact (unexpanded) Schwarzschild-plus-motion rate ratio. For this orbit the two differ by **0.002 ns per day** — about 2 parts in 10⁸ of the net effect, and seven orders of magnitude below the test's own precision. The 1/c² expansion is safe here.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/jason-2-laser-time-transfer-gravitational-redshift-clock-rate.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `shift`, `satellite`, `ground`, `eccAmp`, `balanceRadius` and `exactRatio` from `src/lib/sci/phgps.ts`, the maths behind the [[gps-relativity]] explainer. `shift` returns the gravitational and velocity fractions and their microseconds per day; `balanceRadius` solves −3GM/(2rc²) = (ground potential + ground motion) for r; `exactRatio` evaluates the unexpanded ratio for comparison.

From the paper: the perigee and apogee, the eccentricity, the mean speed, the 100 ps timing accuracy and the α uncertainty. **Our assumptions**: the semi-major axis as the mean of the quoted apsides, and Grasse at 43.75° N, 1320 m. Earth's GM, rotation rate and mean radius are the standard constants listed in the explainer's own sources. Note that a 7716.5 km radius is 1345 km above fibub's *mean* Earth radius, slightly more than the paper's 1335 km, because the orbit's height is usually quoted above the equatorial reference ellipsoid; the clock rates change by under 0.1 % either way.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/gps-relativity.mjs`, which pins this module against published values and against an exact calculation:

- for a GPS satellite (26,560 km radius, receiver on the equator at sea level) the gravitational gain must be +45.718 µs/day, the speed loss −7.110 µs/day and the net +38.608 µs/day, the textbook numbers, with the factory frequency 10.22999999954 MHz and 11.57 km of ranging error a day;
- the first-order net rate must agree with the exact unexpanded ratio, evaluated at 30 digits with mpmath, to better than a part in 10⁶, at the GPS, Galileo, geostationary and low-orbit radii;
- the two effects must cancel at 1.4974 Earth radii (about 9540 km), where the net rate crosses zero.
- **New with this article**: Jason-2's own orbit (7716.5 km, receiver 43.75° N, 1320 m) must give +10.475 and −24.775 µs/day and a net −14.300 µs/day, a period of 112.432 minutes, a speed of 7187.18 m/s, an eccentricity amplitude of 1.234 ns at e = 0.001, and a break-even radius of 9549.9 km that the orbit sits below.

## Check it yourself

On the [[gps-relativity]] page:

1. Set *Orbit* to **Custom** and type **7716.5** into *Orbit radius from Earth's centre (km)*.
2. Type **0.001** into *Orbit eccentricity e*.
3. Type **43.75** into *Receiver latitude (°)* and **1320** into *Receiver height (m)*.
4. Read *Gravity (general relativity)* **+10.47 µs/day**, *Speed (special relativity)* **−24.77 µs/day** (it notes the 7.187 km/s orbit), and *Net rate difference* **−14.30 µs/day**.
5. *Error if ignored* shows **4.29 km per day**, *Eccentricity correction* **±1.23 ns** (±0.37 m of range) and *Break-even orbit* **9550 km radius**.
6. Now switch *Orbit* to **GPS (about 26,560 km)**: the net flips to **+38.55 µs/day**. Same physics, opposite sign, because the orbit is above the break-even radius.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Design a clock-in-orbit experiment**: read off the rate offset, and the timing precision needed per pass, before writing a proposal — the gravitational part is the signal, the velocity part is the thing you must model away.
- **Sanity-check a mission's error budget**: the eccentricity term is ±1.2 ns even for an orbit as round as Jason-2's, so any budget that ignores it for a single pass is wrong.
- **Set the factory offset** for a new navigation or time-transfer payload at any altitude, including the low orbits now being proposed for LEO navigation, where the net effect is negative.
- **Teach the two effects apart**: the break-even radius makes the point that "satellite clocks run fast" is only true above about 3180 km.
- **Plan an observing campaign** around real passes with the [[satellite-tracker]] and an orbit from [[orbit-elements]], then draw the worldlines on a [[spacetime-diagram]].

## The fine print

- The explainer treats Earth as a **point mass** and works to first order in 1/c². It leaves out Earth's flattening (the J₂ term), which matters at the 10⁻¹³ level for the potential at the station — close to the precision of this very test, so a real analysis uses a full geopotential model, as the paper does.
- Our station coordinates and semi-major axis are rounded stand-ins for the paper's own values; the rates shift in the third figure if you move the station by a degree.
- The paper's α and its error bar come from its own fit to 47 passes, which we have not reproduced: we recompute the clock rates that fit is built on, not the fit.
- 14.3 µs/day is the **rate**, not the error of the test. The test watches how the measured rate changes with the potential, so a constant offset in the clock is absorbed by the fit.
- Jason-2's mission ended in 2019; the paper is a re-analysis of archived T2L2 data and a sensitivity study for future missions.
