The two-dimensional Ising model is the one interacting system whose phase transition we know exactly: Onsager solved it in 1944, and the critical temperature of the infinite square lattice is **Tc = 2/ln(1 + √2) = 2.269185** in units of J/k_B. Finite lattices have no sharp transition at all; their heat capacity has a rounded peak, and that peak sits **above** Tc. A 2025 paper in *Entropy* asks how well the infinite-lattice value can be recovered by extrapolating those peaks from lattices small enough to solve exactly, and finds that a one-parameter law in N/(N + 1) does it robustly. fibub's [[ising-model]] playground enumerates small lattices exactly and runs Monte Carlo on larger ones, so the whole argument can be rebuilt in a browser tab. For the statistical mechanics behind it, see the [[partition-function]] explorer and the [[quantum-statistics]] plotter.

## The paper in brief

The authors work with square N × N lattices and, for each, the exact **density of states**: how many spin configurations have each energy. From that they get the heat capacity C(T) exactly, with no simulation and no statistical error. They take the **temperature of the heat-capacity maximum**, Tmax(N), as the finite lattice's stand-in for a critical temperature, locating it with Newton's method on the derivative of C.

Their lattices go well beyond brute force: free boundaries to N = 12 by a transfer-matrix method (checked against brute-force enumeration to N = 5) and to N = 20 with a Pfaffian algorithm, plus periodic boundaries to N = 20 from Kaufman's solution. They then compare several ways of pushing Tmax(N) to N → ∞: scaling models, an "envelope" construction across the C(T) curves, and the Binder cumulant method. The result they settle on is that a **single-parameter model whose correction goes as N/(N + 1)** converges reliably on Onsager's exact value, and they offer a physical reading of why that proportionality appears.

## Checked with the tools

What follows is a **recomputation of the paper's idea with fibub's own code**, not a reproduction of its numbers: the tool enumerates exactly only up to 4 × 4 and only with **periodic** boundaries, where the paper reaches 20 × 20 with both boundary types. Our question is a user's question: how far can you get with the small lattices a phone can enumerate?

**Step 1: exact heat capacities of tiny lattices.** The tool sums over every configuration: 16 of them for 2 × 2, 512 for 3 × 3 and 65,536 for 4 × 4. The peaks sit at

| Lattice | Peak temperature Tmax | Peak height C | Above Tc by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 × 2 | 2.51092 | 0.4148 | 0.2417 |
| 3 × 3 | 2.47648 | 0.6533 | 0.2073 |
| 4 × 4 | 2.43895 | 0.8165 | 0.1698 |
| infinite (Onsager) | 2.269185 | diverges | 0 |

Every peak is above Tc and every one is low and broad, exactly the behaviour the paper's much larger lattices show. Nothing here is fitted: these are exact numbers for exact finite systems.

**Step 3: the choice of variable matters, which is the paper's point.** Fit the same two points against **1/N** instead and the intercept is **2.3264**, 2.52 % high: three times the error for the same data. Add the 2 × 2 lattice to the 1/(N + 1) fit and it gets *worse*, **2.3379** (3.03 % high), because a four-spin lattice is nowhere near the asymptotic regime and drags the line. So the (N + 1) form does converge faster, as the paper reports, and the smallest lattices should be left out rather than added for the sake of more points.

**Step 4: extend with Monte Carlo.** Above 4 × 4 the tool switches to Metropolis Monte Carlo. Scanning temperature and fitting a parabola to the three highest heat-capacity points gives a peak at **2.392 for 8 × 8** and **2.308 for 16 × 16**, against **2.372 and 2.333** predicted by the line drawn from the exact 3 × 3 and 4 × 4 points: agreement to a few hundredths, which is about the accuracy a flat, broad peak allows with single-spin-flip updates near criticality. Fitting all four points (3, 4, 8, 16) in 1/(N + 1) gives **2.2774**, now only **0.36 %** above the exact value.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/finite-lattice-ising-heat-capacity-peak-critical-temperature.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `exactSmall` (full enumeration of a periodic L × L lattice), `Lattice` and `measure` (Metropolis sweeps and block-error averages), `onsager` (the exact infinite-lattice energy and heat capacity, through a complete elliptic integral) and `TC` from `src/lib/sci/phising.ts`, the maths behind the [[ising-model]] playground. Peak temperatures come from a golden-section search on the exact curves and a three-point parabola on the Monte Carlo scans; the extrapolations are plain least squares in 1/(N + 1) or 1/N.

From the paper: the method (heat-capacity maximum as the finite-lattice critical temperature, extrapolated with a correction in N/(N + 1)). **Our own choices**: periodic boundaries only, lattices 2 × 2 to 4 × 4 exactly and 8 × 8 and 16 × 16 by Monte Carlo, the scan windows, the sweep counts (10,000 to 20,000 settling and 120,000 to 200,000 measured sweeps per temperature) and the random seeds. Nothing is taken from the paper's tables.

## Check it yourself

On the [[ising-model]] page, in the *Temperature scan* card (the exact enumeration lives in the tool's maths and its tests, so the page itself uses Monte Carlo):

1. *Lattice size* **8 × 8**, *Lowest T* **2.25**, *Highest T* **2.55**, *Temperatures* **13**, *Settling sweeps per T* **2000**, *Measured sweeps per T* **50000**, *Field h* **0**, *Random seed* **1**. Press **Run the scan**.
2. The headline reads **Heat capacity peaks at T ≈ 2.35 (Onsager Tc = 2.269)**, with *Heat capacity peak* **T = 2.35**, C = **1.21 ± 0.012** per spin.
3. Change *Lattice size* to **16 × 16** and run it again: the peak moves down to **T = 2.3** and up to **C = 1.55 ± 0.024**. The bigger lattice has a taller, sharper peak closer to Tc, which is the finite-size effect the paper extrapolates away.
4. Try **32 × 32** (slower): the peak stays at **T = 2.3** on this grid but grows to **C = 2.01 ± 0.085**. Heat-capacity peaks grow only logarithmically in 2D, so this is the expected creep rather than a divergence.
5. In the *Live lattice* card, set *Temperature T* to **2.269** and press **Run**: domains of every size appear and never settle. That is what the peak is made of.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Teach finite-size scaling honestly**: show that a finite lattice's peak is *above* the critical temperature, then recover Tc by extrapolation, all with exact numbers for the small lattices.
- **Test an extrapolation scheme** on a system whose answer is known exactly before trusting it on a model whose answer is not, as the paper does.
- **Check a simulation code** against exact enumeration at 3 × 3 and 4 × 4, where every configuration is countable, before scaling up.
- **Plan a Monte Carlo campaign**: the scan shows how many sweeps a peak needs at each size, and how quickly the error bars grow near Tc, where single-spin-flip updates slow down.
- **Compare observables**: the heat-capacity peak, the susceptibility peak and the Binder cumulant crossing all converge on Tc at different rates, and the tool prints the first two in the same scan.

## The fine print

- fibub's exact enumeration is limited to 4 × 4 and to **periodic** boundaries. The paper's free-boundary lattices converge differently, and its transfer-matrix and Pfaffian methods reach 20 × 20; our numbers are not comparable with its tables, only with its method.
- Our Monte Carlo peaks carry an uncertainty of a few hundredths from the flat, broad peak and from critical slowing down; the parabola fit through three points is a convenience, not a proper finite-size-scaling analysis.
- The agreement within 1 % from two tiny lattices is partly luck: the correction terms happen to be small in this variable. The paper's many lattices are what make the convergence demonstrable rather than anecdotal.
- Units throughout are J = k_B = 1, no magnetic field, nearest-neighbour coupling only.
