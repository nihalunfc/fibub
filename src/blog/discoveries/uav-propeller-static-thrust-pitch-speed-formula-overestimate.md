Anyone building a drone eventually meets the same question: how much thrust will this propeller actually give? The internet's answer is a one-line "static thrust" formula based on diameter, pitch and rpm. A 2025 paper in *Engineering Proceedings* put that formula on a test bench, running two three-blade 16-inch propellers from 2000 to 7000 rpm and comparing measured thrust with the formula and with an online calculator. The formula came out high, badly so at low speed. This article uses the [[propeller-thrust|propeller thrust tool]] to show which part of the disagreement is physics and which part is the rig, and to work out the shaft power those thrusts need, which the paper does not report. The next steps for a drone build are the [[aircraft-performance|aircraft performance calculator]] and [[drone-flight-time|the drone flight time tool]], with air density from [[standard-atmosphere]].

## The paper in brief

The authors bolted two propellers to a small engine on a thrust stand: **propeller A, 16 × 8 inch**, and **propeller B, 16 × 10 inch**, both three-blade. They recorded thrust at six speeds from 2000 to 7000 rpm, then compared three routes to the same number: the measurement, a textbook static-thrust formula (their Equation 6, built from diameter, pitch and rpm) and a well-known online propeller calculator.

At the top speed the three agree reasonably: for propeller A, **3.39 kgf measured** against **4.30 kgf** from the formula and 4.821 kgf from the calculator. At 2000 rpm they do not: **0.21 kgf measured** against 0.35 and 0.390 kgf. Propeller B is worse still: **0.14 kgf measured** at 2000 rpm against 0.39 kgf. The paper concludes that the formula is usable near the design speed and unreliable at low speed, and that the bigger pitch gives more thrust at high speed.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the paper's formula is a scaling law, and the tool confirms which one.** Static thrust from a fixed propeller must grow as the square of rpm, because both the dynamic pressure on the blade and the momentum flux through the disc do. The paper's own numbers obey that exactly: 4.30 ÷ 0.35 = **12.29**, against (7000/2000)² = **12.25**. Its two propellers differ by the square root of pitch: 4.81 ÷ 4.30 = **1.119**, against √(10/8) = **1.118**. So Equation 6 is `T ∝ rpm² d^3.5 √pitch`, and nothing in it knows about the blade's aerofoil, its chord or stall.

**Step 2: the blade element model reproduces the high-speed measurement, with one free number.** We built each propeller in the tool's blade element momentum (BEM) model: three blades, 0.406 m across, a 50 mm hub, a chord tapering from root to tip in the ratio 38:20 and the tool's simple aerofoil (lift slope 5.7 per radian, CLmax 1.2, cd0 0.012). The one number we fitted is the chord scale, chosen so the curve passes through the measured 7000 rpm thrust. It lands on a **root chord of 19.9 mm and tip chord of 10.5 mm** for propeller A and **24.9 mm and 13.1 mm** for B: sensible widths for a 16-inch propeller, which is itself a check that the measurement is physically reasonable.

**Step 3: the low-speed gap is in the data, not the theory.** With the geometry fixed, the tool's BEM thrust follows the square law exactly, so it predicts **0.277 kgf at 2000 rpm for propeller A** and **0.382 kgf for B**. The measurements there are 0.21 and 0.14 kgf, that is 24 % and 63 % below the square law. A propeller cannot lose 63 % of its thrust just by turning slowly; a small engine at idle, a stand with friction, or a load cell reading near the bottom of its range can all do that. The paper's formula is high at 2000 rpm mostly because the 2000 rpm measurement is low.

| 16 × 8, thrust at 7000 rpm | 3.39 kgf measured, 4.30 kgf by formula | 3.39 kgf (BEM, chord fitted) |
| 16 × 8, thrust at 2000 rpm | 0.21 kgf measured, 0.35 kgf by formula | 0.277 kgf (square law from 7000 rpm) |
| 16 × 10, thrust at 2000 rpm | 0.14 kgf measured, 0.39 kgf by formula | 0.382 kgf |

**Step 4: the number the paper leaves out is power.** Thrust alone does not size an engine. Momentum theory turns the measured thrust straight into the minimum power it could possibly take. For propeller A at 7000 rpm (33.3 N), the induced velocity through the disc is **10.2 m/s** and the ideal power **340 W**; at a realistic figure of merit of 0.65 the shaft needs **523 W**. Propeller B's 4.68 kgf needs 552 W ideal, **849 W** at the same figure of merit: the extra 38 % of thrust costs 62 % more power. The tool's own BEM run, at a figure of merit of 0.74, asks 459 W and 747 W. Figure 2 shows the whole curve, and why disc loading (256 N/m² for A, 354 N/m² for B) is the quantity to watch on a battery-powered aircraft.

A caution the model itself raises: at 7000 rpm it reports **26 of 60 blade stations stalled** for propeller A and 36 for B. That is normal for a static propeller, whose inner blade meets the air at a huge angle of attack, but it does mean the inner third of the blade is doing little useful work, and that no static test tells you much about the propeller in forward flight.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/uav-propeller-static-thrust-pitch-speed-formula-overestimate.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `bem()`, `inducedVelocity()`, `idealPower()`, `hoverPower()` and `figureOfMerit()` from `src/lib/sci/eng-prop.ts`, the maths behind the [[propeller-thrust|propeller thrust tool]].

Diameters, pitches, speeds, measured thrusts and the paper's formula and calculator values come from the paper's text. Air density is 1.225 kg/m³, as in the paper. The hub diameter, the chord taper ratio, the aerofoil model and the figures of merit (0.55, 0.65, 0.74) are **our assumptions**; the chord scale is **fitted** to the measured 7000 rpm thrust, which is the one place we used the data to set the model rather than to test it.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/propeller-thrust.mjs` runs on every change to fibub:

- Momentum theory identities are pinned exactly: thrust from power and power from thrust must invert each other, and the figure of merit recovered from a thrust and power pair must return the one used to make them.
- The BEM solver is checked against an independent SciPy calculation that solves the axial and swirl momentum balances at each blade station with `fsolve`, for the example 10 × 5 inch propeller across a sweep of advance ratios.
- New with this article: the fitted three-blade 16 × 8 inch propeller (chord 19.9 mm to 10.5 mm, pitch 0.2032 m) must give 3.390 kgf of thrust and 459 W at 7000 rpm with a figure of merit of 0.741, and its thrust at 2000 rpm must be 0.277 kgf, the square law to within a tenth of a per cent.

## Check it yourself

On the [[propeller-thrust|propeller thrust tool]]:

1. In the *Momentum theory (actuator disc)* card, set *Rotor diameter* to **0.4064** m, *Number of rotors* **1**, *Altitude* **0**, *Figure of merit FM* **0.65**.
2. Set *Find* to **Power from thrust**, *Thrust per rotor* to **3.39** and *Thrust unit* to **kg (kgf)**, with *Axial speed V* at **0**.
3. It reads **Shaft power 523 W per rotor; ideal 340 W**, with an induced velocity of about 10.2 m/s. Change the thrust to **4.68** kgf for propeller B and the shaft power becomes about **849 W**.
4. In the *Blade element momentum (your propeller)* card, type *Blades* **3**, *Diameter* **0.4064**, *Hub diameter* **0.05**, *Chord at the root* **0.0199**, *Chord at the tip* **0.0105**, *Geometric pitch* **0.2032**, *Speed (rpm)* **7000**, *Airspeed V* **0**, and leave the aerofoil boxes and *Prandtl tip loss* as they are.
5. You should see a thrust of about **33.3 N** (3.39 kgf), a shaft power near **459 W** and a figure of merit of **0.74**, with a warning that some inner stations are stalled.
6. Change *Speed (rpm)* to **2000** and the thrust falls to about **2.72 N** (0.277 kgf), a factor of 12.25 lower. That is the square law, and it is the number the paper's 2000 rpm measurement should be compared with.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a thrust bench** before publishing it: run your geometry through BEM, and check that your measurements follow rpm² across the range. A point that does not is a rig problem, not a propeller.
- **Turn a thrust requirement into a motor requirement** with momentum theory and an honest figure of merit, then check the battery with [[drone-flight-time|the drone flight time tool]].
- **Compare propellers fairly** using CT and CP rather than kgf at one speed, so diameter and rpm drop out.
- **Teach why static tests mislead**: the stalled inner stations and the collapse of ideal efficiency at zero airspeed are both visible in the tool's station table.
- **Correct for the day and the place** with [[standard-atmosphere]]: the same propeller at 1500 m and 30 °C gives roughly a tenth less thrust for the same rpm.

## The fine print

- Our BEM propeller is **not the paper's propeller**. Its blade shape and aerofoil are our assumptions, and its chord scale was fitted to one measured point.
- The paper reports thrust in kgf. We used 1 kgf = 9.80665 N.
- Momentum theory gives a floor on power, never a prediction of a real rotor's power; the figure of merit carries all the loss.
- The paper gives only its 2000 and 7000 rpm values in the text, so we compare at those two speeds; the four speeds in between appear only in its figures.
- A static test says nothing directly about thrust in forward flight, where the advance ratio changes everything.
