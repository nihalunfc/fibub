Gaia was built to map stars, but it also measured the positions of thousands of asteroids, and a few of them wobble. When a small moon circles a bigger asteroid, the glowing centre of the pair drifts slightly to and fro around the point where their weights balance. A 2024 letter in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* used that tiny wobble, plus two stellar occultations, to show that asteroid (4337) Arecibo is a binary, and to fit the moon's orbit: **46.8 km across, one lap every 32.9741 hours**. This article puts those two numbers into fibub's two-body maths and gets the paper's mass back, then shows which of the two numbers matters. The same law weighs planets in the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]] and the worlds of the [[planet-data-explorer]].

## The paper in brief

Arecibo is a small main-belt asteroid, about 20 km wide. The authors took five years of Gaia's high-precision positions (691 measurements in 90 passes of the satellite over the object), fitted the asteroid's path round the Sun, and looked at what was left over. The leftover scatter fell by about a quarter when they allowed for a small to-and-fro motion with the 33-hour rhythm already known from earlier brightness measurements. Two stellar occultations, when the asteroid passed in front of a star, pinned down the scale of the orbit.

The fitted relative orbit has a semi-major axis of **46.8 km** (error bars of +1.8 and −2.0 km), an eccentricity of only 0.06 (so nearly circular), an inclination of 27° and a period of **32.9741 h** (error 0.0004 h, about a second and a half). From the orbit they derive a system mass of **4.3 ± 0.5 × 10¹⁵ kg**, split into about 4.1 for the primary and 0.2 for the moon. With a combined size from infrared measurements, that gives bulk densities around 1200 and 1600 kg/m³: low, close to water ice and well below typical rock, which is why the title says "ice-rich". The paper itself warns that the sizes are poorly known, so the densities are less firm than the mass.

## Checked with the tools

Everything here is a **recomputation from values printed in the paper**: no image, astrometric point or fit of ours is involved. We used the maths of the [[two-body-orbit|Two-body orbit sandbox]], which solves the motion of two bodies pulling on each other exactly.

**Step 1: the mass.** Kepler's third law says the period T, the semi-major axis a and the total mass M are tied together: M = 4π²a³ / (G T²). With a = 46.8 km and T = 32.9741 h, this gives **4.3027 × 10¹⁵ kg**. The paper says 4.3. The match is to every digit the paper prints.

**Step 2: the reverse trip.** We put the paper's masses (4.1 and 0.2 × 10¹⁵ kg, **our split** of the total) into the sandbox with the paper's size and eccentricity, and asked for the period. It reads **32.984 h**, which is 0.03 % above the measured 32.9741 h. The small gap is the rounding of "4.3" in the paper: the mass that fits the period exactly is 4.3027.

**Step 3: where the uncertainty comes from.** The mass goes as a³ but only as 1/T². Figure 1 shows what that means in practice. The period is known to one part in 80,000 and moves the mass by about 0.0001 × 10¹⁵ kg, which is nothing. The size of the orbit is known to about 4 %, and cubing that gives 12 %. Across the paper's range of 44.8 to 48.6 km, the mass runs from **3.77 to 4.82 × 10¹⁵ kg**, almost exactly the paper's own 4.3 ± 0.5. So the whole quoted error on the mass is the error on the orbit's size, not the period.

**Step 4: the balance point.** Because the moon is only about 5 % as heavy as the primary, the two bodies circle a point very close to the primary. In the sandbox, the primary sits **2.18 km** from the centre of mass and the moon **44.6 km** (the rest of the 46.8 km). With an eccentricity of 0.06 the gap between them swings between **43.99 and 49.61 km**, so the distances in Figure 2 move by a few kilometres around each lap.

**Step 5: the wobble that Gaia chases.** The primary itself travels around a circle about 2.2 km in radius. Figure 3 plots it along two directions. The motion is slow: the primary moves at **0.115 m/s** and the moon at **2.36 m/s** around the balance point. Compare that with the primary's escape speed, **7.65 m/s** (from its 4.1 × 10¹⁵ kg and 9.35 km radius): a hard throw from the surface would never come back, yet the moon orbits at less than a third of that speed. Seen from Earth, that small circle shifts the centre of the light slightly and slightly differently each night, which is what the 90 Gaia passes were fitted to find.

## How we made the graphs

The graphs and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/asteroid-4337-arecibo-binary-gaia-astrometry-kepler-mass-two-body-orbit.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `system`, `positionsAt` and `integrate` from `src/lib/sci/phtwobody.ts`, the maths behind the [[two-body-orbit|Two-body orbit sandbox]]: `system` turns two bodies into their orbital elements (shape, size, period), `positionsAt` gives both positions at any time from the exact Kepler solution, and `integrate` steps both bodies directly with Newton's law as an independent check.

Where every input came from: the semi-major axis, period, eccentricity, total mass and diameters are from the paper's Table 1 and text. **Our assumptions:** the split of the mass into 4.1 and 0.2 × 10¹⁵ kg is the paper's own (its mass ratio of 0.05), but we start the bodies at the closest point of the orbit rather than at the paper's reference time; and the bodies are treated as points, so we ignore the primary's shape, which matters little at an orbit 2.5 times its diameter.

## How we test the maths

Every change to fibub runs `tests/sci-tools/two-body-orbit.mjs`. It pins the tool against an independent SciPy integration (relative tolerance 10⁻¹³) for a near-circular, an eccentric and a hyperbolic case, against the closed-form period 2π√(a³/μ), and it checks that a full period returns both bodies to the start and that m₁r₁ + m₂r₂ = 0 about the centre of mass. New with this article, the same file checks the Arecibo numbers: the mass 4.3027 × 10¹⁵ kg from the published orbit, the period 32.984 h and closest and farthest separations 43.99 and 49.61 km from the published masses, the primary's 2.18 km offset, the two densities, and energy and angular momentum conserved to 10⁻¹² over a whole orbit.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Weigh a binary from its orbit.** Any two numbers (a and T) give the system mass with the same law, so a draft table can be audited in a minute.
- **See which measurement dominates the error.** Change a by 4 % in the sandbox and the period moves by 6 %; the mass moves by 12 %. That tells a proposal-writer to spend telescope time on the orbit's size, not on the period.
- **Plan a spacecraft visit.** Slow speeds of 2 m/s and an escape speed of 8 m/s mean a lander would drift for minutes. Try the [[planet-data-explorer]] to compare with Phobos and Deimos.
- **Teach orbits with a real object.** A 33-hour moon around a 19 km rock is a good classroom example of a barycentre that sits almost on the larger body.
- **Check an occultation prediction.** With a and the orbital phase, the sandbox gives the moon's offset from the primary at a given time, in km.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation of published values**, not a new orbit fit. We did not touch the Gaia data, the occultations or the paper's Monte Carlo analysis.
- The paper's main new result is the **detection** of the binary through astrometry; the tool checks the physical consequences of the fitted orbit, not the detection itself.
- The paper says the two bodies' sizes are poorly known (inconsistent by a factor of about 1.5 across sources), so the densities depend on assumptions the orbit cannot test. The mass is firmer.
- The mass ratio of 0.05 and the 4.1 and 0.2 split are values drawn from the paper's fit, and the paper gives no error bars on them. We took them as exact.
- A real moon feels the lumpy gravity of an elongated primary. The sandbox uses two point masses.
