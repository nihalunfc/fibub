When the voltage across a gas-filled gap is high enough, a stray electron gains enough energy to knock more electrons off the gas atoms, the avalanche feeds itself and the gas **breaks down** into a glowing discharge. **Paschen's law** says the voltage this takes depends only on the gas and on pressure times gap (*pd*). Plasma labs, neon signs, vacuum switches and satellite electronics all rely on it. A new preprint (not yet peer-reviewed) from the University of Delhi tests it in a long glass tube of argon and finds it does not hold well when the electrodes are small compared with the gap. This article uses the [[paschen-curve]] tool to see how far the classic law is out, and what it would take to make it fit. The [[ohms-law-lab]] covers the circuit side: the series resistor that limits the current once the gas conducts.

## The paper in brief

The team put two flat aluminium discs inside a glass tube 70 cm long and 20 cm across, pumped it out and let in argon. They used pairs of equal discs (80 and 80 mm, 40 and 40 mm) and pairs with a smaller anode (80 and 20 mm, 40 and 20 mm), moved them from about 1 cm to 30 cm apart, and raised a DC voltage until the gas broke down, over a range of pressures. Each set was repeated on three different days.

Three things stand out. With 80 mm discs the breakdown voltage stayed near **250 V** until the gap reached about 6 cm, then climbed roughly in a straight line, ending about **140 V higher at 30 cm**. With 40 mm discs it stayed near **265 V** up to about 4 cm and rose about **170 V** by 30 cm. A smaller anode raised the voltage by a further 25 to 50 V. Neither the classic law nor common modified versions fitted the data, so the authors propose a more flexible formula with extra fitted powers.

## Checked with the tools

The tool uses the classic law: **V = B·pd / (ln(A·pd) − ln(ln(1 + 1/γ)))**. *A* and *B* describe how the gas ionises; *γ* (gamma) is the cathode's **secondary emission yield**, the number of new electrons each ion knocks out of the negative electrode. The lowest point of the curve, the **Paschen minimum**, is **e·(B/A)·ln(1 + 1/γ)**: it depends only on B/A and γ. We used the argon constants the preprint quotes, A = 10.2 per mbar per cm and B = 176 V per mbar per cm (13.6 and 234.6 in the tool's per-Torr units). Everything below is a **recomputation with the classic law**, not a fit to the preprint's data, which is only shown in its figures.

**Step 1: what the classic law expects.** With a typical textbook yield of γ = 0.07 for argon ions on a metal, the Paschen minimum is **128 V** at pd = 0.73 mbar·cm. The measured voltages, 250 V and more, are about twice that.

**Step 2: what γ would make it fit?** Turning the minimum formula round, 250 V needs **γ = 0.0049** and 265 V needs **γ = 0.0035** (Figure 1). That is low but not impossible for an oxidised aluminium surface, so for short gaps the classic law can be made to fit by choosing γ.

**Step 3: the long gaps give it away.** At 30 cm the voltages are about 390 V and 435 V. The classic law would need **γ = 0.00025** and **0.00009**, ten to fifty times smaller again (Figure 2). The cathode is the same metal at every gap, so γ cannot really change like that. Something else is taking electrons and ions out of the avalanche: in a long gap between small discs, many of them drift sideways to the tube's glass wall and are lost. The classic law has no term for that, because it assumes discs much wider than the gap. Here the gap reaches seven times the disc diameter.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and the table come from `scripts/blog-figures/argon-breakdown-long-gap-paschen-electrodes.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `paschenV` and `paschenMin` from `src/lib/sci/phpaschen.ts`, the maths behind the [[paschen-curve]] tool, and inverts the minimum formula for γ in one line. The argon constants and the measured voltages (read from the preprint's text and rounded) come from the preprint; γ = 0.07 as the "typical" value is our choice for illustration. The γ values in the table are our **back-calculation** with the classic law, not numbers from the preprint.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci-tools/paschen-curve.mjs` (run by `tests/sci.mjs`) checks the tool against SciPy and known values:

- Air (A 15, B 365, γ 0.01) must have its minimum at **pd 0.836 Torr·cm and 305.3 V**, as found by SciPy's minimiser on the same formula.
- Air at 1 atm across 1 cm must break down at **35,509 V**; argon with γ 0.07 at 1 Torr·cm at **121.5 V**.
- New with this article: argon constants given per mbar (10.2 and 176) and converted to per Torr must give the same minimum, **248.74 V** for γ 0.005, with the pd of the minimum scaling by exactly 1.33322.

## Check it yourself

On the [[paschen-curve]] tool:

1. Choose **Argon** in *Gas*, then type **13.6** in *A (1 / Torr·cm)* and **234.6** in *B (V / Torr·cm)* (the preprint's constants in the tool's units).
2. Type **0.005** in *Secondary emission γ*.
3. Type **0.3** in *Pressure* and choose **mbar** in *Pressure unit*; type **5** in *Gap d* and choose **cm** in *Gap unit*.
4. It reads *Breakdown at about 249 V*, and the *Paschen minimum* line reads about **249 V at 1.06 Torr·cm**: the short-gap measurement.
5. Change *Secondary emission γ* to **0.07**: the minimum drops to about **128 V**, the textbook expectation. Type **0.00025** and it rises to about **380 V**, near the 30 cm result.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Read a breakdown measurement**: back out the effective γ a measured minimum implies, and see whether it is physically reasonable or a sign of another loss.
- **Design a discharge cell or plasma source**: pick the pressure and gap that put a given supply voltage just above the Paschen minimum, with the *Voltage to check* box giving the range of gaps that will break down.
- **Convert units safely** between mbar, Torr and Pa with the gas constants, a common source of factor-of-1.33 errors.
- **Check high-voltage spacing** in low-pressure equipment, where the left side of the curve means a smaller gap can be safer.
- **Teach** Townsend breakdown, the meaning of γ, and why real tubes differ from the textbook curve.

## The fine print

- The preprint is **not yet peer-reviewed**, and its numbers here are read from its text, rounded; the full curves are in its figures.
- *A* and *B* are fits valid over a limited range of field per pressure, and published argon values vary by 10 to 30%. Different constants change the γ values in the table, but not the conclusion that γ would have to fall many-fold with the gap.
- The classic law assumes a uniform field between wide, flat electrodes. With small discs far apart the field is not uniform and particles reach the walls; that is exactly the regime the preprint explores.
- Breakdown in real devices also depends on surface state, gas purity and the voltage ramp rate.
