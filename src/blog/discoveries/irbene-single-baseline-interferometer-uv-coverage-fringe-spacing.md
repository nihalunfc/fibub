Most radio interferometry papers are about arrays with dozens of dishes. A 2025 paper in *Galaxies* is about two: a 32 m and a 16 m dish, 800 m apart, at Irbene in Latvia, and the argument the authors make is that a single baseline is still worth running, as long as you ask it for sensitivity and timing rather than pictures. That argument can be checked with numbers, so we put the array into the [[uv-coverage]] planner and asked what one baseline actually measures. For a single dish's sensitivity, the [[radio-telescope-calculator]] does the sums; for spectral-line work at the other end of the band, the [[hydrogen-line-analyser]]; and to see when a source is up, the [[object-altitude]] planner.

## The paper in brief

The Irbene dishes, run by Ventspils University of Applied Sciences, are former Soviet-era antennas rebuilt for astronomy. Both are fully steerable; both can observe the same source at once and have their signals correlated. The paper is a review of what the pair has been used for:

- **Methanol masers at 6.7 GHz.** Bright, compact spots of emission near young massive stars, monitored every 5 to 7 days. Between 20 February 2024 and 28 April 2025 the programme ran 236 sessions, of which 154 worked. One of the sources named is G111.542+0.776, at 92.8 mJy.
- **Quasi-periodic pulsations in stellar flares,** which need high time resolution rather than fine angles: the correlator integrates for 2 s, and the design target for flare work is about 1 s.
- **Ionospheric work with GNSS signals, navigation-satellite orbit determination, and forward-scatter radar on space objects.**

The instrument numbers that matter here: an 800 m baseline, cryogenic receivers from 4.5 to 8.8 GHz, an L-band front end at 1.45 to 1.72 GHz, a stated angular resolution of **about 15 arcseconds**, system temperatures near 30 and 35 K, an SEFD of 300 to 400 Jy in C band, and a spectral resolution of 0.002 MHz after calibration, quoted as about **0.088 km/s** at 6.7 GHz.

## Checked with the tools

We typed the array in as two antennas 800 m apart, at latitude **57.55° N** (**our input**: the paper names the site but gives no coordinates), with a 32 m dish diameter and a frequency of 6.7 GHz.

| Check | Paper | uv-coverage planner |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength at the maser line | 6.7 GHz | 4.47 cm |
| Baseline in wavelengths | 800 m | 17.9 kλ |
| Angular resolution | about 15″ | **11.54″** as λ/B, **14.07″** as 1.22 λ/B |
| Largest scale that can be imaged | not stated | 6.92″ (0.6 λ/B), *smaller* than the resolution |
| Field of view (RT-32 primary beam) | not stated | 5.43′ (1.13 λ/D) |
| Field of view (RT-16 primary beam) | not stated | 10.9′ |
| Velocity per 0.002 MHz channel | about 0.088 km/s | 0.0895 km/s |
| Velocity per 0.01 MHz correlator channel | not stated | 0.447 km/s |
| Angular resolution in L band (1.65 GHz) | not stated | 46.8″ |

**The 15 arcseconds is a convention, not a disagreement.** λ/B for 4.47 cm over 800 m is 11.54″. The same baseline described with the 1.22 λ/B convention that diffraction-limited optics use gives 14.07″, which rounds to the paper's figure. Both describe one thing, the fringe spacing, and a reader comparing Irbene with another instrument needs to know which convention is in play before concluding anything. In the L-band front end the same baseline only reaches 46.8″.

**Why one baseline cannot image.** The planner reports a "largest scale you can image" of 0.6 λ/B from the *shortest* baseline. With one baseline the shortest and the longest are the same 800 m, so the largest scale, 6.92″, comes out *smaller* than the resolution, 11.54″. There is no window of scales at all. That is the quantitative form of the paper's own position: this is not an imaging instrument. The dirty beam the planner draws from a full day's coverage measures about 5.45″ × 6.39″ across its central lobe, but it is a ridge of fringes with sidelobes as strong as the peak, not a beam you could deconvolve a map out of.

**What the day's coverage looks like.** The paper's maser G111.542+0.776 is the source in NGC 7538; converting its galactic name to equatorial coordinates gives about 23h 13m, +61.47°. At Irbene's latitude it never sets: it swings between **29.0°** and **86.1°** above the horizon, so the baseline can hold it for 24 hours. Figure 1 shows every uv point that earns: 289 samples at 5-minute spacing on one thin ellipse, reaching 17.9 kλ. Because the paper does not say which way the baseline runs, we drew both an east-west and a north-south orientation; the ellipse tilts, and the coverage stays just as thin.

**The velocity resolution checks out.** A channel of width Δν at frequency ν covers c Δν / ν in velocity: 0.002 MHz at 6.7 GHz is 0.0895 km/s, which is the paper's 0.088 km/s to the rounding. The coarser 0.01 MHz correlator channel is 0.447 km/s, too coarse for maser line structure, which is why the calibration step matters.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every recomputed number come from `scripts/blog-figures/irbene-single-baseline-interferometer-uv-coverage-fringe-spacing.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls four functions from `src/lib/sci/uvcover.ts`, the maths behind the [[uv-coverage]] planner:

- `uvTracks(antennas, latitude, declination, frequency, startHourAngle, endHourAngle, steps, minElevation)` projects each baseline into the uv plane at every sampled hour angle, in wavelengths, skipping times when the source is too low.
- `elevation(latitude, declination, hourAngle)` gives the source's height, which is how we found the 29.0° to 86.1° range.
- `scales(frequency, shortestBaseline, longestBaseline, dishDiameter)` returns λ/B as the resolution, 0.6 λ/B as the largest imageable scale and 1.13 λ/D as the primary beam, all in arcseconds.
- `dirtyBeam` grids the uv points (with their mirror images, natural weighting) and Fourier transforms them; `beamWidth` measures the half-power widths of the result.

Inputs from the paper: the 800 m baseline, the 32 m and 16 m dishes, the 6.7 GHz line, the 4.5-8.8 GHz and 1.45-1.72 GHz bands, the 0.002 and 0.01 MHz channels and the maser's name. **Our own inputs:** the latitude of 57.55°, the two baseline orientations, the 24-hour hour-angle range, the 15° elevation floor and the 5-minute sampling. The source's declination comes from the standard galactic-to-equatorial transformation of the name G111.542+0.776; as a check on that conversion, it lands on NGC 7538's catalogue position.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs`, which `npm test` runs on every change, pins this tool against cases with known answers:

- **An east-west baseline at transit** must give u exactly equal to B/λ: a 100 m baseline at 1.4 GHz, to 1 part in 10⁹.
- **A source at the celestial pole, seen from the pole,** must trace an exact circle of radius B/λ in the uv plane through a 12-hour track.
- **A Y-shaped 27-dish array** must give 351 baselines (27 × 26 / 2), and the dirty beam of that array on a dec +30° source at 1.42 GHz must measure 57.8″ east-west by 52.1″ north-south, within 2″.
- **New with this article**, in `tests/sci-tools/uv-coverage.mjs`: the Irbene pair. The 800 m baseline at 6.7 GHz must give 4.475 cm, 17.88 kλ, a resolution of 11.537″ with a largest scale of 6.922″ *below* it, primary beams of 5.432′ and 10.864′, elevations of 29.0° and 86.1° for the maser, 289 uv points in a day with none lost to the horizon, and a fringe pattern measuring 5.45″ × 6.39″.
- **The largest scale** for a 23.946 m shortest baseline at 1.42 GHz must be 1091″, within 2″, and the FFT must round-trip to 1 part in 10¹².

The Y-array beam check is the one that matters for this article: it shows the beam measured from real coverage, not from a formula, which is how we can say that a single baseline's "beam" is not an imaging beam.

## Check it yourself

On the [[uv-coverage]] planner:

1. In the *Array* card set *Layout* to **My own positions**, and in the positions box type two lines: **RT32 0 0** and **RT16 800 0**.
2. Set *Dish diameter (m)* to **32**, *Array latitude (°)* to **57.55** and *Frequency (GHz)* to **6.7**.
3. In *Observation*, set *Source declination (°)* to **61.47**, *Start hour angle (h)* to **-12**, *End hour angle (h)* to **12** and *Lowest elevation (°)* to **15**.
4. The facts list reads *Antennas and baselines* **2 dishes, 1 baselines**; *Shortest and longest baseline* **800 m to 800 m** (**17.9 to 17.9 kλ**); *Wavelength* **4.47 cm**; *Resolution (λ / longest baseline)* **11.5″**; *Largest scale you can image* **6.92″**; *Field of view (primary beam)* **5.43′**; *Time on source* **24.0 hours**, 289 uv points, highest at **86°**.
5. The headline above them reads **Beam about 5.45″ × 6.39″ (east-west × north-south)**, and the uv picture is the single ellipse of Figure 1. Note that the largest scale is smaller than the resolution: that pair of numbers is the "no imaging" result.
6. Change *Dish diameter (m)* to **16** and the field of view doubles to **10.9′**, while nothing else moves: the dish sets what you see, the baseline sets what you resolve.
7. Now switch *Layout* to **Y-shaped, 27 dishes, 0.6 km arms** and watch the largest scale jump above the resolution. That is the difference between an interferometer that images and a baseline that measures.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Settle a resolution claim before you compare instruments.** One run gives λ/B, and you can see at once whether a published figure uses λ/B or 1.22 λ/B.
- **Decide whether a project needs an array at all.** If the science is a light curve, a flux or a timing, the largest-scale and resolution pair tells you a single baseline will do; if it is structure, it will not.
- **Plan a session on a circumpolar source.** The elevation range over 24 hours shows how long a northern array can hold a target without a horizon break.
- **Check a spectrometer setup.** The channel-width-to-velocity sum catches a factor-of-five mistake like the 0.01 MHz against the 0.002 MHz channel before the observation, not after.
- **Teach the uv plane.** Two antennas, one ellipse, then 27 antennas and a filled plane, on the same page in two clicks.

## The fine print

- The baseline's orientation is **our assumption**, in two versions. The real geometry will give one particular ellipse between those two.
- The latitude is our own input. A degree or two changes the ellipse's shape slightly and the elevation range by the same amount.
- `scales` uses the usual rules of thumb: λ/B, 0.6 λ/B and 1.13 λ/D. Real synthesised beams depend on weighting, and real primary beams on the dish's illumination.
- We checked geometry and spectral arithmetic, not sensitivity. The paper's system temperatures, SEFDs and detection limits are measurements of the receivers, and the [[radio-telescope-calculator]] is the tool for those.
- The paper reviews several programmes. Nothing here says anything about its maser variability, flare or radar results.
