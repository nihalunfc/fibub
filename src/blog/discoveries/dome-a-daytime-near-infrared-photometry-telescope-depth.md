Astronomy normally waits for the Sun to set. At Dome A, the highest point of the Antarctic plateau, the Sun does not set for months, so waiting means losing the whole summer. A 2025 paper in *The Astronomical Journal* shows that the summer is not lost at all: with a small 150 mm telescope and a near-infrared camera, the team detected stars as faint as **J = 10.06** at five times the noise in a 175 second effective exposure, in daylight, with the Sun still 10° above the horizon. Near-infrared light is the trick, because the daytime sky is far darker at 1.2 µm than it is in visible light. This article rebuilds the instrument and its depth from the numbers in the paper, using the [[telescope-calculator]] for the optics and sampling, the [[magnitude-flux-converter]] for the photon budget and the [[sub-exposure-calculator]] for what read noise would do to frames this short.

## The paper in brief

Dome A sits at about 4,090 m on the East Antarctic ice sheet, where the air is thin, dry and extremely cold. The team observed bright stars through a 150 mm telescope with a near-infrared camera over the 2023/2024 austral summer, with a field of view of **0.87° × 0.69°** and an image scale of **2.5 arcseconds per pixel**.

Their headline measurements are the brightness of the daytime sky and the depth it allows. At local noon, with the Sun 27° above the horizon, the zenith sky in the J band was about **5.2 mag arcsec⁻²**. At local midnight, with the Sun still 10° up, it was about **5.8 mag arcsec⁻²** — six tenths of a magnitude darker. In that darker sky they reached **J = 10.06 at 5σ** in a 175 s effective exposure. They expect a planned 1 m class telescope at the same site to go **about 2 magnitudes deeper**, which would open year-round monitoring of transients brighter than about J = 12 over the quarter of the sky around the south celestial pole.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the values quoted above. We have none of the paper's images or tables, so nothing here is a re-reduction of their data.

**Step 1: the camera the numbers imply.** A field of 0.87° at 2.5″ per pixel needs 0.87 × 3600 ÷ 2.5 = **1,253 pixels** across, and 0.69° needs **994**. That is close to the common 1280 × 1024 format of an InGaAs short-wave infrared sensor, the usual detector for a J-band camera this size — **our inference**, since the paper's abstract does not name the detector. The pixel pitch then fixes the focal length, because image scale = 206.265 × pixel size ÷ focal length:

| Pixel pitch | Focal length for 2.5″/px | Focal ratio at 150 mm | Sensor size for this field |
|---|---|---|---|

| 25 µm | 2,063 mm | f/13.8 | 31.3 × 24.8 mm |

**Step 2: this is deliberate undersampling.** With 2.5″ pixels, a star has to be about 3.8″ wide before the Telescope calculator calls the match "Well matched": at 1.5″ seeing only 0.6 of a pixel spans the star, and the tool says *Undersampled* and suggests 0.75″ per pixel instead. For imaging that is a problem; for a survey that has to cover a quarter of the sky and find things that change, it is the right trade. Keep it in mind when reading the depth: each star's light lands in very few pixels, so each pixel carries a lot of sky.

**Step 3: the photon budget behind J = 10.06.** Feed J = 10.06 through the Magnitude converter's maths with the 2MASS J zero point (1594 Jy at 1.235 µm) and it becomes **0.151 Jy**, or 0.0184 photons per second per cm² per ångström. A 150 mm clear aperture is 176.7 cm². Over a 1,620 Å-wide J band, a 175 s exposure, 60 % detector efficiency and 70 % optical throughput (**our assumptions**, since the paper's abstract gives none), the star delivers about **388,000 electrons**.

The sky is the problem. One 2.5″ × 2.5″ pixel covers 6.25 arcsec², so the midnight sky of 5.8 mag arcsec⁻² is magnitude **3.81 per pixel** — brighter than any star in the frame, and it piles up about **1.2 × 10⁸ electrons per pixel** in the same 175 s. Measuring the star over 3 × 3 pixels (**our assumption**), the shot noise alone gives:

| Check | Paper | Our recomputation |
|---|---|---|
| Sky per pixel, midnight | 5.8 mag arcsec⁻² | mag 3.81 per 2.5″ pixel; 1.23 × 10⁸ e⁻ in 175 s |
| Signal-to-noise at J = 10.06, midnight sky | 5 | 11.7 (shot noise only) |
| 5σ limit, midnight sky | J = 10.06 | J = 10.98 |
| 5σ limit, noon sky (5.2 mag arcsec⁻²) | not stated | J = 10.68 |
| Depth lost from noon to midnight sky | 0.6 mag of sky | 0.30 mag of depth |
| Gain from a 1 m telescope | about 2 mag | 2.06 mag |

The interesting line is the third one. Pure photon counting says this telescope should have reached about J = 11.0, nearly a magnitude deeper than the paper reports. That gap is the honest signature of daytime observing: a sky this bright is not flat or steady, so flat-fielding, background gradients and sky variation between frames, not photon statistics, set the real limit. Our assumed efficiency, bandwidth and aperture size each move the number by a few tenths as well.

**Step 4: the 2-magnitude promise.** At a fixed image scale and exposure, a sky-limited telescope's faintest star goes as its diameter, because the star's signal grows as D² while the sky noise in a fixed pixel grows as D. From the Telescope calculator's light-grasp figures, going from 150 mm to 1,000 mm is 1.25 × log₁₀(grasp ratio) = **2.06 magnitudes** (Figure 2). The paper's "about 2 mag deeper" is exactly right. A 2 m telescope would give 2.81 mag, and a 500 mm one 1.31 mag.

**Step 5: how long should one frame be?** The paper's 175 s is an *effective* exposure, almost certainly the sum of many short frames, because a sky of 1.2 × 10⁸ electrons per pixel per 175 s would saturate any detector in a fraction of a second. With that much sky, the Sub-exposure calculator's rule is almost funny: read noise of 50 electrons would stop mattering after about 35 milliseconds of exposure, and 10 electrons after 1.4 milliseconds. In daylight, frames are short because of the well depth, never because of read noise.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in this article come from `scripts/blog-figures/dome-a-daytime-near-infrared-photometry-telescope-depth.mjs` in fibub's public code.

- `scope()` and `camera()` from `src/lib/astro/optics.ts`, the maths behind the [[telescope-calculator]], give the focal ratio, light grasp, Dawes limit, image scale, field of view and the sampling verdict.
- `toJy()` and `fromJy()` with the `BANDS` list from `src/lib/sci/photometry.ts`, the maths behind the [[magnitude-flux-converter]], turn J magnitudes into Jy and into photons per second per cm² per ångström.
- `subLength()` from `src/lib/sci/subexp.ts`, behind the [[sub-exposure-calculator]], gives the read-noise-limited frame length.

From the paper: the 150 mm aperture, 0.87° × 0.69° field, 2.5″ pixels, 5.2 and 5.8 mag arcsec⁻² skies, J = 10.06 at 5σ, 175 s, and the 1 m class comparison. **Our assumptions:** 60 % detector efficiency, 70 % throughput, a 1,620 Å J bandwidth, a 3 × 3 pixel measuring aperture, 1.5″ seeing for the sampling verdict, and the four pixel pitches in the table.

## How we test the maths

`node tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/telescope-calculator.mjs` on every change to fibub, which pins this arithmetic:

- A 203 mm f/10 Schmidt-Cassegrain (2,032 mm) must give f/10.0, a light grasp of 841×, a Dawes limit of 0.57″ and a faintest-star estimate of mag 14.2 — the textbook values for an 8-inch telescope.
- An IMX571 camera (23.5 × 15.7 mm, 3.76 µm pixels) on a 480 mm refractor must give 1.62″ per pixel and a 2.81° × 1.87° field, and must be called "Well matched" at 3″ seeing but "Oversampled" at 6″.
- New with this article: 15 µm pixels at 1,238 mm must give 2.50″ per pixel and a 0.87° × 0.69° field, the Dome A numbers, and 150 mm to 1,000 mm must be 2.06 magnitudes of sky-limited depth.

On the [[telescope-calculator]]:

1. In the *Telescope* card, type **150** in *Aperture (mm)* and **1238** in *Focal length (mm)*, leave *Barlow or reducer* on **None (×1)**, and type **1.5** in *Typical seeing (″)*.
2. Read the facts under it: *Focal ratio* **f/8.3**, *Light grasp* **459×**, *Faintest stars* **mag 13.6** (under a dark sky, which Dome A's summer sky is certainly not), *Resolution (Dawes)* **0.77″**.
3. In the *Camera* card, type **18.8** in *Sensor width (mm)*, **14.9** in *Sensor height (mm)*, **15** in *Pixel size (µm)*, and leave *Binning* on **1×1**.
4. It shows *Image scale* **2.50″ per pixel** and *Field of view* **52.2′ × 41.4′** — the paper's 0.87° × 0.69°. *Sampling* reads **Undersampled**, with the note that 0.75″ per pixel would suit 1.5″ seeing.
5. Now change *Aperture (mm)* to **1000** and *Focal length (mm)* to **8250** to keep the same f-ratio. *Light grasp* becomes **20408×**. The depth gain is 1.25 × log₁₀(20408 ÷ 459) = **2.06 magnitudes**.
6. On the [[magnitude-flux-converter]], choose the **J (2MASS)** band, type **10.06** as a *magnitude (Vega)* and read **0.151 Jy** and **0.0184 photons s⁻¹ cm⁻² Å⁻¹**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Reverse-engineer an instrument from a paper**: field of view plus image scale gives the detector format, and the pixel pitch gives the focal length, with the Telescope calculator.
- **Judge a reported depth** before trusting it: convert the limiting magnitude to photons with the magnitude converter, add the sky over the real pixel area, and see whether shot noise alone explains it.
- **Plan a bright-sky survey**: pick the image scale from the sky background you can tolerate per pixel rather than from the seeing, and check the sampling verdict so you know what you are giving up.
- **Scale a proposal**: sky-limited depth goes as the diameter, so 1.25 × log₁₀ of the light-grasp ratio answers "how much deeper with a bigger telescope" in one line.
- **Teach the sky-noise trade**: the same two tools show why a 0.6 mag darker sky buys only 0.3 mag of depth.

## The fine print

- The publisher's page could not be read from our network, so the paper's values here come from its abstract, and the CC BY 4.0 licence from the Crossref record for the version of record.
- Detector efficiency, throughput, bandwidth and the measuring aperture are **our assumptions**; the paper's abstract gives none. They shift the computed 5σ limit by several tenths of a magnitude.
- Our photon budget counts shot noise only. Daytime backgrounds are structured and variable, which is why our limit comes out deeper than the paper's measured one.
- The sky brightnesses are zenith values. Stars away from the zenith sit behind more air and a brighter background.
- "Faintest stars, mag 13.6" in the tool is a naked-eye-through-a-telescope rule for a dark night. It says nothing about a daylight camera limit.
