Every Mars mission starts with two numbers: when to leave, and how much launch energy the trip needs. The second one has a name, **C3**: the square of the speed a spacecraft still has after it has climbed out of Earth's gravity, in km² s⁻², and the number printed on every launch vehicle's performance chart. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Astronomy and Space Sciences* designs a single-launch Mars sample return and gives both: a primary window in mid-2029 at C3 about 8.5 km² s⁻², and a 2031 backup at about 10.2. Those are exactly the numbers fibub's [[porkchop-plot]] works out from the planets' real positions, so we checked them. For the burns on either end, the [[orbit-transfer-calculator]] and the [[rocket-equation]] take it from there, and [[mars-time]] turns an arrival date into a Martian season.

## The paper in brief

The study proposes bringing Mars samples home on **one** launch instead of the three the official plans use. A chemical stage lands a rover and a Mars ascent vehicle; a quadruped robot fetches the sample tubes; the ascent vehicle reaches a 200 km circular low Mars orbit; and from there a solar-electric stage spirals out of Mars's gravity and heads back to Earth. The paper puts the whole mission at about 40 months in seven phases and a total Δv near **9.14 km s⁻¹**, with roughly 5.9 to 6.6 km s⁻¹ from chemical stages and 2.5 to 3.2 km s⁻¹ from the electric stage.

Its trajectory section is where a reader can check the work. It states a primary launch window of **May to July 2029**, with the lowest launch energy, about **8.5 km² s⁻²**, around **15 June 2029**, a 21-day stretch with C3 under 10, transits of **7 to 9 months**, and a Mars arrival excess speed (v∞) of **2.65 km s⁻¹**. The backup window is 2031, minimum C3 about **10.2 km² s⁻²**, transits 8 to 9 months.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is a **recomputation** from the planets' real positions: no fitting and no simulated data. For each departure date, the tool solves Lambert's problem (the single orbit that joins Earth's position on the launch date to Mars's position after a chosen flight time) and reports C3 and the arrival excess speed. We took the lowest C3 over flight times of 150 to 420 days, which covers the paper's 7 to 9 months and then some.

**Step 1: where the windows really are.** Earth-to-Mars windows repeat every **780 days** (the synodic period; the tool prints it), so there is one window per 2.1 years, not one per calendar year. Between September 2028 and July 2031 there are exactly two. The first bottoms out on **30 November 2028** at **C3 9.00 km² s⁻²** with a 315-day flight; the second on **23 February 2031** at **C3 8.17** with a 320-day flight.

**Step 2: the paper's primary date is on the wrong side of the dip.** On **15 June 2029** the best C3 we can find, over every flight time from 150 to 420 days, is **39.0 km² s⁻²**, with an arrival excess speed of 8.40 km s⁻¹. That is 4.6 times the paper's 8.5, and it adds 1.24 km s⁻¹ to the Earth departure burn: from a 200 km parking orbit, C3 9.00 costs **3.63 km s⁻¹** while C3 39.0 costs **4.87 km s⁻¹**. The paper's own description of its window ("C3 below 10 for 21 days, transit 7 to 9 months, arrival v∞ 2.65") matches the real window of **late 2028**, not June 2029. In our grid, C3 stays at or below 10 from **14 November 2028 to 25 January 2029**, a 72-day stretch, and the lowest arrival excess speed anywhere in that window is **2.97 km s⁻¹**, close to the paper's 2.65. The most likely reading is that the design is sound but the window has been labelled by its **arrival** year: leaving on 30 November 2028 and flying 315 days lands you at Mars in **October 2029**.

**Step 3: a 7.5-month transfer is not free.** The paper wants 7 to 9 months. A 228-day (7.5-month) flight leaving on 30 November 2028 costs **C3 10.1** instead of 9.00, and raises the arrival excess speed from 3.17 to **5.09 km s⁻¹**, which nearly doubles the capture burn. Figure 2 shows why: below about 280 days the curve turns up steeply. The classic Hohmann flight time between circular, flat orbits is **259 days** (the tool prints this too), so 7 months is already below the cheapest possible coasting transfer. C3 under 10 **and** a 7.5-month transit **and** an arrival v∞ of 2.65 km s⁻¹ cannot hold at the same time.

**Step 4: the ascent budget double-counts the Oberth effect.** The paper's Table 2 lists "low Mars orbit to Mars escape, 1.46 km s⁻¹" and "Earth transfer insertion, 2.94 km s⁻¹" as separate lines. The first one is right: from a 200 km circular orbit the tool gives **1.429 km s⁻¹** to reach escape speed exactly. But you do not pay escape first and acceleration afterwards. One burn from the same orbit that leaves with v∞ = 2.75 km s⁻¹ (the middle of the paper's 2.5 to 3.0 range) costs **2.151 km s⁻¹** in total, not 1.46 + 2.94 = 4.40. Burning deep in a gravity well is cheap, because the extra speed adds to an already large orbital speed, and this is the single largest saving in a Mars-ascent budget.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/mars-sample-return-2029-launch-window-c3-porkchop.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `leg` (one Lambert transfer: C3, departure and arrival excess speeds), `burnFromOrbit` (the single burn out of a circular parking orbit), `synodicDays` and `hohmannDays` from `src/lib/astro/porkchop.ts`, the maths behind the [[porkchop-plot]]. That module gets the planets' heliocentric positions and velocities from the JPL-based `astronomy-engine` library and solves Lambert's problem with `src/lib/sci/lambert.ts`.

Inputs: the departure dates, the flight-time range of 150 to 420 days and the 200 km parking orbit altitude are **ours**, chosen to cover the paper's stated 7 to 9 months and its stated low Mars orbit. The C3 values, the window dates, the arrival excess speeds and the burns are all computed, not taken from the paper. The paper's 8.5, 10.2, 2.65, 1.46 and 2.94 are quoted from its text for comparison.

## How we test the maths

`npm test` runs `tests/sci.mjs` on every change, and its porkchop section pins this maths against published results:

- The Lambert solver reproduces Curtis, *Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students*, Example 5.2: v₁ = (−5.9925, 1.9254, 3.2456) km s⁻¹ and v₂ = (−3.3125, −4.1966, −0.38529) km s⁻¹, to 10⁻³ km s⁻¹.
- The real Mars 2020 launch, 30 July 2020 with a 203-day flight to an 18 February 2021 landing, must come out at **C3 14.5 km² s⁻²** (±0.3), which is the number NASA published.
- The Hohmann flight time Earth to Mars must be **259 days** and the synodic period **780 days**.
- The window-finder, asked for the next window after 8 November 2026, must land within 12 days of 2 November 2026 and give **C3 9.2**.
- New with this article, in `tests/sci-tools/porkchop-plot.mjs`: the late-2028 window must bottom out on 30 November 2028 at **C3 9.00** with a 315-day flight and an arrival v∞ of **3.17 km s⁻¹**; the best C3 on 15 June 2029 must be **39.0**; and the burn out of a 200 km Mars orbit must be **1.429 km s⁻¹** to escape and **2.151 km s⁻¹** to reach v∞ = 2.75 km s⁻¹.

## Check it yourself

On the [[porkchop-plot]] page, in the *Trip* card:

1. Set *From* to **Earth** and *To* to **Mars**. Put **2028-10-01** in *Earliest departure*, **150** in *Departure dates to cover (days)*, **150** in *Shortest flight (days)* and **420** in *Longest flight (days)*. Leave *Colour by* on **Launch energy C3**.
2. The headline reads *Cheapest launch in this range: 28 November 2028, 315 days to Mars, C3 9.00 km²/s²*. Under it, *Departure excess speed* **3.00 km/s** (burn from a 200 km orbit around Earth: **3.63 km/s**) and *Arrival excess speed* **3.14 km/s** (burn to stop in a 200 km orbit around Mars: **2.35 km/s**). *Hohmann transfer* says **259 days** and *Windows repeat every* **780 days**.
3. Now change *Earliest departure* to **2029-06-15** and *Departure dates to cover (days)* to **5**. The cheapest launch becomes **C3 39.0 km²/s²** on a 420-day flight, with an arrival excess speed of **8.40 km/s**. That is the paper's date.
4. Go back to **2028-10-01** and **150** days, then set *Colour by* to **Arrival excess speed**. The new top line, *Lowest arrival excess speed*, reads **2.97 km/s**, for a launch on **20 November 2028** with a 301-day flight.
5. For the 2031 backup, put **2030-12-01** in *Earliest departure* with **150** days covered: **22 February 2031, 319 days, C3 8.18 km²/s²**.
6. Tap anywhere on the plot to read one flight; *Whole grid (CSV)* downloads all 3,600 of them.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a mission study's window** before you believe its mass budget: a wrong month moves C3 by a factor of four, which no propulsion trick recovers.
- **Trade flight time against arrival speed** on one chart, so a shorter cruise is costed honestly in capture Δv rather than hidden.
- **Cost a departure or capture burn properly** with one burn from the parking orbit, not an escape burn plus an injection burn, then feed it to the [[rocket-equation]] for propellant mass.
- **Plan backups**: windows repeat every 780 days for Mars, so the tool shows at a glance what a two-year slip costs.
- **Teach the Oberth effect** with Figure 3: the same chart shows why high thrust low down beats the same Δv far out.
- **Put an arrival date into its Martian season** with [[mars-time]], which matters for dust storms and solar power at landing.

## The fine print

- The tool models **ballistic, single-orbit transfers** with no gravity assists, no deep-space manoeuvres and no aerobraking. The paper's electric stage spirals, which is a different problem; our check applies to its chemical launch leg and its ascent budget.
- Planet positions come from an approximate ephemeris. Errors of a few hundred km are irrelevant at this resolution: our C3 values are good to about 1 %, which the Mars 2020 test confirms.
- We could not read the paper's own porkchop figure, only its text; if its figure axes are arrival dates rather than departure dates, the "May to July 2029" label is consistent with the real late-2028 window.
- The paper has other internal mismatches we did not check: its Figure 2 caption gives 158 days for the electric escape spiral where the text gives 232 to 273, and 3.52 km s⁻¹ for a spiral Δv the text puts at 2.80.
- C3 is launch energy, not propellant. Converting it to a rocket's payload needs that vehicle's own performance curve.
