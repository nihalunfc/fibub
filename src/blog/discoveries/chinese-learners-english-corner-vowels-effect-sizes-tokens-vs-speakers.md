A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Psychology* compared the four "corner" vowels of American English, /i/, /æ/, /ɑ/ and /u/, as spoken by 12 native speakers and 84 learners from Chinese-speaking backgrounds, using a public read-speech corpus. Its central table gives, for each vowel and each of the first two formants, both groups' means, standard deviations and token counts, plus a Cohen's d and a p-value: 16 comparisons in all. That is everything needed to redo the statistics from scratch, which is exactly what the [[hypothesis-tests]] tool is for. The vowel measurements themselves belong to the [[vowel-formant-chart]], and if you only need to move between d, r and odds ratios, that is the [[effect-size-converter]].

## The paper in brief

A vowel's identity is carried mostly by its first two formants, the lowest two resonances of the vocal tract: **F1** falls as the tongue body rises, **F2** falls as the tongue moves back and the lips round. The four corner vowels mark the extremes, so the quadrilateral they trace is a compact picture of a speaker's vowel space.

The author pulled thousands of vowel tokens for each group out of a corpus of read English, measured F1 and F2 in Praat at three points in each vowel and averaged them, then compared groups with t tests and Cohen's d. The reported conclusions: the learners' corner vowels differ from the natives', the biggest differences involve /ɑ/ and /u/, neighbouring consonants such as /w/ pull the values about, and the learners' vowel space is **larger** than the native speakers'. The abstract says the learners show "lower F1 and F2 values".

## Checked with the tools

Welch's t test and Cohen's d depend only on each group's mean, standard deviation and count, so a published table of those three is enough to reproduce the test exactly. We build, for each group, the smallest set of numbers that has precisely the published mean and standard deviation, and run the tool's own `tTwo` on it. Nothing is simulated and nothing is assumed: this is the paper's own arithmetic, redone.

**Step 1: fourteen of the sixteen effect sizes reproduce.** Figure 1 plots the printed Cohen's d against ours. Fourteen points land on the line of equality, agreeing to better than **0.007**. Two do not:

- the women's **/æ/ F1**: printed **−0.037**, recomputed **−0.373**, which looks like a slipped decimal point;
- the men's **/u/ F1**: printed **+0.10**, recomputed **−0.229**, with the sign the wrong way round. The means themselves (369 Hz native against 396 Hz learner) can only give a negative d with the paper's sign convention.

A third cell deserves a note rather than a correction. The women's native /i/ F1 is printed with a standard deviation of **1,235 Hz** on a mean of 405 Hz, which is impossible for formant data. Feed that number in and the printed d of −0.087 comes back exactly (we get −0.0873), so it is the figure the paper's own calculation used. The effect size for that one cell is therefore not interpretable, and the typo is in the table, not in the statistics.

**Step 2: three "p = 0.000" cells are nowhere near significant.** Fifteen of the printed p-values are 0.000 and one is 0.066. We reproduce the 0.066 (we get **0.068**, for the men's /ɑ/ F2), and twelve of the others come out below 1e-5 as expected. But three do not:

| Comparison | Recomputed d | Printed p | Our Welch p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Women's /ɑ/ F2 | 0.031 | 0.000 | **0.655** |
| Women's /æ/ F2 | −0.028 | 0.000 | **0.559** |
| Women's /i/ F1 | −0.087 | 0.000 | **0.387** |

With differences of 7 Hz and 8 Hz on standard deviations of 200 to 300 Hz, no sample size in this study could make those significant. A p-value of 0.000 beside a d of 0.03 should always prompt a second look.

**Step 3: the significance comes from counting tokens, not speakers.** This is the substantive point. The study has **6 native women and 44 learner women** (6 and 40 men), but the tests use **310 to 5,380 vowel tokens** per cell. Tokens from the same person are not independent observations of "how Chinese learners say /u/": one speaker contributes dozens of them. Redo every test with the number of speakers in place of the number of tokens, keeping each group's mean and standard deviation exactly as printed, and **not one of the 16 comparisons reaches p = 0.05**. The smallest p is **0.1146**, for the women's /u/ F2, which is also the largest effect in the study (d 0.879). Figure 2 shows both sets of p-values against the size of the difference.

That does not mean the differences are not real. It means the published p-values measure how many vowels were measured, not how many people were sampled, and the honest reading of this table is its effect sizes: one large (/u/ F2, d 0.88), two moderate (/ɑ/ F1 in both sexes, d around −0.65), and the rest small.

**Step 4: the vowel space really is larger, and we can say why.** The [[vowel-formant-chart]]'s own geometry lets us turn the four corner means into a quadrilateral and measure it. The learners' space is **bigger in both sexes**: 195,047 against 146,300 Hz² for the women (**1.33 times**) and 129,094 against 102,864 Hz² for the men (**1.26 times**). So the paper's claim stands. The reason is visible in Figure 3: the learners' /u/ sits much further back (the /i/-to-/u/ F2 gap is 865 Hz against 646 Hz for the women), and their /ɑ/ is more open (F1 787 against 690 Hz), stretching the quadrilateral in both directions.

That last number also settles the abstract. The learner group's **F1 is higher, not lower, in all eight cells**, and their F2 is lower only for /i/ and /u/ while being slightly higher for /æ/ and /ɑ/. "Lower F1 and F2" does not describe this table; "a more open /ɑ/ and a much backer /u/" does.

## How we made the graphs

The script is `scripts/blog-figures/chinese-learners-english-corner-vowels-effect-sizes-tokens-vs-speakers.mjs`. For every test it calls `tTwo(x, y, true)` from `src/lib/sci/hyptest.ts`, the maths behind the [[hypothesis-tests]] tool, which returns Welch's t, its degrees of freedom, the p-value, the difference with its confidence interval, Cohen's d, Hedges's g and an interval for d.

Because the tool works from data rather than summaries, the script first builds, for each cell, the smallest sample that has exactly the published mean and sample standard deviation: values placed symmetrically either side of the mean, plus one at the mean when the count is odd. Welch's t and Cohen's d depend on nothing else, so this reproduces the published test rather than approximating it. The quadrilateral areas in step 4 are a shoelace sum over the four corner means, in the F2-against-F1 plane the [[vowel-formant-chart]] draws.

Every mean, standard deviation, token count, printed d and printed p comes from the paper's Table 1. The speaker counts used in step 3 are the paper's own (6 and 44 women, 6 and 40 men). Keeping each group's token-level standard deviation while cutting the count to the number of speakers is **our choice**, and it is the conservative one: between-speaker spread is smaller than token-level spread, so a proper speaker-level test would have a slightly larger t than ours and still, at these effect sizes, nothing below 0.05 except perhaps the /u/ F2 cell.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/hypothesis-tests.mjs` on every change, pinning the module against SciPy and R on fixed made-up data. Among the checks:

- Welch's t, its fractional degrees of freedom and its p-value must match `scipy.stats.ttest_ind(equal_var=False)`, and Student's pooled version `equal_var=True`;
- the t distribution's tail must match `scipy.stats.t.sf` across a range of degrees of freedom, including the fractional ones Welch's test produces;
- the gamma-function correction behind Hedges's g must be 0.92274 at 10 degrees of freedom;
- one-sample, paired, Mann-Whitney (exact and with ties), Wilcoxon, ANOVA with Tukey HSD, Levene, chi-square, Fisher's exact and Shapiro-Wilk all have their own reference values from SciPy, R and statsmodels.
- New with this article: the women's /u/ F2 cell of this paper (1832 ± 395 over 423 tokens against 1520 ± 349 over 3,125) must give t = 15.4495 on 515.12 degrees of freedom, d = 0.8794 and p = 1.6e-44; cutting the counts to 6 and 44 speakers must give p = 0.11457.

## Check it yourself

The paper's thousands of tokens cannot be pasted into a browser, but its biggest finding can be tested at speaker level in half a minute. On the [[hypothesis-tests]] page:

1. In card *1*, set *What did you measure?* to **Numbers (weights, scores, times)** and *What are you comparing?* to **Two separate groups**. Set *Roughly bell-shaped (normal)?* to **Yes, or 30+ values per group** and leave *Test* on **Chosen from your answers**, which picks **Welch's t test (two groups)**.
2. In card *2. Your data*, paste two columns: six made-up per-speaker averages of /u/ F2 in hertz for each group, chosen to sit close to the paper's means and spreads.

   ```
   Native  Chinese
   1305    1065
   1590    1310
   1770    1450
   1880    1570
   2160    1795
   2335    1930
   ```

3. Leave *Alternative* on **Two-sided (any difference)**, *Confidence level* on **95 %** and *Significance level α* on **0.05**.
4. You should read a difference of **320 Hz**, Welch's **t = 1.597** on **9.73** degrees of freedom and **p = 0.1423**: not significant. The 95 % interval for the difference runs from **−128 to 768 Hz**.
5. Look at the effect size in the same panel: **Cohen's d = 0.922**, Hedges's g **0.851**. A large effect with a non-significant p is what six speakers per group buys you, and it is the honest summary of this comparison.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Re-test a published table.** If a paper prints means, standard deviations and counts, the tool reproduces its t test exactly; a mismatch means a typo or a different test.
- **Check whether the sample is what the authors think it is.** Swap token counts for subject counts and rerun. If the conclusion changes, the paper needed a mixed model.
- **Report the effect size, not just the star.** The tool shows d, Hedges's g and an interval for both beside every t test.
- **Catch typos before review.** Figure 1 took one script to make, and it found two bad numbers in a published table.
- **Teach the difference between size and significance.** Change the number of rows in the paste box, leave the means alone, and watch the p-value move while d does not.

## The fine print

- The tool fits a two-sample t test; the paper's own model may have included consonant context as a factor, which would change the residual variance and so the p-values. We reproduce the comparisons as its Table 1 presents them.
- The learner group mixes 58 Chinese, 22 Taiwanese and 4 Hakka-speaking participants, and the table's per-sex counts (44 and 40) do not add up to those figures. We used the table's counts.
- Our exact-moment samples have the right mean and standard deviation but not a believable shape, so any test that uses the data's distribution (Mann-Whitney, Shapiro-Wilk) would be meaningless on them. Only t tests and d are reproduced here.
- The native group is 6 women and 6 men. Nothing about American English in general can rest on that, however many tokens they produced.
- The quadrilateral areas are in hertz, so they mix vowel-system differences with vocal-tract length. The learner groups are larger in both sexes, which argues against that being the whole story, but a per-speaker normalisation would settle it.
