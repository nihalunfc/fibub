Eye charts are being replaced by screens, and every new digital chart has to prove it reads the same as the paper one. A 2026 paper in *Upsala Journal of Medical Sciences* tested 42 people aged 55 and over on a custom digital chart and on the standard ETDRS chart, twice each, and found the digital chart gave acuities **0.03 logMAR better**, with a 95 % confidence interval of about **0.04 logMAR** and nothing like the author's **0.1 logMAR** threshold for a real difference. Those are the right units for the job, and they are also units almost nobody can picture. This article uses the [[visual-acuity-converter]] to turn them into ETDRS letters, chart lines and Snellen fractions, and then shows a trap in the same paper's design: averaging acuity. For the next steps, plot the agreement itself with [[bland-altman]], check repeatability with [[reliability-analysis]] and size the next study with [[sample-size]].

## The paper in brief

Forty-two subjects, all 55 or older, were tested with both charts on **two separate occasions**, and sorted into **four acuity classes** so that the comparison could be checked across the range rather than only in people who see well. A nested analysis of variance split the variation into its parts.

The findings: the digital chart read **-0.03 logMAR** compared with ETDRS (a slightly *better* acuity, since lower logMAR is better), with a confidence interval of about **+/- 0.04 logMAR**, well inside the **0.1 logMAR** resolution the author treats as the smallest meaningful step. Most of the variance came not from the charts but from the **testing occasion**: the same eye on a different day differs more than the two charts differ from each other. Only the abstract page of the article could be read from our container, so this article uses only the numbers above.

For readers new to the units: **logMAR** is the base-10 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution in minutes of arc. 0.0 logMAR is 6/6 (20/20), 1.0 logMAR is 6/60 (20/200), and each line of a logMAR chart is **0.1 logMAR**, which is five letters on an ETDRS chart (0.02 logMAR per letter).

## Checked with the tools

Everything in this section is a conversion or an average computed with the maths behind the acuity converter. No measurement is involved, which is the point: these numbers are fixed by the definitions, so any paper can be translated into any notation without the authors' data.

**Step 1: the paper's numbers in letters and lines.** The converter gives, for a difference of 0.03 logMAR, exactly **1.5 ETDRS letters** (0.03 / 0.02) and 1.5 points of the Visual Acuity Score. The 0.1 logMAR threshold is **5 letters**, one whole chart line. In ratio terms, 0.03 logMAR is a **7.2 % change** in the minimum angle of resolution, and 0.1 logMAR is **25.9 %**.

| The paper says | The tool gives |
|---|---|
| Mean difference -0.03 logMAR | 1.5 ETDRS letters better, 1.5 VAS points, a 7.2 % smaller resolution angle |
| 95 % confidence interval about +/- 0.04 logMAR | +/- 2 ETDRS letters, under half a chart line |
| 0.1 logMAR resolution threshold | 5 letters, one line; 6/6 becomes 6/7.6, and 6/60 becomes 6/75.5 |
| Acuity 0.0 logMAR | 6/6, 20/20, decimal 1.00, 85 ETDRS letters |
| Acuity 1.0 logMAR | 6/60, 20/200, decimal 0.10, 35 ETDRS letters |

So the whole result is a letter and a half, with an uncertainty of two letters: smaller than the step between two adjacent lines, and far smaller than the day-to-day variation the paper itself identifies as the largest source of variance.

**Step 2: why Snellen differences cannot be compared.** A fixed step in logMAR is a fixed *ratio*, not a fixed difference in the Snellen denominator. One line (0.1 logMAR) turns 6/6 into **6/7.6**, costing 1.55 metres of denominator; the same one line turns 6/60 into **6/75.5**, costing 15.5 metres. Figure 1 shows the two curves, the acuity and the acuity one line worse, so the widening gap between them is what one line "costs" at each point of the chart. A paper reporting "a mean improvement of 10 Snellen denominator units" has said almost nothing without saying where on the chart it started.

**Step 3: averaging acuity, the mistake that changes conclusions.** The paper groups its subjects into four acuity classes and compares means. Means of acuity must be taken in logMAR (Holladay 1997), because acuity is a ratio scale. Averaging decimal acuities instead flatters the group; averaging the resolution angles (or Snellen denominators) punishes it. The tool's averaging function makes the size of the error plain:

| Two eyes | Correct mean (logMAR) | Averaged as decimal acuity | Averaged as MAR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6/6 and 6/12 | 0.151 logMAR, 6/8.5, 77.5 letters | 0.125, **1.3 letters too good** | 0.176, 1.3 letters too poor |
| 6/6 and 6/24 | 0.301 logMAR, 6/12, 69.9 letters | 0.204, **4.8 letters too good** | 0.398, 4.8 letters too poor |
| 6/6 and 6/60 | 0.500 logMAR, 6/19, 60 letters | 0.260, **12.0 letters too good** | 0.740, 12.0 letters too poor |

Twelve letters is two and a half chart lines, from nothing but the choice of scale. For a group spread like the paper's four classes (we used 6/6, 6/12, 6/24 and 6/60 as **our example**), the correct mean is **0.476 logMAR (6/17.9, 61.2 letters)** while the decimal average gives **0.335 logMAR (68.3 letters)**, a seven-letter gift. Figure 2 shows how the error grows with the spread of the group: it is zero only when everyone sees alike, which is exactly the situation a four-class design avoids.

**Step 4: how precise can a chart be at all?** The ETDRS chart's resolution is one letter, 0.02 logMAR, and five letters per line. A difference of 0.03 logMAR is therefore smaller than two letters, which is inside the published test-retest range of a single ETDRS chart (usually about +/- 0.1 logMAR in healthy eyes, and worse in disease). The tool's nearest-line function makes this concrete: 0.26 logMAR is read as **the 6/12 line plus 2 letters**, and there is no finer reading available. A study chasing a 0.03 logMAR effect needs many more than 42 subjects, which is what [[sample-size]] is for.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/digital-chart-etdrs-visual-acuity-agreement-logmar-letters.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fromLogMAR`, `toLogMAR`, `meanAcuity`, `parseValue` and `nearestLine` from `src/lib/sci/acuity.ts`, the maths behind the [[visual-acuity-converter]]: `fromLogMAR` converts one logMAR value into MAR, decimal, Snellen in feet and metres, the Visual Acuity Score and the ETDRS letter score, and `meanAcuity` averages in logMAR and converts the result back.

From the paper: the mean difference of -0.03 logMAR, its 95 % confidence interval of about 0.04 logMAR, the 0.1 logMAR threshold and the four-class design. **Our examples:** the four acuity classes used for the averaging demonstration (6/6, 6/12, 6/24, 6/60) are our own round numbers, not the paper's classes, which the readable page does not list. No simulation and no fitting is involved anywhere in this article; every number is a conversion.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/visual-acuity-converter.mjs`, which pins the converter against a separate Python implementation and published examples:

- eight Snellen inputs, including 6/12+2 and 20/25-1, must convert to logMAR, MAR, decimal, 20/x, 6/y, VAS and letters exactly as the Python version does (20/40 gives logMAR 0.30103, decimal 0.5, VAS 84.9485 and 69.9485 letters);
- the Gregori (2010) example must hold: 20/40 is 70 ETDRS letters;
- the Holladay (1997) example must hold: the average of 20/20 and 20/200 is 20/63.2, **not** 20/110;
- the 17 chart lines must each satisfy VAS = 100 - 50 x logMAR and letters = VAS - 15, and their rounded labels must sit within 3 % of the exact values;
- new with this article: a difference of 0.1 logMAR must be exactly 5 letters and a MAR ratio of 1.258925; averaging 6/6 with 6/60 in logMAR must give 0.5 logMAR while the decimal average gives 0.259637, a 12.018-letter difference; and our four-class example must give a logMAR mean of 0.475772 against a decimal-average 0.334888.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Translate a paper's effect into letters before judging it.** Any logMAR difference divided by 0.02 is the number of ETDRS letters; the tool does the whole table, including VAS and both Snellen forms.
- **Average acuities correctly**, and show reviewers how big the error would have been the other way. The tool averages in logMAR and prints the equivalent Snellen.
- **Convert a mixed dataset once.** Clinical records arrive as 6/9, 20/30, decimal 0.7 and counting fingers; pasting a column converts them all to one scale with the parsing notes attached.
- **Set a meaningful threshold for your own study**: pick the number of letters you care about, convert it to logMAR, and feed that into [[sample-size]] with a realistic test-retest standard deviation.
- **Check whether a device comparison is even measurable** by converting its claimed precision into letters; anything under two letters is below the resolution of a single chart reading.

## The fine print

- Only the article's **abstract page** was readable from our container, so we use its mean difference, confidence interval and threshold, and none of its per-class means. The licence was read on the journal's page and confirmed in Crossref.
- The four acuity classes in our averaging example are **our own round numbers**, not the paper's classes.
- The ETDRS letter score here is the usual 85 - 50 x logMAR convention; some papers use 100 - 50 x logMAR (the Visual Acuity Score), a fixed 15-point difference that the tool shows side by side.
- Conversions assume the same letter sizes and the same scoring rule on both charts. A chart with four letters per line, or a different progression, changes the letters-per-logMAR step, which the tool lets you set.
- Nothing here says whether the digital chart is good enough for clinical use; that needs the agreement limits and repeatability, which is what [[bland-altman]] and [[reliability-analysis]] are for.
