**Fucoxanthin** is the brown pigment of kelps and other brown seaweeds, and it keeps turning up in diabetes research because it slows the gut enzymes that release sugar from starch. A 2025 paper in *Marine Drugs* reports a new fucoxanthin derivative from the brown alga *Chnoospora minima*, docks it against α-amylase and α-glucosidase, and says it has **one Lipinski violation and one Veber violation**, which it attributes to a molecular weight above 500 Da and a polar surface area above 140 Å². Those two counts are the part of such a paper anyone can check in a browser, and that is what the [[smiles-checker|Drug-likeness tool]] is for. For the structure itself and its mass, see [[molecule-viewer]] and [[molar-mass-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors isolated a carotenoid from *Chnoospora minima*, give its molecular formula as **C42H58O7**, and describe it as fucoxanthin in which an unsaturated ketone at C8′ replaces the allene bond. Docking put it at **−9.4 kcal/mol** against α-amylase and **−8.0** against α-glucosidase, better than acarbose (−8.5 and −7.4), the drug used in the clinic for the same purpose, and molecular dynamics kept the complexes stable. Predicted ADMET values include a plasma protein binding of 78.24 % and a plasma clearance of 7.864 mL/min/kg. The drug-likeness verdict is the sentence we test: one broken Lipinski rule and one broken Veber rule.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation of molecular descriptors**, not a re-docking: nothing here tests the binding energies, which need a protein structure and a scoring function.

**Step 1: get the molecule right first.** The paper prints no SMILES string, so we wrote fucoxanthin from its published structure and let the tool mark its own homework: it returns the formula **C42H58O6**, a molar mass of **658.92 g/mol** and a monoisotopic mass of **658.4233**, all of which match the published values for fucoxanthin. That does not prove every bond is where it should be, but a wrong skeleton almost never lands on the right formula and both masses at once.

**Step 2: the derivative's weight.** From the paper's own formula C42H58O7, the molar mass is **674.92 g/mol** (monoisotopic 674.4183), 16.00 more than fucoxanthin: one extra oxygen, as the formula says. So the Lipinski weight rule is broken, comfortably, and the paper's count of **one Lipinski violation is right**: donors (2) and acceptors (6 or 7) are both well inside their limits, and logP is the only rule left, which needs a program the tool does not pretend to be.

**Step 3: the Veber violation is the wrong one.** The Drug-likeness tool counts **12 rotatable bonds** in fucoxanthin, against Veber's limit of 10. That alone breaks Veber, whatever the polar surface area is. Meanwhile the polar surface area, summed from Ertl's fragment contributions, is **96.4 Å²** for fucoxanthin and **113.4 Å²** for the derivative if the extra oxygen is the ketone the paper describes: both far under the 140 Å² limit the paper blames. You would have to add **two more hydroxyl groups** to the molecule before that rule broke (Figure 2). So the paper's tally of one and one survives, but for a different reason than it gives, and the reason matters: rotatable bonds are a flexibility problem you fix by rigidifying the chain, while a high polar surface area is a polarity problem you fix by removing polar groups. They point medicinal chemists in opposite directions.

| Check | Paper | Drug-likeness tool |
|---|---|---|
| Molecular weight (derivative) | "above 500 Da" | **674.92 g/mol** from C42H58O7 |
| Lipinski violations | 1 | **1** (weight only; donors 2, acceptors 6) |
| Veber violations | 1 | **1** |
| Which Veber rule | polar surface area > 140 Å² | **rotatable bonds: 12 > 10** |
| Polar surface area | > 140 Å² | **96.4 Å²** (fucoxanthin), **113.4** (derivative) |

**Step 4: how bad is 12 rotatable bonds, really?** Figure 1 puts the molecule among others the tool can measure. Lycopene, the tomato carotenoid, has **16** rotatable bonds and also breaks both rules. Retinol (vitamin A) is small and flexible enough to pass everything. And **orlistat**, an approved oral drug for obesity that acts on a gut enzyme just as these compounds are meant to, has **23** rotatable bonds: more than twice Veber's limit, and more than any carotenoid here. A broken rule of thumb is a flag for a chemist, not a disqualification, and for a molecule meant to act in the gut lumen rather than in the bloodstream it may not even be the right flag.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/fucoxanthin-derivative-lipinski-veber-violations.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `check` from `src/lib/sci/druglike.ts`, the maths behind the [[smiles-checker|Drug-likeness tool]] (molecular formula, molar and monoisotopic mass, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, rotatable bonds, rings, and the Lipinski and Veber verdicts), and `parseFormula`, `molarMass` and `monoMass` from `src/lib/sci/chem.ts`, which also power the [[molar-mass-calculator]].

The derivative's formula C42H58O7, the "one Lipinski, one Veber" claim and the docking scores are the paper's. The fucoxanthin SMILES string is **ours**, written from the published structure and validated against its formula and both masses. Reading the extra oxygen as one more ketone is **our assumption**, taken from the paper's own description. The polar surface areas are **our arithmetic** from Ertl's fragment table, not the tool's: the tool asks you to type a polar surface area from another program rather than guess one.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/smiles-checker.mjs`, which pins the descriptors against reference values:

- Aspirin from `CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O` must give C9H8O4, 180.159 g/mol, a monoisotopic mass of 180.0423, 13 heavy atoms, 1 ring, 1 donor, 4 acceptors and 3 rotatable bonds.
- Caffeine, paracetamol, ibuprofen and a sodium-salicylate SMILES must give their published formulas and masses, with the salt stripped and reported separately when the largest-part option is on. In all, the descriptors were checked against RDKit on 67 molecules and 268 rewritings of them.
- Amide bonds must be excluded from the rotatable count when that option is set, as Veber's paper specifies.
- New with this article: our fucoxanthin SMILES must give C42H58O6, 658.92 g/mol, monoisotopic 658.4233, 2 donors, 6 acceptors, 3 rings and 12 rotatable bonds, with exactly one Lipinski and one Veber violation; and the formula C42H58O7 must give 674.92 g/mol.

## Check it yourself

On the [[smiles-checker|Drug-likeness tool]] page, in the *Molecule* card:

1. Paste into *SMILES*: `CC(=O)OC1CC(C)(C)C(=C=CC(C)=CC=CC(C)=CC=CC=C(C)C=CC=C(C)C(=O)CC23OC2(C)CC(O)CC3(C)C)C(C)(O)C1` and type **Fucoxanthin** in *Name (optional)*.
2. The *Properties* table reads **C42H58O6**, **658.92 g/mol**, **2** hydrogen-bond donors, **6** acceptors, **12** rotatable bonds and **3** rings.
3. Read the verdicts: Lipinski shows **one** broken rule (the weight), Veber shows **one** (rotatable bonds).
4. Type **113** into *Your polar surface area, Å² (optional)*. Veber now tests polar surface area instead of donors plus acceptors, it passes, and the rotatable-bond rule still fails: one violation, as the paper says, from the other rule.
5. Type **145** instead, as the paper implies, and Veber now shows **two** broken rules. That is the quickest way to see that the paper's stated reason and its stated count do not fit together.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a drug-likeness table in a paper** before citing it: formula, mass, donors, acceptors and rotatable bonds are all exactly computable from a structure, so there is no reason to take them on trust.
- **Say which rule broke, not just how many.** A rotatable-bond failure and a polar-surface-area failure call for opposite chemistry.
- **Sanity-check a SMILES string you typed** by looking at the formula and monoisotopic mass before you use it in docking: a mis-drawn ring shows up there first.
- **Put a natural product in context** by running a few approved drugs through the same tool, as in Figure 1, rather than comparing it only with the rules.
- **Keep a screening batch tidy**: the tool's *Many molecules* box takes a whole `.smi` list and gives one CSV of properties and verdicts.

## The fine print

- The paper gives no SMILES or InChI for its derivative, so we could not compute its descriptors directly; our fucoxanthin string is a stand-in whose formula and masses match the parent compound.
- logP is not computed by the tool, so one Lipinski rule is untested here; our "one violation" refers to the three rules the tool can check.
- Polar surface areas quoted here are fragment sums we did by hand from Ertl's table. Different programs report slightly different values, but none of them would put this molecule above 140 Å².
- Nothing here tests the docking scores, the dynamics or any ADMET prediction in the paper.
- Fucoxanthin is a food component under study, not a treatment. General education, not medical advice.
