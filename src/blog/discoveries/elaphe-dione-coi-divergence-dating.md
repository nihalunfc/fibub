Dating a split between two groups of animals from their DNA looks simple: count the differences, divide by a rate. The trouble is the rate. A 2026 study of the **steppe rat snake** (*Elaphe dione*), a harmless snake that lives across the dry middle of Eurasia, found 11 genetic lineages and dated their deepest split to about 1.7 million years ago, in the middle of the Pleistocene ice ages. This article takes the distances and the date from that paper and asks the [[molecular-clock]] calculator a plain question: what substitution rate does that date require, and how does it compare with the clock most people quote? Line the sequences up first with the [[sequence-alignment|pairwise sequence alignment]] tool and get the groupings from the [[tree-builder]].

## The paper in brief

The authors sampled 130 snakes from 100 places across the species' range and sequenced three mitochondrial gene fragments. The snakes fall into 11 **phylogeographic lineages**, groups of populations that share a recent maternal ancestor, gathered into three clades. Average **uncorrected COI distances** between those lineages, that is the plain share of sites that differ in the barcoding gene, run from 0.7 % to 6.7 %.

Their dating puts the main west-east split at roughly 1.7 million years ago, with the busiest period of lineage splitting around 1.3 million years ago. That timing matches the **Mid-Pleistocene transition**, when ice-age cycles lengthened from about 41,000 to about 100,000 years and Eurasian steppe habitat was repeatedly broken up and rejoined. The paper's story, then, is climate carving one wide-ranging snake into many lineages.

Everything we use below is from that abstract: the distance range, the two dates and the gene. We could not open the paper's full text, so we have not touched its alignments, its software settings or its confidence intervals.

## Checked with the tools

The clock calculator works both ways round. Given a distance *d* and a rate *r* per lineage it returns the time **T = d / 2r**; given a distance and a date it returns **r = d / 2T**. We ran both on the paper's numbers.

**Step 1: what the standard clock says.** The rate people reach for first is about **2 % pairwise divergence per million years** for vertebrate mitochondrial DNA, that is 1 % per lineage. Typing the deepest lineage distance, 6.7 %, with that rate gives a split **3.35 million years ago**. The shallowest distance, 0.7 %, gives 0.35 million years. So on the conventional clock the paper's lineages would have begun separating in the Pliocene, not the Pleistocene, and the deepest split would be about twice as old as the paper's date (Figure 1).

**Step 2: the rate the paper's date needs.** Turn the question round. Feed the calculator the same 6.7 % distance and a calibration of 1.7 million years, and ask for the rate. It gives **0.0197 substitutions per site per million years per lineage**, which is 3.9 % pairwise per million years, about **1.97 times** the conventional rate. Figure 2 shows how that implied rate changes with the age you assume: a date of 3.4 million years would bring it back to the textbook 1 % per lineage.

**Step 3: how much of the gap is error?** Two things are uncertain. The distance has a sampling error, because a barcode is a few hundred sites: for a 650-site COI fragment (**our assumption**; the usual barcode length) a distance of 6.7 % carries a standard error of **0.0099**. The rate is far shakier, so we gave it a generous standard error of 0.002 (**our assumption**, a fifth of the rate). With both, the standard clock puts the deepest split at **3.35 ± 0.83 million years**, an approximate 95 % range of **1.72 to 4.98**. The paper's 1.7 million years sits just at the bottom edge. Run it the other way and the implied rate is 0.0197 ± 0.0029 per lineage, a 95 % range of **0.0140 to 0.0254**, which does not reach down to 0.01.

**Step 4: does correcting the distance help?** The paper quotes uncorrected distances. Correcting for sites that changed twice makes them bigger, not smaller, so it widens the gap. Counting 6.7 % of 650 sites as 44 differences in the ratio of five transitions to one transversion (**our assumption**) gives a Jukes-Cantor distance of 0.0709 and a Kimura 2-parameter distance of 0.0720, which the standard clock turns into 3.55 and 3.60 million years. At the shallow end the correction is almost invisible: 0.77 % becomes 0.773 %.

None of this says the paper is wrong. A rate about twice the textbook value is exactly what short, recent timescales usually give: rate estimates from young splits come out faster than rates calibrated on fossils, because recent change has not yet been filtered by selection or hidden by repeat hits. Modern dating also uses relaxed clocks, several loci and real calibrations, and a fixed single-rate sum like ours is the crudest possible version. The useful result is the size of the difference: anyone reading this paper should know that a textbook 2 % clock would have placed these snake lineages in the Pliocene, and that the Pleistocene story depends on a faster rate.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/elaphe-dione-coi-divergence-dating.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `distWithSE` (p, Jukes-Cantor and Kimura 2-parameter distances with their standard errors), `divergenceTime` (T = d / 2r with first-order error propagation) and `clockRate` (r = d / 2T) from `src/lib/sci/molclock.ts`, the maths behind the [[molecular-clock]] calculator.

From the paper: the 0.7 % and 6.7 % COI distances, the 1.7 million year date and the gene. **Our assumptions**, none of them from the paper: 650 aligned sites, five transitions per transversion, a standard rate of 1 % per lineage per million years with a standard error of 0.002. Nothing here is a reanalysis of the paper's alignment, because we never had it.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/molecular-clock.mjs` on every change to fibub. It pins the calculator against reference values worked out in Python, with the distance variances re-derived by an independent numerical delta method:

- On a made-up pair of 150-site sequences with 14 transitions and 4 transversions, the K2P distance must be 0.133677 with a standard error of 0.033119, and the Jukes-Cantor distance 0.130765 ± 0.031587.
- The same distance with a rate of 0.01 ± 0.002 per lineage must give T = 6.6839 ± 2.1282 million years, and a calibration of 5 ± 0.5 million years must give r = 0.0133677 ± 0.0035715.
- Tajima's relative rate test on three made-up sequences must count m1 = 6 and m2 = 11, giving χ² = 1.47059 and p = 0.225253, the p-value from SciPy's `chi2.sf`.
- New with this article: 44 differences in 650 sites as 37 transitions and 7 transversions must give a p-distance of 0.0676923, Jukes-Cantor 0.0709444 and K2P 0.0719894, and that p-distance with a rate of 0.01 ± 0.002 must date the split at 3.35 million years with a 95 % range of 1.72 to 4.98.

## Check it yourself

On the [[molecular-clock|molecular clock calculator]] page:

1. Set *Distance from* to **A distance I already have**. Type **0.067** in *Distance d (substitutions per site)* and **0.00985** in *Its standard error*.
2. Leave *Work out* on **When they split (time)**, *Time unit* on **million years** and *Rate given as* on **per lineage (T = d / 2r)**. Put **0.01** in *Rate r* and **0.002** in *Rate standard error*.
3. It reports a split about **3.35 million years ago**, standard error **0.83**, with an approximate 95 % range of **1.72 to 4.98** million years.
4. Switch *Work out* to **The rate (from a known split)** and enter **1.7** as the *Calibration time*, leaving its standard error at 0. The rate comes out at **0.0197** substitutions per site per million years per lineage, 95 % range **0.0140 to 0.0254**.
5. Now take the long way round. Set *Distance from* to **Site counts**, with *Sites compared* **650**, *Transitions* **37** and *Transversions* **7**, and *Correction* on **Kimura 2-parameter (K2P)**. The distance reads **0.0720 ± 0.0112**; back on **When they split**, with the same rate, it dates the split at **3.60** million years.
6. Change *Correction* to **None (p-distance)** and watch the distance drop to **0.0677** and the date to **3.35** million years: that is the whole effect of correcting for repeat hits at this depth.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Sanity-check a published date** before you cite it: put the paper's distance and date in and read off the rate it implies, then ask whether that rate is reasonable for your gene and group.
- **Choose a rate honestly.** Switch between *per lineage* and *pairwise divergence* in the tool and see the factor of two that mixing them up produces, which is the commonest error in quick clock arithmetic.
- **Plan a barcoding study.** See how the sampling error of a distance shrinks as you lengthen the fragment, and decide whether 650 sites can tell two of your lineages apart.
- **Teach the clock in one screen**, with the distance correction, the error range and the relative rate test all visible, then move on to the [[tree-builder]] and [[dnds-calculator|dN/dS calculator]] for the tree and the selection behind the rate.
- **Test rate equality** between two lineages with an outgroup (Tajima's test on the same page) before you trust one rate for both.

## The fine print

- We used only the values in the paper's **abstract**; its full text was not reachable from our machine on 24 November 2026, so we do not know its calibrations, its clock model or its confidence intervals. Its own dating is certainly more careful than the single-rate arithmetic here.
- The 650 sites, the five-to-one transition ratio and the standard error on the rate are **our assumptions**, not the paper's.
- A single fixed rate ignores rate variation between lineages, and rates estimated from young splits are known to run fast; that alone can explain a factor of about two.
- The paper's distances are averages between lineages, not between the particular pair of individuals whose split a date describes, so the two quantities are not exactly matched.
- This is a comparison of arithmetic, not a reanalysis: nothing here can confirm or refute the paper's phylogeography.
