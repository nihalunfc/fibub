A wing lifts and an engine pushes by moving air, so a plane cares about how **dense** the air is, not how high the runway is. Hot air is thin air, and thin air means a longer run before the wheels leave the ground. A 2025 paper in *Aerospace* fed ten climate models into a take-off model of the Airbus A320, the workhorse of European short-haul flying, and asked how much longer take-offs will get at 30 European airports by the 2050s. This article uses the [[density-altitude]] calculator to show that the paper's temperature and pressure sensitivities are two views of one thing, air density, and to add the humidity effect the paper leaves out. For the next steps, try the [[aircraft-performance]] calculator and the [[standard-atmosphere]] table.

## The paper in brief

The authors built a force-balance model of an A320 take-off (thrust, drag, rolling friction and lift, stepped along the runway until the plane reaches take-off speed and clears a 35 ft, or 10.7 m, obstacle) and ran it with daily temperature and pressure from 10 CMIP6 climate models under three emission scenarios. They compared 2035 to 2064 with 1985 to 2014.

The headline findings: the **take-off distance required** (TODR) grows by about **50 to 110 m** on average, with big day-to-day swings. Days that were once 1-in-100 extremes become common in summer at some airports. On short runways, such as Rome Ciampino (2,203 m), the **maximum take-off mass** has to fall on hot days, worth about **5 to 12 passengers per flight by 2065**. The authors also give simple sensitivities for Heathrow under standard conditions: TODR rises **4.2 m per K** of warming and falls **1.2 m per hPa** of extra pressure for a lightly loaded A320, and **7.4 m per K** and **2.2 m per hPa** when fully loaded. Humidity and wind were left out.

## Checked with the tools

**Density altitude** turns air density into a height: the height in the standard atmosphere where the air would be this thin. The tool's maths uses the ICAO standard atmosphere and the gas law. We used it to put the paper's two sensitivities on one scale. This is a **recomputation** from the four numbers in the paper's text; we did not rerun the paper's climate model.

**Step 1: what 1 K and 1 hPa do to the air.** At standard sea level (15 °C, 1013.25 hPa, dry air), warming by 1 K raises the density altitude by **36.07 m** (118.3 ft, close to the pilots' rule of 120 ft per °C). A fall of 1 hPa raises it by **10.28 m**. So **3.51 hPa of pressure equals 1 K of temperature** for the air's density. Figure 1 shows the curves.

**Step 2: the paper's sensitivities, as density.** If take-off distance depends only on air density, the paper's two numbers must stand in that same 3.51 to 1 ratio. For the lightly loaded A320 they do: 4.2 ÷ 1.2 = **3.50**. Put another way, both give the same extra take-off run per metre of density altitude: **0.1164 m** from the temperature number and **0.1168 m** from the pressure number, 0.3 % apart. For the fully loaded plane the ratio is 7.4 ÷ 2.2 = **3.36**, and the two routes give **0.2051** and **0.2141 m** per metre, 4.4 % apart. Figure 2 shows the four sensitivities falling onto two lines. A small gap is expected: an engine's thrust depends on temperature as well as density, and the heavier plane needs more of it.

**Step 3: hot days at a sea-level airport.** With the tool's density altitude and the per-metre rates from Step 2, a 30 °C afternoon at standard pressure (density altitude **525 m**, air 95 % as dense as standard) adds about **61 m** of take-off run for the light A320 and **108 m** for the full one compared with a 15 °C day. At 35 °C the density altitude is 694 m and the extra run 81 to 142 m. These are straight-line uses of sensitivities worked out at 15 °C (**our simplification**); the paper's own model is the better guide at the extremes.

**Step 4: the humidity the paper left out.** Water vapour is lighter than dry air, so humid air is thinner. At 30 °C and 1013.25 hPa, a dew point of 20 °C (a muggy summer day) adds **90 m** of density altitude to the dry-air value, worth roughly **10 to 19 m** more take-off run on the paper's own rates. A dew point of 10 °C adds 47 m (about 5 to 10 m of run); 25 °C adds 122 m (about 14 to 25 m). That is small next to the 50 to 110 m climate signal, but not zero, and it is largest at the humid coastal airports.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/climate-warming-a320-take-off-distance-density-altitude-european-airports.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `densityAlt` from `src/lib/sci/densalt.ts`, the maths behind the [[density-altitude]] calculator: it finds the air density from pressure, temperature and dew point (with the virtual temperature for moist air), then the height in the ICAO standard atmosphere with that density.

The four sensitivities (4.2 and 7.4 m per K, 1.2 and 2.2 m per hPa) come from the paper's text. The 15 °C and 1013.25 hPa reference is the standard atmosphere the paper also uses. The hot-day temperatures, the dew points and the straight-line use of the sensitivities are **our assumptions**, chosen to illustrate.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/density-altitude.mjs`, which pins the calculator against independent software:

- The standard atmosphere's layer bases must give 226.32 hPa at 11 km and 54.749 hPa at 20 km, as the ambiance Python package does, and sea-level density must be 1.225 kg/m³.
- An airfield at 5,000 ft with 29.92 inHg and 30 °C dry air must give a pressure altitude of 5,001 ft and a density altitude of 7,802 ft; a 20 °C dew point must add 340 ft, matching MetPy's moist-air density.
- New with this article: at ISA sea level, 1 K must add 36.07 m and 1 hPa 10.28 m of density altitude (3.51 hPa per K), and 30 °C must give 525.5 m dry and 615.5 m with a 20 °C dew point.

## Check it yourself

On the [[density-altitude]] calculator:

1. Set *Heights in* to **metres**, *Pressure in* to **hPa (mb)** and *Pressure given as* to **Station pressure**.
2. Type **1013.25** as the station pressure, **0** for *Elevation* and **16** for *Air temperature*, and clear *Dew point (blank = dry air)*. The density altitude reads **36 m**: one degree above standard.
3. Change *Air temperature* to **15** and the pressure to **1014.25**. It reads **−10 m**: one extra hectopascal is worth about 0.29 of a degree (10.28 ÷ 36.07), the 3.5 to 1 ratio.
4. Set the pressure back to **1013.25** and the temperature to **30**. It reads **525 m**. Multiply by 0.116 for the light A320's extra take-off run: about 61 m.
5. Type **20** in *Dew point*. It reads **616 m**, and *Density altitude, dry air* says humidity adds **90 m**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a model's sensitivities for consistency**: turn per-degree and per-hectopascal numbers into density altitude with the [[density-altitude]] calculator and see whether they agree.
- **Add humidity to a dry-air study**: run the dew points of a station record through the same maths and see how much density altitude moisture adds.
- **Teach the standard atmosphere**: compare density, pressure and temperature with height in the [[standard-atmosphere]] table.
- **Estimate a take-off ground roll** from mass, wing, drag and engine at a chosen air density with the [[aircraft-performance]] calculator, then repeat at a hot-day density (not for flight planning).
- **Turn climate projections into aviation terms**: take projected summer temperatures for an airport and read off density altitude on the hottest days.

## The fine print

- We recomputed only the link between the paper's sensitivities and air density; we did not rerun its take-off model or its climate projections.
- The sensitivities are for Heathrow under standard conditions; the straight-line hot-day numbers are our simplification.
- The 4.4 % gap for the full A320 suggests thrust depends on temperature beyond density; we did not model the engine.
- Humidity, wind and runway slope change take-off distance too; the paper names them as future work.
- This is general science, not flight planning: pilots use their aircraft's performance charts and official weather.
