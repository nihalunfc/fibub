In a normal reactor the fuel stays where it is, so every delayed neutron from a fission product is born where the fission happened. In a molten-salt reactor the fuel *is* the coolant: it leaves the core, goes round a loop and comes back about 25 seconds later. The fission products go with it, and the delayed neutrons they emit outside the core are lost to the chain reaction. A 2025 paper in *Frontiers in Nuclear Engineering* models the 1960s Molten Salt Reactor Experiment with both a full spatial code and point kinetics, and reports exactly how much is lost. Because it prints its six-group delayed-neutron data, anyone can put the same numbers into the [[point-kinetics]] tool and see what the loss does to the reactor's response. For what happens after a shutdown, carry on to the [[decay-heat]] estimator and the [[decay-chain]] simulator.

## The paper in brief

The Molten Salt Reactor Experiment ran at 8 MW thermal with a fuel salt flowing through a graphite core, inlet 905 K and outlet 928 K, circulating round its loop in 25.2 s. The authors model it in two codes, one solving the full space- and time-dependent neutronics with the precursors swept along by the flow, the other a point-kinetics model, and compare both with the measurements from the 1960s.

Their central number is the **effective delayed-neutron fraction**, β-effective: the share of neutrons that arrive late and so set the pace at which a reactor can change power. With the fuel standing still their U-235 core gives **654 pcm** (a pcm is one part in 100,000 of reactivity), close to the 666 pcm reported for the experiment. Start the pump and it falls to **431 pcm**, a loss of **34.1 %**; the measured value was 454 pcm, a loss of 31.8 %. For the later U-233 loading the same loss runs from 298 pcm down to 188 pcm, or 36.9 %. Starting the pump also costs a steady **212 pcm** of reactivity, with a transient dip of 284 pcm about 13 s in that their simulations miss by 34 pcm.

## Checked with the tools

The Point kinetics tool solves the six-group point-kinetics equations with whatever delayed-neutron data you type in, so the paper's two tables go straight in. We used a prompt neutron generation time Λ of 4 × 10⁻⁴ s (**our assumption**: the paper does not report one). It barely matters for steps this small — the period for a 50 pcm step is 114.4, 114.9 and 115.9 s for Λ of 2, 4 and 8 × 10⁻⁴ s.

**Step 1: the data adds up.** The tool's own total of the paper's six U-235 groups is **653.82 pcm** and of the U-233 groups **298.23 pcm**, matching the totals the paper prints.

**Step 2: where the loss comes from.** A delayed neutron counts only if its precursor is still inside the core when it decays. With plug flow, 9.4 s inside the core and 25.2 s round the loop (standard design figures for this reactor, not from the paper), the share of each group still emitting in the core is 0.374, 0.381, 0.465, 0.672, 0.875 and 0.963 from the longest-lived group to the shortest. The slow groups are mostly thrown away; the fastest group, half-life a quarter of a second, hardly notices the trip. Applying those shares group by group gives **408.5 pcm**, a loss of **37.5 %** — a little more than the paper's 34.1 % from its full 3D model, as a hand calculation with an evenly spread source and no importance weighting should be. Matching the paper's 431 pcm exactly needs a core residence of 10.65 s instead of 9.4 s. (This arithmetic is ours, not the tool's, and not the paper's method.)

The same 50 pcm is 7.6 cents of reactivity in the stationary U-235 core and 29 cents in the flowing U-233 core, and the stable period falls from nearly two minutes to a quarter of a minute. The paper's 212 pcm pump-start loss is **0.32 $** against the stationary fraction but **0.49 $** against the flowing one — most of a dollar's worth of control, which is why a molten-salt reactor needs its pump reactivity worked out carefully rather than estimated.

**Step 4: the total is not enough.** It is tempting to take a published flowing β-effective and scale all six groups by the same factor. The tool says that is not the same reactor. Cutting every group evenly to 431 pcm gives a stable period of **67.5 s** for a 50 pcm step; keeping the per-group shape above but renormalising it to the same 431 pcm total gives **44.2 s**. Same β-effective, a period one third shorter, because the surviving precursors are the short-lived ones that cannot hold the reactor back for long.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/msre-flowing-fuel-delayed-neutron-loss.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `simulate` (the stiff Radau IIA solver for the six-group equations), `inhourRoots` (the seven roots of the inhour equation), `promptJump` and `betaTotal` from `src/lib/sci/eng-pointkinetics.ts`, the maths behind the Point kinetics tool.

From the paper: both six-group tables, the 25.2 s circulation time, the 431 and 454 pcm flowing values and the 212 pcm pump loss. General reference figures: the 9.4 s core residence time. **Our assumptions**: Λ = 4 × 10⁻⁴ s, a 50 pcm step as the test push, plug flow with an evenly spread precursor source and no importance weighting, and no temperature feedback at all — a real core would heat up and push back long before the power had multiplied twenty times.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/point-kinetics.mjs`, which pins the solver against SciPy and NumPy:

- Seven transients (steps of 0.1 $, 0.5 $, −1 $ and 1.2 $, a 1 cent per second ramp and a step with a scram) must match a SciPy `solve_ivp` Radau integration at relative tolerance 10⁻¹¹ to better than one part in a million; for example the 0.5 $ step must reach 473.665498 times its starting power at 30 s.
- The seven inhour roots must match NumPy's polynomial roots to nine figures: for 0.1 $ and Λ = 10⁻⁴ s, the largest is 0.01012988 per second, a 98.7 s period.
- The prompt jump must be exactly β/(β − ρ) = 2 for a 0.5 $ step, and the simulation must show that jump within 0.03 by 0.03 s.
- New with this article: the paper's U-235 groups must sum to 653.82 pcm and its U-233 groups to 298.23 pcm, and the flowing U-235 set must give a 41.1 s period and 3.66 times the power 40 s after a 50 pcm step, against 114.9 s and 1.72 times for the same core with the fuel standing still.

## Check it yourself

On the [[point-kinetics]] tool page:

1. Set *Units* to **pcm**, *Change* to **Step**, *Step size* to **50**, *Time to simulate (s)* to **40** and *Plot* to **Log power**. Put **4e-4** in *Prompt neutron generation time Λ (s)*.
2. In *Six groups: βᵢ (fraction), λᵢ (1/s), one group per row* paste the paper's stationary U-235 data, one group per line: **0.0002284 0.013336**; **0.0011808 0.032739**; **0.0011278 0.12078**; **0.0025281 0.30278**; **0.0010384 0.84949**; **0.0004347 2.853**.
3. Read *Delayed-neutron fraction β* **0.006538**, *Stable period (inhour)* about **115 s** and *Prompt jump* **× 1.083**. The power curve ends near **1.7** times its start.
4. Now paste the flowing set, each group cut by its own share: **0.00008551 0.013336**; **0.0004501 0.032739**; **0.0005249 0.12078**; **0.001698 0.30278**; **0.0009084 0.84949**; **0.0004185 2.853**. β falls to **0.004085**, the period to **41 s**, the prompt jump rises to **× 1.14** and the power reaches about **3.7**.
5. For the U-233 loading with the pump running, paste **0.00009526 0.012911**; **0.0002607 0.034738**; **0.0002462 0.11928**; **0.0006895 0.28617**; **0.0002959 0.7877**; **0.0001174 2.4417**: β **0.001705**, period **16.3 s**, a prompt jump of **× 1.415**, and a power that runs off the top of the chart (about 21 times its start by 40 s).
6. Switch *Units* to **dollars** and type **1** in *Step size* in each case to see what "a dollar" is worth in a core that has given a third of its delayed neutrons to the pump loop.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a published β-effective into a period** before trusting a transient: type the group data, read the inhour period (Point kinetics).
- **Check whether a flowing β-effective was applied group by group** — the same total with the wrong shape changed our period by a third (Point kinetics).
- **Size a control system**: convert a reactivity loss in pcm into dollars for the fuel and flow state you actually have (Point kinetics).
- **Plan a pump-start test**: simulate the measured reactivity history and see what power excursion to expect before feedback (Point kinetics).
- **Follow the shutdown** with the [[decay-heat]] estimator, and the precursor and actinide chains with the [[decay-chain]] simulator and the [[neutron-diffusion]] tool for the shape of the flux.

## The fine print

- Point kinetics has **no feedback** here. The real reactor's negative temperature coefficient would stop the excursions in Figure 1 within seconds; these curves show the kinetics alone.
- The per-group retention factors are **our own plug-flow arithmetic** from published residence times, not the paper's method and not part of the tool.
- Λ is our assumption. It changes the prompt behaviour in the first milliseconds, not the periods quoted here.
- The paper's measured values (666, 454 and 212 pcm) come from 1960s experiments as the authors cite them; we have not gone back to the original reports.
- Delayed-neutron data differ between evaluations. We used the paper's numbers exactly as printed, which is the point: the tool takes your data, not its own.
