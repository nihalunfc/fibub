Observing logs are the most quietly useful tables in astronomy, and also the most often incomplete. A 2026 paper in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* reports nine VLT spectropolarimetry sequences on NGC 1566, a galaxy whose active nucleus switches between broad-line and narrow-line appearance, and prints a log with dates, grisms, exposure times, seeing and **airmass at the start and end of each sequence**. What it does not print is the time of night. This article shows that the missing times are recoverable: with nothing but the target's coordinates, the observatory's position and the published airmass figures, the [[batch-airmass|batch airmass planner]] pins each sequence to a window of a few tens of minutes, and explains in passing why no sequence in the log ever beats airmass 1.2. For one target on one night, the [[object-altitude|target altitude planner]] draws the same thing against your own horizon; for the darkness and Moon side, use the [[dark-sky-calendar]] and the [[observing-session|observing session planner]].

## The paper in brief

NGC 1566 is the nearest and best-studied "changing-look" active galaxy. In 2018 it brightened and grew broad emission lines; the question since has been whether the broad-line region was hidden by passing dust, or whether it weakened and came back. Polarisation is a good test, because light scattered by a dusty torus or by a wind is polarised while direct light is not.

The authors took nine sequences with FORS2 on the VLT between early August and late September 2025, in two grisms covering 3300–6600 Å and 6000–11 000 Å, each integration 1200 s split over four half-wave-plate positions. They caught the galaxy in a **type-2 state**: no broad component in either total or polarised flux, with a low, wavelength-independent polarisation of **0.22 % to 0.24 %** above 4000 Å. They argue that this is hard to square with occultation by dust, and that the broad-line region has weakened or gone. They also note the source is *more* polarised when brighter, which runs against the standard picture.

At 0.2 %, instrumental and atmospheric systematics matter, and both depend on how high the target was and where the Moon was. That is the part the planner can audit.

## Checked with the tools

The planner takes a list of "name, RA, Dec", a site and a night, and returns, for each target, the meridian transit and its altitude, the highest point reached inside the night, the airmass there, the hours spent below an airmass limit, and the Moon's distance. We gave it one target, NGC 1566 at the paper's own coordinates, and Paranal's position, and ran it over astronomical darkness for every night in the log.

**Step 1: the floor is 1.157, and the log respects it.** NGC 1566 lies at declination −54.938°. Paranal is at latitude −24.6275°. The difference is a zenith distance at transit of 30.23°, which the Kasten-Young formula turns into an airmass of **1.157**. No instrument, no weather and no scheduling can do better from that site. The log's best entry is 1.2, which is this floor rounded up. For reference, the planner also reports the altitudes the round airmass numbers correspond to: 1.2 is 56.38° up, 1.3 is 50.22° and 1.4 is 45.52°.

**Step 2: in August, transit happened in daylight.** This is the key to the whole table. On the night of 23 August the target crosses the meridian at **10:50 UT**, but astronomical dawn at Paranal is at **09:44 UT**. The galaxy is still climbing when the sky goes light, so the best airmass available in the dark is only **1.186**. On 3 August it is worse still, 1.28, and on 21 and 22 August 1.19. Only from mid-September does transit fall inside darkness (18 September: transit 09:08 UT, dawn 09:18), and only then does the log record 1.2. Figure 1 draws the two extremes.

**Step 3: the missing times.** An airmass of 1.4 falling to 1.3 can only happen on the rising branch, and the planner can find both crossings to the minute. For the night of 23 August it gives **07:52 to 08:29 UT: 38 minutes**. The sequence was 2 × 1200 s, that is **40 minutes** of integration. The same pair, 1.4 to 1.3, takes 38 minutes on 3 August and on 20 September as well, because it depends on the target's declination and the site's latitude rather than the date. So each "1.4–1.3" sequence in the log fills its window almost exactly, with no room for overheads between the two integrations: the log and the exposure times agree to about two minutes.

The "1.3–1.2" rows are looser, because the airmass curve flattens as the target rises: that step takes **61 minutes**, so a 40-minute sequence sits inside it with 20 minutes of freedom. The "1.2" rows, where the value does not change, are the sequences taken across transit on 20 and 21 September, when the galaxy stayed below 1.25 for nearly four hours, of which the part before dawn was usable.

**Step 4: the Moon was never a problem, except possibly on the first night.** The planner reports the Moon between **98° and 104°** from the target at every epoch, and 0 % to 7 % illuminated on eight of the nine sequences. The exception is the first, 3 August, with the Moon **74 % lit**. That is also the night with the worst airmass (never better than 1.3) and only ten minutes of time below 1.3. If any epoch in this campaign deserves a second look for scattered-light polarisation, the planner says it is that one.

**Step 5: a small date puzzle the planner cannot settle.** The paper's abstract gives 2 August as the start and its observing section gives 3 August. Both work: the 1.4-to-1.3 window is 09:14–09:52 UT on 2 August and 09:11–09:48 UT on 3 August, ending six and ten minutes before astronomical dawn respectively. Geometry cannot choose between them, but it does say the sequence ran right up against dawn either way.

**Step 6: one more cross-check.** The paper describes the sky as clear, with under 10 % of the sky above 30° elevation covered by cloud. The planner's airmass table says 30° altitude is airmass **2.0** by Kasten-Young (29.90° exactly). So ESO's standard "above 30 degrees" phrasing and the airmass-2 sky are the same thing, which is worth knowing when you compare weather conditions quoted in the two different ways.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ngc-1566-spectropolarimetry-airmass-track-paranal-campaign.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `parseTargets`, `altAz`, `airmass`, `altitudeFor`, `plan`, `moonRaDec` and `separation` from `src/lib/sci/airmass.ts`, the maths behind the [[batch-airmass|batch airmass planner]], and `darkNight` from `src/lib/astro/darkness.ts`, which the [[dark-sky-calendar]] uses, to find astronomical dusk and dawn for each night. The airmass-crossing times come from bisecting the planner's own airmass function; the rest is read straight off the plan it returns.

The target's coordinates, the dates, the exposure times and the airmass pairs come from the paper's observing log. Paranal's latitude, longitude and height are public facts and are **our addition**, as is the choice of the Kasten-Young airmass formula (the planner's default) and of astronomical twilight as the usable night. Nothing is simulated: this is the real sky geometry for a real target from a real site.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/batch-airmass.mjs`, which pins this maths against `astropy` 8.0:

- Altitudes and azimuths for three targets at three times must match astropy's `AltAz` frame within 2 arcseconds, and refracted altitudes within 15 arcseconds.
- All four airmass formulas must match NumPy to 12 decimal places at 10°, 30° and 60° altitude, give 1 at the zenith, and give 37.92 (Kasten-Young) and 38.75 (Pickering) at the horizon.
- A meridian transit must land within 3 seconds of astropy's answer on a half-second grid (03:15:44 UTC at 40.9751° for the test target), and the hours below airmass 2 within 0.002 h of 5.1289.
- The Moon's distance from the target must match astropy's `get_body` within 0.01°.
- New with this article: NGC 1566 from Paranal on the night of 23 August 2025 must transit at 10:50:19 UT at an altitude of 59.7676°, giving a minimum airmass of 1.1567; inside astronomical darkness it must reach only airmass 1.1866, spend 1.2334 hours at 1.3 or better, and sit 102.199° from the Moon.

## Check it yourself

On the [[batch-airmass|batch airmass planner]]:

1. In the place row, type the latitude **-24.6275**, longitude **-70.4044** and height **2635** (Paranal), and set *Night of* to **2025-08-23**. Leave *Times in* on your own zone, or switch it to Chile's.
2. In *Targets*, clear the box and paste one line: **NGC 1566, 04 20 00.394, -54 56 16.60**.
3. In *Night and limits*, leave *Plan from* on **Dark (astronomical twilight)** and *Airmass formula* on **Kasten and Young (1989)**. Set *Airmass limit* to **1.3** and *Table step* to **15**.
4. The headline reads that 1 of 1 target reaches airmass 1.3 or better during the night, and the line under it says airmass 1.3 is **50.22°** up and gives the Moon's phase.
5. The summary table gives the transit at **10:50** UT, its altitude **59.77°**, the best point inside the night at airmass **1.19**, **1 h 14 m** below the limit and the Moon **102°** away. The chart shows the target still climbing when the night ends.
6. Change *Night of* to **2025-09-21** and watch the transit move inside the dark and the best airmass fall to **1.16**, with 2 h 39 m below 1.3. That one change is the whole reason the September rows of the paper's log read 1.2 and the August rows read 1.3 and 1.4.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Fill in a published log that omits its times**, as here, which you need if you want to match an observation against a light curve, a satellite pass or a weather record.
- **Check a proposal's feasibility before writing it**: paste the whole target list, set the airmass limit and read the hours each target gives you, night by night.
- **Spot the epoch most likely to carry a systematic.** Low altitude and a bright, nearby Moon together are what you look for, and both come out of one table.
- **Explain a pattern in someone else's data.** A column of airmasses that never drops below a round number usually means the target transits in daylight, not that the observers were careless.
- **Choose a season.** Figure 2 shows that NGC 1566 gives Paranal no dark time below airmass 1.3 before late July, and four and a half hours by November; a campaign that must start in August is paying for that in airmass.

## The fine print

- The recovered windows are only as good as the published airmass values, which are rounded to one decimal. A "1.4" could be anything from 1.35 to 1.45, so each window carries roughly ±10 minutes of slack on each end.
- We assumed every sequence ran as a single continuous block and that the logged airmass pair brackets it. If a sequence was split, or if the pair refers to something else, the windows shift.
- Astronomical twilight (the Sun 18° down) is our definition of usable night. FORS2 spectropolarimetry of a bright galaxy can start in nautical twilight, which would move the earliest windows a little earlier.
- The planner's Moon distance is topocentric and ignores the Moon's brightness profile; it tells you where the Moon was, not how much of its light reached the slit.
- Nothing here touches the paper's own result. The 0.22 % to 0.24 % polarisation, the type-2 classification and the argument about the broad-line region come from the authors' data reduction and are outside what any fibub tool can check.
