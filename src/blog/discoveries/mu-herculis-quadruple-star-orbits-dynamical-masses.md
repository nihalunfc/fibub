µ Herculis (mu Herculis) is a naked-eye star 8.3 parsecs (27 light years) away. It is really **four stars**: the bright star Aa with a small, faint companion Ab, and, much further out, a pair of red dwarfs, B and C, that orbit each other. Astronomers have measured their positions since 1750. A new study combines those centuries of measurements with modern star-wobble speeds and, crucially, the Aa-Ab pair's closest approach in 2023, and solves all the orbits at once. The result is masses good to about 1 % or better. This article shows how the [[double-star-calculator]] checks those masses with Kepler's third law and predicts where each companion sits on the sky; to see Kepler's third law at work in a single orbit, try [[kepler-orbits]].

## The paper in brief

When two stars orbit each other, their period and their distance apart fix their **total mass** through Kepler's third law. That makes binaries the best scales in astronomy. The catch is that a long orbit needs a long record: Aa and Ab take **79 years** to go round, and B and C **43 years**. The team fitted all four stars together, using old visual measurements, modern imaging, radial velocities, and Gaia's distance, and treating the two pairs as one system that also orbits itself over thousands of years.

They find masses of **1.134 Suns** for Aa, **0.229** for Ab, **0.417** for B and **0.445** for C. Aa is a slightly evolved Sun-like star whose surface rings with sound waves (**solar-like oscillations**); the frequencies of those waves give an independent "asteroseismic" mass, and earlier studies found about 1.10 to 1.12 Suns. The new dynamical mass is a precise yardstick for testing that method.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the paper's fitted orbits; the charts are **predictions** from those orbits, not the historical measurements.

**Total masses from Kepler's third law.** The [[double-star-calculator]] works out the total mass as (a/ϖ)³ / P², with the angular semi-major axis a and the parallax ϖ in the same units and P in years. The paper's 20.418 au at a parallax of 120.069 mas is **2.452″** on the sky; with P = 78.987 years that gives **1.364 Suns** for Aa + Ab, against the paper's 1.134 + 0.2286 = **1.363**. For B and C, 11.705 au (**1.405″**) and 43.141 years give **0.862 Suns**, exactly the paper's 0.417 + 0.445.

**Splitting the total.** Each star swings round the shared centre of mass, the lighter one further out. The primary's own orbit (3.427 au) is 16.8 % of the relative orbit (20.418 au), so the companion has 16.8 % of the total mass: 0.168 × 1.363 = **0.2287 Suns**, the paper's Ab. For the B-C pair the same sum gives **0.4171 Suns** for the lighter star, B.

**Where the stars are.** Aa-Ab is an elongated orbit (eccentricity 0.379): the stars are **12.7 au** apart at periastron and **28.2 au** at the far end. Seen at a tilt of 62°, the companion's projected distance on the sky ranges from **0.75″** (early 2027, just after the 2023 periastron, which is why that passage pinned the orbit) to **2.97″** around 2050 (Figures 1 and 2). In late 2026 Ab is about **0.75″** from Aa, a hard target next to a bright star, while B and C are an easy **1.51″** apart for a moderate telescope. Since 1750 Aa-Ab has gone round **3.5 times**, which is what makes the orbit so well determined.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/mu-herculis-quadruple-star-orbits-dynamical-masses.mjs`. It calls `orbitAt` and `systemMass` from `src/lib/sci/visualbinary.ts`, the maths behind the [[double-star-calculator]]. All orbital elements, the parallax and the masses are from the paper; we converted the periastron times from Julian dates to decimal years (2023.577 and 2008.465) and the semi-major axes from au to arcseconds with the parallax. The separations are **predicted** from the fitted orbits. Separations do not depend on how the angles are defined, but position angles do: different papers measure the node and periastron angle from different stars, so a position angle can come out 180° different from a catalogue's.

## How we test the maths

Every change to fibub runs `tests/sci.mjs`, which pins the double star maths to known answers:

- The orbit of η Coronae Borealis (P = 41.623 years, a = 0.907″) must put the companion at a position angle of **318.4°** and a separation of **0.411″** in 1980.0, the answer Meeus works out by hand in *Astronomical Algorithms* (chapter 57).
- Sirius B must be at its widest, more than **11.2″** from Sirius A, around 2023, and closest, under **3.2″**, in 1994.
- Sirius's orbit (a = 7.4957″, P = 50.1284 years, parallax 0.37921″) must give a total mass of **3.07 Suns**.

## Check it yourself

On the [[double-star-calculator]]:

1. Keep **Predict from an orbit**.
2. Type **Period P (years)** 78.987, **Periastron time T (year)** 2023.577, **Eccentricity e** 0.379, **Semi-major axis a (″)** 2.4516, **Inclination i (°)** 62.39, **Node Ω (°)** 267.50 and **Periastron argument ω (°)** 50.303.
3. Type **Parallax (mas, optional)** 120.069. The **Real semi-major axis** reads **20.4 AU** and the **Total mass** **1.36 solar masses**.
4. Set **Date** to 2027-02-01: the **Separation ρ** reads about **0.750″**, the closest Ab comes on the sky.
5. For the B-C pair, type P 43.141, T 2008.465, e 0.179, a 1.4054, i 66.10, Ω 60.48 and ω 173.43: the **Total mass** reads **0.862 solar masses**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Planning observations:** the [[double-star-calculator]] gives the separation for any date, so you know whether a pair is within reach of your telescope or speckle camera.
- **Checking dynamical masses** in a published table in seconds, from a, P and the parallax (the distance in parsecs is 1000 divided by the parallax in milliarcseconds).
- **Testing asteroseismic masses:** a 1 % dynamical mass for an oscillating star like µ Her Aa is a benchmark; compare it with a mass from scaling relations.
- **Teaching Kepler's third law** with a real system measured for nearly three centuries, alongside the [[kepler-orbits]] simulator.
- **Measuring your own images:** the calculator's **Measure my image** mode turns two clicked stars into a separation and position angle to compare with the prediction.
