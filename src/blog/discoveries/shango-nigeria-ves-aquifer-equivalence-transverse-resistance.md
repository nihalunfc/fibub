A **vertical electrical sounding** (VES) is the cheapest way to look for groundwater: push current into the ground through two electrodes, read the voltage between two more, walk the outer pair further apart, and the curve of apparent resistivity against spacing carries the layering below. The trouble starts when the curve is turned into numbers a hydrogeologist wants, such as how thick the aquifer is and how much water it can pass. A 2024 paper in *Discover Water* did exactly that for sixteen soundings near Shango in north-central Nigeria, and it is a good case for asking which of those numbers a sounding really fixes. We rebuilt the problem with the [[ves-inversion|VES inversion tool]]; if you want the next step after the survey, the [[pump-test-analyser]] and the [[darcy-flow-calculator]] take over where resistivity stops.

## The paper in brief

The authors ran sixteen Schlumberger soundings over basement rocks, with a maximum half spread **AB/2 of 200 m**, and fitted **five geoelectric layers** to every curve with IPI2WIN. From the fitted layers they read the aquifer's resistivity (**182 Ω·m** at VES 7 to **288 Ω·m** at VES 8), its thickness (**20.10 m** at VES 1 to **52.20 m** at VES 2) and its depth (**55.6 m** at VES 6 to **68.9 m** at VES 5).

They then worked out the two **Dar Zarrouk parameters**, which combine a layer's resistivity ρ and thickness h: the **transverse resistance** T = Σ ρh (5232.40 Ω·m² at VES 16 to 12,267.00 Ω·m² at VES 2) and the **longitudinal conductance** S = Σ h/ρ (4.502 to 13.383 siemens). High S means a thick, clayey, electrically conductive cover, which protects an aquifer from pollution sinking in from above; that is the paper's aquifer-protection message. Finally they turned resistivity into a **hydraulic conductivity** (0.465 to 0.534 m/day) and multiplied by thickness to get **transmissivity**, the aquifer's ability to pass water: 9.589 m²/day at VES 1 to 26.029 m²/day at VES 2.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the paper's own arithmetic holds up.** Before any modelling, the stated numbers can be checked against each other. VES 2's transverse resistance divided by its thickness, 12,267.00 ÷ 52.20, is **235.0 Ω·m**, which sits inside the quoted 182 to 288 Ω·m. VES 2's transmissivity over its thickness, 26.029 ÷ 52.20, is **0.4986 m/day**, and VES 1's, 9.589 ÷ 20.10, is **0.4771 m/day**; both sit inside the quoted 0.465 to 0.534 m/day. So the tables behind the text are consistent.

One thing the arithmetic does reveal: the hydraulic conductivity range is much narrower than the resistivity range. Resistivity spans a factor of 288 ÷ 182 = 1.58, but conductivity only 0.534 ÷ 0.465 = 1.15. That makes the implied relation **K ∝ ρ^0.30**, not the straight proportionality the text suggests. Worth knowing if you want to reuse the relation elsewhere.

**Step 2: what the conductance values say about the cover.** The two Dar Zarrouk numbers are also a consistency test on the ground model. If a longitudinal conductance of 4.502 to 13.383 S is carried by 55.6 to 68.9 m of cover, the cover's **mean resistivity must be about 4 to 15 Ω·m**. That is clay, and it is what makes the protective capacity good. It also makes the aquifer hard to see: current prefers the conductive layer, and the rule of thumb for how deep a sounding reaches, a quarter to a third of the largest AB/2, puts the useful depth of a 200 m spread at only **50 to 67 m**, which is where the aquifer starts.

**Step 3: build a section and ask the tool.** We built a five-layer section whose aquifer matches VES 2 exactly, 235 Ω·m and 52.2 m thick (transverse resistance 12,267 Ω·m²), with its top at 59.5 m, inside the paper's depth range. Above it we put 1.5 m of topsoil at 150 Ω·m, 6 m of laterite at 60 Ω·m and 52 m of clay at 9 Ω·m (**our assumption**, since the paper's per-layer tables are not in its text); that cover has a longitudinal conductance of **5.89 S**, inside the paper's range. Below the aquifer, fresh basement at 3000 Ω·m.

Then we asked the only question that matters for a thickness: **would a 200 m sounding notice if the aquifer were different?** Figure 1 shows the curve for our section and for three rivals: the same section with the aquifer deleted, and two aquifers with the **same** resistivity-thickness product (470 Ω·m over 26.1 m, and 117 Ω·m over 104 m).

Out to AB/2 = 200 m the four curves lie on top of each other. Figure 2 puts numbers on it: inside the paper's spread, deleting the aquifer altogether changes the reading by at most **3.5 %** (3.54 % to be exact), and halving its thickness while doubling its resistivity by at most **2.6 %**. Field soundings in weathered basement rarely repeat better than 3 to 5 %.

**Step 4: invert the simulated sounding.** We made one noisy sounding from our section (24 spacings from AB/2 = 1 m to 200 m, 3 % lognormal scatter, **our assumption**) and inverted it with the tool's damped least-squares routine. Asked for **four** layers, it returns 148.7, 59.37 and 8.764 Ω·m over 1.496, 6.122 and 51.49 m, on a 5016 Ω·m base, with a 2.40 % RMS misfit and error factors of only ×/÷ 1.02 to 1.09 on the top three layers. The cover is nailed: its longitudinal conductance comes back as **5.99 S** against the true 5.89 S, 1.7 % high. But there is no aquifer in that model at all; its 5016 Ω·m "basement" is the aquifer and the real basement rolled into one.

Asked for **five** layers, the tool fits no better (2.37 % RMS instead of 2.40 %) and spends the extra layer on a meaningless 0.2 m sliver near the surface. Started deliberately from the true section, the five-layer inversion wanders off to an aquifer of 26 Ω·m over 38 m, a transverse resistance of **1000 Ω·m²** instead of 12,267, and reports error factors of ×/÷ 11 and ×/÷ 16 on the lower thicknesses, its own way of saying it does not know.

The conclusion is not that the paper is wrong about groundwater. Its protective-capacity map rests on the longitudinal conductance, which a 200 m sounding measures well. It is the **aquifer thickness, and therefore the transmissivity, that a 200 m spread over a conductive cover cannot deliver**: those numbers are a modelling choice, not a measurement. The honest reporting unit is the transverse resistance.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/shango-nigeria-ves-aquifer-equivalence-transverse-resistance.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `schlumberger` and `schlumbergerIdeal` (the layered-earth forward model: the Pekeris kernel evaluated with the linear filters fibub computed itself), `startModel` and `invert` (damped least squares on the logarithms of every resistivity and thickness) from `src/lib/sci/ves.ts`, the maths behind the [[ves-inversion|VES inversion tool]].

Inputs from the paper's text: the Schlumberger array, the 200 m maximum half spread, five layers, and VES 2's aquifer resistivity and thickness through its transverse resistance. **Our assumptions**: the three upper layers (chosen so the cover's longitudinal conductance lands inside the paper's range), the 24 spacings and MN/2 steps, the 3 % lognormal noise and the random draws. The sounding is **simulated**; the paper's own readings are not published.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/ves-inversion.mjs`, which pins the forward model and the inversion against independent calculations:

- For a three-layer and a four-layer earth, the ideal Schlumberger curve must match direct numerical integration with SciPy's Bessel J₁ to 1 part in 10⁶, and the Wenner and finite-MN Schlumberger curves to 1 part in 10⁴.
- For two layers, the curves must match the **exact image series** at reflection coefficients from −0.99 to +0.99 and spacings from 0.1 to 10,000 m; the worst error over that whole sweep is below 2 × 10⁻⁶.
- Inverting a made-up 16-reading sounding must give 118.63 Ω·m over 3.132 m, 22.63 Ω·m over 10.51 m, on 379.3 Ω·m, exactly what SciPy's `least_squares` finds, and the same answer from a different starting model.
- New with this article: our Shango-style section (150, 60, 9, 235, 3000 Ω·m over 1.5, 6, 52, 52.2 m) must read 11.24 Ω·m at AB/2 = 50 m and 32.24 Ω·m at AB/2 = 200 m with MN/2 = 5 and 20 m, and deleting its aquifer must change no reading inside 200 m by more than 3.6 % (the worst is 3.54 %).

## What researchers can use it for

- **Test resolution before you quote a thickness.** Forward-model your fitted section, then forward-model it again with the layer you care about doubled and halved. If the curves differ by less than your repeatability, the layer is not measured.
- **Report the Dar Zarrouk parameters, not just the layers.** The tool prints the longitudinal conductance for every model it fits; it is usually the number that survives.
- **Plan the spread.** Run your expected section at several maximum AB/2 values to find the spread you actually need, before the crew goes out.
- **Sanity-check a published table** the way we did here: divide transverse resistance by thickness and transmissivity by thickness and see whether the quoted ranges agree.
- **Carry on to the hydrogeology** with the [[pump-test-analyser]] for a real transmissivity, and the [[well-field-drawdown]] tool for what several boreholes would do to each other.

## The fine print

- Our section and sounding are **simulated**. The paper's own readings and per-layer tables are not in its text, so we could not invert its data.
- The paper does not say whether its "aquifer depth" is to the top or the base of the aquifer. We read it as the top, which is the reading its longitudinal conductance values support; the other reading would make the aquifer deeper still and resolution worse.
- Our three upper layers are one choice among many that fit the paper's conductance range. A less conductive cover would make the aquifer easier to see; a more conductive one, harder.
- The quarter-to-a-third rule for depth of investigation is a rule of thumb, not a theorem; the forward models above are the real test.
- We did not test IPI2WIN. A different program on the paper's own data could report a better-constrained aquifer only if the field curve carried information ours does not.
