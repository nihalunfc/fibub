Gallium oxide is a wide-gap semiconductor that people want to use for power switches, and an aluminium-containing alloy of it, β-(AlxGa1−x)2O3, widens the gap further. The difficulty is making it conduct: a wide gap usually means few free electrons. A 2025 paper in *AIP Advances* grows silicon-doped films of this alloy by mist chemical vapour deposition and reaches **971 S/cm**, with **6.21 × 10²⁰ electrons per cubic centimetre** and a Hall mobility of **9.8 cm²/Vs**. That density is so high that every formula in a first semiconductor course stops working, and this article uses fibub's [[carrier-concentration]] tool to show by how much. For the measurement itself see the [[hall-effect]] calculator, and for the optical gap that this much doping shifts, the [[tauc-plot]].

## The paper in brief

Mist chemical vapour deposition sprays a fine mist of dissolved precursors onto a hot substrate, so it needs no vacuum. The authors grew films on the (010) face of a β-Ga₂O₃ substrate, doped them with silicon, and kept the surfaces very smooth (roughness under 1 nanometre). They report unintentional impurities as well: carbon and hydrogen, which can also give electrons, and iron drifting out of the substrate at under 10¹⁸ cm⁻³.

The headline film reaches a conductivity of **971 S/cm**. Hall measurements give its carrier density as **6.21 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³** and its mobility as **9.8 cm²/Vs**; a more lightly doped film sits near **1.5 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³**. The authors then measure the Hall effect as a function of temperature to study how the electrons scatter, and describe the material as **degenerate**. We could not read the publisher's full text from here, so everything below uses only the values in the abstract, with the licence taken from the Crossref record.

## Checked with the tools

Every number here is a **recomputation** from the three measured values and our own rounded material parameters, listed below.

**Step 1: do the three numbers agree?** Conductivity is the carrier density times the charge times the mobility, σ = n·e·µ. With 6.21 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³ and 9.8 cm²/Vs that is **975.1 S/cm**, against the paper's **971 S/cm**: agreement to **0.4 %**, which is rounding. Read the other way, 971 S/cm with that density needs a mobility of **9.76 cm²/Vs**. The film's resistivity is **1.03 mΩ·cm**, about a thousandth of an ohm-centimetre, which for a 5 eV oxide is remarkable.

**Step 2: how full is the conduction band?** The tool works out the effective density of conduction-band states from the electron mass: with mₑ* = 0.28 m₀ (the value usually quoted for β-Ga₂O₃'s nearly isotropic conduction band; **our input**) it gives **Nc = 3.72 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³** at 300 K. The film has **167 times** that many electrons. There is no room for the Boltzmann approximation here: the band is not nearly empty, it is deeply filled.

**Step 3: the Fermi level.** The tool inverts n = Nc·F½(η), where F½ is the Fermi-Dirac integral of order one half and η = (EF − Ec)/kT. For n/Nc = 167 the answer is **η = 36.6**, so the Fermi level sits **0.947 eV above the bottom of the conduction band**. The Boltzmann shortcut, n = Nc·e^η, would put it at only **0.132 eV**, an error of **0.8 eV**; used the other way round at η = 36.6 it predicts 3 × 10³⁴ electrons per cubic centimetre, which is more electrons than there are atoms. For the lightly doped film, n/Nc = 4.03 gives η = 2.75, EF **0.071 eV above the edge**: already degenerate, though only just.

**Step 4: so the film cannot freeze out.** A doped semiconductor normally loses its carriers when it gets cold, because electrons fall back onto the donor atoms. That needs separate donor levels, and above the **Mott density** they no longer exist: the donor states merge into the conduction band. With mₑ* = 0.28 m₀ and a dielectric constant of about 10 the effective Bohr radius is 1.9 nm, and the Mott criterion puts that density near **2.6 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³**. The paper's best film is **240 times** above it and even the lightly doped film is almost six times above it, so neither can freeze out: their carrier density is flat with temperature, and a temperature-dependent Hall measurement on them reads **scattering**, which is exactly what the authors use it for.

Figure 2 shows what freeze-out looks like in the regime where it does happen, worked out by the tool from charge neutrality with 30 meV donors (**our assumption** for the silicon level).

**Step 5: a warning worth repeating.** If you type the measured 6.21 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³ into the tool as a *donor density* with a 30 meV level, it returns **1.13 × 10¹⁹ electrons** and says only **1.8 %** of the donors are ionised, in the "freeze-out" regime. That is not a mistake in the arithmetic: the hydrogenic donor model it solves keeps electrons bound to donors once the Fermi level climbs above the donor level, and that model is simply invalid above the Mott density. For degenerate material, use the measured carrier density and the F½ box, as in steps 2 and 3, and leave the donor fields alone.

**One prediction a reader can test.** Filling the band this deeply pushes the optical absorption edge up by roughly EF(1 + mₑ*/mₕ*), the Burstein-Moss shift. With our masses that is about **1.1 eV**, so the apparent optical gap of the best film should sit about an electronvolt above that of an undoped film of the same composition. Anyone with the paper's transmittance spectra can check this in the [[tauc-plot]] tool.

## How we made the graphs

Both figures and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/si-doped-algao3-film-degenerate-carrier-concentration-fermi-level.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `effDos`, `varshni`, `fermiHalf`, `solve`, `sweep`, `freezeOutTemperature` and `regime` from `src/lib/sci/carriers.ts`, the maths behind the [[carrier-concentration]] tool, and finds η by bisection on the tool's own F½.

From the paper: 971 S/cm, 6.21 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³, 9.8 cm²/Vs and the 1.5 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ film. **Our inputs**, which the paper does not state: electron mass 0.28 m₀, hole mass 2.0 m₀, gap at 0 K 5.2 eV with Varshni α = 0.5 meV/K and β = 650 K, a silicon donor depth of 30 meV, and a dielectric constant of 10 for the Mott estimate. The Fermi level in steps 2 and 3 depends only on the electron mass and the measured density, so it is firm; the freeze-out curves in Figure 2 are illustrations of the model, not the paper's films.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/carrier-concentration.mjs` runs inside `tests/sci.mjs` on every change and pins this module against independent Python results:

- F½(η) must match mpmath's polylogarithm, F½(η) = −Li₃⁄₂(−e^η), to one part in 10¹³ at twelve values of η from −30 to 100, on both sides of the series switch at η = −1;
- F½ must fall back to e^η when the material is non-degenerate and to (4/3√π)η^{3/2} when it is strongly degenerate;
- Nc for mₑ* = 1 at 300 K must be 2.5094 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ (SciPy constants), and the Fermi level from charge neutrality must match SciPy's `brentq` to 10⁻¹⁰ eV in eight doping and temperature cases, including a degenerate 10²⁰ cm⁻³ one and a compensated one at 20 K;
- the 90 % ionisation temperature for 10¹⁶ silicon-like donors must be 132.05 K.

New with this article, in the same file: for our β-(AlxGa1−x)2O3-like material, Nc(300 K) must be 3.7181 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, the reduced Fermi level for 6.21 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³ must be η = 36.6446 (EF − Ec = 0.94727 eV) with F½(η) = 167.02, and the Boltzmann shortcut at that η must give 3.055 × 10³⁴ cm⁻³, which is the point.

## Check it yourself

On the [[carrier-concentration]] page:

1. Type the material: *Gap at 0 K, Eg0 (eV)* **5.2**, *Varshni α (meV/K)* **0.5**, *Varshni β (K)* **650**, *Electron mass mₑ\* (m₀)* **0.28**, *Hole mass mₕ\* (m₀)* **2**, *Temperature T (K)* **300**.
2. Set *Donors ND (cm⁻³)* to **0** and *Acceptors NA (cm⁻³)* to **0** for now. The page reports a gap of about **5.153 eV** and **Nc = 3.718 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³**.
3. Divide the measured density by that Nc: 6.21 × 10²⁰ ÷ 3.718 × 10¹⁸ = **167**.
4. In the small box at the bottom, type *Reduced energy η = (EF − Ec)/kT* **36.645**. It shows **F½(η) ≈ 167**, so this is the film's Fermi level: 36.645 × kT = **0.947 eV above the conduction band edge**. Beside it the Boltzmann value e^η reads about **8 × 10¹⁵**, nearly twenty orders of magnitude out.
5. Now the other regime: type *Donors ND (cm⁻³)* **1e17**, *Donor depth below Ec (eV)* **0.03**, *Donor degeneracy gD* **2**, and set the sweep from **20** to **600** K. The carrier plot bends down at low temperature and the page marks freeze-out below about **342 K** for these shallow-but-few donors.
6. Finally type *Donors ND (cm⁻³)* **6.21e20** with the same 0.03 eV depth and watch the model break: **1.13 × 10¹⁹** electrons, **1.8 %** ionised. Above the Mott density, donor levels are gone; trust step 4 instead.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a transparent-conductor paper in a minute**: multiply n, e and µ and see whether the quoted conductivity follows. It usually does, and when it does not, one of the three numbers is from a different sample.
- **Decide whether you may use Boltzmann statistics at all.** Compare n with Nc from your effective mass; past about 0.1 Nc the shortcut starts to lie, and Figure 1 shows how fast.
- **Predict the Burstein-Moss shift** before measuring an absorption edge, so you know whether an apparent gap widening is band filling or alloy composition.
- **Interpret a temperature-dependent Hall run**: above the Mott density the carrier density is flat, so every change with temperature is mobility, and the slope tells you which scattering mechanism dominates.
- **Teach degeneracy with real numbers.** The F½ box next to the Boltzmann value makes the failure of the exponential approximation concrete in one line.

## The fine print

- Only the paper's **abstract** was readable from here. We did not see its tables, figures or the aluminium content x, and we quote no value that is not in the abstract.
- The electron mass, hole mass, gap, Varshni terms, donor depth and dielectric constant are **our rounded inputs** for a β-(AlxGa1−x)2O3-like material, not the paper's measurements. The Fermi level depends on the electron mass: 0.25 m₀ instead of 0.28 would raise it by about 7 %.
- The Mott density and the Burstein-Moss shift are order-of-magnitude estimates from standard formulas, not fits.
- Figure 2 is a property of the donor model at low doping. It is not the paper's temperature-dependent Hall data, which we could not read.
- Hall measurements give a Hall carrier density, which differs from the true density by a scattering-dependent factor of order one; we treat the two as the same, as the paper does.
