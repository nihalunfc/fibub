Behavioural science has a favourite trick: say the same thing a different way and people act differently. "You could gain years of health" against "you could lose years of health" is the classic pair. A 2024 field study in *JMIR Public Health and Surveillance* put six such framings to a large test, sending check-up reminders by email and text message to more than 113,000 members of a health fund, and found **no difference** in how many people went for their tests. "No significant difference" is not the same as "no effect", so this article rebuilds the study's counts and asks the Bayesian question with the [[bayesian-ab-test]]: how big could a framing effect still be? The classic chi-square comes from [[hypothesis-tests]], and the [[sample-size]] tool shows what a bigger trial would need.

## The paper in brief

Members aged 50 to 74 who were due for a check-up were randomly given one of six reminder messages: a **neutral control**, a **gain** frame (what you get by testing), a **loss** frame (what you risk by not testing), a doctor's **recommendation**, an **implementation intention** prompt (plan when and where you will go) and an **empowerment** message. The study then counted, for each message, how many people who opened it went on to do a **screening test** or a **lab test**.

The rates barely moved. For screening tests they ranged from 12.9 % to 14.1 %, with the neutral message at 14.1 %; for lab tests from 23.3 % to 24.0 %. The paper's chi-square tests give P = .35 to .37 for screening and .88 for lab tests: no framing beat the plain message. What did matter was the channel: emails were opened far more often than text messages (65.9 % against 50.4 %), and shorter subject lines were opened more often.

## Checked with the tools

The paper gives each group's size and its rate to one decimal place, so we rebuilt the counts as size × rate, rounded (for example, 14.1 % of 5,075 is **716** screening tests after the neutral message). This is **our reconstruction**: the true counts may differ by two or three either way. We then compared every framing with the neutral message using `abTest` from the [[bayesian-ab-test]], with flat Beta(1, 1) priors.

**Step 1: the classic test agrees.** A chi-square test of all six groups on our rebuilt counts gives χ² = 5.03 with 5 degrees of freedom, **P = 0.41**, for screening tests and χ² = 1.65, **P = 0.90**, for lab tests. The paper reports .35 to .37 and .88; the small gap on screening comes from rounding the rates. The effect size (Cramér's V) is 0.014: tiny.

**Step 2: the Bayesian view, group by group.** The 95 % credible interval for the neutral screening rate is **13.18 % to 15.09 %**, almost the paper's 13.1 % to 15.1 %. Figure 1 shows each framing's difference from neutral. Every interval crosses zero, but they are not all alike:

**Step 3: what "no effect" can rule out.** No framing has more than a 51 % chance of beating the neutral message for screening tests, so there is no sign of a hidden winner. The intervals also rule out large gains: no framing is likely to lift screening by more than about 1.6 percentage points. Two framings lean the other way: there is about a **93 % chance** that the loss frame and the recommendation do slightly *worse* than the neutral message, by around one point (Figure 2). That is a hint, not a finding; across ten comparisons one or two such leanings are expected by chance. For lab tests the best framing, the recommendation, has a 74 % chance of beating neutral with a difference of +0.40 points (−0.81 to 1.62).

**Step 4: how big would a decisive test be?** With 5,000 people a group and a 14.1 % rate, the 95 % interval for a difference is about ±1.36 points. To pin a difference to ±0.68 points takes 20,000 a group; to ±0.43 points, 50,000. Framing effects smaller than half a point would need groups several times larger than this already large study.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/health-checkup-reminder-message-framing-bayesian-ab-test-no-effect.mjs` in fibub's public code calls `abTest` and `betaPdf` from `src/lib/sci/bayes.ts`, the maths behind the [[bayesian-ab-test]], and `chiIndep` from `src/lib/sci/hyptest.ts`, the chi-square of the [[hypothesis-tests]] tool.

Group sizes and rates come from the paper's results table, as stated in its text. The counts are rebuilt by us from those rates (rounded), and the flat priors are **our choice**; with this many people the prior hardly matters. The sample sizes in Step 4 are our own what-if.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/bayesian-ab-test.mjs`, which checks the tool against SciPy:

- For 120 of 1,000 against 145 of 1,000 with a flat prior, P(B > A) must be 0.950197, as SciPy's numerical integration gives, and within Monte Carlo error of a 2,000,000-draw simulation.
- The same case's expected loss, credible intervals for both rates, and the interval for the difference and lift must match SciPy to 6 or more digits.
- New with this article: the neutral screening group (716 of 5,075) against the recommendation group (641 of 4,890) must give P(B > A) = 0.0729, a difference of −1.00 points with a 95 % interval of −2.35 to 0.35 points, and a neutral credible interval of 13.18 % to 15.09 %.

## Check it yourself

On the [[bayesian-ab-test]], with *A/B test* selected:

1. Type **5075** in *Visitors A* and **716** in *Conversions A* (the neutral message, screening tests).
2. Type **4890** in *Visitors B* and **641** in *Conversions B* (the recommendation message).
3. Leave *Prior* on **Flat, Beta(1, 1)** and *Credible interval* on **95 %**.
4. The big line reads **P(B beats A) = 7.292 %**. The facts show *A rate, 95 % interval* **14.12 % (13.18 % to 15.09 %)** and *Difference B − A* **−0.999 pp (−2.35 pp to 0.348 pp)**.
5. Change *Visitors B* to **4416** and *Conversions B* to **614** (the gain frame): P(B beats A) rises to about **38.8 %**, close to a coin toss.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report a null result honestly**: give the credible interval for the difference, so readers see which effects are ruled out, with the [[bayesian-ab-test]].
- **Re-check a published trial** from its group sizes and rates in a minute, as here; run the chi-square in [[hypothesis-tests]] to compare with the paper.
- **Plan the next trial**: pick the smallest effect worth finding and work out the group size with the [[sample-size]] tool before sending a single message.
- **Decide with expected loss**: the tool's expected loss says how much uptake you risk by switching messages, a practical rule for health services and charities.
- **Teach** why "not significant" does not mean "no effect", using intervals that straddle zero.

## The fine print

- Our counts are rebuilt from rates rounded to 0.1 %, so they may be off by two or three per group; that explains the small gap in the screening chi-square.
- The paper reports rates among people who opened the message; framings may also change who opens it, which this re-check does not cover.
- We compared each framing with the neutral message on its own; with ten comparisons, one or two leanings like the loss frame's are expected by chance.
- General education about research methods, not health advice. Ask your own doctor about which check-ups you need.
