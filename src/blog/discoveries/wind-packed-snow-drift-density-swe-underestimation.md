Snow depth is easy to measure from the air; the water inside it is not. Every map of mountain snow water turns depth into water with a density, and most of those densities come from models fitted to snow courses in flat, forested, easy-to-reach places. A 2026 paper in *The Cryosphere* dug 78 snow pits in the Wind River Range of Wyoming, including fourteen more than 2 m deep, and found that the deep wind-packed drifts are far denser than those models say: a 5.9 m drift in a nivation hollow averaged **585 kg/m³**, while nearby forest snow was as light as 339. Density times depth is arithmetic anyone can do, and the [[snow-slope-tools]] does it with the loads that follow; this article turns the paper's density numbers into millimetres of water and kilonewtons per square metre. For the ice end of the same story see the [[glacier-ice]] tool, and for what a snow load does to a structure, the [[load-combination-helper]].

## The paper in brief

Over four spring seasons the authors cut 78 density profiles with 984 density-cutter samples, walking about 90 km with 5400 m of climbing to reach drifts that snow-course networks never sample. (All 88 SNOTEL sites in Wyoming sit in low, forested terrain.) Across 36 locations in 2025 the mean bulk density was 450 kg/m³ with a coefficient of variation of 13 %, but the spread was structured, not random: **forested 405 against open 471 kg/m³**, snow at least 2 m deep **497 against 438** for shallower snow, and a depth-density correlation of **r = 0.59**.

The extreme case is a 5.9 m drift in a nivation hollow: bulk density 585 kg/m³, individual cutters from 515 to 688, with no increase from top to bottom (the top 10 cm were already 597). That is **14 to 35 % above** what global empirical density models predict. Because the densest snow is also the deepest snow, the authors find gridded snow products underestimate SWE by **65 to 73 %** in a 1 km² glacial cirque, and the standard deviation of SWE at 500 m scale by 33 to 75 %, even though the error for the whole landscape mean is only 2 to 17 %.

## Checked with the tools

Everything below is **recomputed from depths and densities printed in the paper's text**. SWE in millimetres is depth in millimetres times density divided by 1000, which is the same as kg of water per square metre; the ground snow load in kN/m² is that figure times 9.80665 and divided by 1000.

**Step 1: what the drift holds.** 5.9 m at 585 kg/m³ is **3451.5 mm of SWE**, that is 3.45 m of water. The study basin's mean SWE on the same day was 0.21 m, so this one hollow holds **16.4 times the basin average** per square metre. As a load it is **33.85 kN/m²** on level ground: about 2.76 tonnes of water per square metre, or 27.08 kN/m² on a roof with a shape factor of 0.8. **Step 2: what a single mean density costs you.** Use the survey mean of 450 kg/m³ for that drift and you get 2655 mm instead of 3451.5: **796.5 mm of water missing**, 23.1 % of the true figure, from one pit. Use a density 14 % low (513 kg/m³, the gentle end of the paper's model bias) and you still lose 424 mm; 35 % low (433 kg/m³) and you lose **895 mm**, 25.9 %. Each of those shortfalls is larger than the whole basin's mean SWE. Figure 2 shows the shortfall against depth: it grows in proportion to depth, which is exactly why the error concentrates in the deep drifts.

The sign flips for shallow snow. The 0.8 m north-facing slope at 393 kg/m³ and the 1.6 m summit drift at 401 are *lighter* than the mean, so a single mean density **over**estimates them by 14.5 % and 12.2 %. Errors of opposite sign in different places nearly cancel in a landscape average while ruining the map, which matches the paper's own result that the landscape mean is only 2 to 17 % low while the cirque is 65 to 73 % low.

**Step 3: why averaging separately is not enough.** Mean SWE is the mean of depth times density, and that is not the product of the two means: it is mean(depth) × mean(density) + the covariance between them. With the paper's r = 0.59 and a bulk-density spread of 57 kg/m³, the covariance term is 0.59 × SD(depth) × 57. For a landscape with a depth spread of 0.5, 1.0 or 1.5 m that is **16.8, 33.6 or 50.4 mm of SWE** left out by anyone who multiplies an average depth by an average density. (The depth spreads are **our assumption**; the paper does not state one.) The term is always positive when deep snow is denser, so this kind of estimate is biased low, never high.

**Step 4: a layered reconstruction.** The paper gives the upper 2 m of the big pit as 595 kg/m³, the lowest 2 m as 582 and the whole thing as 585. Feeding those two layers plus the unmeasured 1.9 m in the middle into the tool's layer mode, the middle must average **577.6 kg/m³** for the bulk to come out at 585, and the layered SWE then agrees with the simple depth-times-density figure to 0.1 mm. So the published numbers are internally consistent, and the middle of the drift is a little less dense than either end, as the paper's ice-layer description suggests.

## How we made the graphs

The depths, densities, the 14 to 35 % model bias, the r = 0.59 correlation, the 57 kg/m³ spread and the basin mean SWE are from the paper's running text. The depth spreads in step 3 and the roof factor of 0.8 in step 1 are **our assumptions**. Nothing here is a measurement of ours, and no figure or table from the paper was copied.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/snow-slope-tools.mjs`, which pins this arithmetic against an independent Python script:

- For 200 random cases, SWE from depth and density, the snow-tube mean, standard deviation and pooled density, the layer SWE and bulk density, and the ground and roof loads all match NumPy to 1e-13; the slope from two GPS points matches `pyproj.Geod` (Karney, WGS84) to 1e-9.
- Fixed landmarks: 1 m of 300 kg/m³ snow is exactly 300 mm of SWE and 2.941995 kN/m²; a 5 cm tube holding 700 g over an 80 cm core gives 356.51 mm and 445.63 kg/m³; one degree of latitude at the equator is 110 574.389 m.
- The avalanche-angle bands switch at 25, 30, 45 and 60°, so a 43° slope lands in the 30 to 45° band.
- New with this article: 5.9 m of snow at 585 kg/m³ must give 3451.5 mm of SWE and a ground load of 33.848 kN/m², and the three-layer reconstruction (200 cm at 582, 190 cm at 577.6, 200 cm at 595) must return a bulk density of 585 kg/m³ and the same SWE to 0.1 mm.

## Check it yourself

On the [[snow-slope-tools]] page, in the *Snow water equivalent* card:

1. Leave the mode on *Depth and density*. Type **590** in *Snow depth (cm)* and **585** in *Bulk density (kg/m³)*. The heading reads **SWE = 3451.5 mm**, with *Ground snow load* **33.848 kN/m²** and *Water in the snow* 3451.5 kg/m².
2. Change the density to **433** (a model estimate 35 % low) and watch the SWE fall to **2554.7 mm**: nearly 900 mm of water gone from the same snow.
3. Switch the mode to *Layers* and paste three lines into *One layer per line: thickness (cm), density (kg/m³)*: **200, 582**; **190, 577.6**; **200, 595**. It reads *SWE = 3451.4 mm* with a *Bulk density* of **585 kg/m³**, the paper's own bulk figure.
4. In *Snow load on a roof*, leave *Ground snow load from* on **the SWE above** and type **0.8** in *Roof factor*. It shows **Roof load = 27.08 kN/m²** and the same load as mass, 2761 kg/m².
5. In the *Slope angle* card, type **43** in *Or type a measured angle (°)*: the tool puts it in the **30 to 45°** band, where most slab avalanches start. (That is general terrain knowledge, not a forecast.)

## What researchers can use it for

- **Convert any published depth and density into SWE and a load** in one step, so that papers reporting densities can be compared in the units that matter for runoff or for a roof.
- **Size the cost of a density assumption** before a field season: how many millimetres of water does a 10 % density error cost at the depths you care about?
- **Work up a snow pit** from cutter weights or from layer thicknesses and densities, with the bulk density weighted by thickness, instead of by hand.
- **Check a snow-tube set** for consistency: the tool reports each sample's SWE and density plus the spread between them, which is how you catch a short core or a plugged tube.
- **Teach the covariance trap**: deep snow being denser means averaging depth and density separately always underestimates the water, and the [[snow-slope-tools]] makes the size of that bias concrete.

## The fine print

- Our SWE and load figures are arithmetic on the paper's stated depths and densities. The paper's measurements and conclusions are its own.
- The covariance figures in step 3 use the paper's correlation and density spread with **our assumed** depth spreads; they illustrate the size of the effect, they do not reproduce the paper's own gridded-product comparison, which uses lidar depths.
- The ground snow load is the weight of the water on level ground. Roof design uses code factors for shape, drift, exposure and thermal effects; the tool's single factor is a learning aid, not a design code.
- Avalanche-angle bands are general knowledge about where slabs start. They are not an avalanche forecast, and no web page can be one.
- Bulk densities above about 600 kg/m³ start to behave like firn rather than seasonal snow; the paper's 688 kg/m³ cutter is near that edge.
