Landscape connectivity papers almost always report two numbers: the **probability of connectivity** (PC) and the **integral index of connectivity** (IIC) of the habitat network, plus each patch's importance (dPC, dIIC) as the percentage the index would lose if that patch disappeared. Both indices depend on a **dispersal distance** that the analyst chooses, and neither means anything without it. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Environmental Science* maps an ecological security pattern for the transboundary Gaoligong Mountains and reports PC = 0.68 and IIC = 0.138 for its core forest patches, but no distance. Using the [[habitat-connectivity]] analyser's own index code we work backwards: what dispersal distance would give those values, and can one distance give both? For the steps either side, score the habitat itself with the [[biodiversity-indices]] tool and get patch-level detections from the [[camera-trap-tagger]].

## The paper in brief

The authors assembled ecosystem-service layers (soil retention, water yield, carbon sequestration, habitat quality) for a 37,434 km² transboundary region on the China-Myanmar border, picked out 869 ecological source sites covering 12,068.74 km², and kept **23 primary core sources** totalling **4,343.47 km²**, the largest 323.43 km² and the smallest 33.72 km². They built a resistance surface, traced 1,187 corridors totalling 6,989.05 km with circuit theory, and tested how fast the network falls apart when nodes are removed.

For the patch network they report **PC = 0.68** and **IIC = 0.138** overall, and per-patch importance values. They also report that patch **area explains R² = 0.657 of the spread in dIIC and R² = 0.497 in dPC**, which they read as importance being driven by area but not only by area. What they do not report is the dispersal distance, the probability assigned to it, or the landscape area the indices were normalised by, and those three choices set every value in the table.

## Checked with the tools

Because no patch coordinates are published, we did not try to reproduce the paper's table patch by patch. Instead we built a **stand-in landscape** from its published summary numbers: 23 round patches on a 1 km raster, areas running from 36 to 324 km² and adding to 4,349 km² (the paper: 33.72, 323.43 and 4,343.47), strung along an 80 × 600 km north-south strip, because the Gaoligong range is long and narrow. The positions are **our assumption**. Then we ran the tool's real chain on it: `findPatches` for the patches, `patchDistances` for least-cost distances (resistance 1 everywhere, so straight-line distances), and `indices`/`importance` for PC, IIC, dPC and dIIC, with the landscape area set to the habitat total, the only normalisation under which a PC of 0.68 is even possible.

**1. PC = 0.68 implies dispersal over about 200 km.** Sweeping the dispersal distance (the distance at which the chance of a successful move is 0.5, the tool's own definition) and solving for the paper's value gives **204 km** in our strip and **127 km** in a compact 220 × 220 km block with the same patch areas. Either way the figure is in the hundreds of kilometres, far beyond the dispersal of most forest vertebrates and well past the 28.7 km average corridor length the paper itself reports. A PC that high is only reachable if almost every pair of patches counts as connected.

**2. IIC = 0.138 implies a link threshold of about 25 km, and the two cannot both be right.** IIC counts links, not probabilities: a pair is either joined within the threshold or not. Solving for the paper's IIC gives **25.5 km** in the strip and **28.0 km** in the block. At that threshold the same landscape has **PC = 0.18** (strip) or **0.24** (block), not 0.68. Run the other way, at the 204 km that reproduces PC = 0.68, IIC comes out **0.45**, three times the published figure. The gap is a factor of 7 to 8 in distance and survives a complete change of layout, so it is not an artefact of our geometry: the two published indices were computed under different settings, or normalised by different landscape areas, and a reader cannot tell which.

| Published value | Distance that reproduces it (strip) | Distance (compact block) | The other index at that distance |
|---|---|---|---|
| PC = 0.68 | 204 km | 127 km | IIC = 0.45 (strip) |
| IIC = 0.138 | 25.5 km | 28.0 km | PC = 0.18 (strip), 0.24 (block) |

**3. There is a floor under PC that a reader can check in one line.** Every patch contributes its own area to the index even when nothing reaches it, so PC ≥ Σaᵢ² ÷ A_L². For this patch-size distribution that floor is **0.0523**. Any published PC below its own floor is an error, and a PC far above it, like 0.68, means the dispersal assumption is doing most of the work. The tool prints the index for whatever distance you type, so the floor takes seconds to find: set the dispersal distance to something tiny and read PC.

**4. How much of patch importance is just area?** In our landscape, area explains **R² = 0.91** of the spread in dPC at 204 km and **0.79** at 25 km; for dIIC, 0.97 and 0.33. The paper's 0.497 for dPC and 0.657 for dIIC are both lower than our long-distance figures, and they keep the same order (dIIC better explained by area than dPC), which fits a landscape whose patches are spaced much more unevenly than our regular chain: the more irregular the spacing, the more of the importance comes from position rather than size. The practical point is in Figure 2: our **largest** patch (324 km²) ranks only sixth by dPC (12.2 %), while a slightly smaller patch in the middle of the chain (311 km²) tops the list at 16.7 %, because it is the stepping stone everything else routes through. Protecting by area alone would pick the wrong patch.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/habitat-patch-connectivity-importance-area-gaoligong.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `findPatches`, `patchDistances`, `indices` and `importance` from `src/lib/sci/connectivity.ts`, the maths behind the [[habitat-connectivity]] analyser, and `pearson` from `src/lib/sci/csvprofile.ts` for the R² values.

From the paper: the patch count (23), the total area (4,343.47 km²), the largest and smallest patch, PC, IIC and the two R² values. **Our assumptions**: the patch-size curve between the smallest and the largest, the round shapes, the 80 × 600 km strip and the compact block, the 1 km cell, a uniform resistance of 1, and the use of habitat area as the normalising landscape area. None of the paper's own patch areas, coordinates or importance values are reproduced here.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/habitat-connectivity.mjs` on every change to fibub. It pins the index code against standard tools:

- On the built-in example map, the eight patches match `scipy.ndimage.label` with 8-connectivity, and the least-cost distances match scikit-image's `MCP_Geometric` exactly (1,348.528137 m between patches 1 and 2, 3,969.848481 m between 7 and 8).
- IIC and PC of that map at a 5,000 m threshold with p = 0.5 must be 0.009389241536 and 0.015221964749, and every dPC and dIIC must match networkx to 1e-6 (dPC 30.141723, 12.839008, 8.737191, 4.325212, 42.949608, 37.188937, 10.410828, 11.222702).
- Hand-worked cases pin the formulas: one patch filling the landscape gives IIC = PC = 1; two patches of area 1 in a landscape of 4, 50 m apart with a 100 m threshold, give IIC = (1 + 1 + 2 × 0.5) ÷ 16; an unlinked pair adds nothing to IIC but 2 × 0.5^1.5 to PC; a stepping stone makes PC use the best path through it.
- New with this article: the example map at a 1,500 m threshold must give PC 0.008399 and IIC 0.007280, and the index must never fall below the self-contribution floor Σaᵢ²/A_L² at any threshold.

## Check it yourself

On the [[habitat-connectivity]] page, with no map of your own:

1. Press **Use the example** (a made-up 48 × 32 map of 100 m cells: forest patches in farmland with a river, a road and a town).
2. Leave *Chance of reaching that far (PC)* at **0.5** and *Landscape area (ha)* empty (the whole map, 1,536 ha).
3. Set *Dispersal distance (cost units)* to **5000**. The connectivity card gives **PC 0.015222** and **IIC 0.009389** for the eight patches, and the table ranks them by importance: patch 5 at dPC 42.9 %, patch 6 at 37.2 %, patch 1 at 30.1 %, down to patch 4 at 4.3 %.
4. Change *Dispersal distance* to **1500**. PC falls to **0.008399** and IIC to **0.007280**; patch 5 is still first (34.2 %) but patch 4 collapses to **0.9 %**, because at a short distance a small isolated patch stops being a stepping stone.
5. Type **50** into *Dispersal distance*. PC now sits near its floor: this is the "nothing reaches anything" value every published PC has to beat.
6. Press **Download patches (CSV)** to get the areas and importances, and **Download distances (CSV)** for the least-cost matrix, which is what you would send a reviewer along with the distance you chose.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Report the distance, the probability and the landscape area with every PC or IIC.** Our two solved distances differ by a factor of 8 for the same pair of published indices; without those three settings, neither number can be reused.
- **Audit a published index** in minutes: rebuild a plausible patch set, sweep the distance in the tool, and see whether one distance reproduces every reported index. Ours does not.
- **Check the floor.** Σaᵢ²/A_L² is the smallest PC a patch set can have; it also tells you how much of a high PC is dispersal assumption rather than landscape.
- **Rank patches for protection by dPC, not area**, then check how the ranking moves between the shortest and longest dispersal distance your species might use; patches that stay high are the robust choices.
- **Test corridor plans**: paint a new habitat strip onto the map in the tool, recompute, and read the gain in PC, which is exactly the quantity corridor papers set out to improve.

## The fine print

- Our landscape is a **stand-in** built from the paper's summary numbers. The paper publishes no coordinates, so none of its per-patch values is recomputed here.
- The paper's patch table lists 35 rows while its text describes 23 primary core sources, and labels areas in hm² where the text uses km²; we used the text's figures.
- We assumed uniform resistance, so our distances are straight-line. The paper uses a resistance surface, which lengthens effective distances and would push the implied dispersal distances up, not down.
- The paper's text and abstract disagree about the number of key nodes (86 against 127 plus 43), and it reports a "mean dPC" of 2.7 km, a percentage given in kilometres, so some of its reporting is hard to read.
- IIC and PC are not comparable in value, only in ranking; the point here is that one landscape and one distance must give both, and no single distance gives these two.
