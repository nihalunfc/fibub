Stars that pulsate are like bells: the notes they ring depend on their size and density. Stars that eclipse each other let astronomers weigh and measure them directly. HIP 7666 does both. A team has now combined TESS light curves with 63 new spectra to measure both stars to within 1 to 2%, giving a pulsating star of known mass and size to test models against. This article shows how the [[period-finder]], the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]] and the [[blackbody-calculator]] let a researcher or student check the key numbers in minutes.

## The paper in brief

HIP 7666 is a pair of stars going round each other every 2.37 days, lined up so that each passes in front of the other. One of them is a **δ Scuti star**, a type of star a little heavier and hotter than the Sun that rings with many pulsation modes, each lasting under a few hours. The team measured the speeds of both stars from their spectra and fitted the eclipses in the TESS light curve. That gives masses of **1.822 and 1.246 times the Sun's** and radii of **2.154 and 1.170 times the Sun's**. The primary is 7,376 K. The distance worked out from the stars' size and brightness, 316 parsecs, agrees with Gaia's 313. After removing the eclipses, 15 pulsation frequencies remain, mostly in the δ Scuti range. Models put the pair at about 0.9 billion years old, a little richer in heavy elements than the Sun.

Why it matters: most δ Scuti stars are single, so their masses are guessed from models. A δ Scuti star with a measured mass and radius is a calibration point for stellar interiors and for asteroseismology, the science of reading a star's inside from its vibrations.

## Checked with the tools

We used the paper's masses, radii and temperature, the 2005 discovery paper's orbital period (2.37229 days) and strongest pulsation (24.463 cycles per day), and fibub's maths. We did not have the TESS data, so the periodogram in Step 3 uses a **simulated** light curve, and says so.

**Step 1: the size of the orbit.** Kepler's third law with the two masses together (3.07 Suns) and a 2.372-day period puts the stars **10.9 solar radii** (0.051 au) apart. The primary's radius is about 0.20 of that and the secondary's about 0.11: two stars that take up a large part of their orbit, which is why both eclipses are deep and why the paper finds each star fills about half its Roche lobe.

**Step 2: the speeds.** The same law gives each star's speed round the orbit (Figure 1): **94 km/s** for the primary and **138 km/s** for the lighter secondary. The ratio of the two speeds, 1.46, is the inverse of the mass ratio, 1.822 ÷ 1.246. This is how the paper's masses were measured, in reverse: the spectra give the speeds, and the speeds give the masses.

**Step 3: finding the pulsation.** We built a 27-day light curve like one TESS sector, with 2-minute points, a gap in the middle, both eclipses, a 3 millimagnitude pulsation at 24.463 cycles per day and 2 millimagnitudes of noise. We masked out the eclipses and ran the Period finder's Lomb-Scargle maths, telling it nothing about the answer. It found the highest peak at **24.463 cycles per day** (Figure 2), with two small side peaks close by that come from the length of the data and its gap. One pulsation every **59 minutes**.

**Step 4: which note is it?** A star's pulsation period times the square root of its mean density is close to a constant, *Q*, for each type of vibration. The primary's mean density is 1.822 ÷ 2.154³ = **0.18 times the Sun's**. With the 59-minute period that gives *Q* ≈ **0.017 days**. A δ Scuti star's slowest radial note has *Q* near 0.033 days, and higher overtones have smaller values, so 24.46 cycles per day is not the fundamental: it is a higher overtone (or a non-radial mode), assuming the primary is the pulsator. This is our quick estimate, not the paper's mode identification.

**Step 5: the brightness and the spin.** The Blackbody calculator gives a 7,376 K star of 2.154 solar radii a power output of **12.4 Suns**. If the primary's spin keeps pace with the orbit, as close pairs usually do, its equator would move at **46 km/s**. The paper measures 56.6 ± 5.3 km/s, a little faster: the spin may not yet be fully locked to the orbit.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/hip-7666-eclipsing-delta-scuti-binary.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls the tools' own functions:

- `semiMajor` and `semiAmplitude` from `src/lib/sci/rvmass.ts`, the maths behind the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]]. The formulas are the same for a planet and a star; the calculator simply works in planet units.
- `lombScargle` and `peaks` from `src/lib/sci/lombscargle.ts`, the engine of the [[period-finder]].
- `star` from `src/lib/sci/blackbody.ts`, the maths behind the [[blackbody-calculator]].

The simulated light curve's eclipse shape, pulsation amplitude and noise are labelled in the script as our choices. Run the script and it prints every number in this article and redraws both charts.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` checks these functions against known answers:

- The Period finder must recover a 2.345-day sine from 120 unevenly spaced points to within 0.002 days, its fast method must match a direct sum to nine decimal places, and in pure noise about 10% of trials must cross the 10% false-alarm line, as they should.
- The orbit maths must give Jupiter's pull on the Sun as 12.47 m/s, and 51 Pegasi b's orbit as 0.053 au.
- The blackbody maths must give the Sun exactly one solar luminosity and an absolute magnitude of 4.82.

## Check it yourself

**The speeds, on the [[exoplanet-mass-calculator]]:**

1. Choose **Wobble from the mass**.
2. Type **1.822** in *Star mass (Suns)* and **2.37229** in *Orbital period (days)*.
3. Choose **Jupiters** and type **1305** as the planet mass (the secondary's 1.246 Suns is about 1,305 Jupiters).
4. *Velocity semi-amplitude K* reads about **94,000 m/s**: the primary's 94 km/s. Swap the two masses (1.246 Suns, 1,909 Jupiters) to get the secondary's 138 km/s.

**The pulsation, on the [[period-finder]]:**

1. Paste a light curve as time (days) and magnitude, one point per line. TESS light curves of HIP 7666 (TIC 308953703) are public from the MAST archive.
2. Leave *Values are magnitudes* ticked. Type **0.02** in *Shortest period* and **0.1** in *Longest period*, to search the δ Scuti range.
3. The top peak should sit near **0.0409 days** (24.46 cycles per day). Remove points inside the eclipses first, or the eclipses will add peaks of their own.

**The luminosity, on the [[blackbody-calculator]]:** choose *The temperature (K)*, type **7376**, then **2.154** in *Radius (Suns)*. It reads about **12.4 Suns**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a binary's masses** against its measured speeds and period before trusting a table.
- **Find pulsations in a light curve** without installing anything; the data stay on your computer.
- **Estimate the pulsation constant** to see whether a frequency could be the fundamental mode.
- **Plan spectra:** the predicted speeds tell you how far apart the two stars' lines will be, and when.
- **Teach** how eclipsing binaries give stellar masses, the foundation of much of astrophysics.

## The fine print

- The orbital period and main pulsation frequency come from the 2005 discovery paper; the masses, radii, temperature and rotation come from the 2026 paper's abstract.
- Our light curve is **simulated**. It shows the Period finder can recover a signal like this one, not what the real TESS data contain.
- The pulsation constant uses the primary's density; if the pulsation comes from the secondary, *Q* changes.
- We assumed a circular orbit seen edge-on, as the paper finds a circular orbit and the stars eclipse.
