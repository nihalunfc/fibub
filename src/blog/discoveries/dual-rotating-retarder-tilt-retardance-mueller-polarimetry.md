A Mueller matrix polarimeter has no moving parts except the two waveplates it spins, and those are the problem. Every rotation mount wobbles a little, so the beam walks across the detector as the measurement proceeds, and the instrument cannot tell a walking beam from a changing sample. A 2025 paper in *Photonics* measures how bad that is: tilt a tissue sample by 10° and its apparent **linear retardance**, the phase delay the sample puts between two polarisations, shifts by more than 8 %, far more than the tilt itself can explain. This article rebuilds that instrument with the [[polarisation-calculator]] and puts a number on the culprit. For the waveplates and polarisers themselves, see the [[thin-film-coating]] simulator and the [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The instrument is a **dual-rotating-retarder** polarimeter, the standard design since the 1970s: a fixed polariser, a quarter-wave plate, the sample, a second quarter-wave plate, and a fixed analyser. The two waveplates turn together at a fixed **1:5 ratio**, and at each of 30 steps a camera records an image. Because the 1:5 ratio spreads the sample's 16 Mueller elements onto different harmonics of the rotation, all 16 can be pulled back out of those 30 numbers.

The authors look at what happens when something is slightly out of true. They tilt a waveplate and the sample by 0, 4, 8, 12 and 16°, and compare the retardance the instrument reports. A multilayer-tape **phantom** of 1.1 to 1.9 rad retardance behaves well. Human intestinal **tissue boundary regions**, with a much smaller mean retardance of 0.2418, do not: at a 10° tilt the average relative error across ten regions is **0.0841**, that is 8.4 %, and by 12° it is over 10 %. Meanwhile the genuine, physical change in optical path a 16° tilt causes is only about **0.027** by their own calculation with an index of 1.5.

Their conclusion: the error is not the tilt changing the sample, it is the tilt making the beam drift, and a drifting beam looks like a changing sample. They also say plainly what they did not do: they never measured the drift in micrometres.

## Checked with the tools

What follows is a **simulation** of the paper's instrument, built from the tool's own polarisers and retarders. No measurement of ours and none of the paper's images are involved. The point is to supply the number the paper could not: how much wobble it takes.

**Step 1: build the instrument and check it is honest.** The chain, in the tool's own notation, is `polariser 0`, `qwp θ`, `retarder δ α`, `qwp 5θ`, `polariser 90`, with θ stepping 6° at a time through 30 steps. For a sample of δ = 0.2418 rad (13.85°) with its fast axis at 30° (**our choice**; nothing below depends on it), the intensity reaching the camera swings between **0.025 % and 47.6 %** of the light going in. Solving the 30 intensities for the 16 Mueller elements and reading the retardance off with cos δ = trace(M)/2 − 1 gives **0.241800 rad**: the instrument recovers exactly what was put in, so any error later is ours to explain.

**Step 2: add a wobble.** A drifting beam crossing a detector that is not perfectly uniform gives each step a slightly different gain, once per revolution of the mount. We model that as multiplying step *k*'s intensity by (1 + g·cos θ_k) — **our model** of the drift, since the paper reports only fit residuals, not a drift distance. Then:

| Intensity wobble g | Recovered retardance | Error |
|---|---|---|
| none | 0.24180 rad | 0.00 % |
| 0.2 % | 0.23907 rad | −1.13 % |
| 0.5 % | 0.23491 rad | −2.85 % |
| 1 % | 0.22782 rad | −5.78 % |
| 2 % | 0.21292 rad | −11.95 % |
| 5 % | 0.16008 rad | −33.80 % |

The paper's 8.4 % is reproduced by a wobble of **1.43 %**. That is the headline number: a one-and-a-half per cent intensity ripple, utterly invisible in the raw images, is enough to account for everything the paper saw. Figure 1 shows just how invisible: the clean sequence and the sequence with a 2 % wobble lie on top of each other.

**Step 3: the genuine tilt is an order of magnitude smaller.** The paper's own optical-path calculation gives about 0.027 at 16°. A tilt-induced path change grows as the square of the tilt angle for small angles, so reading 0.027 as a relative increase (**our reading** of their equation) scales to **1.05 % at 10°** and **0.011 % at 1°**. Against the 8.4 % observed, the physics of tilting the plate accounts for roughly one part in eight. The paper's conclusion stands, and now with a ratio attached.

**Step 4: why the phantom looked fine and the tissue did not.** This is the part the tool makes obvious. The same wobble hurts a weakly birefringent sample far more than a strongly birefringent one, because a small retardance sits where the cosine is flat: a given error in trace(M) turns into a much larger error in δ. With the 1.43 % wobble that explains the tissue result, the paper's tape phantom at 1.1 rad would show only **1.5 %** error, at 1.5 rad **0.98 %**, and at 1.9 rad **0.66 %**. A phantom five to eight times stiffer in retardance under-reports the problem by a factor of five to ten, which is exactly the trap the paper's own data fell into, and a good argument for validating a polarimeter with a phantom near the retardance of the real sample.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/dual-rotating-retarder-tilt-retardance-mueller-polarimetry.mjs` in fibub's public code. The physics comes from `src/lib/sci/phpol.ts`, the maths behind the [[polarisation-calculator]]:

- `parseElements` reads the instrument as the lines `polariser 0`, `qwp 12`, `retarder 13.8541 30`, `qwp 60`, `polariser 90`;
- `propagate` carries the Stokes vector through the chain to give the generator state at the sample;
- `systemMueller` builds the Mueller matrix of everything after the sample, whose first row is what the camera weights;
- `muellerOf` and `jRetarder` build the sample's own Mueller matrix from its Jones matrix.

Two things are written in the script rather than taken from the tool, because they are data analysis and not optics: the 16-element least-squares solve (normal equations with Gauss-Jordan elimination) and the retardance read-out cos δ = trace(M)/2 − 1.

From the paper we take the instrument layout, the 1:5 ratio, the 30 steps, the tissue retardance 0.2418, the phantom range 1.1 to 1.9 rad, the 0.0841 relative error at 10° and the 0.027 path increment at 16°. **Our assumptions** are the sample's fast axis (30°), the drift model (a cosine gain at the rotation frequency), the reading of 0.027 as a relative rather than an absolute change, and the square-of-the-angle scaling of the tilt effect.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/polarisation-calculator.mjs` on every change to fibub, and it pins the polarisation maths against an independent NumPy implementation that builds Mueller matrices the other way round, as A (J ⊗ J*) A⁻¹:

- horizontal light through a half-wave plate at 22.5° must come out as the Stokes vector (1, 0, 1, 0), then through a quarter-wave plate at 0° as (1, 0, 0, 1), then through a polariser at 30° as (0.5, 0.25, 0.4330127018922193, 0);
- the Mueller matrix of a 60° retarder at 30° must match NumPy element by element to 1e-12;
- Malus's law must come out exactly: unpolarised light through polarisers at 0° and 60° keeps 1/8 of its power, crossed polarisers keep none, and a 45° polariser slipped between crossed ones restores 1/8.
- New with this article: the paper's instrument with the first plate at 12° must pass 36.4 % of unpolarised light through a 0.2418 rad sample at 30°, and solving the full 30-step sequence must give back 0.2418 rad to eight decimal places.

## Check it yourself

You can run one step of the paper's polarimeter on the [[polarisation-calculator]] page:

1. Set *Polarisation* to **Unpolarised**.
2. Clear the *Elements* box and type five lines: **polariser 0**, **qwp 12**, **retarder 13.8541 30**, **qwp 60**, **polariser 90**. That is the instrument with its first waveplate at 12°, so its second one is at 60°, and a sample of 0.2418 rad (13.8541°) retardance with its fast axis at 30°.
3. *Intensity* reads **36.4 %** of the light going in, and the *Stokes vector* reads **0.364, −0.364, 0, 0**: vertical light, as it must be after an analyser at 90°.
4. Change *qwp 12* to **qwp 6** and *qwp 60* to **qwp 30**: the intensity falls to **25.0 %**. Step on to 18° and 90° and it falls again to **11.9 %**, then to **0.5 %** at 24° and 120°. Those are four of the thirty numbers a real measurement records.
5. Now set the sample to **retarder 0 30** (no birefringence at all) and the same step passes **32.6 %**. Each of the thirty steps moves by a different amount when the sample changes, and that pattern of differences is what the 16-element solve lives on.
6. Put the sample back and change it to **retarder 63 30** (1.1 rad, the stiff end of the paper's phantom): the same step now passes **45.3 %**. A stiffer sample moves the numbers much further, which is why it is much harder for a wobble to fool.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Designing a polarimeter before building it.** Typing the real chain into the [[polarisation-calculator]] gives the intensity at every step, so you can check that no step lands near zero where shot noise will dominate.
- **Choosing a calibration phantom.** Figure 2 says to match your phantom's retardance to the sample's. The tool gives the retardance a given waveplate or stack of tape will provide.
- **Budgeting an error.** Pair the tool with the [[uncertainty-calculator]] to turn a known intensity stability, say 0.3 %, into a retardance uncertainty for your own sample.
- **Teaching Mueller calculus with a real instrument.** The five-line chain here is the whole of a research polarimeter, and students can see each element's effect on the Poincaré sphere.
- **Checking a published retardance.** When a paper reports a small retardance with a few per cent error bar, this calculation shows what intensity stability that claim silently requires.

## The fine print

Our drift model is deliberately simple: a single sinusoidal gain at the rotation frequency. A real drift also moves the beam onto different pixels of a non-uniform sample, defocuses slightly, and has components at the second plate's frequency too, so the wobble that reproduces a given error in a real instrument may differ from our 1.43 % by a factor of order one. We also ignore the polarisers' finite 500:1 extinction and the LED's 10 nm bandwidth, both of which the paper has and both of which add their own small errors. What is robust is the shape of Figure 2: whatever the mechanism, an intensity error of a fixed size costs a weakly birefringent sample several times more retardance accuracy than a strongly birefringent one. That is a property of the arccosine, not of our model, and it is the practical lesson worth carrying away.
