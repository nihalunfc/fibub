Most papers on reinforced films report three mechanical numbers: tensile strength, Young's modulus and elongation at break. A 2025 study in *Advances in Polymer Technology* adds **jute cellulose nanocrystals** (rod-shaped crystalline fragments of cellulose, here freed from jute fibre with a fluoride salt in DMSO) to polyvinyl alcohol and reports 102.93 MPa, 3254.18 MPa and 6 to 7 %. Those three numbers are not independent: together they say a great deal about the shape of the curve, and they are enough to work out the one number the paper leaves out, the toughness. This article does that with the [[stress-strain-analyser]]. If you are stacking such films, the [[laminate-calculator]] takes the same moduli further, and [[dsc-tga-analyser]] is the tool for the melting and degradation side of the same paper.

## The paper in brief

The authors cast PVA films with 0 to 3 wt% jute cellulose nanocrystals and compare them with films made using commercial nanocellulose. Films up to **1 wt%** stay smooth; above that the crystals clump. The 1 wt% film is the best of them: a tensile strength of **102.93 MPa**, which is 70.85 % above neat PVA, and a Young's modulus of **3254.18 MPa**, 56.41 % above it, while the **elongation at break stays at 6 to 7 %** all the way to 3 wt%. It also keeps over 79 % visible transmittance, blocks over 40 % of UV, melts at 191 °C and takes up 35.63 % less moisture than neat PVA.

The percentage improvements let us recover the neat-PVA baseline: **60.25 MPa** and **2080 MPa**. From here on, every number is ours.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: are the three numbers consistent with a straight line? No, and that is the point.** If the 1 wt% film were elastic right up to failure, it would reach 102.93 MPa at a strain of 102.93 ÷ 3254.18 = **3.16 %**. It actually breaks at 6 to 7 %. So **at least half of the strain at break is non-elastic**: the film yields and draws before it snaps. Neat PVA is the same story (2.90 % elastic against 6.5 % at break). Any film whose strength divided by its modulus comes out close to its elongation at break is brittle and linear; here the gap is a factor of two, which is a real, reportable property hiding in three numbers.

**Step 2: rebuild curves that match all three numbers.** We simulated each film with a Ramberg-Osgood curve (total strain = σ/E + a plastic term that grows as a power of stress), fixed so that the curve has the published modulus, passes exactly through the published strength at **6.5 %** strain (the middle of the paper's 6 to 7 %: **our assumption**) and is still almost elastic at half the strength. That gives a stress exponent near 6 for both films. Figure 1 shows them.

**Step 3: what the analyser reads back.** Fed those curves with its default settings (modulus fitted between 10 % and 40 % of the tensile strength, 0.2 % offset yield), the tool returns:

| What | Neat PVA | PVA + 1 wt% jute CNC |
|---|---|---|
| Modulus, 10-40 % window | 2,057 MPa | 3,217 MPa |
| Modulus, 5-20 % window | 2,080 MPa | 3,253 MPa |
| 0.2 % offset yield | 38.1 MPa at 2.05 % | 65.5 MPa at 2.23 % |
| Tensile strength | 60.25 MPa | 102.93 MPa |
| **Toughness (area under the curve)** | **2.74 MJ/m³** | **4.58 MJ/m³** |
| Modulus of resilience | 0.354 MJ/m³ | 0.668 MJ/m³ |
| Hollomon exponent n | 0.432 | 0.463 |

Two things stand out. First, **where you fit the modulus matters more than the material**: the standard 10 to 40 % window already catches a little plastic strain and reads 1.1 % low, while a 5 to 20 % window returns 3,253 MPa against the 3,254.18 we put in. A paper that reports a modulus without its fit range has left out part of the measurement.

Second, the **toughness rises by 67 %** and the resilience (the elastic energy a film can store and give back, σ_y²/2E) by **89 %**, even though the elongation at break has not changed. For packaging film, toughness is what resists a dropped box, and it is free to compute from a curve that was already recorded. Figure 2 shows the energy building up along each curve.

**Step 4: a sanity check on the strength claim.** A 70.85 % strength gain at 1 wt% filler is large. It is not impossible for cellulose nanocrystals in PVA, which hydrogen-bond to the matrix, but the numbers above let a reader test it cheaply: the composite's extra toughness (1.84 MJ/m³) has to come from somewhere, and if a repeat measurement gives the same strength but an elongation at break of 3 %, the toughness gain disappears and the film has become brittle rather than stronger. Elongation at break is the number to watch.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number in them come from `scripts/blog-figures/pva-jute-cellulose-nanocrystal-film-tensile-modulus-toughness.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `analyse` and `trapz` from `src/lib/sci/tensile.ts`, the maths behind the [[stress-strain-analyser]]: `analyse` fits the modulus by least squares over a stress window, finds the offset yield by intersecting the offset line with the curve, integrates the curve for toughness and fits the Hollomon exponent to true stress and strain.

From the paper we used the tensile strength, the Young's modulus, the two percentage improvements (to get the neat-PVA baseline) and the 6 to 7 % elongation at break. **Our assumptions** are the curve shape (Ramberg-Osgood), the 6.5 % break strain, and that each film is still nearly elastic at half its strength. The curves are **simulated**, not the paper's measurements: it reports the three numbers, not the traces.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/stress-strain-analyser.mjs` on every change, pinning the analyser against NumPy and against a curve whose answers are known from how it was built:

- On a made-up steel curve, the fitted modulus must be **199,747.79 MPa** (NumPy's `polyfit` on the same 13 points) and within 0.2 % of the 200 GPa used to make the curve.
- The 0.2 % offset yield must be **304.695 MPa**, the tensile strength 437.048 MPa, the toughness 105.652 MJ/m³ (NumPy's trapezoid) and the Hollomon exponent 0.15688, all to NumPy's own digits.
- Toe compensation must move the strain zero to exactly 0.001 on a line drawn with that offset, and a fit window with fewer than three points must raise an error instead of guessing.
- New with this article: the simulated 1 wt% film curve must give a modulus of **3,217 MPa** in the 10-40 % window and **3,253 MPa** in a 5-20 % window, a 0.2 % offset yield of **65.54 MPa**, a toughness of **4.58 MJ/m³** and a resilience of **0.668 MJ/m³**.

## Check it yourself

On the [[stress-strain-analyser]], in the *Your test data* card:

1. Set *Columns are* to **stress, then strain**, *Stress unit* to **MPa** and *Strain unit* to **%**.
2. Replace the box *One reading per line, from the start of the test to fracture* with our rounded composite curve, one pair per line (stress first): **0, 0**; **10.29, 0.316**; **20.59, 0.633**; **30.88, 0.951**; **41.17, 1.278**; **51.47, 1.632**; **61.76, 2.049**; **72.05, 2.598**; **79.77, 3.163**; **87.49, 3.935**; **92.64, 4.609**; **97.78, 5.45**; **100.36, 5.946**; **102.93, 6.5**.
3. In *Analysis*, leave *Modulus fit from (% of UTS)* at **10**, *to (% of UTS)* at **40** and *Yield offset (% strain)* at **0.2**. Untick **Correct the toe** (our curve starts exactly at the origin).
4. You should see a modulus of about **3,190 MPa** from 3 points, R² 0.9999, a 0.2 % offset yield of **65.07 MPa** at 2.23 % strain, a tensile strength of **102.93 MPa** at 6.5 %, a toughness of **4.57 MJ/m³** and a resilience of **0.66 MJ/m³**.
5. Change the fit window to **5** and **20**: the modulus climbs towards 3,250 MPa, the value the paper reports. That one change is the whole lesson about reporting a fit range.
6. Press **Download the curves (CSV)** to get the true stress and true strain columns the tool worked out as well.

With 14 pasted points the modulus comes out a little lower than with our full 81-point curve, because only three of them fall inside the 10 to 40 % window. More points in the elastic region is the cheapest accuracy a tensile test can buy.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Get toughness out of curves you already have**: the area under the curve, in MJ/m³, is a better single number for packaging and film work than strength alone.
- **Report a modulus with its window**, and show how much it moves between a 5-20 % and a 10-40 % fit, so other labs can reproduce it.
- **Check a published triple for consistency**: strength ÷ modulus against elongation at break tells you at once whether a material is brittle or draws.
- **Compare treatments fairly**: run every curve through the same offset, window and toe settings rather than reading values off a plot.
- **Plan the test**: simulate the expected curve, see how few elastic points your data logger would give you, and raise the sampling rate before the specimen is in the grips. Propagate the specimen-size errors with the [[uncertainty-calculator]].

## The fine print

- The curves here are **simulated** from three published numbers per film. The paper's measured traces are not in it, and nothing here should be read as its data.
- The 6.5 % break strain, the Ramberg-Osgood shape and the "almost elastic at half the strength" rule are our assumptions. A film that yields earlier would show more toughness, one that yields later less.
- We took the neat-PVA baseline from the paper's own percentage improvements, so it carries their rounding.
- Film tensile results depend strongly on thickness, humidity and grip speed, none of which we could read; comparisons between papers need those three.
- The paper's own best loading is 1 wt%; the 6 to 7 % elongation figure covers loadings up to 3 wt%, and we applied the middle of that range to both films.
