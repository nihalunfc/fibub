Measuring leaf area properly means destroying the leaf, or at least picking it. So plant scientists lean on a shortcut over a century old: **area = k × length × width**, the Montgomery equation, with one constant *k* per species. A 2026 paper in *Plants* measured 3,679 scanned leaves from 12 crabapple (*Malus*) cultivars and pinned that constant down to 0.6641, with a tight interval, and then asked whether width can be dropped altogether in favour of a **square law**, area ∝ length². It cannot, and the paper shows exactly when it goes wrong. This article rebuilds both findings by drawing leaves of known shape and measuring them with the maths behind fibub's [[leaf-area-analyser]], which measures leaf area from a scan or phone photo in the browser. Nearby: the [[stomatal-density-counter]] for the same leaves under a microscope, and [[plant-growth-analysis]] for turning leaf area into growth rates.

## The paper in brief

The authors collected at least 300 leaves from at least 15 trees of each of 12 crabapple cultivars at a germplasm repository in Jiangsu, China, scanned them at 600 dpi and extracted each leaf's outline, area, length and width with the `biogeom` package in R.

Three results matter here. First, the **pooled Montgomery parameter is 0.6641** (95 % interval 0.6632 to 0.6650), with cultivar values from 0.6313 to 0.7005 — comfortably inside the theoretical range of 1/2 to π/4 that the authors quote. Second, the Montgomery equation fits very well in every cultivar (r² at least 0.9703, RMSE at most 0.0285) while the square law fits badly and unevenly (r² from 0.3332 to 0.9035, RMSE from 0.0406 to 0.0846). Third, and most useful, the square law's error is **predicted by how much the width-to-length ratio varies within a cultivar**: the correlation between that coefficient of variation (7.1 % to 14.9 % across cultivars) and the square law's RMSE is 0.97.

## Checked with the tools

We cannot re-measure the paper's leaves, so we **drew our own** and measured them exactly as the tool does: take the smallest of the red, green and blue values at each pixel, split leaf from paper with Otsu's automatic threshold, group the connected pixels, fill holes, and set the scale from a 1 cm reference square in the same picture. Each leaf outline is a superellipse, |2x/L|^n + |2y/W|^n = 1. That one family is a happy accident for this paper: n = 1 is a rhombus, whose area is exactly L × W / 2, and n = 2 is an ellipse, whose area is exactly (π/4) L × W. The paper's theoretical limits are the two ends of our shape family.

**Step 1: the tool recovers k to four decimals.** For seven shapes from rhombus to ellipse, the area the tool reads off a 40 px per cm synthetic scan differs from the exact area by at most 0.08 %, and the Montgomery parameter it implies is within 0.0006 of the exact value every time.

| Shape exponent n | Exact k | k from the tool's area | Leaf area measured (cm²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 (rhombus) | 0.5000 | 0.5002 | 12.606 (exact 12.600) |
| 1.4 | 0.6562 | 0.6563 | 16.538 (exact 16.537) |
| 1.5 | 0.6845 | 0.6841 | 17.238 (exact 17.248) |
| 1.8 | 0.7516 | 0.7510 | 18.926 (exact 18.941) |
| 2.0 (ellipse) | 0.7854 | 0.7848 | 19.778 (exact 19.792) |

Running it backwards, the paper's pooled **k = 0.6641 is the value of a superellipse with n = 1.427**: a crabapple leaf, on this measure, sits a little under halfway from a rhombus to an ellipse. Figure 1 puts the whole family on one line.

**A warning that came out of the exercise.** The tool's table gives each leaf a *Length* and *Width* taken from the ellipse with the same spread as the leaf — the page says plainly that these are a guide, not a ruler measurement. For a true ellipse they are the real length and width, and k comes out at π/4 exactly. For a pointed leaf they are shorter than the calliper length and width, so a *k* worked out from them is badly inflated: 0.7499 instead of 0.5000 for our rhombus, a 50 % error. If you want a Montgomery parameter, measure length and width across the widest points (or read them off the leaf's bounding box) and take only the **area** from the picture.

**Step 2: the square law fails where the width-to-length ratio wanders.** We made 12 made-up cultivars of 24 leaves each, giving each one its own shape exponent, its own mean width-to-length ratio (0.49 to 0.63, the paper's range) and its own variability in that ratio (6.6 % to 17.1 % as drawn). Every leaf was measured through the tool's pipeline, then both models were fitted on a log scale.

| Check | Paper (3,679 real leaves) | Our tool on 288 drawn leaves |
|---|---|---|
| Montgomery k per cultivar | 0.6313 to 0.7005 | 0.6237 to 0.7516 (our shapes, chosen wider) |
| Montgomery RMSE of ln(area) | ≤ 0.0285 | 0.0019 to 0.0034 |
| Square-law RMSE | 0.0406 to 0.0846 | 0.0672 to 0.1716 |
| Square-law r² | 0.3332 to 0.9035 | 0.7594 to 0.9453 |
| Correlation, ratio CV against square-law RMSE | 0.97 | **0.998** |

The correlation is the paper's central mechanism, and it comes straight back out: the more a cultivar's leaves vary in how long they are for their width, the worse a length-only model does, while the Montgomery fit barely notices (its own correlation with the ratio's CV is −0.15). Our Montgomery errors are much smaller than the paper's because our drawn leaves have no biological scatter at all; only pixel rounding. Our square-law errors are larger because our shape range is wider.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/crabapple-leaf-area-montgomery-parameter-images.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `minChannel`, `histogram`, `otsu`, `threshold`, `leafArea` (with its connected-group labelling, hole filling and reference-square scale), `rect` and `noise` from `src/lib/sci/plantimg.ts`, the maths behind the [[leaf-area-analyser]].

From the paper: the pooled and per-cultivar Montgomery parameters, the theoretical limits, the fit statistics, the width-to-length ratio means and their coefficients of variation, and the 0.97 correlation. **Our assumptions**: the superellipse shape family and its exponents, 40 px per cm, a 1 cm reference square, ± 6 grey levels of noise, 24 leaves per cultivar and the leaf lengths (mean 4.9 to 7.5 cm, 15 % scatter). The leaves are **drawn, not scanned**, and no number in our tables is the paper's measurement.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/leaf-area-analyser.mjs` on every change to fibub:

- On a made-up picture, the automatic threshold must be 143, as scikit-image's `threshold_otsu` gives; the connected groups must be 30 at 8-connectivity and 103 at 4-connectivity, as `scipy.ndimage.label` gives; and the moment ellipse of the largest group must match scikit-image's `regionprops` to 1e-9 px.
- Filling holes must give 7,119 px, as `scipy.ndimage.binary_fill_holes` does, and the example scan must read 7.1946 cm² from its 1 cm square — exactly the 71,946 leaf pixels the page drew — rising to 7.2881 cm² with the insect hole filled.
- New with this article: superellipse leaves with n = 1, 1.5 and 2, drawn at 40 px per cm with a 1 cm square, must give Montgomery parameters within 0.001 of the exact 0.5, 0.6845 and π/4, and the example scan's third leaf must give π/4 to four decimals from its area and ellipse axes.

## Check it yourself

On the [[leaf-area-analyser]] page, with no file of your own:

1. Leave the mode on **Leaf area** and press **Use the example**. It draws a scan at 100 px per cm: a 1 cm black square and three leaves, one with an insect hole.
2. In *Settings*, keep *Background* on **Light (white paper, scanner lid)** and leave *Threshold* empty so Otsu picks it. Keep *Ignore specks smaller than (px)* at **50**.
3. Under *Scale*, keep *Scale from* on **A reference square in the photo**, *Side of the square* **1**, *Unit* **cm**. Press **Tap the square** and tap inside the black square.
4. It reads **Leaf area: 7.195 cm² (3 leaves)**, with *Scale* **100 px per cm** from a 10,000 px square and *Threshold* **56**.
5. In the table, leaf 3 is **2.261 cm²** with length **2.399 cm** and width **1.2 cm**. Divide: 2.261 ÷ (2.399 × 1.2) = **0.7854**, which is π/4 — the upper limit the paper quotes, because that leaf was drawn as a perfect ellipse.
6. Tick **Fill holes inside leaves**: the total rises to **7.288 cm²** as the insect hole is counted back in. Decide which number your trait means before you report it.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Fit your own Montgomery parameter**: scan leaves next to a 1 cm square, read each area in the browser, and divide by your own calliper length × width.
- **Check a published k for plausibility**: it must sit between 1/2 and π/4, and our shape curve turns any k into the leaf shape it implies.
- **Decide whether width is worth measuring**: work out the coefficient of variation of width ÷ length in a pilot sample. Above about 10 %, a length-only model will drift, as both the paper and Figure 2 show.
- **Check a leaf-area meter or an app** against a drawn shape of known area before trusting it on a season's data.
- **Measure canopy cover** with the same tool's second mode, then scale leaf-level work up with [[plant-growth-analysis]].

## The fine print

- Our leaves are **drawn**, not real. They test the measurement chain and the geometry, not crabapple biology.
- Length and width in our tables are the drawn (calliper) values. The tool's own length and width come from a moment ellipse and are smaller for pointed leaves; the article says where that matters.
- We fitted both models on a log scale with the exponent fixed (1 for Montgomery, 2 for the square law). The paper's exact fitting scale is not stated in the text we could read, so the RMSE values are comparable in spirit, not to the last digit.
- The licence came from Crossref. MDPI's page metadata says CC BY 3.0 for articles that are in fact CC BY 4.0.
- A real scan adds shadows, curled leaves and petioles. The paper excludes the petiole; our drawn leaves have none.
