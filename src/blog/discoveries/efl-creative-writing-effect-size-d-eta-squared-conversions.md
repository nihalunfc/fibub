Effect sizes are supposed to make results comparable, but they only do that if you can move between them. A paper that reports **η² = 0.71 to 0.89** has reported something enormous, yet most readers have no feel for eta squared, and a reader who wants to put the study in a meta-analysis needs Cohen's d or a correlation instead. A 2026 study in the *Asian-Pacific Journal of Second and Foreign Language Education* tested a 12-week creative-writing model with e-portfolios on 60 Egyptian EFL undergraduates, and its abstract gives two class means with their standard deviations and sizes: everything the [[effect-size-converter]] needs. We also use the [[hypothesis-tests]] page for the two-sample t and the [[difference-in-differences]] calculator to show what a pretest would have added.

## The paper in brief

Sixty first-year English-as-a-foreign-language undergraduates took part: 30 in an experimental group that worked through an iterative cycle of collaborative drafting and reflective e-portfolios, and 30 in a control group taught in the usual way, over 12 weeks. On the post-test **Total Creative Writing** score the experimental group averaged **83.60 (SD 6.80)** and the control group **46.30 (SD 10.13)**. The authors report that every between-group difference was significant at p < .001 with **large effect sizes, η² between .71 and .89**, and back the numbers with interviews and e-portfolio reflections. They are careful to say the design was quasi-experimental with intact classes, so the result is context-bound rather than proof of cause.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the three printed statistics per group (mean, standard deviation, size). No raw scores are published, and none are needed.

**Step 1: every scale at once.** From 83.60 (SD 6.80, n = 30) against 46.30 (SD 10.13, n = 30), the pooled standard deviation is **8.627** and the gap is **37.30** points, so:

| Effect size | Value | 95 % interval |
|---|---|---|
| Cohen's d | **4.32** | 3.40 to 5.25 |
| Hedges' g (small-sample corrected) | 4.27 | 3.36 to 5.18 |
| Glass's delta (control SD) | 3.68 | - |
| r (point-biserial) | 0.910 | 0.866 to 0.936 |
| η² (share of variance) | **0.829** | 0.749 to 0.877 |
| Cohen's f | 2.20 | - |
| Odds ratio (logistic approximation) | 2545 | 476 to 13610 |
| Cliff's delta | 0.998 | 0.984 to 1.000 |
| Probability of superiority | 0.9989 | 0.992 to 1.000 |
| Overlap of the two distributions | 3.1 % | - |

**Step 2: the paper's own η² range is consistent with this.** Our 0.829 for the total score sits inside the reported 0.71 to 0.89. Read the other way (Figure 2), that range corresponds to Cohen's d from **3.13 to 5.69**, so every measure in the study is in the same extraordinary territory as the total.

**Step 3: what a d of 4.3 looks like.** Figure 1 draws the two classes as normal curves with the printed means and spreads. They share about **3 %** of their area. A student picked at random from the e-learning class scores above a random control student **99.9 %** of the time, and essentially every student in the e-learning class is above the control group's mean (Cohen's U3 = 0.99999). In plain terms: the two groups barely meet.

For comparison, large school interventions usually land near d = 0.3 to 0.6, and a d above 1 is already unusual. A d of 4.3 is not impossible, but it almost always means something other than teaching alone: a test aimed squarely at what one group practised, graders who knew the groups, or classes that differed before the study began. The abstract reports no pretest equivalence, and the groups are intact classes.

**Step 4: the t test, pooled and Welch.** The control group's variance is **2.2 times** the experimental group's, so the equal-variance assumption is shaky. It happens not to matter here: the pooled t is **16.75** on 58 degrees of freedom, and Welch's t is also **16.75**, on 50.7 degrees of freedom, because the groups are the same size. With unequal sizes the two would part company, which is why the [[hypothesis-tests]] page offers both.

**Step 5: what a pretest would have added.** The design is pretest-posttest with two intact classes, which is a 2 x 2 difference-in-differences. If the paper also printed the pretest cells, the [[difference-in-differences]] calculator would give the change in the e-learning class minus the change in the control class, which is immune to a starting gap between the classes. Post-test-only numbers cannot separate "the lessons worked" from "this class was already better".

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/efl-creative-writing-effect-size-d-eta-squared-conversions.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `convert` from `src/lib/sci/effsize.ts`, the maths behind the [[effect-size-converter]], for the table, the intervals and both directions of the d to η² conversion, and the normal density `DIST.normal.pdf` from `src/lib/sci/distributions.ts` (the maths behind the probability distribution explorer) for the two curves in Figure 1.

The inputs are the five numbers in the paper's abstract: two means, two standard deviations and the group size of 30. The curves in Figure 1 assume the scores are normally distributed with those means and spreads, which is **our assumption** for drawing: the real score distribution is not published, and a test with a ceiling near 100 would be skewed.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/effect-size-converter.mjs` runs on every change to fibub, and every conversion in the table above is pinned to an independent Python calculation (numpy 2.5 and scipy 1.18) from Borenstein et al. (2009) and Lakens (2013):

- d = 0.5 with groups of 50 must convert to Hedges' g 0.49616, r 0.24485, odds ratio 2.47663, Cliff's delta 0.27633, overlap 0.80259 and η² 0.05995, with the interval 0.10193 to 0.89807.
- A t of 2.5 with groups of 20 and 25 must give d = 0.75 with interval 0.14194 to 1.35806 and η² = 0.12690, and d from t is checked against the pooled-SD d computed directly from random data, with r matching `scipy.stats.pointbiserialr`.
- Round trips must return the same number: d to r to d, d to log odds ratio to d, d to Cliff's delta to d.
- New with this article: the two classes of this paper (83.60 with SD 6.80 and 46.30 with SD 10.13, 30 each) must give a pooled SD of 8.6272, d = 4.32351 with interval 3.3992 to 5.2478, η² = 0.82862 and an overlap of 0.030636.

## Check it yourself

On the [[effect-size-converter]] page:

1. In *What do you have?*, set *Start from* to **Two means and standard deviations**.
2. Type *Group 1 mean* **83.6**, *Group 1 SD* **6.8**, *Group 2 mean* **46.3**, *Group 2 SD* **10.13**, *Group 1 size* **30**, *Group 2 size* **30**.
3. Leave *Confidence level* on **95 %**. The *Converted effect sizes* card shows Cohen's d **4.32** (3.40 to 5.25), Hedges' g **4.27**, r **0.910**, η² **0.829**, Cliff's delta **0.998** and an overlap of **3.1 %**.
4. Open *Show the working* to see the pooled SD of 8.627 and the formula behind each row.
5. Now change *Start from* to **Eta squared (η²)**, type **0.71** with *Number of groups* **2**: it returns d = **3.13**. Try **0.89**: d = **5.69**. That is the width of the paper's reported range in d units.
6. Finally set *Start from* to **t statistic, two separate groups**, *Value* **16.75**, sizes **30** and **30**: you get back d = 4.32, closing the loop.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Put any paper into a meta-analysis.** Two means with SDs and sizes, or a t, F or chi-square with its degrees of freedom, is enough to get d and its variance.
- **Translate a reviewer's favourite scale.** η², Cohen's f, r, odds ratios and Cliff's delta all describe the same gap; the converter shows the whole set, so you can report the one your field reads.
- **Sanity-check a big claim.** Convert the effect size into overlap and probability of superiority: if a teaching study implies that 99.9 % of one class beats the other, look for a measurement or assignment explanation before believing the method.
- **Check a paper's internal arithmetic.** A reported η² and a reported t must agree; if they do not, one of them is a typo.
- **Design the next study.** Once an honest d is in hand, [[sample-size]] gives the groups needed to detect it, and a pretest turns the design into a [[difference-in-differences]] that survives unequal classes.

## The fine print

- We worked from the paper's abstract, because the journal's article page would not load for us. The licence comes from the publisher's own Crossref deposit (CC BY 4.0).
- Only the total score is recomputed here. The paper reports several sub-measures whose means we do not have; the η² range 0.71 to 0.89 is its own summary of them.
- The conversions to an odds ratio, Cliff's delta, U3 and overlap assume normal distributions with equal spread. At this distance from zero those numbers are near their ceiling and should be read as "almost no overlap", not as precise quantities.
- Effect sizes measure separation, not causation. With intact classes and no published pretest, part of the gap may predate the 12 weeks.
- The quoted interval for d assumes the two groups are independent samples, which is how the post-test comparison was analysed.
