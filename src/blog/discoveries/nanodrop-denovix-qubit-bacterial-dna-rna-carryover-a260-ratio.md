Labs measure DNA in two main ways. A **spectrophotometer** (NanoDrop, DeNovix) shines ultraviolet light through a drop and reads the absorbance at 260 nm, which every nucleic acid absorbs. A **fluorometer** (Qubit) uses a dye that lights up only on double-stranded DNA. A 2024 paper in *PLoS ONE* put both kinds of instrument on 78 DNA extracts from a bacterium and found the spectrophotometers read about three times higher than Qubit, while the usual purity ratio looked clean. This article asks whether leftover RNA could explain both facts, using the [[nucleic-acid-concentration]] tool. For the steps after quantifying, plan reactions with the [[pcr-master-mix]] calculator, and see the light-absorbance law itself in the [[beer-lambert-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The team extracted DNA from 78 strains of *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and measured every sample twice on three instruments, before and after freezing. The Qubit's central value was about 40 ng/µL, against about 127 on the DeNovix and 147 on the NanoDrop (before freezing). The two spectrophotometers agreed closely with each other (an R² of 0.998 at the second timepoint) and both read 3 to 4 times higher than the Qubit. Freezing did not change the DeNovix reading. The purity ratios were typical of a clean sample: the A260/A280 was 1.99 on the DeNovix and 2.01 on the NanoDrop, and the A260/A230 was 2.33 on both. The authors discuss which instrument to trust for sequencing preparation and for spotting impurities.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation** from the paper's central values, with a simple mixing model that is **our assumption**: the Qubit counts DNA only, pure DNA has an A260/A280 of 1.8, and RNA (A260/A280 of 2.0 to 2.1) makes up the rest of the A260. The paper's numbers are medians over different samples, so this is a consistency check, not a fit.

**Step 1: what the reader must have seen.** The tool's rule for double-stranded DNA is concentration = A260 × 50 ng/µL (1 cm path). A DeNovix reading of 126.93 ng/µL therefore means an A260 of **2.54**, and the NanoDrop's 146.72 means **2.93**. If the 40 ng/µL from the Qubit were all of it, the A260 would be only **0.80**.

**Step 2: how much RNA would fill the gap.** The missing absorbance, 2.54 − 0.80 = 1.74, comes from something else that absorbs at 260 nm. If it is RNA, the tool's factor of 40 gives about **69.5 ng/µL of RNA** next to 40 ng/µL of DNA, and RNA would supply **68.5 %** of the A260. For the NanoDrop the numbers are 85 ng/µL and 72.7 %. After freezing: 69.3 ng/µL (DeNovix) and 59.7 ng/µL (NanoDrop), 67 % and 64 %.

**Step 3: does it predict the purity ratio?** This is the test that could have failed. Mixing 31.5 % DNA absorbance at a ratio of 1.8 with 68.5 % RNA absorbance at 2.0 gives a predicted A260/A280 of **1.93**; with RNA at 2.1 it gives **1.995**. The paper reports **1.99** for the DeNovix at the same timepoint. For the NanoDrop the model gives 1.94 to 2.01 against the paper's 2.01; after freezing it gives 1.92 to 1.99 against 1.98 (NanoDrop) and 1.93 to 1.99 against 2.05 (DeNovix, a little above). So the model reproduces ratios close to 2 in three of four cases without fitting anything to them (Figure 1). It does not prove RNA is the cause: other absorbers, such as free nucleotides, would act the same way, and the paper does not tell us.

**Step 4: a clean ratio hides it.** The tool's purity rule flags a DNA sample only when the 260/280 is below 1.7 (protein) or above 2.0 (RNA). Typing the DeNovix pair (1.99, 2.33) gives **no note at all**, although the tool's concentration is about 3.2 times the DNA-specific one. The second timepoint's DeNovix 2.05 is flagged ("RNA may be present"). So a ratio near 2.0 does not rule out a large overstatement, because the ratio of RNA is only slightly above that of DNA.

**Step 5: the subset the paper calls pure.** For extracts with a DeNovix A260/A280 between 1.7 and 2.0, the paper reports a spectrophotometer-to-Qubit ratio of about 2.06. Under our model that is an RNA share of **51.5 %** and a predicted ratio of **1.90 to 1.94**, inside 1.7 to 2.0 as it should be. Even "pure by the ratio" extracts can read twice too high (Figure 2).

**Step 6: what it means for copies.** A *S. pneumoniae* genome is about 2.1 million base pairs (**our assumption**, a round figure). The tool gives a circular double strand that size a molar mass of about 1.30 × 10⁹ g/mol. At the Qubit's 40 ng/µL that is **1.86 × 10⁷ genome copies per µL**; at the DeNovix's 126.93 ng/µL, **5.89 × 10⁷**, 3.17 times as many. A pipetting plan based on the wrong instrument would load 3 times too little DNA.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/nanodrop-denovix-qubit-bacterial-dna-rna-carryover-a260-ratio.mjs` calls `FACTOR` (50 and 40), `purity`, `molarMass` and `copies` from `src/lib/sci/nucconc.ts`, the maths behind the [[nucleic-acid-concentration]] tool. The instrument values and A260/A280 ratios come from the paper's text; the mixing model (Qubit equals DNA, DNA at 1.8, RNA at 2.0 to 2.1) and the 2.1 million base pair genome are **our assumptions**. Nothing is simulated or fitted.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/nucleic-acid-concentration.mjs`, a new file added with this article. It pins the tool to hand calculations: an A260 of 2.54 must give 127 ng/µL (double-stranded DNA), the same absorbance must give 101.6 ng/µL as RNA, and a 1.99 and 2.33 pair must give no purity note while 2.05 must flag possible RNA. It also checks the copy number of a 2.1 million base pair circle at 40 ng/µL (1.86 × 10⁷ copies/µL).

## Check it yourself

On the [[nucleic-acid-concentration]] page:

1. Under *Sample type*, leave *What you measured* on **Double-stranded DNA** and leave the dilution at 1 and the path length at 1 cm.
2. Under *Readings*, replace the example with one row: **DeNovix, 2.54, 1.276, 1.09** (name, A260, A280, A230). It shows about **127 ng/µL** and a 260/280 of **1.99**, with no purity warning.
3. Change *What you measured* to **RNA**: the same row reads **101.6 ng/µL**.
4. Back on DNA, use the row **Qubit, 0.8** (A260 only) (the Qubit's 40 ng/µL as absorbance): you should read **40 ng/µL**.
5. Under *Size, for molarity and copies*, type a length of **2100000** bp and set *Shape* to **Circular (plasmid)**: at 40 ng/µL the tool shows about **1.9 × 10⁷ copies/µL**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Deciding which instrument to trust** before a library preparation: compare an A260 reading with a dye-based one on a few samples and see the ratio.
- **Estimating carry-over**: if a spectrophotometer reads twice or more than a fluorometer, ask what else absorbs at 260 nm; RNase treatment is one test.
- **Converting a concentration to copies** for qPCR standards, with the [[nucleic-acid-concentration]] tool's molar mass.
- **Teaching** why a purity ratio is a screening hint, not a guarantee.
- **Planning dilutions** from either number and seeing how far apart the two answers land.

## The fine print

- Our mixing model assumes the Qubit measures only DNA and that the DNA is pure at 1.8. Both are simplifications; the paper itself does not say RNA is the cause.
- The paper's values are central values over 78 samples; real samples differ one by one, so the match is approximate.
- Medians do not mix like individual samples. A sample-by-sample test would need the raw data.
- This article describes measurement maths only. Check your own instrument's documentation for its settings and limits.
