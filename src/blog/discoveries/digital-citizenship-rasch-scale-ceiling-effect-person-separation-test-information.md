A questionnaire can be reliable and still be the wrong questionnaire. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Education* built a 13-item scale of digital citizenship for trainee civics teachers in Indonesia, analysed it with the **Rasch model**, and reported something awkward in its own results: the 243 respondents averaged **2.69 logits** of the trait while the items averaged **0.00**, so almost everyone agreed with almost everything. The authors call this a ceiling effect. The numbers they print are enough to work out exactly what it costs, and that is a job for the [[irt-item-analysis]] tool. For the other ways of looking at the same answers, the [[reliability-analysis]] tool handles alpha, omega and agreement, and the [[factor-analysis]] tool checks whether 13 items really measure one thing.

## The paper in brief

The Rasch model puts people and items on one scale measured in **logits**. An item's **measure** is the point on that scale where a person has a 50-50 chance of endorsing it; a person's **measure** is their position on the same ruler. Subtract one from the other and the difference fixes the probability of agreement. If the people sit far above the items, every answer is close to certain, and a certain answer carries almost no information about where exactly that person is.

The authors collected 243 responses to 13 five-point items across three universities, and ran a rating-scale Rasch analysis. The scale came out with one dominant factor, extremely high classical reliability (alpha 0.982) and no meaningful gender bias. Two warnings: item **CE4** misfits badly (infit 1.63, outfit 2.13), and the person distribution sits well above the items, with **person separation 1.94** and person reliability **0.79** against an item separation of 6.05. The middle "neutral" category also failed to work as a step on the ladder.

## Checked with the tools

The paper publishes its item measures but not its answers, so there are two different jobs here: **recomputation** of what its own published numbers imply, and a **simulation** to see the ceiling effect happen. We label each clearly.

**Step 1: the published table checks out.** The 13 item measures average **0.001** logits and have a standard deviation of **0.844** (dividing by 13; 0.878 if you divide by 12). The paper reports 0.00 and 0.84, so its table and its summary agree, and we now know it uses the population standard deviation. The person mean of 2.69 logits therefore sits **3.20 item standard deviations** above the middle of the scale.

**Step 2: separation and reliability are two names for one number.** A separation index G and a reliability R are tied together by R = G² / (1 + G²). The paper's G of 1.94 gives **R = 0.790**, exactly the 0.79 it reports; going the other way, 0.79 gives G = 1.940. The same identity turns the item separation of 6.05 into an item reliability of 0.973, against the 0.97 printed. So nothing is inconsistent: a person reliability of 0.79 simply *is* a separation of under two, which by the usual rule of thumb means the scale can tell apart about two or three levels of digital citizenship and no more.

**Step 3: where the information actually is.** The tool's information function says how much each item contributes at each ability, and the contributions add up. Figure 1 plots the total for the paper's 13 measures. It peaks at **2.80**, at −0.093 logits, and has dropped to **0.93**, a third of the peak, at the person mean of 2.69. One standard deviation above that, at 4.14 logits, only **0.28** is left.

**Step 4: what that means for one person's score.** The standard error of a measure is one over the square root of the information. For a right/wrong version of these 13 items it is **0.598 logits** at the item mean and **1.036** at the person mean: 1.7 times worse (Figure 2). A five-point item is not one right/wrong question, though; it has four steps, and if those steps were spread tightly around the item's measure it would carry roughly four times the information, halving the error to about 0.30 and 0.52 logits. The paper's own indices imply a person error of 1.45 × √(1 − 0.79) = **0.66 logits**, which sits between our two estimates, so the real five-point scale carries somewhere between one and four right/wrong questions' worth of information per item.

**Step 5: watch the ceiling happen (simulation).** We built two make-believe samples of 243 people answering 13 **right/wrong** items placed at the paper's difficulties, and fitted both with the tool's 1PL (Rasch) model. One sample's abilities are centred on the items; the other is centred at the paper's 2.69 logits, with the paper's standard deviation of 1.45 in both. These are our simulated answers, not the paper's.

- The tool recovers the item measures from both samples: every one of the 13 lands within **0.37 logits** (on-target sample) and **0.28 logits** (off-target sample) of where we put it, after the usual rescaling (Figure 3).
- Precision collapses. The on-target sample gives a root-mean-square person error of **0.431 logits**, a reliability of **0.772** and a separation of **1.84**. The sample placed where the paper's is gives **0.608**, **0.415** and **0.843**.
- **83 of the 243** off-target people answered every single item correctly, against 8 in the on-target sample. A perfect score contains no upper bound on ability at all: the tool still returns a score for those people, but it is pulled towards the middle and carries the widest error in the table.

## How we made the graphs

Everything is in `scripts/blog-figures/digital-citizenship-rasch-scale-ceiling-effect-person-separation-test-information.mjs`. It uses three functions from `src/lib/sci/irt.ts`, the engine behind the [[irt-item-analysis]] tool:

- `info({ a, b }, theta)` for every point of figures 1 and 2 (summed over the 13 items, with a = 1 as the Rasch model requires);
- `icc({ a, b }, theta)` to draw the simulated answers in step 5;
- `fitIrt(U, '1pl')` for the marginal-maximum-likelihood fit, which also returns each person's EAP score and its standard error.

The 13 item measures, the person mean and SD, the separation and reliability indices and the counts all come from the paper. The right/wrong rebuild, the random draws and the "four times the information" allowance for a five-point item are **our assumptions**; the paper's model is a rating-scale Rasch model whose category thresholds we do not have. The reliability we quote for the simulations is the standard one, 1 − (mean squared error ÷ observed variance of the scores).

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/irt-item-analysis.mjs` on every change. It pins the fitter against an independent Python fit (SciPy BFGS on the marginal likelihood, 61-point Gauss-Hermite quadrature, standard errors from a finite-difference Hessian) on 300 people and 8 simulated items:

- the 1PL log-likelihood must be −1382.558853 and the 2PL −1371.781240, both to 1e-6;
- every difficulty and discrimination must match SciPy to 1e-5 (for example a common slope of 1.107756 and difficulties from −1.0757 to 1.7004 in the 1PL), and every standard error to one part in 10,000;
- the likelihood-ratio test between the two models must have 7 degrees of freedom and a p-value under 0.01;
- the quadrature must integrate the normal moments to 1e-8 as far as the eighth, and an item everyone answered correctly must be refused with a message that says so;
- new with this article: the 13 published item measures must give a test information of 2.797562 at 0 logits, 0.932287 at 2.69 and 0.280170 at 4.14, and the separation identity must turn 1.94 into 0.790075 and 6.05 into 0.973406.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Judge targeting before you trust a reliability figure.** Paste your pilot answers, press 1PL, and compare the *Information* curve with where your respondents actually score, as in Figure 1.
- **Turn a published item table into a planning tool.** With item measures alone you can compute the information and the standard error at any ability, and decide how many items you need and how hard they should be.
- **Check a paper's indices against each other.** Separation and reliability must satisfy R = G²/(1+G²); if they do not, one of them is mistyped.
- **Compare 1PL with 2PL.** The tool's likelihood-ratio test says whether letting items differ in discrimination is worth the extra parameters, which is often what a "misfitting item" really means.
- **Teach what a logit is.** Students can move one item's difficulty, watch the information curve shift and see a reliability figure move with it.

## The fine print

- The paper's scale is five-point and analysed with a rating-scale Rasch model. The tool fits right/wrong (1PL and 2PL) data, so our rebuild is a dichotomous stand-in: the shape of the targeting argument carries over, the absolute information does not.
- Steps 3 to 5 use the paper's published item measures as if they were exact. Each carries a standard error of 0.09 or 0.10 logits.
- The simulated samples are ours. They show what a ceiling effect does to precision; they are not the paper's respondents and cannot confirm or deny its particular findings about item CE4 or the neutral category.
- Our "four times the information" allowance for a five-point item assumes its four steps sit close together. Widely spread thresholds flatten the curve instead.
- Classical alpha of 0.982 on 13 items alongside a Rasch person reliability of 0.79 is not a contradiction; the paper says so too. Alpha rewards items that repeat each other, which a ceiling effect encourages.
