Dating an ice core gets hard once the annual layers are too thin to count. One way out is to find the same signal in a core that is already well dated and tie the two together. A 2026 paper in *Climate of the Past* does that for the Alto dell'Ortles glacier in the Eastern Alps: it matches the core's **lead (Pb) record**, the fallout of ancient mining and smelting, against an Arctic record from Severnaya Zemlya, sets 11 tie points between 175 BCE and 1755 CE, and reports that after synchronisation the two records correlate at r = 0.44, where before they correlated at 0.26. Those are exactly the numbers fibub's [[proxy-stacker]] is built to scrutinise, so we rebuilt the test on simulated records that behave like theirs. For the age model itself, see the [[age-depth-model]]; for trends in a single series, the [[trend-analyser]].

## The paper in brief

The Alto dell'Ortles core reaches about 7,000 years back in its deepest metres, where layer counting fails. The authors use lead, corrected for dust with a Pb/Rb ratio, as the matching signal, because historical lead pollution is hemispheric: Roman mining, the medieval revival and early industry all show up in both the Alps and the Arctic.

They align 60 to 73 m of the Alpine core to the Arctic chronology with 11 tie points, after averaging both records to **10-year resolution**. The age corrections are small in recent centuries (10 to 20 years back to 1600 CE, about 40 years at 1550 CE) and very large deeper down (120 to 270 years between 1250 and 250 CE, and 240 to 280 years the other way around the start of the Common Era). Their evidence that the new chronology is better is the correlation: **0.26 before, 0.44 after** over the full record, and 0.09 before against 0.46 after for the part older than 1750 CE. Seven of the 11 tie points agree with the new chronology within one standard deviation and the other four within two. For the 20th century, where annual layers can be counted independently, the two records correlate at 0.61.

## Checked with the tools

We cannot download their records, and we would not copy their data if we could. What we can do is **simulate** two decadal lead records that behave like theirs and run the paper's own test on them, which tells you what a correlation of 0.44 is and is not worth. The shared history is a made-up curve (three broad rises plus a slowly varying component), each record gets its own red noise, and the noise level was chosen so that the aligned pair correlates at 0.444, the paper's value. The window is the paper's: 175 BCE to 1755 CE in 10-year steps, 194 points.

**Result 1: the correlation survives the correction that most papers skip.** Decadal proxy records are autocorrelated: one decade resembles the next, so 194 points do not carry 194 independent pieces of information. The tool measures the lag-1 autocorrelation of each record (0.675 and 0.685 here) and reduces the sample size accordingly, following Bretherton et al. (1999): **71.3 effective points instead of 194**. The p-value for r = 0.444 moves from 8.6 × 10⁻¹¹ to 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ — six orders of magnitude weaker, and still comfortably below the paper's stated p < 0.001. So the headline claim holds, with a much smaller margin than the raw number suggests.

**Result 2: the improvement from 0.26 to 0.44 is the right kind of evidence, but it is weaker in the old part of the record.** We applied age shifts of the sizes the paper reports to the simulated Arctic record and re-ran everything. Over the full window the correlation falls from 0.444 to 0.366, a modest drop, because after 1600 CE the shifts are only 10 to 20 years and the large industrial rise still lines up. Over the part older than 1250 CE, where the shifts are 120 to 280 years, the fall is decisive: **0.234 aligned, 0.156 shifted**, and the autocorrelation-corrected p goes from 0.04 to 0.26. In other words, a global correlation is dominated by the youngest, best-dated centuries; the ancient end of the record is where the tie points have to do the work, and where a correlation on its own proves little. That is precisely why the paper uses 11 individually assessed tie points rather than one number.

**Result 3: how precisely can a correlation find an alignment?** The tool's lag scan slides one record past the other and recomputes r at every offset.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/ice-core-lead-record-lag-scan-synchronisation.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `interpolate`, `zscore`, `stack`, `bootstrap`, `correlate`, `pearson`, `lag1`, `lagScan` and the seeded `rng` from `src/lib/sci/proxystack.ts`, the maths behind the [[proxy-stacker]].

From the paper: the window (175 BCE to 1755 CE), the 10-year resolution, the correlations, the tie-point count and the sizes of the age shifts. **Ours:** the shared lead history, the red-noise model (lag-1 autocorrelation 0.86 for the shared component and 0.5 for each core's noise), the noise amplitude tuned so that the aligned pair reaches r = 0.444, and the knot positions of the shift history. The records are **simulated**; none of the paper's measurements appear here.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/proxy-stacker.mjs`, which pins the stacker against NumPy, pandas and SciPy:

- Pearson r, the lag-1 autocorrelations, the Bretherton effective sample size, the p-values and the whole lag scan must match `scipy.stats.pearsonr`, `pandas.Series.shift` and `scipy.stats.t` to 1 × 10⁻⁹ on the built-in example, both on a shared age grid and on a wider grid with gaps.
- Interpolation must refuse to bridge a gap longer than the limit you set, and z-scores must use only the window you choose.
- The bootstrap envelope must match `numpy.nanpercentile` for the same resample indices, and a stack of identical records must have a flat envelope.
- New with this article: the simulated decadal pair above must give r = 0.4443 with 194 points, lag-1 autocorrelations of 0.675 and 0.685, an effective sample size of 71.3, a corrected p of 1.00 × 10⁻⁴ against a naive 8.6 × 10⁻¹¹, and a lag-scan peak at −1 step.

## Check it yourself

You can run the paper's test on your own two records in the browser. On the [[proxy-stacker]] page:

1. Press **Use the example** to see the format: `record, age, value`, one sample per row. Replace it with your two records (paste or **Open files**).
2. Set *Age grid from* **-175** *to* **1755** *step* **10**, and *Age unit (for labels)* to **years CE**.
3. Set the *z-score window from* **-175** *to* **1755** so both records are scaled over the same span, and *Stack* to **Mean**.
4. Set *Envelope* to **±1 SD of the records** while you have only two records, and *Records needed at an age* to **2**.
5. Set *Lag scan up to (± grid steps)* to **12**, which is ±120 years at this step, and pick your two records as *Record A* and *Record B*.
6. In *Correlations between records* read r, n, the lag-1 autocorrelations, the effective sample size and both p-values. The corrected one is the one to quote.
7. In *Lag scan*, check that the peak is at zero offset. If it is not, your age models disagree by about that much.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Quote the honest p-value.** Any correlation between two proxy records needs an effective sample size; the tool gives it in the same table as r, with the lag-1 autocorrelations it used.
- **Test an age model by sliding it.** If the lag scan peaks away from zero, the two chronologies differ by that offset over the window you chose.
- **Decide how much precision a correlation can give you** before you promise it: run the scan on records like yours and see how fast r falls away from the peak.
- **Compare windows.** A correlation over the whole record can hide a weak old half, as it does here; recompute over the part that matters.
- **Build a stack with an honest envelope** (±1 SD for a few records, bootstrap for many), and carry the result into an age model with the [[age-depth-model]].

## The fine print

- The records here are **simulated**, not the paper's data. They are built to share its resolution, window and correlation, nothing more. A real pair of Pb records has trends, volcanic spikes and uneven sampling that change the numbers.
- Our age shifts follow the sizes the paper reports, but we chose where the shift history bends; real dating errors are not smooth.
- The Bretherton correction assumes AR(1) autocorrelation. Records with long memory need a stronger correction, so our corrected p-values are optimistic rather than pessimistic.
- The paper's tie-point assessment, its dust correction and its 20th-century annual-layer check are outside what a stacking tool can test.
- A high correlation between two proxy records never proves they record the same cause; here the shared cause (hemispheric lead pollution) is argued from history, not from statistics.
