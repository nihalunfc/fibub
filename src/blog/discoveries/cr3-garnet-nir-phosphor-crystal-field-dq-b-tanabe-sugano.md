Pocket food scanners and some medical sensors need lamps that shine in the **near infrared**, just beyond red. One way to make them is a blue LED coated with a phosphor that turns blue light into infrared, and chromium (Cr³⁺) in an oxide crystal is the favourite ingredient. Whether Cr³⁺ gives a broad infrared glow or a narrow deep-red line depends on its **crystal field**, usually reported as one number, Dq/B. A 2024 paper in *Molecules* made a new garnet phosphor and reported Dq/B = 1.99. This article recomputes that number from the paper's own excitation peaks with the [[tanabe-sugano]], and shows how much it depends on which peak goes in. For the orbital picture behind it, try the [[crystal-field-calculator]]; to see where the glow sits against a warm lamp's spectrum, the [[blackbody-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The author made Ca₃Mg₂ZrGe₃O₁₂ (a garnet, the same crystal family as many LED phosphors) with 1 % of chromium and measured how it absorbs and emits. Under blue light it glows from 600 to 900 nm, peaking at **758 nm**, with a width of 89 nm. The excitation spectrum, which shows which colours make it glow, has two bands at **467 nm** and **657 nm**, assigned to the ⁴A₂ → ⁴T₁ and ⁴A₂ → ⁴T₂ transitions of Cr³⁺ (d³). The phosphor converts 68.7 % of absorbed photons, and, unusually, glows brighter when warm: about 133 % of its room-temperature output at 150 °C and still 92 % at 250 °C.

The crystal-field analysis is the core of the explanation. For Cr³⁺, **Dq** is the ligand-field strength (10Dq = Δ, the octahedral splitting) and the **Racah B** measures how strongly the d electrons repel. When Dq/B is low, the broad ⁴T₂ state is the lowest excited state and the emission is broad; when it is high, the sharp ²E state is lowest and the emission is a narrow line near 690 nm. The paper states **Dq = 1319 cm⁻¹, B = 663 cm⁻¹ and Dq/B = 1.99**, below the commonly quoted boundary of 2.3, so a **weak crystal field** that explains the broad infrared glow.

## Checked with the tools

The method the paper names takes Dq from the ⁴T₂ band (Dq = ν1 / 10) and B from the gap to the ⁴T₁ band. We put the two excitation peaks in: 657 nm is 15,221 cm⁻¹ (ν1) and 467 nm is 21,413 cm⁻¹ (ν2); a wavenumber in cm⁻¹ is 10,000,000 divided by the wavelength in nm.

**Step 1: the tool's fit.** For d³ the [[tanabe-sugano]] gives **Dq = 1522 cm⁻¹, B = 622.9 cm⁻¹ and Dq/B = 2.44**. The paper's own formula, 15(x − 8)/(x² − 10x) with x = (ν2 − ν1)/Dq, gives the same 2.444, and the textbook closed form for B gives the same 622.9 cm⁻¹: three routes, one answer. The tool also predicts the third band, ⁴A₂ → ⁴T₁(P), at 33,592 cm⁻¹ (298 nm), in the ultraviolet.

**Step 2: where the paper's numbers come from.** The stated Dq of 1319 cm⁻¹ is exactly what the **758 nm emission peak** gives (13,193 cm⁻¹ ÷ 10 = 1319.3), not the 657 nm excitation peak. The emission sits 2028 cm⁻¹ lower because the excited ion relaxes before it glows (the Stokes shift), so emission is not the right input for Dq. But using the emission peak with 467 nm does not give the stated B either: the tool returns B = 1168 cm⁻¹ and Dq/B = 1.13, and a B larger than the free ion's 918 cm⁻¹ is impossible in a crystal. Working backwards, the stated Dq and B together need ν2 at 19,385 cm⁻¹ (516 nm), a wavelength the paper does not mention. We could not find a pair of the paper's peaks that gives 1319, 663 and 1.99 together.

|---|---|---|
| Dq | 1319 cm⁻¹ | 1522 cm⁻¹ |
| Racah B | 663 cm⁻¹ | 622.9 cm⁻¹ |
| Dq/B | 1.99 | 2.44 |
| β = B / free-ion B (918 cm⁻¹) | 0.72 | 0.68 |
| ⁴T₁(P) band | not given | 298 nm (predicted) |

**Step 3: does it change the story?** Figure 1 shows the d³ diagram. With the usual C/B of 4.5 (**our assumption**; the paper gives no C), the ²E level crosses ⁴T₂ at Dq/B = 2.07; the boundary moves with C/B (2.17 at 4.8, 2.23 at 5.0), and the familiar "2.3" corresponds to a C/B above 5. The tool's 2.44 sits on the **strong-field** side of all of these, where a narrow ²E line would be expected, yet the phosphor glows broadly. That is not a contradiction in practice: the excitation peak lies above the relaxed ⁴T₂ energy, so peak-based Dq/B overstates the field, and many broad-emitting Cr³⁺ phosphors sit near the boundary, where ⁴T₂ and ²E mix at room temperature. The honest summary is "intermediate field, close to the crossover", not "clearly weak".

## How we made the graphs

The charts and numbers come from `scripts/blog-figures/cr3-garnet-nir-phosphor-crystal-field-dq-b-tanabe-sugano.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `fitTwoBands` (Δ and B from two bands), `levels` and `diagram` (the full d³ energy matrix) from `src/lib/sci/tanabe.ts`, the maths behind the [[tanabe-sugano]]. The paper's formula and the textbook closed form are recomputed in the script as cross-checks.

The 467, 657 and 758 nm peaks and the stated Dq, B and Dq/B come from the paper's text. C/B = 4.5 is **our assumption** and affects only the ²E crossover, not Dq or B. The free-ion B of 918 cm⁻¹ is a textbook value. This is a **recomputation**; we did not have the spectra.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/tanabe-sugano.mjs`:

- Every level of d² to d⁸ at Δ/B = 7.3 and 25 must match an independent NumPy calculation to 10⁻⁸ B.
- The d³ and d⁸ spin-allowed bands must match the closed form 7.5B + 15Dq ∓ ½√(225B² + 100Dq² − 180B·Dq) at four field strengths, and the two-band fit must match B = (2ν1² + ν2² − 3ν1ν2)/(15ν2 − 27ν1) on four test spectra.
- New with this article: 657 and 467 nm for d³ must give B = 622.9 cm⁻¹ and Dq/B = 2.444, and at C/B = 4.5 the ²E level must cross ⁴T₂ at Δ/B = 20.74.

## Check it yourself

On the [[tanabe-sugano]]:

2. In *Δ and B from a spectrum*, type **15221** in *ν1, lowest band (cm⁻¹)* and **21413** in *ν2, second band (cm⁻¹)*.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a phosphor paper's Dq/B** from its stated excitation peaks in under a minute, with the [[tanabe-sugano]].
- **Report the crossover with its C/B**: the ²E/⁴T₂ boundary moves from Dq/B 1.92 at C/B 4.0 to 2.23 at 5.0, so say which you used.
- **Catch wrong inputs**: a β above 1, or a predicted ⁴T₁(P) band where none is seen, points to a mis-assigned peak.
- **Design new hosts**: aim for a Δ/B below the crossover for broadband infrared, or above it for narrow red lines, and sketch the d-orbital splitting with the [[crystal-field-calculator]].
- **Teach** how excitation, emission and the Stokes shift fit together on one Tanabe–Sugano diagram.

## The fine print

- We used only the numbers in the paper's text. Band peaks read from a broad, overlapping excitation spectrum can shift by several nanometres, and a fit of the whole band shape would be better.
- The paper's abstract gives the blue excitation as 457 nm and the main text as 467 nm; we used 467 nm. With 457 nm (21,882 cm⁻¹) the tool gives Dq/B 2.21, still not 1.99.
- C/B = 4.5 is our assumption. The tool treats a perfect octahedron; real Cr³⁺ sites in garnets are slightly distorted.
- Nothing here questions the paper's measured brightness, efficiency or heat stability, which we did not check.
