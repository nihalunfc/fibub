A tilted layer does not look equally steep from every side. Cut through it along the dip and you see its full slope; cut along the strike and it seems to lie flat. That simple fact decides how seismic lines across a steeply tilted ore body should be read. A 2026 paper in *Solid Earth* describes seismic surveys at the Per Geijer iron deposit near Kiruna, in northern Sweden, where the mineralisation dips east at 50 to 60 degrees and two of the 2D lines run almost along strike. We use the numbers to show what an **apparent dip** is and how it changes depth, thickness and drilling plans. For the sums, use the [[apparent-dip]] tool; to draw the result, the [[cross-section-profile]]; to plot the planes, the [[stereonet]].

## The paper in brief

The authors describe 2D reflection profiles, downhole **vertical seismic profiles** (VSP: a source at the surface and sensors down a drill hole) in two holes, and a 3D survey of about 13 km². The paper reports that reflections line up with the iron mineralisation and continue beyond the drilling, and that they come mainly from the **density** contrast between ore and host rock, not from a contrast in wave speed. A strong reflection from a steep structure outside the survey area is also noted.

The geometry facts we use: the ore bodies are roughly disc-shaped, strike about north-south and dip 50 to 60 degrees to the east, as do the host volcanic units. The Kiirunavaara body is about 4 km long and on average 80 m thick, thickening to about 200 m in the north. Two 2D lines run north-south, almost parallel to strike, so their reflections appear nearly flat, while a profile that crosses the strike shows steep apparent dips. Reflections on the third line are strongest at a cross-dip of 30 degrees to the east. A major part of the mineralisation is deeper than 500 m, and one deep reflection sits near 1.5 km depth.

## Checked with the tools

The paper gives a range of dips rather than one value, and no table of apparent dips, so we worked with a plane that **strikes 000 and dips 55 degrees east** (the middle of the range; our assumption), with 50 and 60 degrees as the limits. Everything below was computed with the Apparent dip tool's maths.

**Step 1: the same plane from different directions.** A section running east shows the true dip, 55 degrees. A section running north shows none. In between, the apparent dip follows tan(apparent) = tan(true) × sin(angle from strike). Figure 1 plots the whole range for 50, 55 and 60 degrees. A line only 5 degrees away from strike shows 7.1 degrees; 10 degrees away shows 13.9 degrees; 20 degrees away, 26.0 degrees. So a "nearly parallel" north-south line can see a gently dipping reflector, and a small heading error changes the apparent dip a lot. Looking the other way, a section heading 190 degrees would see the body rising at 13.9 degrees.

**Step 2: going back to the true dip.** If the strike is known, one apparent dip is enough: the 26.03 degrees seen towards 020 gives back exactly 55 degrees. Without the strike, two apparent dips from two sections do the same job: 26.03 degrees towards 020 and 55 degrees towards 090 give a plane striking 000 and dipping 55 degrees (the two lines are 57.6 degrees apart, a good angle; sections nearly parallel give a poorly determined plane).

**Step 3: true and vertical thickness.** Take the average thickness of 80 m. Walking across the strike on flat ground, you would cross a horizontal width of 97.7 m (80 ÷ sin 55°). A vertical drill hole, however, would cut a longer piece of ore: the **vertical thickness** is 80 ÷ cos 55° = 139.5 m. The tool gives 80.03 m true and 139.53 m vertical thickness for a 97.7 m horizontal width. Quoting "thickness" without saying which kind is a common source of confusion between geologists and miners.

**Step 4: where a hole meets the body.** Put a collar 1000 m east of the line where the plane would reach the surface (our assumption, flat ground). A vertical hole reaches the 55 degree plane at 1428 m. For the 50 and 60 degree limits it is 1192 m and 1732 m: a ten degree uncertainty in dip moves the target by more than half a kilometre. A hole drilled at 70 degrees towards the west, across the dip, hits it after 1000 m along the hole (940 m deep) and cuts 97.7 m of ore for an 80 m thick body; the same hole drilled towards the east, with the dip, would take 3165 m (2974 m deep) and cut 309 m, because it runs almost parallel to the body (angle 15 degrees). The paper notes that its VSP hole length of about 1700 m is along the hole, not true vertical depth, which is the same kind of difference.

## How we made the graphs

The script `scripts/blog-figures/kiruna-orebody-seismic-profile-apparent-dip-strike.mjs` calls `apparentDip`, `trueDipFromApparent`, `planeFromTwoApparent`, `thickness` and `borehole` from `src/lib/sci/apparentdip.ts`, the maths of the [[apparent-dip]] tool.

The range of dips (50 to 60 degrees), the north-south strike and the 80 m average thickness come from the paper's text. The exact strike of 000, the mid-value 55 degrees, flat ground and the 1000 m collar distance are **our assumptions**. No figure or data table from the paper is used.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/apparent-dip.mjs` on every change. It compares random cases for each function with an independent NumPy implementation: apparent dip from the section-plane intersection, true dip from two apparent dips, thickness from a triple product, borehole depth from a 3 × 3 solve and the three-point problem (all matched to 1e-7 or better). It also pins hand-worked cases such as strike 030, dip 30 seen towards 075 (22.21 degrees). For this article we added strike 000 / dip 55 giving 7.10, 13.93, 26.03 and 55.00 degrees towards 005, 010, 020 and 090, and a vertical hole 1000 m from the outcrop line meeting the body at 1428.15 m and cutting 139.48 m of an 80 m body.

## Check it yourself

On the [[apparent-dip]] page:

1. Under *The bed*, set *Typed as* to **Strike, dip (right-hand rule)**, *Strike* to **000** and *Dip* to **55**.
2. Under *Apparent dip*, type **020** in *Direction of the section or cliff face*. It shows about **26.03°** towards 020. Type **190** and it shows the bed rising at about **13.93°**.
3. Under *True thickness from outcrop width*, type **97.7** in *Outcrop width*, choose *Width measured* **horizontally (map)**, *Direction of the traverse* **090** and *Ground slope* **0**. You should read a true thickness of about **80.03 m** and a vertical thickness of about **139.53 m**.
4. Under *Depth to the bed in a borehole*, type **1000** for the distance, **090** for the direction, **0** for the collar height, **000** and **90** for the hole direction and plunge, and **80** for the true thickness. It should read about **1428.15 m** down, and **139.48 m** of core.
5. Under *True dip from two apparent dips*, type **020** and **26.03**, then **090** and **55**. It should return strike about **000** and dip **55.0°**.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Planning seismic lines:** work out how a heading error against strike turns a steep body into an apparent dip you might misread as a gentle one.
- **Reading old sections:** convert an apparent dip shown on a section that is not perpendicular to strike back to a true dip, before adding it to a model.
- **Drill planning:** compare vertical and inclined holes, depth to target and the core length for a known thickness, and see how the dip range spreads the depth.
- **Reporting thickness clearly:** state whether a number is true, vertical or horizontal; the tool gives all three.
- **Teaching:** the whole of strike, dip and apparent dip fits on one chart; pair it with a stereonet of the plane.

## The fine print

- Our plane strikes exactly 000 and dips exactly 55 degrees on flat ground. Real ore bodies curve, thicken and split, and the paper's bodies are disc-shaped, not planes.
- The paper does not give numeric apparent dips, so the apparent-dip values here are **our predictions** from its stated dip and strike, not its measurements.
- A reflection's position in a 2D section also depends on out-of-plane effects (the paper mentions a cross-dip of 30 degrees on one line); our sums treat only the geometry of the plane.
- Depth values assume straight holes and a planar contact.
