A fruit fly weighs about a milligram, and 25 of them in a glass tube breathe so little that the air leaving the tube is barely different from the air going in. A 2026 study in *eLife* did exactly that measurement around the clock, and reported **respiratory quotients** (the CO₂ an animal gives off divided by the O₂ it takes in) from 0.94 to 1.20 in different sleep mutants. Those numbers sound small and harmless, but each one hides a measurement near the edge of what gas analysers can do, and each one changes how many joules a millilitre of oxygen is worth. This article rebuilds the gas readings behind them with the [[respirometry-calculator]], and shows how far off you land if you skip the nitrogen correction. For the next steps, turn oxygen uptake into blood flow with the [[fick-cardiac-output]] and scale metabolic rate with body size using the [[allometry-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors put wild-type flies and three sleep mutants (*per01*, which has no working clock; *fumin*, which sleeps very little; and *sleepless*, which sleeps even less) into a flow-through respirometry system, 25 flies per chamber, with air pulled through at **25 mL per minute**. They recorded O₂ uptake and CO₂ output through whole days, in a light-dark cycle and in constant darkness, and measured metabolites from the same flies.

Two results matter here. First, the **24-hour average respiratory quotient** differed between genotypes: **1.20** for wild type in light-dark, **1.19** in constant dark, **1.20** for *per01*, **1.09** for *fumin* and **0.94** for *sleepless*. Second, the rhythms in CO₂ output were clear in every line, while the oxygen rhythm was weaker, and in *fumin* the paper found no significant V̇O₂ rhythm at all. The authors read the low RQ of *sleepless* as a shift towards burning fat, and the values above 1 in wild type as carbohydrate use with some conversion of sugar into fat.

## Checked with the tools

The paper does not print its gas readings, so we worked backwards. Flow-through respirometry has an exact mass balance: nitrogen passes through the chamber unchanged, so the flow on the side you did not measure follows from the nitrogen fractions, and the gas exchange follows from the flows and the four gas fractions. The respirometry calculator solves that balance forwards. We inverted it numerically, asking: **what excurrent readings would give these respiratory quotients at 25 mL per minute?** The incurrent air is dry outside air at 20.95 % O₂ and 0.04 % CO₂ (**our assumption**; the paper does not state it), and the whole-chamber oxygen uptake is 0.00125 mL/min, which is 25 flies at 3 µL O₂ per fly per hour (**our assumption**, a plain mid-range value for adult *Drosophila*).

**Step 1: the signal is tiny in oxygen and large in carbon dioxide.** At that uptake and flow, the air leaving the chamber is short of **52.1 ppm of O₂** and carries **60.0 ppm more CO₂** at RQ 1.20 (49.4 ppm and 47.0 ppm at RQ 0.94). Those two numbers look alike, but they are not alike at all as measurements. The O₂ reading has to change by **0.0249 % of itself**, because it sits on a background of 209,500 ppm. The CO₂ reading changes by **15.0 % of itself**, because its background is only 400 ppm. The carbon dioxide channel has about **600 times** the relative signal of the oxygen channel at these rates.

That single ratio explains the paper's own observation that CO₂ rhythms came out cleanly while oxygen rhythms were weaker and sometimes absent. It is not biology; it is the arithmetic of a dilute signal. Anyone planning a small-animal respirometry run can read Figure 1 the other way: pick the smallest O₂ change your analyser resolves, and it tells you the lowest flow rate you can get away with.

**Step 2: the nitrogen (Haldane) correction, and why it changes sign at RQ 1.** When an animal takes up more O₂ than it gives out CO₂, the gas leaving the chamber is slightly less than the gas going in; above RQ 1 it is slightly more. A common shortcut is to multiply the one measured flow by both gas differences. Feeding our rebuilt readings back through the tool shows what that costs:

| Line in the paper | RQ in the paper | RQ the shortcut gives | Error in RQ | Error in V̇O₂ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wild type, light-dark | 1.20 | 1.1517 | −4.03 % | +4.19 % |
| Wild type, constant dark | 1.19 | 1.1444 | −3.83 % | +3.98 % |
| *per01* | 1.20 | 1.1517 | −4.03 % | +4.19 % |
| *fumin* | 1.09 | 1.0698 | −1.85 % | +1.89 % |
| *sleepless* | 0.94 | 0.9520 | +1.28 % | −1.26 % |

The errors are a few per cent, they run in opposite directions above and below RQ 1, and they are largest exactly where the paper's wild-type values sit. A difference of 0.26 in RQ between wild type and *sleepless* is far bigger than this, so the paper's conclusion is safe; but a shortcut like this would be enough to erase a difference of 0.05 between two treatments. Figure 2 shows the error across the whole range.

**Step 3: the same oxygen is worth different joules.** Respiratory quotient is not only a fuel label; it sets the exchange rate between oxygen and energy. With the Lighton oxycalorific equivalent (16 + 5.164 × RQ joules per mL O₂), our fixed uptake of 0.00125 mL/min comes to **462.4 µW at RQ 1.20** and **434.5 µW at RQ 0.94**, a spread of **6.44 %** (5.77 % with Weir's equation). So two chambers with identical oxygen uptake but the paper's two extreme RQs differ by about six per cent in heat production. Converted to a day, that is 0.03995 against 0.03754 kJ per chamber of 25 flies.

| Check | Paper says | Tool gives (our rebuilt readings) |
|---|---|---|
| RQ, wild type light-dark | 1.20 | 1.200 from 20.94479 % excurrent O₂ and 0.04600 % CO₂ |
| RQ, *sleepless* | 0.94 | 0.939 from 20.94506 % and 0.04470 % |
| Which gas carries the rhythm | CO₂ clear, V̇O₂ weak | CO₂ signal 603 times larger in relative terms |
| Joules per mL O₂ (Lighton) | not stated | 22.197 at RQ 1.20, 20.854 at RQ 0.94 |

A last point worth saying plainly: an RQ above 1 cannot come from burning any ordinary fuel. Carbohydrate gives 1.0, fat about 0.7, protein about 0.8. Values of 1.19 and 1.20 need carbon dioxide from somewhere else, and the usual explanation, which the paper gives too, is net conversion of sugar into fat, which releases CO₂ without using O₂. The tool will happily take an RQ of 1.2; the biology is the part that needs the argument.

## How we made the graphs

Both figures and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/drosophila-respiratory-quotient-flow-through-respirometry.mjs` in fibub's public code. It imports `openBoth`, `joulesPerMl`, `watts` and `kjPerDay` from `src/lib/sci/respiro.ts`, the maths behind the [[respirometry-calculator]].

- `openBoth` is the full flow-through balance with the nitrogen transformation. The "shortcut" row in the table is one measured flow times each gas difference, written out in the script, not a tool option.
- The rebuilt excurrent readings come from inverting `openBoth` by fixed-point iteration on the excurrent flow, which converges in a few steps.
- Inputs from the paper: the flow of 25 mL/min, 25 flies per chamber, and the five respiratory quotients. **Our assumptions:** incurrent air of 20.95 % O₂ and 0.04 % CO₂, a whole-chamber oxygen uptake of 0.00125 mL/min, and dry gas (the paper re-humidified its air, so a real set-up would scrub water before the analysers).
- Nothing here is the paper's data. It is a reconstruction of what readings would produce the paper's stated quotients.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/respirometry-calculator.mjs`, which pins the flow-through equations against an independent NumPy solution of the N₂, O₂ and CO₂ mass balances:

- With 500 mL/min measured before the chamber, incurrent air of 20.94 % O₂ and 0.04 % CO₂ and excurrent 20.31 % and 0.52 %, the tool must give V̇O₂ 3.342402425 mL/min, V̇CO₂ 2.395073892, RQ 0.7165725688 and an excurrent flow of 499.0526715 mL/min.
- With the same readings but the flow measured after the chamber, V̇O₂ 3.348747153 and an incurrent flow of 500.9491268 mL/min: the two sides differ, which is the Haldane correction itself.
- The energy routes are pinned too: 4 mL O₂/min with 3.3 mL CO₂/min is 1.353788987 W by Weir and 1.350686667 W by the Lighton equivalent.
- A closed-chamber slope and R² are checked against SciPy's `linregress`, and the water-vapour equation against IAPWS values to within 0.1 %.
- New with this article: the rebuilt wild-type reading (25 mL/min incurrent, 20.95 % and 0.04 % in, 20.94479 % and 0.046 % out) must give V̇O₂ 0.00125 mL/min, RQ 1.2000 and an excurrent flow of 25.0002 mL/min, and the one-flow shortcut on the same numbers must give RQ 1.1516.

## Check it yourself

On the [[respirometry-calculator]] page, with **Flow-through, O₂ and CO₂** selected:

1. Set *Flow rate (mL/min)* to **25**, *Flow measured* to **Before the chamber (incurrent)** and *Flow is* to **Already at STPD (mass flow meter)**.
2. Type *Incurrent O₂ (%)* **20.95**, *Incurrent CO₂ (%)* **0.04**, *Excurrent O₂ (%)* **20.94479**, *Excurrent CO₂ (%)* **0.046**.
3. Under *Energy and body mass*, set *Energy from O₂* to **Lighton: 16 + 5.164 × RQ J/mL** and leave *Body mass* empty.
4. You should see **V̇O₂ 0.00125 mL/min**, **V̇CO₂ 0.0015 mL/min** and **RQ 1.200**, with an excurrent flow of **25.0002 mL/min** and about **462 µW** (0.03996 kJ/day). Switching *Energy from O₂* to **Weir (1949)** gives about 459 µW.
5. Now change *Excurrent O₂ (%)* to **20.94506** and *Excurrent CO₂ (%)* to **0.0447**: RQ falls to **0.939**, the *sleepless* value, and the power drops to about 435 µW although the oxygen uptake barely moved.
6. To see the Haldane correction on its own, switch *Flow measured* to **After the chamber (excurrent)** with the first set of readings and watch V̇O₂ and the reported incurrent flow shift.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Size a respirometry rig before building it**: enter your intended flow and the smallest gas change your analyser resolves, and read off the metabolic rate you can actually detect.
- **Decide which gas to trust**: for very small animals the CO₂ channel usually carries far more relative signal, as Figure 1 shows, so an apparent lack of an O₂ rhythm may be an analyser limit, not biology.
- **Re-derive a paper's energy numbers**: convert a published V̇O₂ and RQ into watts and kJ per day with the Weir or Lighton route, and see how much the choice matters (here, under 1 %).
- **Check a reported RQ for plausibility**: anything above 1 or below about 0.7 needs an explanation beyond ordinary fuel use.
- **Teach the Haldane transformation**: switch *Flow measured* between the two sides and watch V̇O₂ move by the few per cent that Figure 2 predicts.

## The fine print

- Our excurrent readings are a **reconstruction**, not the paper's measurements, which are not printed in the article.
- The whole-chamber oxygen uptake of 0.00125 mL/min and the incurrent air composition are our assumptions. The per-cent errors in Step 2 depend hardly at all on them; the ppm signals in Step 1 scale directly with the uptake.
- The paper's system re-humidified its air. We treated the gas as dry, as a real set-up does by scrubbing water before the analysers.
- This is general science education. The respirometry calculator is for study and teaching, not for clinical use.
