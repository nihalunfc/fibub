Gene therapies delivered by adeno-associated virus work once: the immune system remembers the capsid, and killer (CD8) T cells that recognise short pieces of it can clear the treated cells. Knowing exactly which pieces those are tells you what to watch for in a trial. A 2025 study in *Frontiers in Immunology* mapped them on the AAV9 capsid two ways: a library of 182 overlapping peptides covering the whole VP1 protein, and 41 peptides that the cells themselves had displayed, found by mass spectrometry and screened in pools. This article rebuilds both designs with the [[peptide-overlap-designer]], weighs the winning epitope with the [[peptide-mass-calculator]], and shows with the tool's own deconvolution where pooled screens stop giving a single answer. The capsid sequence itself can be read with the [[protein-calculator]].

## The paper in brief

The authors took blood from healthy donors, about half of them carrying antibodies against AAV9 from a past natural infection. Straight from the blood, neither peptide set detected capsid-specific T cells: the cells are too rare. After a short expansion in culture, the overlapping peptide pool gave an interferon-γ response in **16 of 20 donors**.

The mass-spectrometry route (immunopeptidomics, which reads the peptides an antigen-presenting cell actually puts on its HLA class I molecules) gave **41 candidate peptides**. These were screened in **13 matrix pools of 6 to 7 peptides**, with every peptide in two pools, so a positive row and a positive column point at one peptide. Screening 24 donors that way found **ten epitopes, nine of them new**, with the most popular one, "Peptide 21" (**FQFSYEFENV**), recognised by 7 donors.

## Checked with the tools

This is a **recomputation of the paper's designs** from the numbers in its text, not a reanalysis of its measurements.

**Step 1: does the library size add up?** AAV9 VP1 is 736 amino acids (UniProt Q6JC40; the paper does not say). Asking the tool for 15-mers that step 4 residues at a time, with a last peptide pulled back to finish at the C-terminus, gives **exactly 182 peptides**, the published number, the last one covering residues 722 to 736. Without that last peptide you get 181 and residue 736 is in no peptide at all. The fit is tight: a protein of 735 residues would have given 181 peptides and one of 740 would have given 183, so the paper's library and the catalogue length agree.

**Step 2: what the overlap guarantees.** With an 11-residue overlap, the tool reports that **every stretch of 12 amino acids lies wholly inside at least one peptide**, and most residues sit in four peptides at once. Class I epitopes are 8 to 11 residues long, so this design cannot cut one in half: the 10-residue FQFSYEFENV is caught whole, four times over. That is the property worth quoting in a methods section, and it is the reason to prefer an overlap of 11 over the cheaper offset of 5 or 6 that some screens use.

**Step 3: the pool layout is reproduced exactly.** Given 41 peptides, the tool lays out a near-square grid of 7 rows by 6 columns: **13 pools**, each peptide in two of them, pool sizes of 6 and 7 (with one pool of 5; typing 6 into *Rows* gives six pools of 7 and seven of 6). That is the paper's design, and it cuts 41 tests to 13. Figure 1 shows how the saving grows: the paper's 182-peptide overlapping library, screened as one pool in the paper, could have been deconvolved in **27 pools** instead of 182 single wells.

**Step 4: where pooling stops being unambiguous.** Matrix pooling identifies a single peptide only when a single peptide responds. We asked the tool's deconvolution what the 13 pools would allow if a donor responded to several peptides at once, drawing 400 random sets for each count. One true positive leaves **1 candidate**; two leave **3.4** on average; five leave **14**; seven leave **21** of the 41 (Figure 2). So a broad responder's pools point almost everywhere, and the follow-up on single peptides that the paper did is not optional. This also explains why a 10-epitope landscape is found across donors rather than within one donor.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/aav9-vp1-overlapping-peptide-library-matrix-pools-epitope-mapping.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `tile`, `guaranteedSpan`, `matrix`, `deconvolve`, `flags` and `hydroShare` from `src/lib/sci/peptide-overlap.ts`, the maths behind the [[peptide-overlap-designer]], and `peptideMass` and `mz` from `src/lib/sci/peptide.ts`, behind the [[peptide-mass-calculator]].

The peptide length, overlap, peptide count, pool count and the Peptide 21 sequence come from the paper's text. The VP1 length of 736 residues comes from UniProt Q6JC40 (**a catalogue value**, not from the paper). The 400 random positive sets in Figure 2 are **our simulation of the pooling design**, not the paper's donor data.

## How we test the maths

On every change to fibub, `tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/peptide-overlap-designer.mjs`, which pins the tool against an independent Python version:

- Peptide spans, monoisotopic masses (against pyteomics, to 0.000002 Da) and average masses (within 0.05 Da of pyteomics and Biopython) on the tool's example protein and five random proteins.
- The longest guaranteed stretch and the pool layout checked by brute force, plus pool deconvolution against the same Python code.
- New with this article: a 736-residue protein cut into 15-mers stepping 4 must give 182 peptides with the last at 722 to 736 (181 without the closing peptide), a guaranteed stretch of 12, and 41 peptides must lay out as 7 × 6 = 13 pools; FQFSYEFENV must weigh 1308.5663 Da monoisotopic and 1309.398 Da average.

## Check it yourself

On the [[peptide-overlap-designer]] page, with any 736-residue protein sequence (your own capsid sequence pasted into the box):

1. Set *Peptide length* to **15** and *Overlap* to **11** (an offset of 4).
2. Keep **Add a last peptide that ends at the C-terminus** ticked.
3. The list shows **182 peptides**, P001 at residues 1 to 15 and P182 at 722 to 736, and the line below reads **every stretch of 12 amino acids lies wholly in at least one peptide**.
4. Untick the closing peptide and the count drops to **181**, ending at residue 735.
5. Under *Matrix pools*, leave *Rows* empty: the 182 peptides fall into **14 rows and 13 columns, 27 pools**, each peptide in two of them.
6. To see the paper's own layout, make a 41-peptide library instead: paste the first 615 residues of the same protein and set *Overlap* to **0**. The grid becomes **7 rows by 6 columns, 13 pools** of 6 and 7 peptides; typing **6** into *Rows* gives pools of 7 and 6.
7. Tap one row pool and one column pool: a single candidate is shaded. Tap two rows and two columns and four candidates appear, which is the ambiguity in Figure 2.
8. For the mass, paste **FQFSYEFENV** into the [[peptide-mass-calculator]]: it reads **1308.5663 Da** monoisotopic and **655.2904** for the doubly charged ion.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check a published library** before you cite or copy its design: the peptide count, the overlap and the length have to agree, as they do here.
- **Choose an overlap by what it guarantees**: say in the methods that every 12-residue stretch is caught whole, instead of quoting an offset and leaving readers to work it out.
- **Plan pools honestly**: Figure 2 is the number to quote when a reviewer asks why single peptides were retested after a pooled screen.
- **Cut the cost of a whole-proteome scan**: 27 pools for 182 peptides, with the deconvolution worked out in the browser and an order list downloaded as CSV.
- **Order the right peptides**: the tool flags hard-to-make sequences and gives each one's mass for quality control with the [[peptide-mass-calculator]].

## The fine print

- We rebuilt the paper's designs; we did not reanalyse any donor response. The epitopes themselves are the paper's finding.
- The VP1 length of 736 residues is a catalogue value for AAV9 (UniProt Q6JC40). The paper's library may have been designed on a slightly different construct, in which case the match to 182 peptides is a coincidence rather than a check.
- Figure 2 assumes every truly responding peptide makes both its pools positive and nothing else does. Real pooled assays also lose weak responses by dilution, which makes matrix pooling harder, not easier.
- Peptide masses are for free peptides with no counter-ions or modifications; a synthesised peptide usually arrives as a salt.
- This is laboratory and study material about immune responses to a gene-therapy vector, not medical advice.
