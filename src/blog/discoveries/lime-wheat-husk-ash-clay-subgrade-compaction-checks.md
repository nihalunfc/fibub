Road subgrades in the wet tropics are often a fat lateritic clay that swells, shrinks and loses strength. A 2026 paper in *Frontiers in Built Environment* treats one such clay with a blend of **lime and wheat husk ash**, an agricultural waste, and reports the plasticity, compaction and strength of eight mixes. Papers like this are easy to read and hard to check, because everything hangs on a handful of index numbers. This article runs those numbers through the [[atterberg-compaction|Atterberg limits tool]]: three of the paper's results come back exactly, and one of them turns out to be physically impossible. The classification side of the job belongs to [[soil-classification]], and the design side to [[bearing-capacity]] and [[consolidation-settlement]].

## The paper in brief

The natural soil is a lateritic clay with a **liquid limit of 50.1 %**, a **plastic limit of 24.76 %** and **57.86 % passing the 0.075 mm sieve**, classed AASHTO A-7-6 and USCS CH: a clay subgrade a road engineer would want improved. Compacted at standard effort it reaches a **maximum dry density of 1739 kg/m³ at 17.8 % water**, with an unconfined compressive strength of **209.3 kPa**.

The authors then mixed in lime and wheat husk ash in eight proportions totalling 8 % by mass. The best of them, **6 % lime with 2 % ash**, raised the maximum dry density to **1798 kg/m³** and cut the plasticity index to **10.83 %**; **7 % lime with 1 % ash** gave the highest strength, **291.71 kPa**. The paper reads this as the lime doing the chemistry and the ash supplying silica for it, with microscope images to support the claim.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: the plasticity arithmetic is right.** The tool's `indices` function gives a plasticity index of **25.34 %** from the reported limits, exactly the paper's value, and an **activity of 0.99** using the reported 25.52 % clay fraction: a normal, not a swelling-prone, clay by Skempton's measure, which is worth knowing beside a paper about a problem soil. On Casagrande's chart the A-line at this liquid limit sits at **PI = 21.97**, the soil is above it, and the liquid limit is 50 or more, so the tool's classifier returns **CH, fat clay**, matching the paper. It is a close call, though: the liquid limit is 50.1 %, a tenth of a point above the line that separates CH from CL.

**Step 2: the plasticity index fell by 57 %, not 134 %.** The paper describes the drop from 25.34 to 10.83 % as a "134 %" reduction. A fall of 14.51 points from 25.34 is **57.3 %**; 134 % would mean a negative plasticity index. The maximum dry density gain, 1739 to 1798 kg/m³, is **3.4 %**.

**Step 3: the group index is about 12, not 156.** Feeding 57.86 % fines, the liquid limit and the plasticity index into the AASHTO group index formula behind [[soil-classification]] gives **12.3, reported as 12**, so the soil is **A-7-6(12)**. The group index is conventionally capped at 20, so the paper's stated 156 cannot be right, whatever route produced it. The practical reading does not change: A-7-6 with a double-digit group index is a poor subgrade.

**Step 4: the specific gravity and the compaction result contradict each other.** This is the one the tool is really for. No compacted soil can be denser than its zero-air-voids line, the density it would have with every pore full of water:

`ρd = Gs ρw / (1 + w Gs)`

At 17.8 % water, a specific gravity of **2.12** puts that ceiling at **1539 kg/m³**. The paper reports 1739 kg/m³, which is 13 % **above** the ceiling: the tool reports a degree of saturation of **172 %** and **−13 % air voids**, its way of saying the inputs disagree. Turn the equation round and the lowest specific gravity that can carry 1739 kg/m³ at 17.8 % water is **2.52**, and at exactly 2.52 the soil would be perfectly saturated at the optimum, which never happens. A lateritic clay's usual **2.65** gives a saturation of **90 %** and 3.4 % air voids, which is exactly where a standard Proctor optimum sits. So 2.12 is almost certainly a typing or calibration slip; the compaction result looks fine.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/lime-wheat-husk-ash-clay-subgrade-compaction-checks.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `indices`, `airVoids`, `saturation`, `dryDensity` and `compaction` from `src/lib/sci/atterberg.ts`, the maths behind the [[atterberg-compaction|Atterberg limits tool]], and `aLine`, `finesGroup` and `groupIndex` from `src/lib/sci/soilclass.ts`, which power [[soil-classification]].

The limits, fractions, specific gravity, maximum dry densities, optimum water contents, plasticity indices and strengths are the paper's own stated values. The specific gravities of 2.52 and 2.65 used for comparison, and the small parabola we draw through the natural soil's peak in Figure 1 (losing 10 kg/m³ per percentage point of water squared), are **our assumptions**: the paper gives its compaction curves only as figures. Nothing here is a new measurement.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/atterberg-compaction.mjs` runs on every change to fibub:

- A Casagrande flow curve must give the liquid limit, flow index and R² that an independent Python least-squares fit gives, and the one-point method must reproduce `LL = wN (N/25)^0.121`.
- The Proctor curve fit must find the same maximum dry density and optimum water content as a Python polynomial fit, and the phase relations must close: a point on the zero-air-voids line must come back at exactly 100 % saturation.
- New with this article: the paper's peak (17.8 % water, 1.739 Mg/m³) must give a saturation of 172.2 % with Gs = 2.12 and 90.0 % with Gs = 2.65, a zero-air-voids density of 1.5392 Mg/m³ at Gs = 2.12, and a lowest workable Gs of 2.519; and its limits must give a plasticity index of 25.34 % and an activity of 0.993.

The classification side is pinned in `tests/sci-tools/soil-classification.mjs`: 27 random gradings must match a Python implementation's USCS symbol, AASHTO group and group index, and the A-line and group index formulas are checked by hand.

## Check it yourself

On the [[atterberg-compaction|Atterberg limits tool]]:

1. In the *Liquid limit* card set *Method* to **One-point (one trial near 25 blows)**, then *Blows N* **25**, *Water content wN (%)* **50.1**, *Exponent k* **0.121**. At 25 blows the correction is 1, so the liquid limit reads **50.1 %**.
2. Under *Plastic limit and natural state* type *Plastic limit PL (%)* **24.76**, clear *Natural water content w (%)*, and type *Clay fraction finer than 2 µm (%)* **25.52**.
3. It reads *Plasticity index PI* **25.3 %**, with "on the plasticity chart: CH (A-line at this LL: 22)", and *Activity* **0.99**, "0.75 to 1.25: normal".
4. In the *Compaction curve (Proctor test)* card set *Density unit* to **kg/m³** and *Specific gravity of solids Gs* to **2.12**, then paste three points, one pair per line: **15.8 1967.4**, **17.8 2048.5**, **19.8 2035.4**. (These are ours, built so the curve peaks at the paper's reported maximum.)
5. The headline reads **Maximum dry density 1739 kg/m³ at 17.8 % water**, and below it *At the optimum* **S = 172 %, air voids −13.0 %** with *Points above zero air voids* **3**: the tool telling you the data and the specific gravity cannot both be right.
6. Change *Specific gravity of solids Gs* to **2.65**. Now *At the optimum* reads **S = 90 %, air voids 3.4 %**, *Points above zero air voids* **0**, void ratio 0.52. Try **2.52** and the peak sits all but exactly on the zero-air-voids line, at 100 % saturation.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check your own index sheet before submitting it**: plasticity index, A-line position, activity and classification in one card, from the limits you measured.
- **Use the zero-air-voids line as an error detector.** Any Proctor point above it means the specific gravity, the mould volume or the water content is wrong. The tool counts those points for you.
- **Report the saturation at the optimum** (usually 80 to 90 %) as a quality check on a compaction test, alongside the maximum dry density.
- **Classify for a road spec** with [[soil-classification]], which gives both the USCS symbol and the AASHTO group with the group index worked out step by step.
- **Carry the improved soil forward**: a stabilised subgrade changes bearing capacity and settlement, which the [[bearing-capacity]] and [[consolidation-settlement]] tools estimate from the same strength numbers.

## The fine print

- This is a **recomputation** of the paper's stated index values, not a re-measurement of its soil.
- The compaction curve in Figure 1 is **ours**, made to peak at the paper's reported values; the paper's own curves are only in its figures, so we cannot refit them.
- The specific gravity contradiction could be resolved another way: if Gs really is 2.12, then the maximum dry density must be nearer 1500 kg/m³. Either way the two published numbers cannot both stand.
- We could not read the paper's grading curve, so the AASHTO group index uses only the stated 57.86 % fines, the liquid limit and the plasticity index, which is all the formula needs.
- Lime stabilisation is a design job governed by local road specifications; check the official source.
