We live inside the Milky Way, so its map is built from angles and velocities, not pictures, and this week a new study suggests one piece of that map is mislabelled. Using new simulations of a barred galaxy, a 2026 paper in Nature Astronomy argues that young **masers** (bright, laser-like radio sources in star-forming clouds) that had been assigned to the **Norma spiral arm** actually sit on the **shock front along the leading edge of the Galactic bar**, its dust lane. Matching the masers to that shock fixes the bar at **4.3 kpc** long, **20°** from our line to the centre, and turning at **40 km/s/kpc or slower**. How a spiral arm and a bar can be confused comes down to how we measure distances in the Galaxy, which is exactly what the [[hydrogen-line-analyser]] does: it turns a radio velocity into a distance with a rotation curve. To fit that rotation curve from your own data, use the [[rotation-curve-fitter]]; to move positions between Galactic and sky coordinates, the [[sky-coordinates]].

## The paper in brief

Most barred spiral galaxies form few stars along their bars: the gas there is squeezed into narrow shocks so fast that it tends to be sheared apart before it can collapse. The Milky Way may be different. The authors ran N-body and hydrodynamic simulations of a bar like ours and found that its **quadrant I shock front**, the dust lane on the bar's near side, lines up with a set of masers about a million years old that had been read as part of the Norma arm. If those masers belong to the shock instead, the bar's dust lane is forming stars.

Fitting the masers to the simulated shock gives the bar's shape: a **semi-major axis of about 4.3 ± 0.2 kpc** (about 14,000 light years), a **semi-minor axis of about 2.3 ± 0.2 kpc**, an angle of **20 (+5/−2)°** to the Sun–centre line, and a pattern speed of **Ω_p ≲ 40 km/s/kpc**. The paper's summary: the Milky Way's bar is **long, slow and star-forming**.

## Checked with the tools

Only the abstract was available to us, so everything below is a **recomputation from the abstract's bar parameters**, using the kinematic-distance maths of the [[hydrogen-line-analyser]] with its modern constants (R0 = 8.2 kpc from the Sun to the centre, V0 = 233 km/s rotation). It is not a refit of the masers.

**Step 1: where the bar's near end sits on our sky.** A bar 4.3 kpc long at 20° to the Sun–centre line puts its near tip **19.47° of Galactic longitude** from the centre, **4.41 kpc** from the Sun. The paper's ranges move that between **17.9° and 22.9°** (angle 18° to 25°) and 17.9° to 21.2° (length 4.1 to 4.5 kpc). That longitude band, inside the Sun's orbit in the first quadrant, is where the masers in question lie on the sky.

**Step 2: what velocity circular rotation predicts there.** Gas on a circular orbit 4.3 kpc from the centre, seen at l = 19.47° with a flat rotation curve, would move away from us at **70.45 km/s** (local standard of rest). Feed that velocity back into the tool's `kinematicDistance()` and it returns **R = 4.300 kpc** from the centre and **4.412 kpc (near) or 11.05 kpc (far)** from us, the bar's tip and a point on the far side of the Galaxy. The terminal velocity on this sightline is **155.33 km/s**, at a tangent point 2.73 kpc from the centre.

**Step 3: why bar gas is easy to misplace.** A kinematic distance assumes the gas moves on a circular orbit. In a bar it does not: gas streams along the bar and jumps in speed at the shock. The tool shows how much that matters. If the gas moves **20 km/s slower** than circular rotation, the same longitude gives R = **4.97 kpc** and a near distance **0.83 kpc** closer to us; **20 km/s faster** gives R = **3.79 kpc** and **0.70 kpc** farther. At ±40 km/s the shifts are **−1.90 and +1.32 kpc**. Streaming of a few tens of km/s is normal near a bar's shock, so masers that really sit on the shock can easily be given distances that make them look like a separate arm at a different radius. That is also why the paper leans on simulations of the bar's full velocity field rather than on a circular rotation curve.

**Step 4: "long and slow".** A pattern speed of at most 40 km/s/kpc with a flat 233 km/s rotation puts **corotation**, where stars orbit at the bar's own speed, at **5.83 kpc or more**: at least **1.35 bar lengths** out. Bars with corotation beyond about 1.4 bar lengths are called slow, so the abstract's upper limit sits right at that line, and any lower pattern speed makes the bar firmly slow, as the paper says. The axis ratio b/a is **0.535**, about two to one.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number above come from `scripts/blog-figures/norma-arm-masers-milky-way-bar-shock-front-4-3-kpc-kinematic-distance.mjs` in fibub's public code. It imports `kinematicDistance()`, `tangentPoint()` and `GALAXY` from `src/lib/astro/hline.ts`, the code the [[hydrogen-line-analyser]] runs in your browser. `kinematicDistance()` solves for the galactocentric radius R = R0 V0 sin l / (v + V0 sin l) and then the near and far points on the sightline at that radius; `tangentPoint()` gives the radius and rotation speed where the sightline grazes its smallest orbit. The bar's tip comes from plain geometry (a cos φ, a sin φ), and the circular velocity from v = V0 sin l (R0/R − 1).

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` checks the analyser's maths on every change: the radio velocity convention, the LSR correction against reference values, the baseline fit, and that a kinematic distance's near and far points both lie at the returned radius (for example l = 30°, v = 50 km/s). With this article `tests/sci-tools/hydrogen-line-analyser.mjs` adds a round trip: gas on a circular orbit at the bar's end (l = 19.473°, 70.45 km/s) must come back at R = 4.300 kpc and 4.412 kpc from the Sun.

## Check it yourself

On the [[hydrogen-line-analyser]] page, in the first card:

1. Set *Milky Way constants* to **Modern: R₀ 8.2 kpc, V₀ 233 km/s**.

Then in the card *Rotation curve of the Milky Way*:

2. In *Galactic longitude (°) and terminal velocity (km/s) of each pointing*, type **19.47 155.33**, the flat-curve terminal velocity at the bar's end longitude. The note under the chart reads *mean speed 233 km/s*: the tool recovers the flat curve.
3. Change the line to **19.47 175.33**, as if gas near the tangent point streamed 20 km/s faster than circular. The mean speed jumps to **253 km/s**: the same kind of non-circular motion that misplaces masers also bends a rotation curve measured near a bar.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Turn a radio velocity into a distance** for a cloud, maser or HI peak, with both the near and far answers, and see how much an assumed velocity error moves them.
- **Flag sightlines where kinematic distances are unsafe**: inside about 5 kpc of the centre, in the first and fourth quadrants, the bar's streaming motions can shift a distance by a kiloparsec or more.
- **Build a rotation curve from tangent-point velocities**, from your own 21 cm spectra or published ones, and compare it with a fit from the [[rotation-curve-fitter]].
- **Teach how the Milky Way is mapped from inside**, with a small backyard radio telescope and this one page.

## The fine print

- Only the abstract was available from here. We used none of the paper's maser positions or velocities, and the bar-tip longitude, circular velocities and distance shifts above are our own geometry on the abstract's parameters, not values from the paper.
- The tool assumes a flat rotation curve and circular orbits; that is the point of Step 3, but it also means it cannot model the bar itself. The paper's simulations do.
- Corotation from V0 / Ω_p assumes a flat rotation curve at that radius; with a curve that is still rising it would be a little different.
- Masers here means interstellar water or methanol masers in star-forming regions, not laboratory masers.
