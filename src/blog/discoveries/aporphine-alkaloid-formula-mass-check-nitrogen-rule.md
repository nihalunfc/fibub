When a paper announces a new natural product, the first line of evidence is always the same: a molecular formula, read off a high-resolution mass spectrum, and the number of rings and double bonds that formula allows. A 2025 paper in *Molecules* reports three new **aporphine alkaloids** (four-ring nitrogen compounds built on the aporphine skeleton) from the parasitic vine *Cassytha filiformis*, and prints the usual line for each: formula, measured m/z, calculated m/z. Those three numbers can be checked in seconds with the [[unsaturation-calculator]], which gives rings plus double bonds, the exact mass of the protonated ion and the nitrogen rule. We did that for all three compounds, and two of them agree to a couple of parts per million. The third printed mass cannot belong to any one-nitrogen ion at all. For the neighbouring jobs, the [[isotope-pattern-calculator]] draws the isotope cluster the same formula should show, and the [[molar-mass-calculator]] gives the ordinary molar mass for weighing out a sample.

## The paper in brief

The authors extracted about 50 kg of dried whole herb with 95 % ethanol, separated the alkaloids by acid-base partitioning and purified them by column chromatography and semi-preparative HPLC. Three compounds were new: **10-demethylcassythine** and **3-demethylcassythine** (both C₁₈H₁₇NO₅, differing only in which methyl group is missing) and **N-demethyllastourvilline** (C₁₈H₁₉NO₄). Structures came from NMR, with optical rotations and calculated spectra for the stereochemistry. Compound 1 raised glucose consumption in liver-derived HepG2 cells at 20 µmol/L, which is the biological hook, and the authors say plainly that the position of the methyl group changed the activity.

Our interest here is only the formula evidence, which anyone can re-derive from the printed numbers.

## Checked with the tools

**Step 1: rings and double bonds.** For a neutral molecule the count is 1 + ½Σn(v − 2) over the atoms. The tool gives **11** for C₁₈H₁₇NO₅ and **10** for C₁₈H₁₉NO₄. Both are whole numbers, which is the first test a formula has to pass: a neutral molecule with a half-integer count would have an odd number of electrons, so the hydrogen count would be wrong. Eleven fits an aporphine easily (four rings plus two aromatic rings' worth of double bonds plus a carbonyl), and ten fits one with a methylenedioxy group and no extra unsaturation. Figure 2 shows how the count moves: every two hydrogens you add or remove changes it by one.

**Step 2: the exact mass of the protonated ion.** Here the tool is stricter than most papers. The monoisotopic mass of **C₁₈H₁₈NO₅⁺** is **328.11795**, and of **C₁₈H₂₀NO₄⁺** it is **314.13868** — both after subtracting one electron mass, because an ion is lighter than the sum of its atoms. The paper's "calcd." values follow the common convention of adding atomic masses and ignoring the electron, which is **1.7 ppm high** at these masses. That matters when a journal asks for agreement within 5 ppm:

| Compound 1 and 2 (C₁₈H₁₇NO₅) | Value | Deviation |
|---|---|---|
| Printed in the results | 328.1190 | +1.5 ppm from the paper's calcd., +3.2 ppm from the ion's true mass |
| Printed in the experimental section | 328.1186 | +0.3 ppm from the paper's calcd., +2.0 ppm from the true mass |
| Paper's "calcd. for C₁₈H₁₈NO₅" | 328.1185 | +1.7 ppm (the missing electron) |
| Tool's monoisotopic [M+H]⁺ | 328.11795 | — |

So the measurement is good: 2 to 3 ppm, which is what a decent instrument gives. The tool also searched every even-electron protonated C, H, N, O formula with 12 to 26 carbons, up to 9 oxygens, up to 3 nitrogens and 5 to 16 rings and double bonds within 10 ppm of 328.1190, and **C₁₈H₁₈NO₅⁺ is the only one**. The formula assignment is safe.

**Step 3: compound 3, and the nitrogen rule.** For C₁₈H₁₉NO₄ the paper prints "[M + H]⁺ ion peak at m/z 341.1391 (calcd. for C₁₈H₂₀NO₄ 341.1392)", in both the discussion and the experimental section. But C₁₈H₂₀NO₄⁺ weighs **314.1387**, not 341.1392 — a gap of **27.0004 Da**. Two independent checks say the printed mass, not the formula, is what slipped:

- The **nitrogen rule**: a protonated molecule with one nitrogen is an even-electron ion, and its nominal mass must be **even**. 314 is even; **341 is odd**, so no protonated one-nitrogen molecule can sit there. Figure 1 shows this as a map: one-nitrogen ions fill the even columns and leave the odd ones empty.
- The same **formula search** within 10 ppm of 341.1391 turns up exactly one even-electron candidate, **C₂₀H₂₁O₅⁺** at 341.1384, which contains no nitrogen and so cannot be an alkaloid.

Meanwhile 341.1392 and 314.1392 use the same digits in a different order, and 314.1392 is to the last decimal what the paper's own convention gives for C₁₈H₂₀NO₄. Almost certainly a transposition when that line was typed: **the structure and the formula are fine, and the printed m/z should read 314.139**. It is the kind of slip no reviewer catches by eye and any formula calculator catches at once.

## How we made the graphs

The charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/aporphine-alkaloid-formula-mass-check-nitrogen-rule.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `dbeFromFormula` from `src/lib/sci/dbe.ts`, the maths behind the [[unsaturation-calculator]], which parses a formula (charges included), returns rings plus double bonds, the monoisotopic and nominal masses and the nitrogen-rule verdict, and uses `src/lib/sci/chem.ts` for the atomic masses.

The formulas and the printed m/z values are the paper's, quoted from its text. The formula search and the mass-defect map are **ours**: the search covers protonated C, H, N, O formulas with 12 to 26 carbons, up to 9 oxygens, up to 3 nitrogens and 5 to 16 rings and double bonds, and the map in Figure 1 is drawn for the narrower window a C₁₈ alkaloid of this size sits in (17 to 20 carbons, 3 to 6 oxygens, 10 or 11 rings and double bonds). Radical ions are dropped from both, because an [M+H]⁺ is an even-electron ion. A wider window would add candidates further from the mass; none of them changes the nitrogen-rule argument, which does not depend on the window at all.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci.mjs` runs `tests/sci-tools/unsaturation-calculator.mjs` on every change to the site. That file checks the formula maths against **RDKit 2026.09** on 622 SMILES strings for 164 molecules, each written up to five ways: element counts, charge, monoisotopic mass to 2 × 10⁻⁵, ring count and the rings-plus-double-bonds value all have to agree, which is 4,930 comparisons with none failing. On top of that:

- Caffeine, C₈H₁₀N₄O₂, must give 6 rings plus double bonds, a monoisotopic mass of **194.0803756** and an average molar mass of 194.19; benzene must give 4 and ethanol 0.
- Protonated pyridine, C₅H₆N⁺, must give **3.5** (an even-electron ion), a monoisotopic mass of **80.049475** with the electron taken off, and the nitrogen-rule line "even-electron ion of charge +1: the nominal mass is even (80)".
- An impossible formula such as CH₅ must come back as −0.5 with the message that it has too many hydrogens, instead of a silent answer.
- New with this article: C₁₈H₁₇NO₅ must give 11 rings plus double bonds and C₁₈H₁₉NO₄ must give 10; the protonated ions C₁₈H₁₈NO₅⁺ and C₁₈H₂₀NO₄⁺ must weigh **328.11795** and **314.13868** with even nominal masses; and the printed 341.1391 must be more than 27 Da away from the second of those.

## Check it yourself

On the [[unsaturation-calculator]]:

1. Leave the input on **Formula** and type **C18H17NO5** in *Molecular formula*.
2. The *Result* card reads **11** rings plus double bonds, nominal mass **327**, monoisotopic mass **327.11067**, average molar mass **327.34**, and a green pill saying the nitrogen rule holds (one nitrogen, odd nominal mass for a neutral molecule).
3. Now type the ion: **C18H18NO5+**. The count becomes **10.5**, the monoisotopic mass **328.11795** and the pill says the nominal mass is **even (328)** for an even-electron ion with one nitrogen. This is the number to compare with a measured [M+H]⁺.
4. Type **C18H20NO4+**: monoisotopic mass **314.13868**, nominal mass **314**, even again. There is no way to reach 341 with one nitrogen on a protonated molecule.
5. Open *Rings and π bonds that fit* for C18H17NO5 and you get the combinations of rings, double bonds and triple bonds that add to 11; press **Combinations (.csv)** to keep them.
6. For a sanity check of the convention, type **C18H18NO5** without the plus: the mass rises by about 0.00055, the electron the ion is missing.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Check every formula in a manuscript before submitting it**: paste each formula as its ion, compare the monoisotopic mass with the measured m/z in ppm, and confirm the nominal mass parity.
- **Catch typing slips in m/z values**, which survive review easily: the nitrogen rule alone finds any protonated one-nitrogen mass that has been printed as odd.
- **Know which convention a journal wants**: with or without the electron mass is a 1.7 ppm difference at m/z 330, enough to turn a 4 ppm result into 6.
- **Narrow a formula from a mass**: search formulas within your instrument's accuracy and see how many survive; if more than one does, say so rather than choosing.
- **Read the degree of unsaturation as structural evidence**: 11 rings and double bonds leaves very little room for a four-ring alkaloid, and the tool lists the combinations that fit.
- **Teach it**: the chart of rings and double bonds against hydrogen count makes the ½-per-hydrogen rule obvious in one look.

## The fine print

- We checked only the formula and mass arithmetic. Nothing here tests the structures, the stereochemistry or the biology, which rest on the paper's NMR and calculated spectra.
- The 27.0004 Da gap for compound 3 is between the paper's **printed** m/z and the mass its own assigned formula demands. We read those numbers from the publisher's HTML on 2026-11-24; a correction may follow.
- The paper prints two slightly different measured values for compound 1 (328.1190 and 328.1186) in different sections. We report both rather than pick one.
- Our formula search covers a window chosen for compounds of this size. A formula outside it, or one containing sulfur, chlorine or an isotope label, would need the window widened.
- Monoisotopic masses use the most abundant isotope of each element; the average molar mass the tool also prints is the one to use for weighing.
