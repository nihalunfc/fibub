The European spruce bark beetle, *Ips typographus*, kills standing spruce when its numbers rise, and foresters watch for that rise with pheromone traps. A 2026 paper in *Insects* reports four years of trap catches near the northern edge of the beetle's range and uses a simple rule to mark the spring swarming: the average catch is **above ten beetles per trap per day**. That rule is a catch per unit effort, and it is exactly what the [[trap-count-index]] works out from a list of trap checks. This article uses the tool to ask what the rule costs when the traps are emptied less often — which the paper itself had to do for one year. Time the next trapping round with the [[insect-degree-days]], and turn a season's catches into diversity scores with the [[biodiversity-indices]].

## The paper in brief

The authors ran barrier pheromone traps 1.3 m above the ground, **three traps at each point**, at two locations in north-west Russia from 2022 to 2025, inspecting them **every 5 days** from early May to late August. On the Karelian Isthmus in 2024, though, the traps were inspected at **14-day intervals**.

Their results are mostly about timing. Spring swarming ran 23 to 30 May in 2022, 6 to 25 May in 2023 and 16 to 25 May in 2025; in 2025 almost nothing was caught after 25 May, with a re-colonisation flight on 12 to 13 July and a second-generation flight on 3 August. Swarming began at **84 to 97 growing degree-days** above 5.0 °C in 2022, 81 to 124 in 2023 and 122 to 137 in 2025 (19 to 20, 22 to 25 and 39 to 52 above a base of 8.3 °C), far below the 666 and 420 degree-days reported for Sweden. Catch correlated with temperature at r = 0.76, rising to 0.79 when a 5-day moving average was used first — a small but telling detail, because the smoothing is part of the measurement.

## Checked with the tools

The paper's catch numbers live in its tables and figures, so there is nothing to recompute directly. Instead we **simulated one beetle flight** (clearly ours, not the paper's) shaped to the phenology it describes for 2025: a quiet background, a sharp swarming peak around 20 May, a small re-colonisation flight on 12 July and a second-generation flight on 3 August. Then we read that one flight through the tool twice: as checks every 5 days and as checks every 14 days, three traps each time, exactly as the paper's two schedules.

**Step 1: the peak halves.** With 5-day checks the index peaks at **21.5 beetles per trap per day** on 21 May. With 14-day checks the same beetles give **11.8** on 29 May. Nothing was lost — the catch is the same beetles — but the long interval averages the quiet days in with the busy ones, and the peak drops by 45 %.

**Step 2: the alarm comes eight days late.** Both schedules cross the paper's swarming threshold of ten, so the rule survives; but the 5-day schedule flags it on **21 May** and the 14-day schedule only on **29 May**. For a beetle that has already flown, bored into bark and begun laying, eight days is the difference between a sanitary felling and an outbreak.

**Step 3: the totals are safe, the index is not.** Pooled over the season, the two schedules caught almost the same beetles, **676** against **675**, which is the point of a trap-day index. But the season index differs, **1.878** against **2.009** beetles per trap per day, because the schedules claim different trap-days (360 against 336: 24 checks of 5 days against 8 of 14). The tool's *Days before the first check* setting matters for the same reason, and should be the real interval, not a default.

**Step 4: the moving average can move the peak.** With only eight checks in a season, a 3-check centred moving average smears the one busy check across its neighbours and the tool marks the apparent flight peak on **15 May**, a check before the real one. Switch the moving average off and the peak returns to **29 May**. On the 5-day series, with 24 checks, the same smoothing leaves the peak where it belongs. The lesson is to match the smoothing window to the check interval: the paper's 5-day moving average is right for 5-day checks, and wrong for 14-day ones.

**Step 5: with long gaps, luck decides.** Figure 2 runs the same flight through every check interval from 1 to 14 days. Daily checks show the true peak of **26**; a 5-day gap shows 21.5; a 14-day gap shows 11.8. The fall is not smooth, because what you see depends on where the check boundaries happen to land in the flight: a 7-day gap shows **19.3** while a 9-day gap shows **11.0**. Two forests monitored a week apart can report peaks that differ by a factor of two with no difference in the beetles.

## How we made the graphs

Both charts and every number come from `scripts/blog-figures/ips-typographus-trap-catch-per-trap-day-check-interval.mjs` in fibub's public code. It calls `trapIndex`, `summarise` and `isoWeek` from `src/lib/sci/trapcount.ts`, the maths behind the [[trap-count-index]].

From the paper: the three traps per point, the 5-day and 14-day inspection intervals, the May-to-August season, the swarming threshold of ten beetles per trap per day, and the 2025 dates of the spring swarming, the July re-colonisation and the August second-generation flight. **Our simulation**: the daily flight curve itself (a background of 0.15 beetles per trap per day plus three bell-shaped flights), its peak height and its widths. The paper's own catches are not used, reproduced or implied anywhere.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/trap-count-index.mjs` runs on every change to fibub, against an independent pandas implementation of the same arithmetic:

- On the page's made-up two-orchard example, the index for the North orchard on 25 May must be 6.523810 per trap per day, and 4.428571 on 15 June when one trap was lost.
- Its smoothed series must match `rolling(3, center=True, min_periods=1)` at both ends of the season (0.833333 on the first check), and the peaks must fall on 25 May and 29 June.
- The pooled season index must be 3.9825784 per trap per day from 1143 beetles over 287 trap-days — different from the mean of the check indices, 3.9931973, which is the whole reason the tool reports both.
- New with this article: the 14-day schedule above must give indices 0.64 and 11.76 with 675 beetles over 336 trap-days and a season index of 2.009; the swarming check must be flagged at a threshold of 10; and a 3-check moving average must move the apparent peak to the check before, while no smoothing leaves it on 29 May.

## Check it yourself

On the [[trap-count-index]] page, in the *Trap checks* box, clear the example and paste our simulated 14-day schedule (date, site, traps, count):

```
2025-05-15, Isthmus, 3, 27
2025-05-29, Isthmus, 3, 494
2025-06-12, Isthmus, 3, 6
2025-06-26, Isthmus, 3, 6
2025-07-10, Isthmus, 3, 12
2025-07-24, Isthmus, 3, 35
2025-08-07, Isthmus, 3, 87
2025-08-21, Isthmus, 3, 8
```

1. Set *Index* to **per trap per day**, *Days before the first check* to **14**, *Moving average* to **None** and *Flag at or above (index)* to **10**.
2. The *Population index* table shows **11.76** on 29 May, the only flagged check, and **2.07** on 7 August. The site summary gives **675** beetles, **336** trap-days and a season index of **2.009**.
3. Switch *Moving average* to **3 checks**: the peak marker moves to **15 May**, a check too early. Switch it back to **None** and it returns to 29 May.
4. Now change *Days before the first check* to **5** and watch the season index rise to **2.184**: that one field is worth 9 % of the index.
5. Set *Index* to **per trap per week** and the swarming check reads **82.3** per trap per week — the same catch, a different unit. Always say which one a threshold is written in.

## What researchers can use it for

- **Compare seasons or sites with different schedules** fairly, by working in catch per trap per day rather than catch per check.
- **Decide an inspection interval** before a season starts: run a past flight through the tool at several intervals and see what peak each would have reported.
- **Audit a threshold rule** like "ten beetles per trap per day" for the schedule it was written for; the same rule is harder to trip on a long interval.
- **Match the smoothing to the data**: a 3- or 5-check moving average helps a dense series and can mislead a sparse one.
- **Prepare a figure and a CSV** of the index, the week table and the site summary in one pass, ready for a report or a supplement.

## The fine print

- The flight curve is **our simulation**. Its shape follows the dates the paper gives for 2025, but its heights are invented, and the real series had its own weather-driven bumps.
- Our two schedules sample one identical flight, which is the cleanest way to isolate the effect of the interval. In the paper the 14-day year (2024) and the 5-day years are different years, so its own numbers cannot be compared this way.
- A trap-day index assumes a trap keeps catching at the same rate while it waits. Barrier traps with a saturating catch surface, or lures that fade, break that assumption, and long intervals break it most.
- We did not touch the degree-day side of the paper. Its onset sums (84 to 137 above 5.0 °C) depend on the base temperature, the start date and the daily-mean method, and the paper's own Methods and Discussion differ slightly (420 against 418 degree-days for the Swedish reference).
