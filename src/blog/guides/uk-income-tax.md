A UK payslip can take off Income Tax, National Insurance (NI), a student loan repayment and a pension contribution, and each follows its own rules. The UK tax calculator puts them together for the 2026/27 tax year (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027) and shows the take-home pay a year, a month and a week, for Scotland or for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. It is for employees checking a payslip, graduates wondering what their loan costs, and anyone choosing how much to put into a pension. To see how a pension contribution is topped up, try the [[uk-pension-relief]] calculator; to plan what to do with the rest, use the [[budget-calculator]] or the [[loan-payoff-planner]].

## What it is

Take-home pay is what is left after four separate calculations.

**1. Income Tax.** Most people pay no tax on the first £12,570, the **personal allowance**. Above £100,000 of income, the allowance shrinks by £1 for every £2, so it is gone by £125,140. What remains is **taxable income**, cut into bands:

- England, Wales and Northern Ireland: 20% on the first £37,700, 40% up to £125,140, 45% above.
- Scotland: six bands, from 19% (starter) through 20%, 21%, 42% and 45% to 48%.

**2. National Insurance.** Employees pay 8% on pay between £12,570 and £50,270 a year and 2% above that. NI is charged on salary only, never on rent or pension income.

**3. Student loans.** Each plan takes 9% of pay above its threshold: Plan 1 £26,900, Plan 2 £29,385, Plan 4 £33,795, Plan 5 £25,000. A postgraduate loan takes 6% above £21,000, on top of any other plan.

**4. Pension.** Money can go in three ways, and each saves different things:

- *Relief at source*: you pay 80% of the amount from take-home pay and your provider claims 20% from HMRC. If you pay a higher rate, your bands are stretched by the gross amount.
- *Net pay*: your employer takes it before tax, so it saves Income Tax but not NI.
- *Salary sacrifice*: your contract pay is lowered, so it saves tax, NI and student loan repayments.

## How to use it

1. Choose **where you live**: Scotland, or England, Wales or Northern Ireland.
2. Type your yearly **salary** before anything comes off, and any **other taxable income**, such as rental profit or a pension you already draw.
3. Pick **how you pay into a pension**, or leave it at "No pension".
4. Set **into your pot** as a percentage of salary or pounds a year. With relief at source, this is the gross amount including the 20% top-up.
5. Tick each **student loan** you repay.
6. Read the take-home boxes, the marginal and average rates, and the note under them, which explains a reduced allowance or how your pension is paid. The bar and table split every pound into tax, NI, loans, pension and take-home.

## A worked example

Isla lives in Glasgow and earns £35,000. First, with no pension or loan:

- Taxable income = £35,000 − £12,570 = £22,430.
- Tax = 19% × £3,967 + 20% × £12,989 + 21% × £5,474 = £753.73 + £2,597.80 + £1,149.54 = **£4,501.07**.
- NI = 8% × (£35,000 − £12,570) = **£1,794.40**.
- Take-home = £35,000 − £4,501.07 − £1,794.40 = **£28,704.53** a year.

Now add a Plan 2 loan and a 5% pension paid by relief at source.

- Into the pot: 5% × £35,000 = £1,750. She pays 80%, which is £1,400, and her provider adds £350.
- Her bands from 20% upwards are stretched by £1,750, so £1,750 moves from the 21% band into the 20% band. Tax falls by 1% × £1,750 = £17.50, to **£4,483.57**.
- Plan 2 = 9% × (£35,000 − £29,385) = 9% × £5,615 = **£505.35**.
- NI is unchanged at £1,794.40.
- Take-home = £35,000 − £4,483.57 − £1,794.40 − £505.35 − £1,400 = **£26,816.68** a year, about £2,235 a month.

Her take-home falls by £1,887.85, yet £1,750 has gone into her pension. Her marginal rate is now 38%: 21% tax, 8% NI and 9% loan on each extra pound.

## Where it is used

- **Checking a new job offer**, including the difference between living in Scotland and elsewhere.
- **Choosing a pension method or amount**, by switching between relief at source, net pay and salary sacrifice.
- **Graduates** working out what their student loan really takes, and how it stacks with a postgraduate loan.
- **Earners near £100,000**, who can see the very high marginal rate that the allowance taper creates between £100,000 and £125,140, and how pension contributions bring the allowance back.
- **Landlords with a day job**, who can add rental profit as other income and see the extra tax.
- **Money lessons**, to show how bands, NI and loans combine into one marginal rate.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering take-home pay instead of salary.** Type the figure before anything is taken off, from your contract or payslip.
- **Choosing Scotland because you work there.** Scottish rates depend on where you live, not where your employer is.
- **Typing the net amount for relief at source.** The tool expects what goes into the pot, including the 20% top-up.
- **Adding savings interest or dividends.** They have their own allowances and rates, which the tool leaves out.
- **Expecting an exact match to your payslip.** Payroll works month by month with your tax code. If yours is not 1257L, the result will differ. Check the official source.

## Questions people ask

**Why does my student loan seem small?** Only the pay above your plan's threshold is charged, so on £35,000 a Plan 2 loan takes 9% of £5,615, not 9% of £35,000.

**What does the marginal rate include?** Tax, NI and student loan repayments on your next £100 of salary. Pension payments are not counted.

**Can two undergraduate plans both be charged?** No. With more than one, the tool takes 9% once, from the lowest threshold. A postgraduate loan is added on top.

**Is this advice?** No. It is general education based on the 2026/27 rates the tool uses. Rates change each April; check the official source on GOV.UK or ask HMRC.
