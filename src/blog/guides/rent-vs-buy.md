"Rent is dead money" is one of the most repeated lines in personal finance, and also one of the most misleading. Owners pay a lot that never comes back too: interest, property tax, repairs, insurance and the fees to buy and later sell. The [[rent-vs-buy]] tool follows a renter and a buyer side by side, month by month, and shows which one ends up with more, and from which year. It is for first-time buyers, movers and anyone testing the "dead money" idea. It is general education, not personal advice. To size the loan itself, open the [[mortgage-calculator]]; to check that either path fits your monthly money, use the [[budget-calculator]]; and to see how rent might climb over the years, try the [[inflation-calculator]].

## What it is

A fair comparison gives both people the same money and sees where each ends up.

The **buyer** spends a down payment and buying costs on day one, then pays each month:

monthly cost of owning = mortgage payment + (property tax % + maintenance %) × home value ÷ 12 + insurance ÷ 12

The mortgage payment uses the standard level-payment formula, P = L × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n), where **L** is the loan, **i** the monthly interest rate and **n** the number of monthly payments. Tax and upkeep follow the home's value, so they rise as the price grows.

The **renter** keeps the down payment and buying costs and invests them at the return you choose. Each month, whoever has the lower housing cost invests the difference. Rent rises once a year by the rate you set.

At the end of each year the tool works out each person's **net worth**:

- Buyer: home value minus selling costs, minus the mortgage still owed, plus anything the buyer invested in months when owning was cheaper.
- Renter: the value of everything the renter invested.

The **break-even year** is the first year the buyer's net worth is at least the renter's.

## How to use it

1. Under Renting, enter today's monthly rent, how much it rises each year, and the yearly return you could earn by investing.
2. Under Buying, enter the home price, down payment (as a percent), mortgage rate and mortgage length in years.
3. Enter the yearly running costs: property tax and maintenance as a percent of the home's value, and home insurance as an amount.
4. Enter how fast you expect the home price to grow, and the costs of buying and selling as percents of the price.
5. Tick the Canadian mortgage box if your rate compounds twice a year, and set how many years to look ahead (1 to 60).
6. Read the headline, the chart of both net worths, and open "Year by year" for the full table. "Download the table (CSV)" saves it for a spreadsheet.

## A worked example

Priya rents for 1,600 a month and expects rent to rise 3% a year. She could invest at 6% a year. She is looking at a 350,000 home with 15% down, a 4.5% mortgage over 25 years, property tax 0.8% and maintenance 1% of the value each year, insurance 1,000 a year, home price growth 2.5% a year, 2% to buy and 5% to sell. She looks 20 years ahead.

- Down payment: 15% × 350,000 = 52,500, so the loan is 297,500.
- Buying costs: 2% × 350,000 = 7,000. The renter invests 52,500 + 7,000 = 59,500 on day one.
- Monthly mortgage payment: about 1,653.60.
- First month of owning: 1,653.60 + 1.8% × 350,000 ÷ 12 + 1,000 ÷ 12 ≈ 2,261.93. That is about 661.93 more than rent, which the renter invests.

The tool says **buying pulls ahead in year 7**. At year 5 renting is still ahead (about 121,622 against 114,816). At year 7 buying edges past (about 150,740 against 148,721). After 20 years, buying has about 460,536 and renting about 363,295, with the home worth about 573,516 and about 88,698 still owed.

If Priya expects to move within six years, renting comes out ahead on these guesses.

## Where it is used

- **Deciding when to buy.** Comparing the break-even year with how long you expect to stay is often the clearest test.
- **Moving for a job.** People on a two- or three-year posting can see whether buying makes sense at all.
- **Housing counselling and classes.** The side-by-side model shows why upfront and selling costs matter so much.
- **Planning a down payment.** Try a bigger or smaller down payment and watch the break-even year move.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving out selling costs.** Agent fees and taxes on a sale can wipe out years of gains. Enter a realistic figure, even if a sale feels far away.
- **Assuming the renter spends the savings.** The model only works if the renter really invests the difference. If you would not, set the investment return near zero to see the difference.
- **One set of guesses.** Home growth, rent growth and investment return each move the answer by years. Run a cautious case and a hopeful one.
- **Forgetting local taxes and rules.** Transfer taxes, mortgage insurance and tax on gains differ by place, and the tool does not include them. Check the official source and add known costs to the buying and selling percents.

## Questions people ask

**Why is renting ahead in the early years?** The renter's money starts working at once, while the buyer has paid fees and mostly interest so far. The buyer only catches up as the loan shrinks and the home grows.

**Can the tool model a home losing value?** Not directly. Negative growth is not accepted, and a growth of 0 keeps the price flat. For a falling market, try 0 and a higher selling cost.

**What does the Canadian box change?** It works out the monthly interest from a rate that compounds twice a year, as Canadian fixed-rate mortgages usually do, which gives a slightly lower payment for the same rate.
