Strength programmes are often written as percentages: "three sets of five at 80%". Eighty percent of what? Of your one-rep max, the most you could lift once with clean form. Finding that number by trying it is hard on the body and needs a spotter, so the [[one-rep-max]] tool estimates it from a set you already did, then lists the weights for each percentage. It is for gym-goers, coaches writing plans and students learning about training. For the cardio side of a week, set your effort bands with [[heart-rate-zones]], and if you are training to gain or lose weight, the [[calorie-calculator]] gives a daily energy figure.

## What it is

The more reps you can do with a weight, the further that weight is below your best single lift. Both formulas in the tool turn that idea into a line of maths. Here **w** is the weight lifted and **r** is the number of reps done close to failure.

- **Epley**: 1RM = w × (1 + r ÷ 30). Each rep adds one thirtieth of the weight.
- **Brzycki**: 1RM = w × 36 ÷ (37 − r). The gap between 37 and your reps shrinks as reps go up, so the estimate climbs faster on long sets.

For sets of about 2 to 10 reps the two land close together, and the tool shows their average as the main number. A single rep needs no estimate: both formulas give the weight itself.

The tool then works backwards for the table. Each row is a percentage of the average, from 100% down to 50% in steps of 5. The **About reps** column flips Epley around, reps ≈ 30 × (100 ÷ percent − 1), to suggest how many reps that weight allows. It is a rough guide, because real people tire in different ways.

## How to use it

1. Type the **Weight lifted** and choose kg or lb. Changing the unit converts the number already in the box.
2. Type the **Reps done** in that set. Use a set you pushed close to failure; an easy set gives a low estimate.
3. Read the **Epley**, **Brzycki** and **Average** results. The line below repeats your set and both formulas.
4. Pick **Round the table to**: no rounding, or the nearest 1, 2.5 or 5, to match the plates in your gym.
5. Read the **Percentage table** to find your working weights.
6. Press **Reset** to return to the starting example.

## A worked example

You bench press 60 kg for 8 hard reps.

- Epley: 60 × (1 + 8 ÷ 30) = 60 × 1.2667 = **76.0 kg**
- Brzycki: 60 × 36 ÷ (37 − 8) = 2,160 ÷ 29 = **74.5 kg**
- Average: (76.0 + 74.48) ÷ 2 = **75.2 kg**

Your plan asks for sets at 75% and 85%. With rounding to the nearest 2.5 kg:

- 75%: 75.24 × 0.75 = 56.4 kg, rounded to **57.5 kg**, about 10 reps.
- 85%: 75.24 × 0.85 = 64.0 kg, rounded to **65 kg**, about 5 reps.

A handy check: the 80% row is 60.2 kg, rounded to **60 kg**, with about 8 reps. That is the set you typed in, so the table agrees with itself.

The same lift in pounds: switch the unit to lb and the box shows 132.3 lb, since 60 ÷ 0.45359237 = 132.28.

## Where it is used

- **Strength plans**: most published programmes give loads as percentages, so a 1RM estimate turns them into real numbers.
- **Tracking progress**: if the weight for 5 reps rises over a few weeks, the estimate rises too, without a risky max test.
- **Coaching groups**: a coach can set one plan in percentages and let each athlete fill in their own weights.
- **Physical education and sport science**: the formulas are a clear example of a model fitted to data, and of how accuracy drops outside the range it was built for.

## Common mistakes

1. **Using a long set.** Above 12 reps the tool shows a warning, because endurance starts to matter more than strength. At 15 reps with 60 kg, Epley says 90.0 kg and Brzycki 98.2 kg, a gap of over 8 kg. Use a set of 3 to 10 reps.
2. **Using an easy set.** If you stopped with several reps left, the estimate comes out low. Count only sets close to failure, with good form.
3. **Mixing exercises.** A squat estimate says nothing about your deadlift. Work out each lift on its own.
4. **Training at 100% every week.** The top row is an estimate of your limit, not a training weight. Most work happens well below it.
5. **Forgetting the rounding.** If your gym has only 5 kg steps, choose that rounding, or the table will show weights you cannot load.

## Questions people ask

**Why do Epley and Brzycki give different numbers?** They were fitted in different ways, and they bend differently as reps rise. Their average sits between the two.

**Can I use it for machines or dumbbells?** Yes, the maths is the same, but machines and dumbbells feel different from a barbell, so treat the table as a starting point.

**Does it matter if I lift in pounds?** No. Pick lb and every result and table row is shown in pounds.

**Should I test my real max instead?** That is your choice to make with a coach. A real test needs a warm-up, safety bars or a spotter, and rest afterwards.
