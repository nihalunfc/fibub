## What it is

A **molecular formula** such as C₅H₁₀O only counts atoms. It says nothing about how they are joined. **Isomers** are different compounds that share a molecular formula. When the atoms are connected in a different order, they are **structural isomers**. Structural isomers come in four kinds:

- **Chain isomers** have different carbon skeletons: one straight chain, another branched.
- **Position isomers** share a skeleton and a family, but the functional group (or a double bond) sits on a different carbon.
- **Functional isomers** belong to different families altogether, such as an aldehyde and a ketone, or an alcohol and an ether.
- **Metamers** are ethers or esters whose carbon groups are split differently on the two sides of the oxygen.

Two further marks appear on some cards. **Cis and trans** means the compound has a C=C double bond with two different groups on each end, so it exists as two **geometric isomers** that cannot turn into each other without breaking the bond. **Chiral carbon** means one carbon holds four different groups, so the molecule has a mirror-image twin it cannot be laid on top of: a pair of **optical isomers**.

The tool finds every carbon tree, then every place for the double bond or oxygen, and drops repeats. Rings are left out.

## A worked example

Pick **C₅H₁₀O (aldehydes and ketones)**. The page finds **7** open-chain isomers.

Aldehydes (–CHO at the end of a chain):

1. pentanal, CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CHO
2. 2-methylbutanal, CH₃–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CHO
3. 3-methylbutanal, CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CHO
4. 2,2-dimethylpropanal, CH₃–C(CH₃)₂–CHO

Ketones (C=O inside the chain):

5. pentan-2-one, CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–C(=O)–CH₃
6. pentan-3-one, CH₃–CH₂–C(=O)–CH₂–CH₃
7. 3-methylbutan-2-one, CH₃–CH(CH₃)–C(=O)–CH₃

That is **4 aldehydes + 3 ketones = 7**. Now compare pairs:

- **pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one**: same five-carbon chain, C=O on carbon 2 versus carbon 3, so **position isomers**.
- **2-methylbutanal and 3-methylbutanal**: same branched skeleton, the –CHO attached at a different end, so the tool also calls these **position isomers**.
- **pentanal and 2,2-dimethylpropanal**: straight chain versus a heavily branched one, so **chain isomers**.
- **pentanal and pentan-2-one**: an aldehyde and a ketone, so **functional isomers**.

Only one card carries a **chiral carbon** tag: 2-methylbutanal. Its carbon 2 holds H, CH₃, C₂H₅ and CHO, four different groups. None of the seven has a C=C, so none is marked cis and trans.

## Where it is used

- **Lab identification.** Isomers have the same mass, so a chemist confirming a product must rule out the others with boiling points or spectra. A full list shows what to rule out.
- **Medicines.** Many drugs have a chiral carbon, and the two mirror forms can act very differently in the body, which is why manufacturers test each form separately.
- **Fuels.** Branched alkane isomers burn more smoothly in engines than straight ones; octane rating is built on comparing such isomers.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting the same molecule twice.** Bending or flipping a drawing does not make a new compound. If two drawings get the same IUPAC name, they are one isomer.
- **Forgetting the other family.** C₄H₁₀O is not only alcohols; ethers fit too. Ask which families a general formula allows before you start.
- **Calling every same-family pair "position".** If the skeletons differ, the pair are chain isomers, even if the group also moved.
- **Mixing up metamers and position isomers in ethers.** For ethers, the question is how the carbons split around the oxygen (2 + 2 versus 3 + 1). That is metamerism.
- **Missing a chiral carbon.** Check each carbon for four different groups; CH₃ and C₂H₅ count as different.

## Questions people ask

**Why does the count grow so fast with more carbons?** Each extra carbon can attach in several new places, and every new skeleton gives new positions for the functional group, so the numbers multiply rather than add.

**Is a cis–trans pair one isomer or two?** Two compounds, but one structural isomer. The note under the count tells you the total when you count them separately.

**Do isomers have the same properties?** Usually not. Pentanal boils at a different temperature from pentan-3-one, and branched isomers tend to boil lower than straight ones because their molecules touch less.
