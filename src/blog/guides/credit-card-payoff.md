A credit card statement shows a small minimum payment, and paying it feels like progress. Often it is barely progress at all. The [[credit-card-payoff]] calculator runs a card balance two ways side by side: paying only the minimum each month, and paying a fixed amount you pick. It shows how long each takes, how much interest each costs and what a move to a balance-transfer card could change. If you have several debts, the [[debt-snowball]] helps you choose which to clear first, and the [[budget-calculator]] helps you find the money for a bigger payment.

## What it is

Each month the card adds interest to what you owe. The calculator works it out like this:

interest this month = balance × APR ÷ 12

- **Balance** is what you owe at the start of the month.
- **APR** is the annual percentage rate on your statement, as a decimal (20% is 0.20).

Your payment first covers that interest. Only the part left over brings the balance down.

A typical minimum is a small percentage of the balance, with a floor so it never drops below a set amount. Here is the catch: as the balance shrinks, the minimum shrinks with it. You pay less and less each month, so the balance falls more and more slowly. Most of the money goes to interest for years.

A **fixed payment** stays the same every month. As the balance falls, less of that payment goes to interest and more goes to the debt itself, so it speeds up toward the end.

A **balance transfer** moves the debt to another card that charges a low promotional rate for a set number of months. There is usually a fee, a percentage of the amount moved, which is added to the new balance. After the promo months the normal rate applies to whatever is still owed.

## How to use it

1. Type your **Card balance** and the **APR** from your statement.
2. Enter your card's minimum rule: the percentage of the balance, and the smallest amount it can be.
3. Type the **fixed payment** you could make each month.
4. To test a transfer, tick the balance-transfer box, then enter the transfer fee, the promo rate and the number of promo months. Put the new card's normal APR in the APR box, as it applies once the promo ends.
5. Read the table. For each column it gives the time to pay off, total interest, the transfer fee when used, the total you pay and the first payment. A line under the table spells out what the fixed payment saves.
6. Look at the chart of what you still owe, month by month, for both ways of paying.

## A worked example

Maya owes 4,500 on a card at 22% APR. Her card's minimum is 3% of the balance, and never less than 30. Her first-month interest is 4,500 × 0.22 ÷ 12 = 82.50.

- **Minimum only:** the first payment is 3% of 4,500, which is 135.00. Because it keeps shrinking, the card takes 181 months, about 15 years, to clear. The interest adds up to about 6,059.60, so she pays about 10,559.60 in all.
- **Fixed 200 a month:** the card is clear in 30 months (2 years 6 months). Interest is about 1,355.71 and the total paid is about 5,855.71. That saves about 4,704 in interest and more than 12 years.
- **Fixed 300 a month:** 18 months and about 810.94 in interest.

Next Maya tests a transfer: a 3% fee, 0% for 15 months, and she keeps paying 200. The fee is 135, so she starts the new card owing 4,635. She clears it in 24 months, paying about 152.62 in interest after the promo ends. Interest plus fee comes to about 287.62, against 1,355.71 without the transfer.

One warning from the same numbers: a fixed 80 a month is less than the first month's interest of 82.50, so the balance would never go down. The calculator flags this.

## Where it is used

- **Getting out of card debt:** seeing the minimum-only time in years is often what makes people pick a fixed amount.
- **Deciding on a balance transfer:** the fee and the rate after the promo can wipe out the saving if the debt is not cleared in time. Running both versions makes that clear.
- **Store cards and buy-now-pay-later plans** that charge interest on a balance work the same way, as long as you type their real rate.

## Common mistakes

1. **Spending on the card while paying it down.** The calculator assumes no new purchases. New spending resets the clock, so a payoff plan works best when the card is not also being used for new purchases.
2. **Typing the wrong minimum rule.** Some cards use interest plus 1% of the balance instead of a flat percentage. Read your statement and choose the percentage that gives the same first payment.
3. **Using the old card's APR for a transfer.** After the promo the new card's own rate applies. Put that rate in the APR box when you test a transfer.
4. **Not finishing before the promo ends.** If the balance is still large when the promo stops, the full rate returns. Check the month you would be clear against the promo months.
5. **Picking a fixed payment you cannot keep.** A smaller plan you keep beats a bigger one you drop.

## Questions people ask

**Why is my statement figure slightly different?** Cards usually work out interest daily on the balance each day, and payment dates move. The calculator uses one monthly step, so treat its figures as close estimates.

**What if a higher fixed payment becomes hard to keep up?** Many cards let people go back to the minimum, but late or missed payments can bring fees and a lower credit score in many countries. The terms are in the card agreement, so check the official source for the exact rules.

**What if I have more than one card?** Run each card here to see its own cost, then use the [[debt-snowball]] tool to set an order for clearing them together.
