To see which planets are up tonight, you need their rise, set and highest times for your exact place, plus how bright they are. Open [[sky-tonight]], set your city and the date, and the **Planets and the Moon** table lists all seven, with the ones too low or too close to the Sun greyed out.

## What you need

- **Your place.** A city from the list, your latitude and longitude, or your phone's location. The position never leaves your device.
- **The date** of the evening you plan to look. The tool treats the night as one unit, so 1 a.m. still belongs to the previous evening.
- **A view of the sky** in the direction the planets will be. Planets follow roughly the same path across the sky as the Sun and Moon, so a clear view low to the east, south (from the northern half of the world) and west matters most.

## Step by step

1. Press **Change** at the top of the page and pick your city, or type latitude and longitude, or press **Use my location**. Tick **Remember my place on this device** if you want it next time.
2. Choose the night in **Night of**, and pick the zone you want in **Times in**. It starts on your device's own zone; choose the place's local time if you are planning for somewhere else.
3. Read **The night**: sunset, the hours when it is properly dark, sunrise, and the Moon's phase with its rise and set.
4. In **Planets and the Moon**, each row gives the planet's rise, the time and height when it is highest, its set, its magnitude (**Mag**) and its constellation (**In**). The last column, **Tonight**, gives the best time to look and how high it will be then.
5. Under the table, a chart plots each visible planet's height through the night over a band that shows daylight, twilight and full dark.
6. Tap a planet's name to open its own height chart in the [[object-altitude|altitude planner]].

Two numbers decide whether you will see a planet:

- **Height (altitude)** in degrees above the horizon. 0° is the horizon, 90° is straight up. Below about 10°, trees, buildings and haze usually hide it; the tool greys out any planet that never gets above 10° during the night.
- **Magnitude**, a brightness scale where smaller numbers are brighter. Anything brighter than about 6 can be seen with the eye under a dark sky; city skies stop near 3 or 4.

## A worked example

Take Delhi on the night of 20 November 2026, with times in India Standard Time.

- The Sun sets at 17:25 and it is properly dark from 18:47 to 05:26.
- The Moon is a waxing gibbous, **81% lit**, and sets at 03:02. It will brighten the sky until then.
- **Saturn** (mag 0.5, in Cetus) is already up at sunset. It is highest at 20:57, 62° up. This is the planet for the evening.
- **Mars** (mag 0.6) and **Jupiter** (mag −2.1), both in Leo, rise just before midnight (23:50 and 23:44) and are about 70° up by 05:17, just before dawn.
- **Venus** (mag −4.8) rises at 04:07 and is 14° above the eastern horizon at 05:17: low, but by far the brightest point in the sky.
- **Mercury** is greyed out. It sits too close to the Sun to see.
- **Uranus** (mag 5.6) is up nearly all night, but only just at the eye's limit; **Neptune** (mag 7.8) needs binoculars or a telescope.

So for an early evening look, go out after 19:00 and face south for Saturn. Early risers get Jupiter, Mars and Venus together before sunrise.

## Check your answer

- **Bright, steady light.** Planets shine with a calm light, while stars near the horizon flicker. If your "planet" twinkles hard and changes colour, it is probably a star.
- **Right part of the sky.** Planets stay close to the Sun's yearly path, the line called the ecliptic, which runs through Leo, Taurus, Pisces and their neighbours, such as Cetus. A bright point far from that band is not a planet.
- **Venus and Mercury only at the ends of the night.** Their orbits are inside Earth's, so they always appear near the Sun: in the west after sunset or the east before sunrise, never high at midnight.
- **Night to night.** Check the next evening. A planet should rise and set a few minutes earlier, not jump to a new part of the sky.

## Questions people ask

**Can I see planets from a city?**
Yes. Venus, Jupiter, Mars and Saturn are bright enough to cut through light pollution. The faint outer two, Uranus and Neptune, need a dark site and optics.

**When is the best time to look at a planet?**
When it is highest, because you look through less air and the image wobbles less. The **Highest** column gives that time. If it falls in daylight, use the **Tonight** column's best time.

**How do I know when a planet is at its best for the year?**
The [[sky-events-calendar]] lists oppositions (when an outer planet is closest and brightest) and the best times to spot Mercury and Venus, month by month, and the [[moon-phase-simulator]] shows why the Moon's phase changes how dark your night will be.
