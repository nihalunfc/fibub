One level US cup of all-purpose (plain) flour weighs about 125 g. A 250 ml metric cup holds about 132 g and an old UK cup about 150 g, so it matters which cup your recipe means. The [[cups-to-grams]] converter does the sum for any amount and any of 42 ingredients, and lists the result in every unit at once.

## What you need

- **The amount in the recipe**, such as 2¼ cups or 3 tablespoons.
- **Which cup it means.** American recipes use the US cup (about 237 ml). Recipes from Australia, Canada and India often use the 250 ml metric cup. Older British books sometimes use a 284 ml cup.
- **Which flour.** Bread flour is a little heavier per cup than all-purpose; whole wheat is a little lighter.
- **A kitchen scale**, if you want to weigh the flour rather than measure it.

## Step by step

Flour is light and fluffy, so a cup of it weighs far less than a cup of water. To change a volume into a weight you need the flour's weight per cup:

**Grams = number of cups × grams per cup**

For any other measure, first find the grams per millilitre (125 g ÷ 236.588 ml = 0.528 g/ml for all-purpose flour), then multiply by the size of your measure in ml.

**Grams = volume in ml × 0.528**

## A worked example

A cookie recipe from a US website asks for 2¼ cups of all-purpose flour, and you want to weigh it.

- 2.25 × 125 g = 281.25 g, which the tool shows as **281 g**.

Your friend in Canada has the same recipe written with metric cups. Each 250 ml cup of the same flour weighs 250 × 0.528, which is about **132 g**, so 2¼ metric cups is about 297 g. That is 16 g more than the US version: not a disaster for cookies, but enough to make a cake a little dry.

A spoon is small, but the same rule applies. One US tablespoon (14.787 ml) of all-purpose flour weighs 14.787 × 0.528, about **7.8 g**.

If the recipe used bread flour, 2¼ US cups would weigh 2.25 × 137 = 308 g; with whole wheat flour, 2.25 × 120 = 270 g.

## Check your answer

- **Quick check:** 8 US cups of all-purpose flour make about 1 kg (8 × 125 = 1,000 g). Half a cup is about 62 g; a quarter cup about 31 g.
- **Compare with water.** A cup of water weighs about 237 g. Flour should come out at roughly half that. If your answer is close to 237 g per cup, you probably picked water or milk by mistake.
- **Weigh one cup yourself.** Spoon flour into your own cup, level it with a knife and weigh it. If you get 140 g or more, you are packing it down; if you get much under 120 g, you sifted it first. Either way, use your own figure next time.

## Questions people ask

**Why does scooping give more flour than spooning?**
Dipping the cup into the bag presses the flour together, so more of it fits. That can add 10 to 20%: 2¼ scooped cups may weigh about 320 g instead of 281 g. For a fair cup, spoon the flour in loosely and sweep the top flat.

**Why do bakers prefer grams to cups?**
A gram is the same in every kitchen, while a cup depends on the cup, the flour and the hand filling it. Weighing also means one bowl on the scale instead of a stack of measuring cups to wash.

**Should I sift before or after measuring?**
Follow the wording. "1 cup sifted flour" means sift first, then measure, which gives a lighter cup. "1 cup flour, sifted" means measure first and sift afterwards, so the weight stays at a normal level cup.
