To set a freelance hourly rate, work backwards: decide the yearly take-home you need, gross it up for tax, add your business costs, and divide by the hours you can actually invoice in a year. The [[freelance-rate]] calculator does each step and shows how the rate moves if you bill more or fewer hours. Once you have a number, [[invoice-maker]] turns it into a bill.

## What you need

- **The take-home pay you want in a year**, after tax. A household budget is the honest place to get this.
- **Yearly business costs**: laptop and software, insurance, a phone line, accounting fees, a desk at a shared office, training.
- **The share you set aside for tax and savings**, as a percentage of what you bill. Income tax and social contributions for the self-employed differ widely by country, so check the official source or ask an accountant.
- **Weeks you will not bill**: holidays, public holidays, sick days, and the quiet weeks between clients.
- **Billable hours in a normal week**: only hours a client pays for. Emails, quotes, bookkeeping and finding work are real hours but nobody is invoiced for them.
- **Days you work each week**, to turn the hourly figure into a day rate.

## Step by step

The method in three lines:

**Billing needed = take-home ÷ (1 − tax share) + business costs**

**Billable hours a year = (52 − weeks off) × billable hours a week**

**Hourly rate = billing needed ÷ billable hours a year**

Dividing by (1 − tax share) is the key move. If you keep 25% aside, you take home only 75% of each sale, so the take-home has to be divided by 0.75, not multiplied by 1.25.

## A worked example

Tom is a freelance illustrator.

- He wants **45,000** a year to take home.
- His costs are **4,500** a year (software, a drawing tablet replacement fund, insurance, an accountant).
- He sets aside **25%** for tax.
- He plans **6 weeks** off: holidays plus a cushion for sick days and gaps.
- He expects **25 billable hours** a week over **5 days**.

Billing needed = 45,000 ÷ 0.75 + 4,500 = 60,000 + 4,500 = **64,500**.

Working weeks = 52 − 6 = 46. Billable hours = 46 × 25 = **1,150**.

Hourly rate = 64,500 ÷ 1,150 = **about 56.09**. A day of 5 billable hours is about **280.43**.

The table shows how much hangs on the hours guess. At 20 billable hours a week the rate rises to about 70.11; at 30 hours it falls to about 46.74. If Tom is new and not sure clients will keep him busy, the 20-hour row is the safer place to start.

To see how 56 an hour compares with a staff job, the [[hourly-salary|salary converter]] can turn an employee's yearly salary into an hourly figure. Remember that the employee's number already includes paid holidays and an employer paying for the laptop.

## Check your answer

- **Run it forwards.** 1,150 hours × 56.09 is about 64,500. Take off 4,500 of costs to leave 60,000; keep 75% of that and you have 45,000. Back where you started.
- **Compare with a rough rule.** Some freelancers start from "a salary divided by 1,000 gives an hourly rate". 45,000 ÷ 1,000 = 45, below Tom's 56, because his tax share and costs are higher than that shortcut assumes. A result wildly above or below it is worth a second look at your inputs.
- **Check the hours are believable.** 1,150 billable hours is about 25 a week. Track a month of real work; if you bill fewer hours, the rate must rise to match.

## Questions people ask

**Should I charge by the hour or by the project?** Work out the hourly rate either way. For a fixed-price job, estimate the hours, multiply by your rate, then add a buffer for changes. The hourly figure is your floor.

**Is the tax share the same as my tax rate?** Not quite. Use your best estimate of income tax plus any self-employed contributions on profit, and some people add a slice for pension savings too. Rules differ by country, so check the official source. This is general education, not tax advice.

**What if the market rate is lower than my number?** Then one of the inputs has to give: fewer weeks off, lower costs, more billable hours, a lower take-home, or a niche where clients pay more. Seeing which lever moves the rate most is the point of the exercise.
