The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to **Henri B. Kagan** of Paris and **Kenso Soai** of Tokyo for discoveries about molecules that come in mirror-image pairs: how a catalyst's purity can be magnified in its products (Kagan), and how a reaction can copy its own handedness until only one form is left (Soai). To see why that matters, this first part goes back to the start: what "handed" molecules are, why life uses only one hand, and how chemists learned to make the hand they want. You can explore each idea with the [[chirality-checker|R/S checker]], the [[racemisation-age|Racemisation age]] tool and our new [[chiral-amplification]].

<div class="nb-short">

**In short**
- Many molecules exist as two forms that are **mirror images** but cannot be laid on top of each other, like your hands. Such molecules are **chiral**.
- Life builds proteins from only **left-handed amino acids** and uses only **right-handed sugars**. Nobody knows for sure how that one-handedness, called **homochirality**, began.
- The two forms can smell different and act differently as **medicines**, so chemists want to make just one. The tools for that, **asymmetric catalysts**, won the 2001 Nobel Prize. Kagan and Soai found surprising ways such reactions can **amplify** a small preference.

</div>

## Hands, gloves and molecules

Hold your hands palm to palm: they are mirror images. Now try to place your left hand exactly on top of your right, both palms down. The thumbs end up on opposite sides. No turning will make them match. Objects like that are called **chiral**, from the Greek *kheir*, hand.

Molecules can be chiral too. The most common reason is a carbon atom bonded to **four different groups**. Its four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, and the four groups can be arranged in two ways that are mirror images. Try it: drag the molecule on the left and try to make every ball sit on the same colour on the right.

You will never manage it with four different groups. Switch to "two groups the same" and a perfect match appears: the molecule is no longer chiral. The two forms of a chiral molecule are called **enantiomers**. Chemists name them **R** and **S** with priority rules (the [[chirality-checker|R/S checker]] walks you through them), or for amino acids and sugars **L** and **D**.

## How chemists first noticed

- **1815:** Jean-Baptiste Biot found that some liquids, like turpentine and sugar solutions, rotate the plane of polarised light. One form of a chiral molecule turns it clockwise, its mirror twin the same amount anticlockwise. A 50:50 mixture (a **racemic** mixture) does not turn it at all.
- **1848:** the young Louis Pasteur looked at crystals of a salt of tartaric acid, made in a factory, under a microscope. He saw two kinds of crystal, mirror images of each other, and sorted them by hand with tweezers. Dissolved, one kind turned light one way, the other kind the other way. The molecules themselves had to be handed.
- **1874:** Jacobus van 't Hoff and Joseph Le Bel independently explained why: carbon's four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron. (Van 't Hoff received the first ever Nobel Prize in Chemistry, in 1901, for other work.)
- **1894:** Lord Kelvin coined the word *chirality*.

## Why it matters: life, smells and medicines

Mirror-image molecules have the same boiling point, the same colour, the same everything, until they meet something that is itself chiral. And living things are chiral all the way down. Enzymes, receptors and DNA are built from one-handed parts, so they tell the two forms apart, just as a right-handed glove fits only a right hand.

- **Smell:** one form of the molecule carvone smells of spearmint, its mirror image of caraway seeds.
- **Medicines:** often only one form does the job. The other may do nothing, or something harmful. The tragedy of **thalidomide** (sold 1957–61) is often told this way: one form calmed morning sickness, the other caused birth defects. The full story is harsher still, because thalidomide's two forms turn into each other inside the body, so even a pure form would not have been safe. It made regulators demand that both forms of a new drug be tested, and today most new chiral drugs are sold as a single form.
- **Life itself:** proteins use only **L-amino acids** and DNA and RNA only **D-sugars**. Life without this one-handedness probably could not work: a protein chain built from a random mix of hands would not fold properly. How the first excess of one hand arose before life began is one of the great open questions in science, and it is exactly where Soai's discovery (part 3) comes in.

Life keeps its molecules one-handed only while it keeps repairing and replacing them. Once that stops, the molecules slowly flip, back towards a 50:50 mix. That slow drift is a clock, used to date fossils and even to estimate a person's age from their teeth. We plot it below with the [[racemisation-age|Racemisation age]] tool's maths.

## Measuring how pure: enantiomeric excess

Chemists describe a mixture of two enantiomers by its **enantiomeric excess (ee)**:

ee = (R − S) ÷ (R + S)

A 50:50 mixture has ee = 0 %. A 75:25 mixture has ee = 50 %. A pure sample has ee = 100 %. Optical rotation is proportional to ee, which is how ee was measured for a century; today chromatography on a chiral column separates and counts the two forms directly.

## Making one hand on purpose

There are three ways to get a single enantiomer:

1. **Separate them** after making both, as Pasteur did (today usually with a chiral partner that forms separable salts). At least half the material is wasted.
2. **Start from nature's own chiral molecules**, such as amino acids and sugars (the "chiral pool").
3. **Use a chiral catalyst** that steers a reaction towards one form, so that a little catalyst makes a lot of the wanted product. This is **asymmetric catalysis**.

The third way is the most elegant, and it took off around 1970. Using metals such as rhodium surrounded by chiral organic molecules ("ligands"), chemists made reactions that gave mostly one hand. In 1971 Henri Kagan, then in Orsay near Paris, introduced **DIOP**, a ligand made from tartaric acid (Pasteur's molecule) with two identical halves related by a two-fold rotation. That "C₂ symmetry" cut down the number of competing ways the reaction could go, and became a design rule used for decades. William Knowles at Monsanto used a related rhodium catalyst to make **L-DOPA**, a drug for Parkinson's disease, on an industrial scale. Ryoji Noyori made the famous BINAP catalysts, and Barry Sharpless found catalysts that add oxygen to one face of a molecule. Knowles, Noyori and Sharpless shared the 2001 Nobel Prize.

How can a catalyst choose? Both hands of the product are made through a high-energy "transition state", the top of an energy hill. A chiral catalyst makes the hill to one hand a little lower than the hill to the other. Because reaction rates depend exponentially on hill height, a small difference goes a long way. Try it:

<details class="deeper"><summary>The exact relation, and why cooling helps</summary>

From transition-state theory each path's rate is proportional to exp(−ΔG‡/RT). The ratio of the two rates, the enantiomeric ratio, is er = exp(ΔΔG‡/RT), where ΔΔG‡ is the difference in hill heights. Then ee = (er − 1)/(er + 1) = tanh(ΔΔG‡/2RT). At 25 °C, RT is about 2.48 kJ/mol, so 98 % ee needs ΔΔG‡ ≈ 11.4 kJ/mol, about the energy of a weak hydrogen bond. At −78 °C (dry ice) the same 98 % needs only 7.5 kJ/mol, which is why so many selective reactions are run cold. The ratio also explains why a catalyst's own purity should matter in a simple way: a catalyst that is 50 % R and 50 % S makes 50 % of each product. That simple expectation, that the product's ee is proportional to the catalyst's ee, is exactly what Kagan found can break down (part 2).
</details>

## Checked with the tools

**1. Life's one-handedness fades after death, and the clock runs on temperature.** Aspartic acid in the dentin of living people's teeth flips from L to D at about 0.08 % a year at body temperature (Helfman and Bada, 1975). With the [[racemisation-age|Racemisation age]] tool's reversible first-order kinetics, after 70 years the D/L ratio has risen from about 0.01 to **0.068**: the basis of forensic age estimates from teeth. Moving the rate to a cool 10 °C burial with the Arrhenius equation (activation energy assumed 130 kJ/mol) slows it more than a hundredfold: D/L takes about **50,000 years** to reach 0.33 (a 75:25 mix).

## How we made the graphs

`scripts/blog-figures/nobel-chemistry-2026.mjs` imports `ratioAt()` and `arrhenius()` from `src/lib/sci/racemisation.ts` (the Racemisation age tool) and `R_GAS` from `src/lib/sci/kinetics.ts` (the Reaction kinetics fitter). The aspartic acid rate is Helfman and Bada's, rounded; the activation energy is our assumption, typical of published values for aspartic acid. Everything else is calculated.

## How we test the maths

`tests/sci-tools/racemisation-age.mjs` checks the racemisation equations against a direct numerical solution of the rate equations (SciPy's ODE solver), both ways (a D/L ratio turned into an age and back), the Arrhenius shift, and the uncertainty propagation against finite differences and NumPy Monte Carlo runs. The R/S checker is tested on the standard textbook molecules (lactic acid, alanine, glyceraldehyde, butan-2-ol). These checks run on every change.

## Check it yourself

1. Open the [[chirality-checker|R/S checker]], pick a molecule such as alanine, and press **Mirror image**. The letter flips between R and S; press **Swap two groups** and it flips again.
2. Open the [[racemisation-age|Racemisation age]] tool. Set **Rate constant** to **I know k**, type **Rate constant k (per year)** 0.00083 and **Temperature that k is for (°C)** 37.
3. Set **Effective temperature of your sample (°C)** to 37, **D/L at time zero** to 0.01 and **Sample D/L ratio** to 0.068. The age should come out close to **70 years**.
4. Now change the sample temperature to 10 °C and **Activation energy Ea (kJ/mol)** to 130, and set the Sample D/L ratio to 0.33. The age jumps to tens of thousands of years.
5. Work the energy gap by hand: at 25 °C, RT = 0.008314 × 298.15 ≈ 2.48 kJ/mol. For ΔΔG‡ = 11.4, er = e^(11.4/2.48) ≈ 99, so ee = 98/100 = 98 %.

## What you can use it for

- **Students:** check R/S assignments for homework with the [[chirality-checker|R/S checker]], and understand why ee and optical rotation go together.
- **Archaeology and forensics:** amino acid racemisation dating, with uncertainties, in the [[racemisation-age|Racemisation age]] tool.
- **Synthesis planning:** estimate how much colder a reaction must run to reach a target ee, from the energy gap at one temperature.
