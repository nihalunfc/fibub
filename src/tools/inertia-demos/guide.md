## What it does

Inertia is the habit of every object to keep doing what it is already doing: stay still, or keep moving in a straight line at the same speed. This page shows three everyday cases and lets you change the numbers. It is a teaching model with round numbers, not a measurement of a real coin or bus.

In the coin demo a coin sits on a card over a glass. In the bus demo you see which way a passenger leans. In the third demo you push blocks of different mass. When you are ready, step on to [[newtons-laws]] for the force rules behind this, or [[collision-carts]] to see inertia in crashes.

## How to use it

1. Choose a demo at the top.
2. Move the slider and read the sentence under the picture.
3. Switch to Class 11 for the extra numbers.
4. Answer the quiz questions. They change each time.

## What each setting does

- **Card speed**: how fast the card is pulled. Below about 0.35 m/s the coin rides along; above that the card escapes and the coin drops.
- **Bus acceleration**: positive means speeding up, negative means braking. The lean is always opposite to the acceleration.
- **Mass of the block**: a bigger mass is harder to get moving, so it takes longer to reach the same speed with the same push.

## Worked example

A 10 kg block gets a 10 N push. Acceleration = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 m/s², so it takes 1 s to reach 1 m/s. A 20 kg block takes 2 s. Same push, double the mass, double the time.

## FAQs

**Why do I lean forward when the bus brakes?** Your feet are slowed by the floor, but the rest of your body keeps its old speed for a moment.

**Is inertia a force?** No. It is a property of matter. A force is needed to change the motion.

**Does a heavier object have more inertia?** Yes. Mass is the measure of inertia.

**Why does the coin need a fast flick?** A fast flick means friction pushes the coin sideways for a very short time, so the coin barely moves sideways and gravity pulls it down.
