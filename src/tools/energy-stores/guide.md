## What it does

Energy is never used up. It moves from one store to another, and some of it always ends up in places we do not want, usually as heat in the surroundings. This tool shows that for a handful of everyday devices. You choose a device and the energy put in, and it draws a simple energy transfer diagram where the width of each arrow matches the amount of energy, then works out how much was useful, how much was wasted and the efficiency.

The efficiencies are rounded typical values for teaching, so a real lamp or engine can differ. To follow energy as it changes form in a moving system, use [[energy-track]], and for the power side of the story see [[power-bill]] and [[spring-energy]].

## How to use it

1. Choose a device.
2. Set the energy supplied in joules.
3. On GCSE, set the time too to see the power going in and the useful power coming out.
4. Read the useful and wasted energy and the efficiency, and look at the widths in the diagram.
5. Answer the questions at the bottom.

## What each setting does

- **Device**: sets the share of the input that ends up as useful energy.
- **Energy in**: the total energy supplied, in joules.
- **Time (GCSE)**: the seconds over which the energy is supplied. Power is energy divided by time.
- **KS3 / GCSE**: KS3 shows the stores and the diagram. GCSE adds efficiency as a percentage and the power values.

## Worked example

An electric kettle takes in 1000 J of electrical energy. About 90% goes into heating the water, so the useful output is 900 J and 100 J is wasted, mostly warming the kettle and the air. Efficiency = 900 ÷ 1000 = 0.90, or 90%. If it takes 2 s, the input power is 500 W and the useful power is 450 W. A filament lamp given the same 1000 J turns only about 100 J into light, so 900 J is wasted as heat.

## FAQs

**Can a device be more than 100% efficient?** No. That would create energy, which cannot happen. If a calculation gives more than 100%, a number has been put in the wrong place.

**Where does wasted energy go?** It spreads out into the surroundings, mostly as thermal energy, which makes it hard to collect and use again.

**Is efficiency a percentage or a decimal?** Either. 0.90 and 90% are the same. Exams usually accept both if the working is clear.

**Why are the values only typical?** Real devices differ with age, design and how they are used, so the figures here are only for practising the idea.
