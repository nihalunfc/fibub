## What it does

Some salts hold water inside their crystals in a fixed ratio. This **water of crystallisation** is written after a dot: blue copper(II) sulfate is CuSO₄·5H₂O, which means five water molecules for every CuSO₄. Heat the crystals and the water leaves as steam; the salt left behind (the **anhydrous** salt) often changes colour, and it weighs less.

This page is a safe virtual version of the school practical, followed by the calculation.

- **Virtual practical.** Choose a hydrated salt and how many grams to add. Weigh the empty crucible, then the crucible with the crystals. Press **Heat, cool and weigh** again and again: the balance reading falls, quickly at first and then by less each time, until two weighings agree. That is **constant mass**, and it means all the water has gone. The crystals change colour as they dry (blue to white for copper sulfate, pink to blue for cobalt chloride).
- **Find x.** The page fills in the three weighings and works out the mass of water, the moles of water, the moles of dry salt and their ratio, x. You can also type weighings from a real lab.
- **How much is water?** A table gives each hydrated salt's molar mass and its percentage of water by mass.

It fits the Class 10 science chapter on acids, bases and salts (crystals of salts, washing soda, plaster of Paris) and the Class 11 mole concept. Moles and grams are linked in [[mole-bridge]], molar masses of any formula come from [[molar-mass-calculator]], and the salts' acidic or basic nature is in [[ph-scale-explorer]].

## How to use it

1. Pick a salt and the mass of crystals (0.5 g to 10 g).
2. Press **Weigh crucible and crystals**.
3. Press **Heat, cool and weigh** until the page says constant mass. Each line of the table is one weighing.
4. Read the steps in **Find x**. To use your own lab results, type the three masses.
5. **Start again** gives a fresh run (changing the salt does too).

## A worked example

Say the crucible weighs 25.00 g, 27.50 g with copper(II) sulfate crystals, and 26.60 g after heating to constant mass.

- Hydrated salt = 2.50 g; dry CuSO₄ = 1.60 g; water = 0.90 g.
- Moles of water = 0.90 ÷ 18.02 = 0.0500 mol.
- Moles of CuSO₄ = 1.60 ÷ 159.60 = 0.0100 mol.
- x = 0.0500 ÷ 0.0100 = 4.98, so the formula is CuSO₄·5H₂O.

## Tips

- Heat to constant mass. If some water is left, the dry mass is too big and x comes out too small.
- Let the crucible cool in a dry place before weighing, or it takes water back from the air (and a hot crucible gives a wrong reading).
- Gypsum heated to about 373 K gives plaster of Paris, CaSO₄·½H₂O. Add water and it sets back into hard gypsum, which is why plasters and moulds work.

## Check yourself

Three fresh questions each time: x from two weighings, the mass of water in a sample, and the percentage of water in a hydrated salt. Nothing is saved.

## FAQ

**Are the weighings real?** No. The heating run is a model that removes the same share of the remaining water each time, rounded like a 0.01 g balance. Real runs are messier.

**Is this from the NCERT book?** It covers the same topic with fibub's own examples. fibub is not part of NCERT.
