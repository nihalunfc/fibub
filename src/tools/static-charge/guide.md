## What it does

Everything is made of atoms with positive protons and negative electrons, and a thing with equal amounts of both is **neutral**. When two different materials are **rubbed**, a few electrons move from one to the other. The one that gains electrons becomes negative, and the one that loses them becomes positive. Here a balloon rubbed on wool gains electrons, so the balloon shows minus signs and the wool shows the same number of plus signs. Rubbing never makes charge, it only moves it, so the total stays zero.

A second picture hangs two balloons side by side. **Like charges repel and unlike charges attract**, so they swing apart or together. It follows the Class 8 Science topic on forces, where friction and electrostatic force are called contact and non-contact forces. For another push-and-pull picture try [[tug-of-war-forces]], and for the maths of the force use [[coulomb-forces]].

## How to use it

1. Slide **Rubs of balloon A** and watch charges collect on the balloon and the wool.
2. Tick **Scatter small bits of paper** to see them jump to a charged balloon.
3. Choose what happens to **Balloon B** and watch the two balloons swing.

## What each setting does

- **Rubs of balloon A**: each rub moves one more unit of charge from wool to balloon.
- **Balloon B**: negative (rubbed on wool), positive (electrons rubbed off) or not rubbed at all.
- **Rubs of balloon B**: how much charge B has.

## Worked example

Rub balloon A five times: A has −5 and the wool has +5, and −5 + 5 = 0. If B is also rubbed on wool 5 times, both balloons are −5, the product (−5) × (−5) = +25 is positive, so they repel.

## FAQs

**Why do the paper bits jump?** The charged balloon pulls the nearby side of each bit, so it moves up. Real paper is neutral overall, which this model only hints at.

**Why does it work best on dry days?** Moist air lets charge leak away; this model ignores that.

**Are the numbers real?** No. Real balloons hold billions of electrons, and the picture is exaggerated.
