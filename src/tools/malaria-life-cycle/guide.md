## What it shows

Malaria is caused by a single-celled parasite, **Plasmodium**, that needs two hosts to complete its life: a **human** and a **female Anopheles mosquito**. This page draws the whole cycle as a loop, with the human side on the left and the mosquito side on the right. Tap a stage, or use **Back** and **Next**, to follow the parasite from one bite to the next:

1. sporozoites enter with the mosquito's saliva;
2. they multiply in **liver cells**;
3. merozoites invade **red blood cells**;
4. the red blood cells burst together and release **haemozoin**: chills and high fever;
5. some parasites become male and female **gametocytes**;
6. a mosquito sucks them up with blood;
7. the gametes **fuse in the mosquito's gut** (fertilisation);
8. the zygote forms an oocyst on the stomach wall, which makes thousands of sporozoites;
9. the sporozoites move to the **salivary glands**, ready for the next bite.

A second card shows **why the fever comes back in a rhythm**: the red blood cells burst at about the same time, every 48 hours for P. vivax and every 72 hours for P. malariae.

It fits **Class 12 Biology: human health and disease** (common diseases in humans: malaria, the pathogen, its two hosts, symptoms and prevention). How the body fights back is in [[immune-response]]; the cells involved are drawn in [[cell-explorer]], and how a disease spreads through a whole town is in [[epidemic-model]].

## How to use it

1. Read stage 1, then press **Next** to move round the loop. The current stage turns red; its host and place are named under the picture.

3. In the fever card, switch between **P. vivax** and **P. malariae** and read the fever days.

## Worked example

P. vivax: red blood cells burst every 48 hours. If the first attack of fever is on day 1, the next are 48, 96, 144 … hours later, so on days **1, 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11** in the first 12 days. That "every third day, counting the first" pattern is why it was called *tertian* fever. P. malariae bursts every 72 hours: days **1, 4, 7 and 10** (*quartan* fever).

## Which host is which?

The parasite reproduces **sexually** (gametes fuse) only inside the mosquito, so the mosquito is the **primary (definitive) host**. In the human it only multiplies asexually, so the human is the **secondary (intermediate) host**.

## Breaking the loop

Every stage is a chance to stop malaria: bed nets and repellents stop bites (stages 1 and 6), draining standing water and keeping fish that eat larvae (such as Gambusia) cut mosquito numbers, and treating patients removes the gametocytes that mosquitoes would pick up.

## FAQ

**Do male mosquitoes spread malaria?** No. Only females take blood meals, so only they pick up and pass on the parasite.

**Is this medical advice?** No. It is a simplified study diagram. Anyone with a fever in or after visiting a malaria area should see a doctor quickly.

**Is this from my textbook?** It covers the standard school topic with its own words and diagram. It is an independent study aid.
