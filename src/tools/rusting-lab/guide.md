## What it does

Iron and steel slowly turn into flaky reddish-brown rust. This page is a safe, fast version of the classic test-tube experiment that finds out what rusting needs. Six clean iron nails sit in six tubes:

- **A. Dry air:** a corked tube with calcium chloride to soak up moisture. Air, no water.
- **B. No air:** boiled water (which has lost its dissolved air) under a layer of oil. Water, no air.
- **C. Air and water:** ordinary tap water, open to the air.
- **D. Salt water:** the same, with salt dissolved in it.
- **E. Painted:** a painted nail in tap water.
- **F. Galvanised:** a zinc-coated nail in tap water.

Drag the **day** slider or press **Watch 3 weeks** and rust spots grow on the nails that can rust. The results table fills in with yes/no for air and water and how much rust there is. Tick **Scratch the coated nails** to see the big difference between paint and zinc: the painted nail rusts where the paint is broken, while the galvanised nail stays clean because zinc, being more reactive than iron, corrodes first (sacrificial protection).

A second card gives the equation for rust and works out the mass of iron(III) oxide made from any mass of iron, showing that a rusted nail gets heavier.

It fits the Class 10 science chapters on chemical reactions (corrosion, an effect of oxidation) and on metals and non-metals (corrosion and how to prevent it). Rusting is a redox reaction you can sort in the [[reaction-type-sorter]]; why zinc protects iron comes from the [[reactivity-series]].

## How to use it

1. Look at the six tubes on day 0: six shiny nails.
2. Drag the slider from day 0 to day 21, or press **Watch 3 weeks** (press again to pause). **Back to day 0** starts again.
3. Read the results table: tube, air, water, and how much rust (as a description and a percentage of the surface).
4. Tick **Scratch the coated nails** and watch tubes E and F.
5. In the second card, type a mass of iron to see the mass of rust it makes.

The speeds are a teaching model with made-up rates. Real nails vary with temperature, the steel used and how clean they are, but the pattern is the one you would see in a school lab.

## A worked example

At **day 7** in the model, nail C (air and water) has some rust over about 39% of its surface and nail D (salt water) is covered (about 75%). Nails A, B, E and F have none. By **day 21**, C is at 77% and D at 99%.

The rust card starts with 5.6 g of iron: 5.6 ÷ 55.845 = 0.1003 mol of iron, which makes half as many moles of Fe₂O₃, 0.05014 mol. Multiplying by its molar mass of 159.687 g/mol gives 8.007 g of iron(III) oxide. The extra 2.407 g is oxygen taken from the air. Iron is 69.94% of Fe₂O₃ by mass.

## Check yourself

Four fresh questions each time: which of two nails rusts first, the mass of rust from a mass of iron (with Fe = 56 and O = 16), the oxidation number of iron in rust, and whether a rusted nail is heavier. Nothing is saved.

## FAQ

**Why does boiled water need oil on top?** Boiling drives out the air dissolved in water. Without the oil, air would dissolve back in within hours and the nail would rust.

**Is rusting the same as corrosion?** Rusting is the corrosion of iron. Other metals corrode too: silver goes black, copper goes green. Aluminium forms a thin oxide layer that protects it.

**Why does salt speed it up?** Salt makes the water conduct electricity better, which helps the electron-transfer reactions of rusting. That is why cars rust faster where roads are salted and why ships need extra protection.
