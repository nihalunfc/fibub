## What it does

A **cyclone** is a huge storm that spins around a centre of very low pressure. This page shows one being born, seen from above. Air from all around rushes toward a low over the sea. Because the Earth is spinning, moving air is turned sideways: to the **right** in the northern hemisphere and to the **left** in the southern. This is the **Coriolis effect**. Instead of reaching the middle, the air circles around it, **anticlockwise** north of the equator and **clockwise** south of it.

Over a warm sea, water evaporates into the air. When that moist air rises and its vapour turns back into cloud and rain, it gives out heat, which keeps the air rising and the pressure falling. The storm feeds itself, cloud bands spiral in, and an **eye** forms in the middle. Move it over cool water, switch off the Earth's spin or put it on the equator, and it fades.

It follows the school topics on storms: Class 8 Science, pressure, winds, storms and cyclones (how cyclones form over warm seas, why the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea get them, staying safe), and Class 11 Geography, atmospheric circulation and weather systems (the Coriolis force, conditions for tropical cyclones, their structure and eye). ## How to use it

1. Press **Start again** to begin with still air around a weak low. Each streak is a parcel of air, pointing the way it moves.
2. Set the **latitude**: 15° N is about as far north as the middle of the Bay of Bengal; negative values are south of the equator.
3. Set the **sea surface temperature**. The sea's colour gets warmer as you raise it.
4. Untick **Earth spins (Coriolis effect)** to see what the air would do on a planet that does not spin.
5. Read the strength bar, which way the air is circling and why the storm is growing or fading. At **Class 11** the page adds the Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sin φ.

## Worked example

At the start the page is set to **15° N** with the sea at **29 °C** and the Earth spinning. The air begins to circle **anticlockwise**, and within about 15 to 20 seconds the strength bar passes the middle and the label reads **Growing: a cyclone**, with spiral cloud bands and an eye.

At Class 11 the page shows f = 2 × 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ × sin 15° = **3.775 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹**. Move to **15° S** and press Start again: now f is negative and the storm spins **clockwise**. Move to **0°**: f = 0, the air runs straight into the low and the label says it is too close to the equator.

Cool the sea to **25 °C** and the storm fades: the page explains that below about 26.5 °C there is not enough warm, moist air to feed it. That is also why cyclones weaken quickly once they cross the coast.

## Tips

- The Coriolis effect is not a push from anything. It is what straight-line motion looks like from a spinning Earth.
- Cyclones over the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea are most common before and after the monsoon, when the sea is warmest.
- When a cyclone warning is given, listen to the official weather service and move to a safe shelter if told to.

## FAQ

**Do bathtubs and sinks drain the other way in the south?** Not reliably. The Coriolis effect is far too weak over a few seconds and a few centimetres; the shape of the basin and how the water was poured matter much more. It only wins for things that last hours and stretch hundreds of kilometres, like cyclones.

**Are the strength numbers real?** No. The strength bar and its words (weak low, growing storm, cyclone, strong cyclone) are a cartoon, not the official categories of a weather service.

**Is anything stored?** No. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is saved or sent.
