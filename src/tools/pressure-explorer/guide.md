## What it does

Push a drawing pin into a board with your thumb and it goes in easily, yet you could push just as hard with your palm and leave no mark. The difference is **pressure**: how much force lands on each square metre. This page presses two objects into a slab of pretend soft clay with exactly the **same force**, side by side. A needle point puts the whole force on a tiny spot, so the pressure is enormous and it slides into the clay; a flat palm spreads the same force over a big area and hardly marks it.

The numbers update as you go: the pressure under each object, how many times bigger one is than the other, and the sum **P = F ÷ A** with your force and area filled in. A graph plots pressure against contact area for your force, with both objects marked on it, so you can see that halving the area doubles the pressure.

It follows the school topic on pressure: Class 8 Science, pressure, winds, storms and cyclones (pressure as force on an area, its unit the pascal, sharp and broad objects in daily life), and, at the **Class 11** level, thrust and pressure in the physics of fluids, with powers of ten. Next, [[liquid-pressure]] shows how the pressure in water grows with depth, and [[barometer-altitude]] shows the pressure of the air around us falling as you climb.

## How to use it

1. Choose the **left** and **right** objects: needle point, nail tip, knife edge, fingertip, flat palm, a brick on its end or lying flat, a school shoe or a snowshoe. Pick **Your own area** on the left to set any area from 0.01 mm² to 1 m².
2. Set the **force** with the slider, or drag up and down on the picture to press harder or more gently. Both objects always get the same force.
3. Read the pressures. The colour tells you what the clay does: it hardly marks, it dents, or the object goes in.
4. Switch the **Level** to **Class 11** to show the pressure in powers of ten. There you can also make the force come from **a resting mass**: the weight of the mass (mass × 9.8 m/s²) is the **thrust**, the force pressing straight down on the surface.
5. Try the **Check yourself** questions at the bottom. They change every time you press **New questions**.

## Worked example

When the page opens, a **needle point** (0.01 mm² = 0.00000001 m²) and a **flat palm** (100 cm² = 0.01 m²) are each pressed with **100 N**, about the weight of a 10 kg bag.

- Needle: P = 100 N ÷ 0.00000001 m² = 10,000,000,000 Pa = **10 GPa**. It goes into the clay.
- Palm: P = 100 N ÷ 0.01 m² = 10,000 Pa = **10 kPa**. It only makes a dent.

The needle makes **1,000,000 times more pressure**, because its area is a million times smaller.

At Class 11, rest a **2 kg** brick on the clay. Its thrust is 2 × 9.8 = **19.6 N**. Lying flat (200 cm²) it presses with 19.6 ÷ 0.02 = **980 Pa**; standing on its end (50 cm²) it presses with **3,920 Pa**, four times as much, because the area is four times smaller.

## Tips

- Sharp tools (knives, needles, nails, the teeth of a saw) have tiny contact areas so a small force makes a big pressure.
- Wide things (snowshoes, tractor tyres, the broad straps of a school bag, the foundations of a building) spread a force out so the pressure stays small.
- Areas often come in cm² or mm². Change them to m² before you divide: 1 cm² = 0.0001 m² and 1 mm² = 0.000001 m².

## FAQ

**Is a pascal a big or a small pressure?** Small. One pascal is one newton on a whole square metre, about the pressure of a sheet of paper lying on a table. That is why kilopascals (kPa, a thousand pascals) and megapascals (MPa, a million) are common.

**What is the difference between thrust and pressure?** Thrust is the total force pushing straight onto a surface, in newtons. Pressure is that force shared out over the area, in pascals. The same thrust can make a small or a huge pressure.

**Are the clay limits real?** No. Real clay depends on how wet and soft it is. The limits are made up so you can see the idea: below a certain pressure nothing happens, and above another the object pushes in.
