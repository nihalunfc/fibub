## What it shows

In a water molecule the oxygen pulls the shared electrons towards itself, so each H is slightly positive and the O slightly negative, with two lone pairs. The slightly positive H of one molecule is attracted to a lone pair on the O of the next: that attraction is a **hydrogen bond**, drawn here as a dashed blue line from H to O. It is much weaker than the O–H bond inside the molecule, but strong enough to change how water behaves.

- In **liquid water** the molecules move and turn all the time. Hydrogen bonds keep breaking and re-forming; the colder the water, the more of them hold at any moment.
- In **ice** every molecule is locked in place with **four** hydrogen bonds: it gives two (through its H atoms) and takes two (on its lone pairs). That holds the molecules apart in an open lattice, so the same box holds fewer molecules: **ice is less dense than water and floats**.

The second card sorts molecules: a hydrogen bond needs an H on N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F.

It follows hydrogen bonding in chemical bonding and states of matter in Class 11 chemistry, and the unusual properties of water met in Classes 9 and 10. ## How to use it

1. **Slide the temperature.** Above 0 °C you see liquid water; at 0 °C and below, ice. The big line counts the hydrogen bonds in the box right now.
2. **Pause** to freeze the picture and count bonds yourself. The facts show molecules in the box and bonds per molecule.
3. **Tap a molecule** in the sorter to see whether it can hydrogen bond, and why.

## Worked example

In the ice picture the box holds 6 × 6 = **36** molecules. Each gives 2 hydrogen bonds, so there are 36 × 2 = **72** bonds, and each molecule takes part in 4 of them (2 given + 2 taken). The liquid box holds **40** molecules, about 11% more, because molecules that are not locked in the lattice can pack closer. (In real water the difference is about 9%.)

Water boils at 100 °C. Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, is heavier but has no real hydrogen bonds and boils at about −60 °C. The hydrogen bonds in water are why it is a liquid at room temperature.

## Check yourself

Three questions: whether a molecule can hydrogen bond, how many bonds each molecule has in ice, and why ice floats. Nothing is saved.

## FAQ

**Is a hydrogen bond a real bond?** It is an attraction between molecules, about a tenth as strong as an O–H bond, but the strongest of the forces between neutral molecules.

**Why is the ice drawn as squares?** Real ice is a 3D lattice of tetrahedra. A flat screen cannot show four neighbours at 109.5°, so the model squares them off; the bond count is the same.
