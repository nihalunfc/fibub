## What it does

Food tests are the simplest way to find out which **biomolecules** a sample contains. Each test uses a reagent that changes colour with one kind of molecule:

- **Iodine test** for **starch**: orange-brown turns **blue-black**.
- **Benedict's test** for **reducing sugars** such as glucose: blue turns green, yellow, orange or **brick red** when heated, depending on how much sugar there is.
- **Biuret test** for **proteins**: blue turns **violet**.
- **Sudan III test** for **fats and oils**: a **red-stained oily layer** floats on top.

This page is a virtual bench. Choose a sample (water, glucose, sucrose, starch, egg white, oil, milk, potato or groundnut), choose a test, add the reagent, heat it if it needs heating, and watch the tube. Each finished test goes into a results table. Then try the **mystery sample**: test it four ways and tick what it contains.

It fits Class 8 and Class 9 Science (food, nutrients and their tests), Class 10 (life processes: nutrition) and Class 11 Biology (biomolecules: carbohydrates, proteins and lipids, and why sucrose is not a reducing sugar). After this, see how proteins fold in [[protein-structure]] and how enzymes break food down in [[enzyme-activity]].

## How to use it

1. Pick a **Sample** from the list. For glucose you can set its **strength** from 0 to 2.5 %.
2. Pick a **test**: Iodine, Benedict's, Biuret or Sudan III. The line underneath says what it finds and what to add.
3. Press **Add reagent**. For Benedict's, then press **Heat in water bath**.
4. Read the result under the tube. It is added to the **Results table**.
5. Press **Fresh tube** to start again with the same sample and test.
6. For the **Mystery sample**, press the button, run all four tests, tick what you think it contains, and press **Check my answer**.

## Worked example

Choose **Glucose solution** at 1.0 % and **Benedict's**. Add the reagent: the tube is blue and nothing happens, because Benedict's needs heat. Heat it: the colour moves through green to **orange**, a fair amount of reducing sugar. At 0.3 % it stops at green (a trace), and at 2.0 % it goes all the way to brick red.

Now choose **Sucrose**. Heated with Benedict's it **stays blue**, because sucrose is not a reducing sugar. Tick **First boil with dilute acid, then neutralise** and test again: the acid splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and the result turns brick red.

Test **Milk** with all four: iodine stays orange-brown (no starch), Benedict's goes orange (lactose is a reducing sugar), biuret goes violet (protein) and Sudan III shows a red layer (fat).

## Tips

- Always run the **water control** with each test. It shows the reagent's own colour, so you can tell a real change.
- Benedict's is **semi-quantitative**: the colour gives a rough amount, not an exact one.
- Use the same volume of sample each time, so results can be compared fairly.

## FAQ

**Why is it called a "reducing" sugar?** Glucose and similar sugars reduce the blue copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution to red copper(I) oxide, which makes the colour change.

**Is the emulsion test for fat the same as Sudan III?** No, it is another fat test: shake the sample with ethanol, then add water; a milky white emulsion means fat. Both are used in schools.

**Are these colours exact?** No. They are drawn to look like typical results; real ones depend on the reagent, the amounts and the heating time.
