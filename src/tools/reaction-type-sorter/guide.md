## What it does

Chemical reactions come in a few common shapes. This page shows real reactions as **particle pictures**: each ball is one atom, labelled with its symbol. Press **Play** or drag the slider and the atoms of the reactants move into their places in the products. Nothing is created or destroyed; the atoms only change partners.

For each reaction you choose its type:

- **Combination:** A + B → AB, such as magnesium burning in oxygen.
- **Decomposition:** AB → A + B, such as limestone breaking down when heated. The page says whether heat, electricity or light supplies the energy.
- **Displacement:** A + BC → AC + B, such as iron pushing copper out of copper sulfate solution.
- **Double displacement:** AB + CD → AD + CB, such as barium chloride and sodium sulfate giving a white precipitate, or an acid and a base neutralising each other.

After each answer you get the rule, what you would see in the lab, and whether the reaction is also **redox**. With the **Class 10** level the redox part uses the oxygen and hydrogen view (oxidation is gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen); with **Class 11** it gives the oxidation-number changes and the electron-transfer view.

It fits the Class 10 science chapter on chemical reactions and equations (types of reactions, oxidation and reduction, corrosion and rancidity) and the Class 11 chapter on redox reactions. To balance an equation of your own, use the [[equation-balancer]]; to see why some metals displace others, try the [[reactivity-series|reactivity series lab]].

## How to use it

1. A reaction appears with its reactants and a particle picture. Press **Play** or drag the slider to see the products form.
2. Tap the type you think it is. The right answer turns green; a wrong pick turns red.
3. Read the explanation, what you would see, and the redox box.
4. Press **Next reaction** for another. Every one of the 20 comes up once before any repeats. Your score is shown at the top and is forgotten when you leave the page.
5. Use the **Reaction** list to jump to any reaction, and the **Class 10 / Class 11** switch to change the redox explanation.

## A worked example

Pick **Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)**. Before the reaction the copper atom is joined to the sulfate group (one S and four O); afterwards the iron atom has taken its place and the copper stands alone. One element has swapped places with another inside a compound, so it is a **displacement** reaction.

It is also redox. In Class 10 terms, iron is more reactive than copper and takes its place in the compound. In Class 11 terms, iron goes from oxidation number 0 to +2 (it loses two electrons, so it is oxidised) and copper goes from +2 to 0 (it gains them, so it is reduced). In the lab you see a brown coat on an iron nail while the blue solution fades to pale green.

Now pick **CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)**: one substance gives two, so it is decomposition (by heat). No oxidation number changes, so it is not redox.

## FAQ

**Is oxidation a fifth type?** Not in this sorting. Redox describes what happens to electrons and can happen alongside the other types: burning magnesium is both combination and redox.

**Why does the hydrogen in CuO + H₂ count as displacement?** Hydrogen takes copper's place next to the oxygen. Many books show this reaction mainly as an example of redox: copper oxide is reduced and hydrogen is oxidised.

**Are these experiments safe to try?** Some are done in school labs under a teacher's supervision. Others, like heating lead nitrate, give off poisonous gases. Do not try them at home.
