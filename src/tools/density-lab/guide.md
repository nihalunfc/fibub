## What it does

Density tells you how much matter is packed into a space: **density = mass ÷ volume**. A steel block and a pine block can be the same size, but the steel one has much more mass, so it is denser.

This lab follows the three methods from the school practical. For a **regular block** you read its sides from a ruler and multiply to get the volume. For an **irregular stone** you lower it into a eureka can and the water that spills out into a measuring cylinder is its volume. For a **liquid** you weigh a measuring cylinder empty and then with liquid in it. Each time the balance gives the mass and the page works out density in g/cm³ and kg/m³. Then you can drop the object in water, oil or syrup to see whether it floats. To go on from here, try [[buoyancy-lab]], [[liquid-pressure]] and [[states-of-matter]].

## How to use it

1. Choose Block, Stone or Liquid.
2. Pick a material (or liquid) and press New object for a different size.
3. Block: read the three ruler values. Stone: slide it fully into the can and read the overflow. Liquid: set the volume in the cylinder.
4. Read the mass on the balance and the density line under the picture.
5. Choose a liquid under Drop it in to see if the object floats or sinks.
6. Answer the quiz questions, which use new numbers each time.

## What each setting does

- **Material:** what the object is made of. Its true density is hidden in the numbers, so you find it from your measurements.
- **Lower the stone:** how far the stone is in the can. Only when it is fully in does the overflow equal its volume.
- **Liquid in the cylinder:** the volume you measure; a bigger sample reduces the percentage uncertainty.
- **Drop it in:** water, cooking oil or golden syrup.

## Worked example

An aluminium block reads 3.0 cm × 4.0 cm × 5.0 cm. Its volume is 3.0 × 4.0 × 5.0 = 60 cm³. The balance reads 162.0 g. The density is 162.0 ÷ 60 = 2.7 g/cm³, which is 2700 kg/m³. In water (1.0 g/cm³) the block sinks, because 2.7 is bigger than 1.0. Pine at 0.55 g/cm³ would float, with about 55 % of it under the surface.

## FAQs

**Why does 1 mL equal 1 cm³?** A millilitre is defined as a cubic centimetre, so the overflow in mL is the stone's volume in cm³.

**Why is the stone's density slightly off the textbook value?** The cylinder reads to 1 mL, so a small stone gives a bigger percentage error. Use a larger stone.

**Does a floating object have a different density from a sinking one?** Yes: it floats when its density is less than the liquid's.
