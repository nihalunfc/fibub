## What it does

Glucose is drawn three ways in Class 12 chemistry: as an open chain (the **Fischer projection**), as a six-membered ring (the **Haworth projection**), and as one of two rings, **α** or **β**, that differ only at the first carbon. This page puts the chain and the ring side by side so you can see how one becomes the other.

- Pick **D-glucose, D-galactose, D-mannose or L-glucose**. The Fischer projection is on the left and the ring on the right.
- **Tap a carbon** (or the C1 to C5 buttons). The same carbon lights up in both drawings, and a line explains where its –OH went.
- Flip between the **α ring and the β ring** and watch only C1 change.
- **Mutarotation**: start with pure α or pure β glucose in water, play or drag the time, and see the rotation of polarised light drift to +52.7° as the ring opens and closes.

It fits **Class 12 Chemistry: biomolecules** (glucose structure, cyclic forms, anomers, D and L configuration, mutarotation). For the stereocentres themselves see [[chirality-checker]], and for the reactions that link the aldehyde group to other families use the [[organic-reaction-map]].

## How to use it

1. **Choose a sugar** and **α or β**.
2. **Tap a carbon.** The table lists every carbon with the side of its –OH in the Fischer projection and the direction in the ring; the row you picked is bold.
3. Read the rule: **right in Fischer → down in Haworth; left → up**. C5's –OH closes the ring, so it becomes the ring O and its CH₂OH points up for a D sugar and down for an L sugar.
4. At C1, **α** puts the –OH on the opposite face to CH₂OH and **β** on the same face.
5. In **Mutarotation**, press *Start pure α* or *Start pure β*, then **Play** or drag the time slider up to 6 hours. The line under the graph fills in the mixture rule with the live shares of α and β.
6. **Check yourself** gives five fresh questions: Fischer to Haworth, α or β, a rotation to work out, a share of α from a measured rotation, and D or L.

## Worked example

Pick **D-glucose**. In the Fischer projection the –OH groups on C2, C3, C4 and C5 are on the right, left, right and right. In the ring, C2 points **down**, C3 **up**, C4 **down**, and the CH₂OH on C5 **up** (it is a D sugar). In the **β** ring the C1 –OH points **up**, so the groups alternate all the way round: this is why β-glucose is the more stable form.

Now the mutarotation numbers. α is +112°, β is +18.7°, and the equilibrium mixture is +52.7°. The share of α at equilibrium is (52.7 − 18.7) ÷ (112 − 18.7) = 34 ÷ 93.3 = **0.364**, so about **36 % α and 64 % β**. Drag the time to 60 minutes after starting with pure α: the page shows about 67 % α and a rotation of about +82°.

## Tips

- Galactose differs from glucose only at C4 and mannose only at C2: such sugars are **epimers**.
- D and L describe the side of the C5 –OH in the Fischer projection, not the direction the sugar turns light. D-glucose happens to be dextrorotatory (+), but D-fructose is laevorotatory (−).
- The open chain is always there, but under 1 %; that is enough for glucose to reduce Tollens' and Fehling's reagents.

## FAQ

**Why can't glucose's ring be seen in its reactions with NaHSO₃?** Because so little open chain is present. That puzzle is one of the clues that led chemists to the ring structure.

**Is the mutarotation speed real?** The rotations are real rounded values; the speed is illustrative. Temperature, acid and base all change it.

**What about fructose?** Fructose is a ketose that forms a five-membered (furanose) ring, which this page does not draw.
