## What it does

A light microscope uses two lenses: the **eyepiece** you look into and the **objective** close to the slide. Each one magnifies, so the **total magnification = eyepiece × objective**. This page draws the view down a school microscope. Pick a slide (onion peel, cheek cells or pond water), choose the 4×, 10× or 40× objective, turn the **focus knob** until the picture is sharp, set the **light**, and add a **stain** to bring out the nucleus. Drag the picture to move the slide around, just as you would push the slide on the stage.

It follows the school topics Class 8 Science, the invisible living world (using a microscope, cells and microorganisms) and Class 9 Biology, the fundamental unit of life (plant and animal cells). After looking at real-looking cells, the [[cell-explorer]] labels each part of a cell, and [[microscope-telescope]] shows how the two lenses form the image with ray diagrams.

## How to use it

1. Choose a **slide**.
2. Start with the **4×** objective. Move the **focus knob** slider until the line under the picture says "in focus".
3. Switch to **10×**, then **40×**. You only need a small turn of the focus knob each time, and at 40× the picture goes blurry with the slightest turn.
4. Tick **Add a stain**: iodine for onion peel (brown nucleus), methylene blue for cheek cells (blue nucleus).
5. Lower the **light** to see why a bright, even lamp matters.
6. Drag the picture to explore. The scale bar at the bottom shows how big things really are.

The **Level** switch (Class 9 and up) adds the **field of view** (how wide the circle is on the slide) and a ruler: choose "Drag measures" and drag across a cell to read its real size in micrometres (1 µm = 1/1000 mm).

## Worked example

With the 10× eyepiece and the 40× objective, total magnification = 10 × 40 = **400×**. The field of view is the field number divided by the objective: 18 mm ÷ 40 = **0.45 mm**, which is 450 µm. An onion cell that stretches across about half the circle is therefore about 225 µm long. With the 4× objective the total is 40× and the field of view is 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5 mm, ten times wider, so you see many more cells, each one smaller.

If a drawing of a cell is 12 mm wide at 400×, its real width is 12 ÷ 400 = 0.03 mm = 30 µm.

## Check yourself

Five fresh questions: total magnification, how the field of view changes with the objective, real size from a drawing, which lens to start with, and why we stain. Nothing is saved.

## Tips

- Always focus at low power first, then move up. The view is wider and easier to find.
- Plant cells look like bricks with thick walls; animal cells are rounder with thin edges and no wall.
- Things that move in pond water are alive. Bacteria are so small that even at 400× they are only dots.

## FAQ

**Why does the picture get darker at high power?** A strong objective gathers light from a smaller area, so you often need more light at 40×. On this page the light slider shows the effect.

**Are these real photographs?** No. The page draws the specimens with rounded sizes so they look like what you would see, without copying any images.
