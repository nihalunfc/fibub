## What it does

Metals such as sodium and magnesium have only one, two or three electrons in their outer shell. Non-metals such as chlorine and oxygen are a few electrons short of eight. When they meet, the metal **gives** its outer electrons to the non-metal. Both end up with a full outer shell, like a noble gas, but now the metal atom is a **positive ion** and the non-metal atom is a **negative ion**. The strong pull between opposite charges is the **ionic bond**.

This page draws it as a **dot-and-cross** picture. Pick a metal and a non-metal. The page puts down as many atoms of each as are needed (one Mg and two Cl, two Al and three O …). Press **Move one electron** and one metal electron (a cross ×) jumps to a non-metal atom (whose own electrons are dots •). When an atom's outer shell is full its ring turns green and its charge appears. The steps explain the shells, why the numbers of atoms are what they are, and the final formula and name.

It fits the Class 10 science chapter on metals and non-metals (how metals and non-metals react, properties of ionic compounds) and the Class 11 chapter on chemical bonding. Covalent bonds, where electrons are shared instead, are in [[lewis-structures]]; the same charges give formulas of many more compounds in [[formula-writer]], and which metals give up electrons most easily is shown in [[reactivity-series]].

## How to use it

1. Choose a **metal** and a **non-metal**.
2. Press **Move one electron** again and again, or **Move them all**.
3. Read the shells next to each atom: before the move they are the atom's shells, after it the ion's.
4. **The compound** card gives the formula, name, ions and number of electrons moved.
5. **Start again** puts the electrons back.

## A worked example

Pick **magnesium** and **chlorine**.

- Mg is (2, 8, 2): it has 2 outer electrons to give.
- Cl is (2, 8, 7): each chlorine atom needs 1.
- So one Mg gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms: Mg²⁺ (2, 8) and two Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8).
- The formula is **MgCl₂**, magnesium chloride, and 2 electrons moved.

With **aluminium** and **oxygen**, each Al gives 3 and each O takes 2. The smallest number that works for both is 6, so two Al and three O: **Al₂O₃**.

## Tips

- The charge on the metal ion equals the number of electrons it gives; the charge on the non-metal ion equals the number it takes.
- The total charge of the compound is always zero.
- Ions of these elements have the same shells as a noble gas: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, O²⁻ and F⁻ are all (2, 8), like neon.

## FAQ

**Does the electron really jump like that?** The picture is a model for counting. In a real reaction, a huge number of atoms react at once and the ions settle into a lattice.
