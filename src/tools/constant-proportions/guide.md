## What it does

Is the water in rain the same as the water in the sea, once it is purified? Chemists around 1800 argued about this. Joseph Proust showed that a pure compound always contains the **same elements in the same proportion by mass**, wherever it comes from and however it is made. This is the **law of constant proportions** (or definite proportions).

This page is a virtual lab. Pick a compound and where the sample came from, choose its mass, and press **Analyse**. The page splits the sample into the mass of each element, as read on a balance that shows 0.01 g, and adds a row to the table. Do it for several sources and sizes: the masses change, the ratio does not. Each row also shows that the element masses add up to the sample, the **law of conservation of mass**.

The **Class 11** level adds the **law of multiple proportions** (John Dalton): when two elements make more than one compound, such as CO and CO₂, the masses of oxygen that join 1 g of carbon are in a simple ratio, here 1 : 2.

It fits the Class 9 science chapter *Atomic Foundations of Matter* and the Class 11 chemistry chapter *Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry*. Writing the formulas is in [[formula-writer]], and turning masses into moles is in [[mole-bridge]].

## How to use it

1. Choose a **compound** and **where it came from**.
2. Set the sample mass in grams.
3. Press **Analyse this sample**. The source moves on to the next one, so pressing again gives a new source.
4. Read the table and the summary under it. **Clear table** starts again.
5. Switch to **Class 11** to compare compounds of the same two elements.

## A worked example

Analyse 18 g of water. The balance reads about 2.01 g of hydrogen and 15.99 g of oxygen, so H : O ≈ 1 : 7.94. Now analyse 50 g of sea water (distilled): about 5.60 g of hydrogen and 44.40 g of oxygen, again 1 : 7.94. With school rounded masses (H = 1, O = 16), water H₂O has 2 g of hydrogen to 16 g of oxygen, which is the 1 : 8 quoted in textbooks.

In Class 11 mode, choose C and O. One gram of carbon joins 1.332 g of oxygen in CO and 2.664 g in CO₂: a ratio of 1 : 2.

## Tips

- The tiny differences between rows come from the balance (±0.01 g), not from the compound.
- To find how much of an element is in a sample, use its share: oxygen is 8 parts of 9 in water.
- Mixtures (like air or sea water before purifying) do not follow this law: their make-up can change.

## FAQ

**Is the ratio exactly 1 : 8 for water?** Almost: with today's atomic weights it is 1 : 7.94. The 1 : 8 comes from rounding hydrogen to 1 and oxygen to 16.
