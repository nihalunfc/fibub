## What it does

Oil and water don't mix, so water alone cannot wash oily dirt out of clothes. Soap can, because each soap molecule has two very different ends: a long **oil-loving tail** and a charged **water-loving head**. This page shows what happens in a simple model you can change:

- **S** sets how many soap molecules go into the water, **H** how many hardness ions (calcium and magnesium) it has, and **O** how many drops of oily dirt are on the cloth,
- the first molecules line up at the **surface**, tails out of the water,
- once the surface is full, the rest gather into **micelles**: balls with the tails inside and the heads outside. Each one picks up a drop of oil and carries it away,
- in **hard water**, each Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ion takes two soap molecules out as grey **scum**, so less soap is left to clean,
- switch to **Detergent** and the scum disappears, because the calcium and magnesium salts of detergents dissolve.

It follows the Class 10 chapter on **carbon and its compounds** (soaps and detergents, micelles, hard water).

Soap is an ester split by alkali, the reverse of [[esterification]]; the alkali, sodium hydroxide, comes from the [[chlor-alkali]] process. Why the tail avoids water comes down to polarity, shown in [[dipole-polarity]].

## How to use it

1. Move the **S**, **H** and **O** sliders, or type numbers in the boxes.
2. Choose **Soap** or **Detergent**.
3. The big line says whether the cloth is clean; the line under it counts micelles, free molecules and scum, and (for soap in hard water) the least soap that would clean it.
4. Try the quick buttons: **Soft water**, **Hard water** and **Too little soap**.

## A worked example

In this model, the first 20 free soap molecules stay single at the surface, every 24 more make one micelle, and each micelle holds one oil drop.

**Soft water**, 300 soap molecules, 4 oil drops: no scum, so all 300 are free. (300 − 20) ÷ 24 = 11.7, so 11 micelles form, more than enough for 4 drops: **clean**.

**Hard water** with 120 calcium ions: scum uses 2 × 120 = 240 soap molecules, leaving 60. (60 − 20) ÷ 24 = 1.7, so only 1 micelle forms and 3 drops stay on the cloth. The least soap that cleans is 240 + 20 + 4 × 24 = **356**. With a detergent, the same 300 molecules make 11 micelles and clean it.

## Tips

- The numbers are made up to keep the picture small; a real micelle has around 50 to 100 molecules, and a real wash has billions.
- Hard water can be softened (by boiling for temporary hardness, or washing soda) so soap is not wasted.
- Soap works best in soft water; detergents work in both, which is why washing powders use them.

## Check yourself

Four fresh questions each time, using the model's numbers: soap lost as scum, micelles formed, the least soap needed, and the parts of a soap molecule. Nothing is saved.

## FAQ

**Is this a measurement of real soap?** No. It is a counting model to show the ideas: surface first, then micelles, and scum in hard water. The real amounts depend on the soap and the water.

**Is this from the NCERT book?** It covers the same topic with fibub's own model and pictures. fibub is not part of NCERT.
