## What it does

A **floral formula** describes a whole flower in one line of symbols, and a **floral diagram** shows the same flower as if you sliced across the bud and looked down from above. This page makes both from your choices:

- **Symmetry:** ⊕ actinomorphic (radial) or % zygomorphic (one plane of symmetry).
- **Sex:** ⚥ bisexual, ♂ male (stamens only) or ♀ female (carpels only).
- **Calyx K** (sepals) and **corolla C** (petals), or **perianth P** when sepals and petals look alike (as in lilies).
- **Androecium A** (stamens), with an option for **epipetalous** stamens attached to the petals.
- **Gynoecium G** (carpels), joined (syncarpous) or free, with the ovary **superior** (line under G) or **inferior** (line over G).

Counts are written as in a real formula: 5 for five free parts, (5) for five joined parts, 1+2+(2) for groups, 3+3 for two whorls, ∞ for many. The diagram draws the whorls as rings (sepals outside, then petals, stamens, and the ovary in the middle with one chamber per joined carpel), with joined parts linked by a grey band and the mother axis as a dot at the top.

It fits the Class 11 biology chapter on the morphology of flowering plants (the flower, and the description of some important families). See where flowers fit in a plant's life cycle on the [[life-cycle-wheel]], and the [[five-kingdoms]] sorter for where plants sit among living things.

## How to use it

1. **Start from** one of four families, or change anything to describe your own flower.
2. Tap the **symmetry** and **sex** buttons. Tick **Sepals and petals look alike** to use P instead of K and C.
3. Type each whorl. The box under the inputs explains the notation; a mistake (an unclosed bracket, a letter) shows a message instead of the formula.
4. Tick **Carpels joined** and **epipetalous** as needed, and choose the **ovary** position.
5. Read the formula in proper notation (counts as subscripts, the ovary line, an arc over C and A when stamens are on the petals), the same formula in plain text, the diagram, and a description in words.

## Worked example

Pick the **pea family (Fabaceae)**. The formula reads % ⚥ K(5) C1+2+(2) A(9)+1 G1, with a line under G:

- %: zygomorphic, so the diagram has a dashed line of symmetry;
- K(5): five sepals joined into a cup;
- C1+2+(2): one large standard petal (drawn bigger, at the top), two wings, and two keel petals joined (linked by the grey band);
- A(9)+1: ten stamens, nine joined and one free ("diadelphous");
- G1: a single carpel with a superior ovary.

Now pick the **potato family**. The arc over C(5) A5 shows that the stamens grow from the petal tube. Untick epipetalous and the arc goes.

## Tips

- Sepals and petals usually alternate, and so do petals and stamens: the diagram staggers the rings for that reason.
- For a monocot, look for parts in threes: P(3+3) A3+3 G(3) in the lily family.
- A unisexual flower leaves out what it lacks: a male flower has no G, a female flower no A.

## FAQ

**Why is my flower's diagram not exactly like a textbook one?** Textbook diagrams are drawn by hand for each family, with parts in their real positions and shapes. This page places parts evenly round each ring, so it shows the counts, fusion and order correctly but not every detail of shape.

**Can I use other symbols?** The page uses the common symbols of school botany. Some books write Ca and Co for calyx and corolla, or Br for a bract; they mean the same thing.
