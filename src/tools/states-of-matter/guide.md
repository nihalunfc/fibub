## What it does

Everything is made of tiny particles that are always moving. How strongly they hold on to each other, and how fast they move, decides whether a substance is a **solid**, a **liquid** or a **gas**. This page puts a few particles in a box and lets you **heat** or **cool** them. In a solid they only shake about fixed places; heat them to the **melting point** and they break free and slide past each other as a liquid; heat them to the **boiling point** and they fly apart as a gas that fills the box. A second view shows **diffusion**: two colours of particles spreading into each other once a divider is lifted.

It follows the school topics on matter: Class 8 Science, the particulate nature of matter (states of matter and the particle model), and Class 9 Science, Matter in Our Surroundings (characteristics of particles, states of matter, change of state, melting and boiling points, the kelvin scale, diffusion and evaporation). Next, [[heating-curve]] shows why the temperature stops rising while ice melts, and [[kinetic-theory]] shows how gas particles make pressure. For the maths of particles wandering, see [[random-walk-diffusion]].

## How to use it

1. Pick a **substance**. Its rounded melting and boiling points are shown under the slider.
2. Press **Heat (+10 °C)** or **Cool (−10 °C)**, or drag the **temperature** slider. The buttons stop exactly at the melting and boiling points so you can see two states together.
3. Read the state, the spacing of the particles and how they move. Switch the **Level** to **Class 9** to add the temperature in kelvin and the average energy of a particle.
4. Choose **Diffusion**, pick gases or liquids and a temperature, and press **Lift the divider**. The page shows how mixed the colours are and how many seconds they took to reach 80%. **Start again** puts the divider back.
5. **Pause** freezes the particles.

## Worked example

The page opens with water at 20 °C: a **liquid**. Press **Cool** twice: at 0 °C the water is at its melting point, part solid and part liquid; once more and it is ice at −10 °C, with its particles locked in a pattern. Heat it back up and keep going: at 100 °C it boils, and at 110 °C it is steam.

On the **Class 9** level at 20 °C the page shows 293 K (20 + 273; 293.15 K with the exact 273.15) and an average particle energy of (3/2)kT = 6.07 × 10⁻²¹ J. At 313 K (40 °C) the energy is about 7% higher, and it would double at 586 K.

## Tips

- During melting and boiling the temperature stays the same even though you keep heating: the heat goes into pulling particles apart (latent heat).
- Evaporation happens at any temperature, from the surface only; boiling happens all through the liquid at the boiling point.
- Gases diffuse much faster than liquids because their particles move freely and have lots of space between them.

## FAQ

**Are real particles this big?** No. A drop of water holds about 10²¹ particles. The picture uses a few dozen, drawn huge, and a simple made-up motion that shows the idea.

**Why do the boiling points change on a mountain?** Lower air pressure lets particles escape more easily, so water boils below 100 °C. The page uses normal air pressure.
