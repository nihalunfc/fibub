## What it does

Earth's outer shell is broken into plates that creep over the softer rock below, a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Where two plates meet, one of three things happens: they pull apart (divergent), push together (convergent) or slide sideways (transform). Almost all earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain belts sit along these edges.

Choose a boundary and the page draws a side view and a view from above. It moves the plates at the speed you set, over the time you choose, and shows how far they travel, where earthquakes happen at their true depth, and what new features form. To locate real quakes, use [[earthquake-locator]] and [[seismogram-viewer]], and for the long timescales see [[geological-time]].

## How to use it

1. Pick a boundary from the menu. The real example and its usual speed appear.
2. Press Play, or drag the time slider, to move the plates.
3. Change the speed slider and see the distance moved change.
4. Look at the coloured earthquake dots: warm ones are shallow, red ones are deep.
5. Try every boundary, then answer the quiz.

## What each setting does

- **Boundary:** ocean ridge and rift valley (divergent), ocean-continent, ocean-ocean and continent-continent (convergent), and transform.
- **Speed:** how fast one plate moves relative to the other, in cm per year. 1 cm per year is 10 km per million years.
- **Time:** how many million years have passed.
- **Stripes at the ridge:** the field of Earth flips now and then. New rock records the direction at the moment it cools, so dark and light bands (normal and reversed) match on both sides of the ridge.

## Worked example

The Mid-Atlantic Ridge opens at about 2.5 cm per year. In 100 million years that is 2.5 × 100 × 10 = 2,500 km. To open an ocean about 4,500 km wide takes 4,500 ÷ (2.5 × 10) = 180 million years, which matches when the Atlantic started to open.

## FAQs

**Why are deep earthquakes only at subduction zones?** Only there does cold rock sink far into the mantle. At ridges and transform faults the rock is thin or hot, so quakes are shallow (under about 15 km).

**Do the real speeds change?** Yes, a little over millions of years. The values here are rounded averages, so treat them as "about".

**Why do the stripes mirror each other?** Each side of a ridge gets the same new rock at the same moment, then moves away at the same speed.
