## What it does

Wheel nuts (or wheel bolts) have to be tight enough that the wheel cannot work loose, but not so tight that the studs stretch or the brake disc warps. Car makers give a figure, called the **torque**, in newton metres (N·m) or pound-feet (lbf·ft). This tool helps you use that number when all you have is the wheel brace from the boot. Pick your kind of vehicle or the stud thread to see a **typical** range, or type the figure from your handbook. The tool converts it between N·m, lbf·ft and kgf·m, works out how hard to push on a brace of your length, compares that with your body weight, and draws the star order to tighten 4, 5, 6 or 8 nuts.

It goes with the [[change-flat-tyre|change a flat tyre]] lesson, and with [[tyre-pressure]] to set the spare's pressure afterwards; for bolts in machines and joints, use [[bolt-torque-calculator]].

## How to use it

1. **How tight?** Choose "My handbook value" and type the torque from your handbook, or pick a kind of vehicle or a stud thread to see a typical range. The middle of the range is filled in, but your handbook value always wins.
2. **Push on the brace.** Measure the brace from the centre of the nut to where you push, and type it in cm or inches. Add your weight if you want a feel for how hard that is.
3. **Tightening order.** Choose how many nuts the wheel has. Follow the numbers on the picture, twice round: snug first, then full torque.

## What each number means

- **Torque (N·m, lbf·ft, kgf·m):** the turning effect on the nut. 1 lbf·ft is about 1.36 N·m; 1 kgf·m is about 9.81 N·m.
- **Brace length:** the lever. Torque = push × length, so a brace twice as long needs half the push.
- **Push (N, kgf, lbf):** the force at right angles to the handle at the point you measured to. 1 kgf is the weight of 1 kg, about 9.81 N.
- **Share of body weight:** a rough feel. If it is more than about half, use a longer bar or a torque wrench instead of bouncing on the brace.
- **Star order:** the order to tighten the nuts, going across the wheel so it pulls on evenly.

## Worked example

Your handbook says 110 N·m. That is 110 ÷ 1.3558 = 81.1 lbf·ft, or 110 ÷ 9.807 = 11.2 kgf·m. Your brace is 40 cm (0.40 m) from the nut to your hand, so the push is 110 ÷ 0.40 = **275 N**, which is 275 ÷ 9.807 = 28.0 kgf or 61.8 lbf. For a 70 kg person (about 687 N), that is about 40 % of body weight: a firm lean, not a jump. On five nuts, numbered clockwise from the top, you tighten 1, 3, 5, 2, 4.

## FAQs

**Is a brace and my weight as good as a torque wrench?** No, it is an estimate: you may not push exactly at right angles or at exactly the length you measured. It gets you close at the roadside. Check with a torque wrench as soon as you can.

**Why does the typical range matter if the handbook wins?** It catches mistakes, such as reading 110 lbf·ft as 110 N·m (that is 149 N·m, a big difference) or using a figure meant for a different vehicle.

**Should I re-check the nuts?** Yes, after 50 to 100 km (30 to 60 miles), especially with alloy wheels or new wheels, as the wheel beds in.

**Should I oil the threads?** Not unless the handbook says so. Torque figures are for clean, dry threads; oil lets the same torque clamp much harder.
