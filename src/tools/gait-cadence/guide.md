## What it does

Every step jolts your body up and down. A phone's accelerometer feels those jolts, so a short recording while you walk holds your **cadence** (steps per minute) and how regular your steps are. Clinicians and coaches use the same idea with research-grade sensors. This page does it with your own phone, in the browser: it records the motion sensor, or opens a CSV file of time and acceleration from any sensor app, then finds each step and reports cadence, step and stride times, their variability and a spectrum.

Find the step rhythm in any signal with the [[fft-workbench]], and turn your sessions into a training load with the [[training-load|training load tracker]].

## How to use it

1. **Record on this phone:** hold the phone against your waist or put it in a front trouser pocket, press **Start recording**, walk at least 20 steps on flat ground at your usual pace, then press **Stop**. On an iPhone the browser asks to allow motion access first.
2. **Or open a CSV** with columns t, ax, ay, az (m/s²), from a sensor-logging app or a lab device. Time can be in seconds or milliseconds; the page decides from the size of the time steps.
3. Adjust the three settings if steps are missed or doubled:
   - **Low-pass cut-off**: smooths away jolts faster than steps. 3 Hz suits walking; try 4 to 5 Hz for running.
   - **Shortest step**: peaks closer together are merged, the lower one dropped.
   - **Peak threshold**: how far above the mean, in standard deviations of the smoothed signal, a peak must reach.
4. Read the results and the three charts, and download the steps as CSV or the page as an image, PDF or Word file.

## How it works

The page takes the magnitude √(ax² + ay² + az²) of each reading, so the phone's direction does not matter, and resamples it evenly at the median sampling rate. It removes the mean (mostly gravity) and applies a second-order **Butterworth** low-pass filter forwards and backwards, so the peaks do not shift in time. Each remaining peak above the threshold, and at least the shortest step after a higher one, is a step. This is the same method as SciPy's filtfilt and find_peaks, and the test suite checks that both give identical steps.

- **Cadence** = 60 × (steps − 1) ÷ time from the first step to the last.
- **Step time** is the time between consecutive steps; **stride time** spans two steps (the same foot to the same foot). Their coefficient of variation (CV = SD ÷ mean) measures how regular the gait is (Hausdorff, 2005).
- The **spectrum** shows how strongly each frequency is present; its highest peak between 0.5 and 4 Hz is the step frequency, a second, independent estimate of cadence.

## Worked example

The page opens with a made-up 20-second walk sampled at 50 readings a second. It finds **37 steps** and a cadence of **111.2 steps/min**. The mean step time is 539 ms with an SD of 15.5 ms, a CV of 2.87 %. The mean stride time is 1080 ms with a CV of 2.29 %. The spectrum peaks at 1.855 Hz, which is 111.3 steps/min, so the two methods agree.

## Tips

- Walk on a straight, flat path and leave out the first and last few steps, where you speed up and slow down.
- A phone loose in a bag or swinging in a hand gives a messy signal. Keep it close to the body.
- Compare like with like: the same placement, path and footwear each time.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is my recording uploaded?** No. Readings go from the sensor into this page and nowhere else, and nothing is stored after you close it.

**Why does my phone record nothing?** Some browsers and computers have no motion sensor, and some only give access on secure pages after you allow it. Use a sensor-logging app and open its CSV instead.

**Is a CV of 3 % normal?** Healthy adults walking steadily often have low step-time variability, but values depend on the sensor, the method and the person. This is a teaching tool, not a clinical test; ask a qualified professional about your gait.
