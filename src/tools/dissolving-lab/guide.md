## What it does

When you stir sugar into tea it vanishes: it **dissolves**. The sugar is the **solute**, the water the **solvent** and the sweet tea a **solution**. But water cannot take an endless amount. Keep adding and a point comes when no more dissolves and the extra just sits at the bottom: the solution is **saturated**. How much it takes before that happens is the **solubility**, written as grams of solute in 100 g of water, and it depends on the temperature.

This lab gives you a beaker of water, a thermometer and three solids: **table salt**, **sugar** and **potassium nitrate** (KNO₃). Add spoons and watch the colour deepen until crystals start to pile up. The **solubility curve** below shows your beaker as a dot: under the line there is room for more, on the line it is saturated. Change the temperature and the dot and the crystals move with it. A second view, **A gas**, shows the opposite rule for gases: warm water holds **less** oxygen and carbon dioxide, which is why fish struggle in warm ponds and warm drinks go flat.

It fits Class 8 Science (solutes, solvents and solutions; saturated and unsaturated solutions; the effect of temperature), Class 9 (concentration of a solution as mass percent, crystallisation) and Class 12 Chemistry (solubility of solids and gases, molality). For what happens to the particles themselves, see [[states-of-matter]]; for water moving through a membrane into a stronger solution, try the [[osmosis-lab]]; and for how temperature changes when a solid melts, the [[heating-curve]].

## How to use it

1. Choose a **Level**. **Class 8** shows grams and saturation. **Class 9 to 12** adds the **mass percent** and the **molality** of the solution.
2. Under **A solid**, pick **Salt**, **Sugar** or **KNO₃**, and how much **water** is in the beaker (50, 100 or 200 g).
3. Set the **temperature** with the slider or type it (0 to 100 °C).
4. Press **Add 1 spoon (5 g)** or **Add 5 spoons**, or type the grams added. **Empty** starts again.
5. Read the big line: *unsaturated* (with how much more will dissolve), *just saturated*, or *saturated* (with how much is left at the bottom).
6. Below the curve, a line works out how many grams of crystals come out if a saturated beaker is cooled by 40 °C.
7. Switch to **A gas** and move the water temperature (0 to 40 °C) to see oxygen for fish and carbon dioxide in a drink.

## Worked example

The page opens with 100 g of water at 20 °C. Pick **Salt** and press **Add 5 spoons** once (25 g): it all dissolves, with room for 11.4 g more, because salt's solubility at 20 °C is about 36.4 g per 100 g of water. Press **Add 1 spoon** twice more (35 g): still unsaturated. One more spoon makes 40 g, and the page says *Saturated: 3.6 g will not dissolve*. On the Class 9 to 12 level the mass percent is 36.4 ÷ (36.4 + 100) × 100 = 26.7%.

Now try **KNO₃** at 60 °C: about 110 g dissolves in 100 g of water. Cool it to 20 °C, where only about 31 g can stay dissolved, and about 78 g of crystals come out. Salt's flat curve gives back hardly anything, which is why salt is made by **evaporating** sea water rather than by cooling it.

## Tips

- Solubility is always "per 100 g of water". Double the water and you can dissolve double the solid, but the solubility stays the same.
- For most solids, solubility rises with temperature, but by very different amounts: compare the three curves.
- Gases are the other way round. Warm water has more energetic particles, and gas escapes more easily.
- Stirring and crushing make a solid dissolve **faster**, but they do not change how **much** can dissolve.

## FAQ

**Are these exact values?** No. The curves are smooth rounded fits that stay within a few percent of common textbook-style values. A real experiment depends on purity and how long you stir.

**Can a solution hold more than its solubility?** Carefully cooled solutions can briefly be *supersaturated*, then crystallise all at once when disturbed. This lab leaves that out.
