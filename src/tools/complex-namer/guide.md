## What it does

A **coordination compound** has a metal atom or ion at the centre, held by **ligands**: ions or molecules that each give it a lone pair of electrons. The metal and its ligands are written inside square brackets, such as [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺, and any **counter ions** outside the brackets balance the charge.

- **Build it.** Pick a metal, its oxidation state and up to six donor atoms' worth of ligands with the + and − buttons. Bidentate ligands (ethane-1,2-diamine, oxalato) count twice. If the complex is charged, pick a counter ion; the page offers only the ones with the right sign.
- **Formula and name.** The formula follows the bracket rules; the name follows the IUPAC rules schools use: ligands alphabetically, -ido for anions, aqua, ammine and carbonyl for neutral ones, the metal's oxidation state in Roman numerals, and -ate when the complex is negative (ferrate, cuprate, argentate).
- **Facts.** Coordination number and the usual shape for it, the charge on the complex ion with the working, how many ions one formula unit gives in water, and **Werner's test**: how many moles of AgCl silver nitrate precipitates, because only the chloride outside the brackets is free.
- **Examples.** Tap Werner's three cobalt ammine chlorides, the two potassium cyanidoferrates, nickel tetracarbonyl and more.

This fits **Class 12 Chemistry: coordination compounds** (Werner's theory, definitions such as ligand, coordination number and oxidation number, IUPAC nomenclature). To see why these complexes are coloured and magnetic, go on to the [[crystal-field-calculator]]; to practise oxidation states in general, use the [[oxidation-number]] tool; and to see the shapes, try [[vsepr-shapes]].

## How to use it

1. Tap an example, or choose a metal and an oxidation state.
2. Add ligands with **+** and remove them with **−**. The coordination number so far is shown above the list; + is greyed out at 6.
3. Choose a counter ion if the complex is charged.
4. Read the formula, the name and the facts. Follow the charge working under them.
5. Try the **Check yourself** questions.

## Worked example

Werner's CoCl₃·5NH₃: cobalt(III) with five ammonia and one chloride inside the brackets. Charge = +3 + 1 × (−1) = +2, so two chloride ions sit outside: **[CoCl(NH₃)₅]Cl₂**, named **pentaamminechloridocobalt(III) chloride**. The coordination number is 6 (octahedral); it gives 3 ions in water, and 1 mol precipitates 2 mol of AgCl. CoCl₃·6NH₃ gives 3 mol and CoCl₃·4NH₃ only 1, which is how Werner worked out which chlorides were bound to the metal.

K₃[Fe(CN)₆]: +3 + 6 × (−1) = −3, so the complex is an anion and the name ends in -ate: **potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III)**. Change iron to +2 and you get K₄[Fe(CN)₆], potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II).

[Co(en)₃]Cl₃: three bidentate ethane-1,2-diamine ligands give coordination number 6; the name is **tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)cobalt(III) chloride**.

## Tips

- "Ammine" (two m's) is ammonia as a ligand; "amine" is an organic compound.
- The prefixes di-, tri-, tetra- don't count when you sort ligands: tetraammine comes before dichlorido because a comes before c.
- Older names (chloro, cyano) are still seen; this page uses the current -ido endings.
